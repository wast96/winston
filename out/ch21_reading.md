## Du Yuesheng's Ties to Dai Li and the Juntong

{d} By Guo Xu

### I. When Du and Dai First Met

The exact time when Du Yuesheng and Dai Li first became acquainted I do not know; I understand only that the two of them had met long before, around 1924 — that is, before Dai entered the sixth class of the Whampoa Military Academy, when he was living the life of a drifter in Shanghai.

When Dai Li was in his twenties, back in his native place, the town of Xiakou in Jiangshan county, Zhejiang, he was down and out and utterly at loose ends, running a gambling stall to cheat people of their money; when he was found out he fled to Shanghai, where he lodged in the home of Zhang Guanfu (also called Zhang Gunfu) — a cousin who was at the same time the husband of another of his cousins, and who was then a clerk at the Commercial Press — sleeping on the floorboards. His cousin thoroughly disliked him and would often bar the door against him; Shen Zui has touched on this in his article "The Dai Li I Knew," in volume 22 of the national CPPCC's Selected Historical and Cultural Materials. The cousin disliked him chiefly because of his mixing with Du Yuesheng. Dai had by then met Du and sworn brotherhood with him; Du was eight years Dai's senior, and Dai called him "Third Brother," the two coming and going constantly. His cousin loathed this, and would often say to her husband that Dai kept no honest occupation and had sworn brotherhood with "Fruit Ah-san" (Du's old nickname), the husband of the hairdressing "elder sister" — that is, Du's principal wife, née Shen, who dressed the hair of Huang Jinrong's wife; it was too disreputable for words. She had even reproached Dai to his face, but Dai would not mend his ways and would often come home only deep in the night, so she frequently shut the door on him. It was only then that Dai left the Zhang household and went off to Guangdong to sit the entrance examination for the Whampoa Academy. This whole matter was told to us by Zhang Guanfu in 1941, when I was serving in Chongqing as head of the general-accounting section of the Juntong's accounting office, on an occasion when Zhang Guanfu, Lin Yaomin (both then deputy directors of the accounting office) and I went together to Du's house at Wangshan.

Dai Li studied for a time in the cavalry section of the sixth Whampoa class but did not graduate; he left the school to do secret-service work gathering intelligence for Chiang Kai-shek. Later, at the General Headquarters of the Army, Navy and Air Force of the National Revolutionary Army, Chiang set up a secret investigation group, appointing his personal aide Hu Jing'an as its head, with Dai Li, Xu Zhongwu, Cheng Xichao, Dongfang Bai and others as its members. This was the first rudimentary establishment of a small secret-service apparatus, to investigate for Chiang the activities of dissident elements, whether the various unit commanders under him were loyal, and the general state of military discipline. Before long, when Chiang stepped down for the first time, on the eve of his departure for Japan he disbanded this investigation group and issued it three months' maintenance funds. Unexpectedly, once Hu Jing'an had drawn these funds, he kept Dai, Xu, Cheng and the rest lodged at his own home in Shanghai, providing only their board while withholding the whole of the three months' salary due them, and quietly took his own family back to Jiangxi. When Dai, Xu and the others had run through their food money, they carried Hu's clothes-trunk to a pawnshop to pawn it; but when the trunk was opened it held nothing but children's clothes, and the pawnshop would not take it. Xu Zhongwu, knowing that Dai and Du Yuesheng were sworn brothers, egged Dai on from the side to ask Du for a loan. Dai thereupon borrowed fifty yuan from Du, and Xu was put in charge of provisions by common consent; but no sooner had Xu taken the money than, within a few days, he too made off, fleeing back to his native Hankou. Dai had no choice but to borrow another fifty yuan from Du, and so got through the pinch. Later, when Chiang Kai-shek resumed office, Dai and Zhou Weilong and others organized the "Group of Ten" and went on carrying out secret-service work for Chiang. On April 1, 1932, Chiang expanded the Group of Ten into the Special Services Department, with Dai as its chief. At this point Xu Zhongwu came to Dai seeking work; and although Dai did at various times appoint him a plainclothes-squad commander and head of the detective squads of the Hankou and Xi'an police, among other posts, he kept a very tight rein on him, and Xu was often locked up. In the autumn of 1942, when Xu was serving as head of the detective squad of the Chongqing municipal police, someone secretly informed on him for running a gambling den at home and taking a house cut; Dai went in person to raid it, but did not catch anyone actually gambling, turning up only a single set of mahjong tiles and a stack of chips — whereupon he detained Xu. By the Juntong's standing practice, when a member of the bureau was detained, sixty percent of his original salary was paid out to be drawn by his family to keep them going; and as I was then deputy director of the Juntong's accounting office, in charge of disbursing this allowance, Xu's wife, every time she came to draw the money, would pour out her grievances to me in tears, saying that Xu himself had not played mahjong at all, that it was she who now and then played a little with others, that she was willing to take the punishment, and begging me to report on her behalf to Dai and ask that Xu be released. I relayed the situation to Mao Renfeng (then acting chief secretary of the Juntong), and Mao told me to report to Dai Li when a suitable moment offered. One day, when Dai was lunching at the Juntong and talking in high good humor, Mao signaled me to make the plea for Xu; I truthfully relayed what Xu's wife had said, asking Dai to deal leniently and release Xu soon, and Mao too seconded the plea from the side. Dai then told us that this fellow Xu was thoroughly bad — he would even swindle Dai's own money, to say nothing of other people's — and went on to tell us the whole story, given above, of their past in Shanghai. He finished by saying that had it not been for his old acquaintance and sworn brotherhood with Du Yuesheng in Shanghai, and Du's lending him money again and again, then — apart from the fact that his own long habituation to a drifter's life in Shanghai had kept him from starving — those several men of theirs, with no kin, no support, and not a thing to their names in Shanghai, would have had to turn either to robbery or to begging; this fellow Xu had to be put through the mill and toughened up, or he would surely make big trouble one day. Hearing Dai talk this way, Mao Renfeng and I did not think it proper to say anything more. In the end Xu Zhongwu was held by Dai half a year that time before being let out.

These two facts are enough to fix the time when Du Yuesheng and Dai Li formed their connection.

### II. The Mutual Collusion and Exploitation of Du and Dai

The instances of mutual collusion and exploitation between Du Yuesheng and Dai Li are many, and I know all too few of them. Leaving aside what Shen Zui has already written in the article mentioned above, which I shall neither repeat nor supplement, I here set down, from what I heard in the past and experienced at first hand, the more significant facts concerning the dealings between Du and Dai on the economic side, as follows:

#### (i) Du's Disciples Made to Serve as Hired Thugs

After Dai Li became chief of the Special Services Department in 1932, and because Shanghai then held the concessions and spheres of influence of the various imperialist powers, where certain progressive figures opposed to Chiang sheltered and were active, he set up a secret-work "Shanghai District" and, besides it, took control of such units as the reconnaissance brigade of the Songhu Garrison Command, the police office of the Nanjing-Shanghai and Shanghai-Hangzhou-Ningbo Railways, and the detective squad of the Shanghai police. But at that time his agents, operating within the two concessions, still had their misgivings, and his secret-service underlings were as yet not many; so, since Du Yuesheng's power ran through both concessions and the sphere of Japanese imperialism, he made use of Du to carry out reactionary work for him. Whenever Dai came to Shanghai he would be sure to meet with Du. The meeting place was not fixed: sometimes at Du's house at No. 66 Rue Wagner (today's Ninghai West Road), sometimes at a hotel taken for the occasion. When Dai was not in Shanghai, he kept in touch with Du regularly through the commander of the Songhu Garrison Command's reconnaissance brigade. That post was held in succession by Weng Guanghui and Wu Tingxian, and from the spring of 1935 until the Japanese army occupied Shanghai it was held throughout by Wang Zhaohuai. While Weng and Wu held the post they had little contact with Du; but after Wang became brigade commander he often went to Du's house, keeping in very close touch and making use of Du's disciples as hired thugs. In 1945, in the Juntong's management office in Chongqing, I heard from Lin Qing — formerly a section head under Wang Zhaohuai in Shanghai and later deputy head of the cashier's section in the management office — that Du Yuesheng had a great many disciples in Shanghai, in every trade and calling: some of very high standing, some very rich, some jobless riffraff, and within every concession he had his followers. Wang was then chiefly assisting the reconnaissance brigade of the Songhu Garrison Command, because Wang Zhaohuai was one of Du's favorite disciples; and the brigade held many others of Du's disciples besides, of whom Lin himself was one. He said, too, that at the funeral of Mr. Lu Xun, Dai Li, having had prior word that Song Qingling and many others opposed to Chiang would take part in bearing the pall, instructed Wang Zhaohuai to send men to give warnings, as the chance offered, to the progressive figures joining the cortege, and told Wang, if he was short of hands, to ask Du Yuesheng to send men to help. On receiving Dai's instructions Wang, on the one hand, conferred with his men to make arrangements, and on the other asked Du's help. Du assigned several dozen toughs to Wang's command; Wang appointed Lin as one of the group leaders, and on the day of the funeral Lin took a few squad men and a few of Du's disciples to work a stretch of North Sichuan Road. Mingling with the onlooking crowd, they saw Song Qingling and many university professors bearing the pall, and the great numbers of students joining the funeral procession, all of them filled with grief and indignation, shouting slogans, with many students acting as marshals to keep order; and seeing that this stretch of North Sichuan Road lay within the Japanese sphere of influence and offered no opening to exploit, they dared not make a move, and the several groups all had quietly to withdraw.

#### (ii) Du Used to Organize Secret-Service Armed Forces

After Dai Li became chief of the Special Services Department, his ambition swelled and he built up his forces step by step. He held that to do politics one must have armed force to back it, and would often say to people, "In politics you must have armed force behind you, or you are peddling quack plasters and no one will take you seriously." He saw that generals of the Fuxing clique, such as Chen Cheng and Hu Zongnan, all held military command, and above all that Kang Ze — of the other secret-service system, the Fuxing clique — controlled the Special Operations Headquarters Column and the patrol regiment under the smuggling-suppression office of the Opium Suppression Inspectorate; and in order to hold his own against Chen, Kang and the rest, he took control of the police of the various provinces and cities within Chiang Kai-shek's reach and set up within the Special Services Department a special-service squad (later expanded into a special-service regiment). But he regarded the police as a mere appendage of the army, shifting with military and political changes, and was not content with this. He knew Chiang Kai-shek would never permit him a regular army, so he cast about for a chance to build a powerful secret-service armed force. After the 1936 crisis in the two Guangs was settled and Chiang gained the Guangdong domain, he appointed Song Ziliang finance commissioner of the Ministry of Finance for Guangdong, concurrently head of the finance department of the Guangdong provincial government and opium-suppression commissioner, and ordered Dai to assist Song in taking over Chen Jitang's smuggling-suppression organs and forces. Dai seized this chance to expand Chen's smuggling-suppression squads into the Guangdong Revenue Guard (equivalent to a division), keeping it in his own hands. But he considered that even with this Revenue Guard in hand his strength was still not enough, all the more since it was a force of merely local character. He therefore set his whole mind on finding a chance to build up, besides this, a nationwide force.

When the August Thirteenth war of resistance broke out at Songhu in 1937, he, under cover of the resistance, proposed to Chiang Kai-shek that Du Yuesheng be used to organize the strength of the Shanghai Green and Red Gangs — ostensibly to prevent the Japanese from landing at Shanghai, and, should they land, to be able to operate behind enemy lines. Chiang approved this crafty scheme and designated Dai to take charge. Dai at once went from Nanjing to Shanghai to confer with Du Yuesheng, and it was decided that Du would come forward to convene the Shanghai Green and Red Gang chieftains — Yang Hu, Xiang Songpo, Liu Zhilu, Yu Zuobai and others — for a meeting, at which Chiang's instructions were conveyed and they were called on to mobilize the gang forces they led and organize them jointly. It was then agreed with one voice to set up a "Jiangsu-Zhejiang-Anhui Action Committee," with Du pushed forward as chairman and Dai, Yang, Xiang, Liu and Yu all as members, Dai concurrently serving as secretary-general and bearing the actual responsibility. After the committee was established, Dai appointed Zhou Weilong secretary to act for him (the post was later taken over by Wen Qiang). Before long, Dai set up under this committee a Special Operations Army, recommending Liu Zhilu as its commander-in-chief; it comprised five detachments (zhidui), the first commanded by He Xingjian, the second by Lu Jingshi, the third by Zhu Xuefan, and the fourth by Zhang Fan. Each detachment, larger than an ordinary regiment, held four or five dadui (equivalent to battalions), each dadui four zhongdui (equivalent to companies), and each zhongdui three xiaodui (equivalent to platoons). The men numbered some twenty thousand in all, and were all Green and Red Gang members.

When the Japanese landed, a part of the Special Operations Army's soldiers, stirred by patriotic fervor, did indeed fight bravely, and there were sacrifices. The battalion commander Liao Shudong, for one, led his men against the Japanese and, suffering heavy casualties, was left with only a hundred-odd men, yet went on resisting. When they were surrounded and the Japanese called on them to surrender, they held firm in resistance; in the end both of Liao's legs were blown off, and when the enemy came to seize him he flung his remaining grenades at them, killing ten-odd of them, and rolled into the Huangpu River to his death.

When Shanghai fell, the Special Operations Army withdrew to Pudong, southern Jiangsu, western Zhejiang, southern Anhui and other districts of Jiangsu and Zhejiang; being a rabble to begin with, without rigorous training, most of them — apart from a portion who died in the fighting with the Japanese — scattered of their own accord, and few were left. For a time it was reorganized as the "Military Affairs Commission Instruction Regiment." Then, in the region of southern Zhejiang and southern Jiangsu, it took in more than eight thousand of Chiang's troops who had come routed out of Shanghai; the two were mixed together, and on Dai Li's report and with Chiang Kai-shek's approval it was renamed the "Loyal and Patriotic Army," subordinate to the Military Affairs Commission and under the Juntong's leadership — from then on becoming a secret-service armed force.

After the Loyal and Patriotic Army was formed, the Jiangsu-Zhejiang-Anhui Action Committee ceased activity as a matter of course; but since many of the army's officers and men were Du's disciples and other Shanghai Green and Red Gang members, Dai went on making use of Du's influence over these gang elements. After the Japanese occupied Shanghai, the Loyal and Patriotic Army had one detachment in southern Jiangsu and an independent battalion in the Pudong area carrying on so-called guerrilla work; and as the members of these two parts were mostly Shanghai Green and Red Gang men, Dai held that they must be given training, and so summoned by wire the cadres of these two parts to come from Shanghai to Hong Kong for a meeting. At that time Du was staying in Room 706 of the Gloucester Hotel in Hong Kong; after Dai reached Hong Kong, he convened them for a meeting and a dinner at the home of Chen Zhiping (a Juntong stalwart, then head of the inspection section of the Hong Kong branch of the Military Affairs Commission's Southwest Transport Office, who handled liaison between Dai and Du) at No. 7 Wanjinglou, Causeway Bay, and invited Du to attend and address them. According to Guo Lanxin, who was then serving as Du's secretary, Du's speech on that occasion was drafted for him by Guo, and Du played a certain part at the meeting on Dai's behalf.

In June 1945, Dai invited Du to accompany the deputy director of the "Sino-American Cooperative Organization," the American secret-service chief Miles, on an inspection tour of the various parts of the Southeast, to arrange for the Loyal and Patriotic Army to coordinate with U.S. military activities. The army's several detachments were drawn in batches, in turn, to receive training at the Xiongcun, Dongfeng, Yuliang and other training classes belonging to the Sino-American Cooperative Organization, and were entirely re-equipped with American gear. At that time the U.S. forces were preparing to land at various points along our southeastern coast and wanted the Loyal and Patriotic Army to respond and coordinate in a joint operation. To encourage the gang elements in that army to cooperate with the Americans in racing to seize Hangzhou, Shanghai, Nanjing and other great cities ahead of the New Fourth Army, so as to keep them from being taken over by it, Dai specially invited Du to come along. Du, since his personal property and his dark-world power all lay in Shanghai, was willing to go too. They set out from Chongqing on June 25, going by way of Guiyang to Zhijiang, then changing to a plane to Jianyang in Fujian and on to Dongfeng and to Zhejiang, Anhui and elsewhere. I heard from Ye Shibo, a section head of the Juntong's management office who had accompanied Dai Li and who spoke of it after returning to Chongqing, that Dai, Du, Miles and the others, on reaching Dongfeng, stayed five days at the Sino-American Organization's Dongfeng training class, then went by car to Yanshan for secret talks with Gu Zhutong, commander-in-chief of the Third War Zone, who played host, staying there three days; then went on to the Sino-American class at Xiongcun in Anhui, where they stayed more than ten days. Whenever Dai visited Dongfeng and the other training classes, he would gather the officers and men in training at each class, and those of the Loyal and Patriotic Army detachments stationed nearby, and address them. Because more detachments were stationed in the Xiongcun area, they spent the longer time at the Xiongcun class. After each of Dai's addresses to the officers and men, he would always ask Du Yuesheng to speak. Du each time spoke very briefly, the gist being to urge the officers and men to fight their way back soon to their old home, Shanghai. After Du left the Xiongcun class with Dai, he went to Chun'an and other places to inspect the work of the Juntong's open and secret units; on the way back Du stayed on at Chun'an to assist the work of the Loyal and Patriotic Army, and Dai left behind the deputy chief Du Yuan (then deputy chief of the Juntong's First Department and concurrently head of the Third Section of the Second Bureau of the Ministry of Military Operations, who had come with Dai on the southeastern inspection) to act for the time as chief of staff of the Loyal and Patriotic Army and handle liaison with Du Yuesheng.

On this trip Dai spent over a month visiting all the above places, not returning to Chongqing until the end of July. Du Yuesheng stayed on at Chun'an, leaving only after Japan's surrender, and returned to Shanghai on September 3.

This secret-service armed force, right up to the moment Japan announced its surrender, never once fought the Japanese army; yet after Japan's surrender it was the first to enter Hangzhou, Shanghai, Nanjing and other cities, helping Chiang's troops to snatch the fruits of the people's victory in the war of resistance.

#### (iii) Engineering Tao Xisheng and Gao Zongwu's Defection from Wang to Chiang

In December 1938, Wang Jingwei left Chongqing for Kunming, and by way of Hanoi and Hong Kong went on to Shanghai to defect openly. Chiang Kai-shek — because Wang had defected ahead of him, and also because Wang had in the past joined him in secretly suing to Japan for peace and surrender, so that Wang's open disclosure of the secret was in every way to his disadvantage — after Wang reached Hanoi secretly instructed Dai Li to send men to Hanoi to kill him. Acting on the instruction, Dai sent the Juntong agent Yu Lexing to lead action men — Wang Luqiao and others — to Hanoi, where they climbed over the wall into Wang's residence and shot Zeng Zhongming dead, but failed to find Wang. Wang, having reached Hong Kong, issued the so-called "Yan Telegram," and before long, under Japanese protection, set up a puppet regime at Nanjing. Chiang thereupon further instructed Dai, on the one hand, to keep sending men to watch for a chance to assassinate Wang, and on the other to send men to find means of inducing the traitors who had defected with Wang to break away, and so split the Wang puppet organization. Accordingly, Dai once sent the Juntong's secretary-general Wu Gengshu, together with the Military Affairs Commission's senior staff officer Dai Jingyuan, the Shanghai branch manager of the Kailuan Coal Mines Company Xu Tianmin, and others, at the head of ten agents, from Chongqing by way of Hong Kong to Shanghai to assassinate Wang; but because the plan was not kept close, they were exposed by Li Shiqun of the Wang puppets' Secret Service Headquarters, and Wu and the rest were killed. As for inducing traitors to break away from the Wang puppet organization: because the Juntong's Shanghai organization had been badly affected — after Chen Gongshu, chief of the No. 1 Shanghai District in 1938–1939, was arrested and defected, it had been almost entirely wrecked — Dai, seeing that Du Yuesheng's acquaintance in Shanghai had formerly been very wide, wired orders to Wang Xinheng, who was serving as chief of the South China District in Hong Kong, to get in touch with Du and ask him to find a way; and Du agreed to look for someone to thread the needle.

In 1939, through the introduction of Xu Jiqing, Du made contact with Huang Suchu, who had connections with the arch-traitors Gao Zongwu and Tao Xisheng; Du twice flew from Hong Kong to Chongqing for secret talks with Dai, and even saw Chiang Kai-shek, and in the end drew Gao and Tao over. The course of it, as I heard afterward from Wang Xinheng, was roughly as follows.

Xu Jiqing and Du Yuesheng were both, at that time, standing directors of the Shanghai General Chamber of Commerce, and were on quite intimate terms. Xu, pressed hard by the enemy and puppets, had left Shanghai for Hong Kong, and lived in the home of Huang Suchu in Kowloon, coming and going with Du constantly. Huang Suchu — named Qun — was, like Xu, a member of Liang Qichao's Progressive Party, and also a fellow townsman from Wenzhou. Huang had failed in running the Tongyi Trust Company in Shanghai and fled to Hong Kong, where Xu had helped him wind up his affairs, so that the two were on very close personal terms. Huang was a near-fellow-townsman of both Gao Zongwu, foreign minister of the Wang puppet government, and Tao Xisheng, and was said moreover to stand in a teacher-pupil relation to Gao. Du, on Dai's commission, consulted Xu, and Xu then brought Huang to see Du; Du judged that Huang could be used to go to Shanghai and induce Gao and Tao to come over to Chiang, and had Wang Xinheng wire Dai Li. Dai, having been detained for more than two hours by the Hong Kong Police when he went there in 1938, was unwilling to go to Hong Kong again, and so invited Du to Chongqing to talk it over. Du flew to Chongqing the next day (the Lantern Festival, by the lunar calendar, of 1939); Dai went in person to the airport to meet him and take him to his residence to confer, and it was decided that Huang should go at once to Shanghai to make contact with Tao and Gao, see how they responded, and — if they were willing to turn back — see what they wanted, and then ask Chiang Kai-shek's instructions before acting. So after lingering three days in Chongqing Du returned to Hong Kong, and Huang hurried to Shanghai to work on it, Du advancing the expenses beforehand. About half a month later Huang returned to Hong Kong and reported to Du that Tao and Gao, having seen the traitorous secret pact Wang had concluded with the Japanese, both regretted having followed Wang into treason, but were for the moment unwilling to return to Chongqing, fearing that, having once been traitors, they would be punished. Du told Wang Xinheng to relay this to Dai, and Dai replied by wire that, on Chiang Kai-shek's instruction, Du was invited to Chongqing to discuss the whole matter face to face; so Du flew to Chongqing again the next day. This time Du lingered five or six days in Chongqing before returning to Hong Kong, and on his return told Wang that when he landed at the Chongqing airport Dai had met him and taken him to his residence, and that the next day, Dai having announced him, he saw Chiang Kai-shek, who instructed him to his face that so long as Tao Xisheng and Gao Zongwu broke away from the Wang puppet government and returned to Chongqing, they would be given suitable posts if they wanted office, funded to go abroad if they wished to travel and observe, and by no means punished; and that if they could bring out the Japanese-Wang secret pact, there would be a further heavy reward. Chiang also had the finance minister H. H. Kung issue Du one hundred thousand Hong Kong dollars, as expenses and as living funds for Tao and Gao. This time Du charged Huang, for the second time, to go to Shanghai and pass Chiang's instructions on to Tao and Gao, with everything guaranteed by Du, and at the same time issued Tao and Gao forty thousand each out of the hundred thousand Hong Kong dollars. Before long, Tao and Gao, carrying the Japanese-Wang traitorous secret pact, came to Hong Kong together with Huang. Wang Xinheng, again on Du's charge, reported to Dai by wire only, and at the same time sent men to convey the Japanese-Wang secret pact to Chongqing. On Dai's report and Chiang Kai-shek's instruction, the secret pact was published openly in the press under the name of Tao and the others. At that point Tao was already willing to go to Chongqing, while Gao asked to go to the United States to study; on Dai's report and with Chiang's approval, the finance ministry issued Gao fifty thousand in American exchange and let him go to the United States, and Tao before long flew to Chongqing, where Chiang employed him at his side, later as deputy minister of the Central Propaganda Department. For this affair Chiang issued Du a reward of two hundred thousand yuan in fabi, and the Juntong issued Wang Xinheng and the staff who had handled the matter within the South China District a reward of five thousand Hong Kong dollars.

The foregoing is the course of Tao Xisheng and Gao Zongwu's defection from Wang to Chiang. According to Mr. Yan Esheng, it was on account of his exertions in this business that Xu Jiqing, after the war of resistance ended, obtained the post of speaker of the Shanghai Provisional Consultative Council.

#### (iv) Du's Financial Support to Dai

From 1939 on, Dai Li, acting on Chiang's secret orders, tightened the secret-service organizations everywhere to watch and restrict the activities of the dissident party; and besides running training classes to train large numbers of agents, he vastly expanded the Juntong's inner and field organization. He would not report the increased establishment and budget to Chiang beforehand, but simply notified the Ordnance Regiment of the Ministry of Military Administration to add to the allocation; and as the Ordnance Department still issued funds each month according to the bureau's original budget, the monthly shortfall was enormous, and had to be met on the spot by borrowing here and shifting there, only to be reimbursed at year's end, on report to Chiang, and repaid by the Ordnance Department once approved. I was transferred to the Juntong's accounting office in the spring of 1941, my main work being to draw and disburse funds and to raise them; besides drawing the fixed monthly allocation from the Ordnance Regiment, I would, for the shortfall, borrow from units under the Juntong's leadership, such as the Ministry of Finance's Smuggling Suppression Department and the Military Affairs Commission's Combined Land and Water Inspection Office. When even so the turnover ran short, I would report to Dai. That winter, Dai told me and the deputy directors of the accounting office, Lin Yaomin and Zhang Guanfu — the three of us — to go together to Wangshan to see Du Yuesheng and borrow fifty thousand yuan in fabi from him. When we explained our errand, Du, without the least hesitation, agreed to lend the whole sum; the manager of the Xi'an branch of the Commercial Bank, Wang Baokang, was then in Chongqing, staying at Du's house, and Du told Wang to see to our board and lodging. After breakfast the next morning, Wang handed us a check for fifty thousand. Later, when I went to the Commercial Bank to repay the principal and interest of the loan, the manager, Pang Anmin, said Du had given word that no interest was to be taken. Afterward I went alone three more times to borrow: twice I saw Du on the upper floor of the Bank of Communications, and once at Fan Shaozeng's house at Lailong Lane; twice I borrowed one hundred thousand each, and two years later, at the last borrowing (in the summer of 1944), because of the swift inflation of the currency the figure reached fifty million. Each time the money was repaid, Du would refuse to reckon interest. I remember that on the occasion when Dai told me to go to Fan's house to see Du, Du was in the middle of a game of mahjong; Fan Shaozeng came out to receive me, and we went into an inner room. The five of them were playing in turns; Fan was shuffling the tiles, and I took the chance, while he was resting his hand, to come especially and explain my errand. He said, "Yunong has already telephoned me — it was I who asked him to have you come here." Then he handed me the hundred-thousand-yuan check he had made ready.

In the winter of 1944 the Juntong reported to the chief of the general staff and concurrent minister of military administration, He Yingqin, requesting an increase of the establishment and budget, to be additionally issued by the Ordnance Department. At the same time, with the approval of the Ordnance Department chief Chen Liang, it borrowed three months' funds in advance to ease the turnover; and in this way it no longer had to keep borrowing from Du in Chongqing.

Besides this, Du Yuesheng also arranged in Chongqing to transfer funds for the Juntong's secret units in Shanghai. The funds for the Juntong's various Shanghai units had originally been remitted from Hong Kong; after that place fell to the Japanese in 1941, they were remitted from Chongqing, at first through private channels such as the Jincheng Bank, which charged heavy remittance and handling fees and sometimes could not remit on time. In the winter of 1943 I reported the situation to Dai, and Dai answered that Du Yuesheng might be able to solve it, and that he would tell me after talking it over with Du. Two days later Dai told me that the matter of remitting money to Shanghai had already been settled with Du, that Du had instructed the Commercial Bank to make the transfers for us, and that I was to go and arrange it with the bank. The Juntong's Shanghai funds were handled by the bureau's East China District accounting section, which came under the accounting office; and I, together with the section head Zhang Zhen, went to the Commercial Bank, where the manager, Pang Anmin, told me that Du had already given clear instructions by telephone and I need not trouble myself. That one time nine million yuan in fabi was transferred at a stroke, and the handling fee charged was far cheaper than the Jincheng Bank's. Thereafter Zhang Zhen handled the arrangements with the bank for the transfers, once every half year, until Japan's surrender.

#### (v) Setting Up the Tongji Company

In the spring of 1943, Zhu Huiqing, a special commissioner of the economic research office of the Nationalist government finance ministry's Wartime Freight Control Bureau, proposed to Dai Li that men be sent to Shanghai to set up a firm to buy up cotton, yarn, cloth and the like, to be shipped into the Chiang-controlled area to regulate the supply of goods. Dai thought it feasible, but held that to set up a firm in Shanghai to buy cotton, yarn and cloth, suitable men must first be found before it could be made to work; and although the Juntong then had many agents in Shanghai, they were all engaged in secret intelligence work, could not act openly, and moreover did not much understand doing business. So he consulted Du Yuesheng, and Du put forward Xu Caicheng and Wan Molin and others, whom he had left in Shanghai; since Xu and Wan were from time to time in collusion with the Japanese secret-service organ, the Ume Kikan, they could buy up cotton, yarn, cloth and other goods through the Ume Kikan and ship them to the rear. Du agreed to cooperate with Dai, and Dai thereupon drew up a plan for the Wartime Freight Control Bureau and Du to jointly set up a freight firm, and submitted it to Chiang Kai-shek, who referred the plan to the "National General Mobilization Committee's National Economic Mobilization Committee" for deliberation. The secretary-general of that committee was held concurrently by the vice-president of the Executive Yuan and finance minister H. H. Kung, with Liu Gongyun as deputy secretary-general. Kung, seeing that there was profit to be had, proposed to enlarge the organization and increase the capital, with the four banks — Central, China, Communications and Farmers — and the Postal Remittance Bureau taking part in the investment; and Chiang, thinking it feasible, told Dai to redraft an enlarged plan and send it back to the National Economic Mobilization Committee, where Kung completed the approval procedures, and Liu Gongyun notified Du and Dai. On the strength of this document Du and Dai convened the heads of the four banks and one bureau, together with the deputy director of the Freight Bureau, Wang Fuzhou, for a meeting, at which Dai had Zhu Huiqing appear as his representative. The Central Bank was represented by Zhong E (Bingfeng); the Bank of China by its general manager Bei Zuyi (Songsun); the Bank of Communications by Qian Yongming (Xinzhi); the Farmers Bank by Zhang Runsheng, on behalf of its general manager Gu Yiqun; and the Postal Remittance Bureau by its general manager Xu Jizhuang. At the meeting, by prior understanding, Du was pushed forward as chairman of the board; Qian, Dai, Zhong, Bei, Gu, Xu, Zhu and Yang Guanbei (a retainer of Du's, able to represent him) as standing directors; Wang Fuzhou, Zhang Runsheng and others as directors; and Liu Bingyi as general manager. The capital was a hundred million yuan in fabi, originally set with the four banks and one bureau each investing ten million, the Freight Bureau twenty million, and Du Yuesheng at the head of the private shares with thirty million. Later the Freight Bureau's twenty million was not approved, the finance ministry holding that as an administrative organ the bureau was barred by regulation from investing. After discussion among Du, Qian and Dai, it was changed to private-share investment, the private shares being raised to fifty million; but the Freight Bureau was still credited with twenty million, so that the total capital was in effect raised to a hundred and twenty million. It was agreed that the Freight Bureau's twenty million need not actually be paid in: whatever goods the Tongji Company shipped inland would be carried free of charge by the Freight Bureau's trucks, to offset the bureau's investment.

The Tongji Company was established in the autumn of 1943, its head office on the upper floor of No. 49 Linsen Road in Chongqing; Dai posted Zhu Huiqing there permanently to conduct business in the name of a standing director, and also sent Lin Yaomin, formerly head of the business department of the Freight Bureau, to serve as chief auditor, while Du appointed Guo Lanxin as the company's chief secretary.

From its founding until after Japan's surrender, the Tongji Company bought and shipped two consignments of goods — cotton yarn and cloth — from Shanghai to the Chiang-controlled area. The first consignment, three thousand bales of yarn and cloth in all, was shipped in the winter of 1943 from the Shangqiu branch to Jieshou, and thence carried by the Jieshou freight-office head, Wang Zhaohuai, to Xi'an, Chongqing and other places. What reached Chongqing was sent by the Freight Bureau to the Cotton, Yarn and Cloth Control Bureau, priced at eighty million yuan, and handed to the Tongji Company; at what price the goods reaching Xi'an were valued, I do not know. The second consignment, five hundred bales of yarn and cloth, was passed by the Chun'an branch to the Zhejiang freight-office head, Zhao Shirui, and carried to Jinhua, where it was detained by Gu Zhutong, commander-in-chief of the Third War Zone. Gu telegraphed Dai Li asking leave to keep these goods for the war zone's military use; Dai replied by wire that of these goods he was responsible only for the transport and had no authority to dispose of them, and that Chiang's instructions must be sought. At the same time the Ordnance Department chief Chen Liang, on the report of the Third War Zone's ordnance bureau, likewise put to Dai the request that the goods be kept for that bureau's military use, and Dai gave Chen the same reply. He reported to Chiang Kai-shek on the one hand, and on the other told me to inform Chen Liang to his face that Chen must ask Chiang's approval. Later Chiang approved handing them to the Third War Zone for military use; in the summer of 1944 they were priced at more than twenty-six million yuan (fabi having by then depreciated a great deal from 1943), and the finance ministry carried out the transfer of accounts.

After Japan's surrender, the Tongji Company moved from Chongqing to the Zhonghui Building in Shanghai (today the Shanghai Museum on Yan'an East Road); the Freight Bureau having by then been abolished, Zhu Huiqing and Lin Yaomin stayed on to work at the company.

After Dai Li's death, Zheng Jiemin succeeded as director of the Juntong, and in October 1946 the Juntong was reorganized into the Ministry of National Defense's Bureau of Confidential Investigation, with Zheng as director. In the summer of 1947 the Tongji Company convened a board meeting in Shanghai, which Zheng attended in person; on returning to Nanjing he told me (I was then director of the management office of the puppet Ministry of National Defense's Bureau of Confidential Investigation, in charge of the bureau's commercial organs) that Du Yuesheng had sought him out to attend the Tongji board, and that the meeting had decided to convert the company's capital into fifty thousand U.S. dollars. Although the company had acknowledged the Freight Bureau's investment of twenty million, the bureau had never actually paid in the shares, offsetting them only with the free transport of goods by its vehicles; and since the bureau had long been abolished, we would not take this advantage, and he had told Du that the company's affairs would be left to Du to run and that we would have nothing further to do with them.

In the autumn of that year, Chiang Kai-shek ordered the Bureau of Confidential Investigation to hand over its commercial organs to the "Sanyou Company" run by Mao Qingxiang; and when the bureau carried out the transfer, it did not turn over to Mao, among its commercial organs, the investment the Tongji Company had originally credited to the Freight Bureau, but only noted its relation with the Tongji Company in the transfer inventory.

#### (vi) Dai Using Du as a Bridge in the Gangs

Besides using Du Yuesheng to organize secret-service armed forces, Dai Li also used Du to mediate his conflicts with people in the gangs and to court the Sichuan Paoge.

1. Dai once used Du to mediate the conflict with Wang Yaqiao. Wang was the head of the Anhui gang in Shanghai and had originally been a sworn brother of Chiang Kai-shek's; after Chiang rose in the world he grew distant from Wang, and Wang later carried on activities to assassinate Chiang, and so fell to Dai's murderous hand. This fact Shen Zui has related in his article "The Dai Li I Knew," and I shall not repeat it here. What I would add is the way Dai, before killing Wang, twice used Du to court him.

In late November 1936, Dai Li sent Wu Gengshu, formerly head of the Special Services Department's Hunan station, to lead action men — Gu Yulin and others — to Guangzhou and then on to Wuzhou to assassinate Wang Yaqiao. I was at that time head of the ordnance office of the Guangdong Revenue Guard, concurrently head of the accounting section of the Guangdong Smuggling Suppression General Office and chief auditor of the Juntong's Guangdong District, and Dai wired me orders to assist Wu in his work. After Wu reached Guangzhou, I gave him a thousand yuan in Guangdong silver notes for expenses, and on his behalf borrowed the small anti-smuggling launch Guangkai from the Guangdong Smuggling Suppression General Office for his use. When Wu returned to Guangzhou after assassinating Wang at the Central Hotel in Wuzhou, he told me the following: that after the failure of the Fujian Incident Wang Yaqiao had, through an intermediary, appealed to Chiang, and Chiang had handed him to Dai Li to be employed, telling Wang to make contact with Dai. Wang was then living in Shanghai, and Dai — since Wang was a former sworn brother of Chiang's and a chief of the Anhui gang — went from Nanjing to Shanghai to see him first. But Wang, considering Dai's standing at the time low, was extremely arrogant toward him, and Dai then asked Du Yuesheng to mediate: as one of the chiefs of the Shanghai gangs, Du gave Wang a dinner at the Xinya Hotel, with Huang Jinrong, Zhang Xiaolin and others invited to keep him company, to draw Wang over on Dai's behalf. But Wang still put forward, as his condition, that he be made commander of the Songhu Garrison, and declared he was unwilling to rank below Dai, saying Dai was a stripling with the milk not yet dry on his lips — who would take orders from him? Dai reported Wang's condition to Chiang; Chiang would not agree, and it came to a break again. Later, in November 1935, Wang organized the failed attempt to assassinate Chiang at the Nationalist Central Party Headquarters in Nanjing, wounding Wang Jingwei instead. After this happened, Chiang agreed to the condition Wang had earlier put forward and told Dai to make contact with Wang; Dai, learning that Wang had once come from Hong Kong to Shanghai, again asked Du Yuesheng to find Wang and make contact, but Wang, fearing that Chiang would seize the chance to deal with him, would not agree, and left Shanghai for Hong Kong. Afterward Dai learned that Wang was in contact with the Guangxi clique, reported it to Chiang, and with approval had Wang secretly killed. At first the plan was to act in Hong Kong: Chen Zhiping had already seduced and bought over Wang's wife, Jin Shixin, as an inside informant; and in the middle of that month, on learning that Wang had gone from Hong Kong to Wuzhou and was staying at the Central Hotel, Dai sent Wu to take charge of the work of assassinating him. Wu led Gu Yulin, Li Xiukai and others in shooting Wang dead in his bedroom, and at once took the launch Guangkai back to Guangzhou, the work going off fairly smoothly. From Wu Gengshu's account one may see an instance of Dai Li's using Du Yuesheng to mediate conflicts among people in the gangs.

2. Dai used Du to court the Sichuan Paoge. In the winter of 1938, not long after the Juntong, along with the various organs of the Chiang regime, had withdrawn from Wuhan and Changsha to Chongqing, Dai Li — seeing that in the rear provinces of the Chiang-controlled area, and above all in Sichuan, the power of the Paoge was very great — planned to control and use them on the model of the earlier "Jiangsu-Zhejiang-Anhui Action Committee." With Chiang's approval he set up, under the National General Mobilization Committee, a "National People's Mobilization Committee" (the "People's Mobilization Committee" for short), under the Juntong's leadership. Dai held the post of chairman of this committee concurrently, and pulled Du in as a standing member; at that time, Du Yuesheng being in Hong Kong, he was represented by the member Zhu Xuefan. The committee's premises were at No. 1 Daijiabu in Chongqing.

The committee's work in Chongqing was mainly to keep in touch with the various chieftains of the Green and Red Gangs. After Du Yuesheng came from Hong Kong to Chongqing, Dai made use of him to keep in touch with the Sichuan Paoge chieftains. Du's house was at Wangshan on the south bank of Chongqing; when he came into the city he would often be at Fan Shaozeng's house at No. 13 Lailong Lane, using the gambling parties there to keep in touch with the Sichuan Paoge chieftains and draw connections on Dai's behalf. Whenever he came into the city he would be sure to telephone Dai, and sometimes went, too, to Dai's residence at No. 151 Zengjiayan for a talk, or Dai would go to the upper floor of the Bank of Communications to call on Du. Dai knew that Du often gambled at Fan's house with Fan Shaozeng, Kang Xinru and others; and as there was a gambling ban in force in Chongqing at the time, and Dai felt awkward about descending on their game, he seldom went to Lailong Lane to call on Du, and moreover let it be understood that the detective squad of the Chongqing police was secretly to protect them. The detective squad's headquarters was likewise within Lailong Lane, not far from Fan's house. Every time Du and the others gambled at Fan's, carriages thronged the gate and the party broke up only deep in the night; the detective squad, though it knew perfectly well what was afoot inside, would on no account go in, and even sent extra men to patrol outside, pretending to direct traffic but in fact protecting the gambling. In the autumn of 1944, Tan Rongzhang, the Juntong man then serving as head of the detective squad, invited me and the head of the Juntong's personnel office, Gong Xianfang, to his home to eat bean curd; and Gong said jokingly to Tan, "Rongzhang, your squad catches gamblers out on the street every day — why not go and catch the big gambling next door at Fan's?" Tan answered that Dai Li had solemnly given word that Du Yuesheng went to Fan's to play cards for diversion and to make friends among the Sichuan men, and had charged him to protect Du well — so how would he dare send men to make an arrest? On the contrary, he even had to send men to protect them.

#### (vii) Their Collusion in Shanghai After Japan's Surrender

On August 15, 1945, Japan announced its unconditional surrender. Chiang Kai-shek was anxious to seize the places where the Four Big Families plundered their wealth; but as the bulk of his crack troops were in the mountains of the Southwest, he — besides ordering the surrendered puppet troops to hold their positions and refuse to surrender to the people's armed forces — relied on American aircraft, transport ships and the like to rush his troops to the various parts of East China, North China and the Northeast. At the same time he had Dai Li hurry to Shanghai to collude with the arch-traitor Zhou Fohai and to direct every usable secret-service armed force, along with the various local enemy-and-puppet forces, to bar the New Fourth Army from entering the cities. Dai first set up in Shanghai a Joint Office of the Juntong and the Sino-American Cooperative Organization, appointing the Sino-American Cooperative Organization's chief of staff, Li Chongshi, to be director of the office concurrently, while he himself directed matters in Shanghai much of the time. At this point Du Yuesheng too returned to Shanghai and came and went very closely with Dai; the facts of their collusion are many, but as I was still in Chongqing at the time I know rather few of them, and I here set down the two facts I handled myself, as follows:

1. Du used Dai to recover the house at No. 70 Route Doumer (today's Donghu Road) and lent it to Dai for use as the Joint Office of the Juntong and the Sino-American Cooperative Organization. This house had been occupied by the Wang puppets' finance ministry as its Shanghai office; and because the house had been occupied by the enemy and puppets, and because Du had still not paid off the half of the building costs he owed on it, it was being handled as enemy-and-puppet property, so he lent it to Dai to use rent-free. At the end of January 1946, when Dai returned from Shanghai to Chongqing, he said to me that the office building of the Shanghai office had originally been Du Yuesheng's, that Du had lent it to us rent-free, that we were to keep it in repair for him, and that when it was returned in future it must be repaired before being handed back. Accordingly, when the Juntong office borrowed it, it engaged the Lu Genji Construction Works to renovate it and to put up a large garage; and after the office was wound up in August 1946, the Lu Genji works was again engaged to repair the whole house. I even went specially from Nanjing to Shanghai to inspect and accept the work, and, together with the head of the wind-up office, Pang Huaping, returned it to Du. I heard that Du afterward sold this house to the U.S. Embassy for eight hundred thousand U.S. dollars.

2. Du also used Dai's power to recover the school premises of the Zhengshi Middle School on Fahua Road. This building had been newly put up after the January Twenty-Eighth war of resistance; Du Yuesheng was chairman of the school's board of directors, and the principal was Chen Qun. After Shanghai fell, Chen Qun became a traitor while still serving concurrently as principal; after Chen's death the school closed, and the premises were occupied by the Wang puppets' finance ministry's Revenue Guard for use as a Revenue Guard training class, with a part serving as an ordnance warehouse. After Japan's surrender, Dai, on Chiang's orders, took over the Revenue Guard forces, reorganized them into a Traffic Police Column, and the premises too were taken over by the Juntong's Shanghai office. At the time the premises of the puppet Revenue Guard training class should have been handed to the Enemy-and-Puppet Property Liquidation Office for disposal; but Du used Dai's takeover to ask Dai to return them to him, and Dai granted his request: the Juntong notified the Enemy-and-Puppet Property Liquidation Office that this building had originally been the premises of the Zhengshi Middle School, of which Du was board chairman, and had been returned to Du; and once the Enemy-and-Puppet Property Liquidation Office received the Juntong's official letter, it asked no further questions.

Du recovered this house and prepared to revive the Zhengshi Middle School; his second son, Du Weiping, having returned from study in the United States, became the school's dean of studies. But in a row of houses at the rear, which had served as a warehouse of the Revenue Guard headquarters, were stored more than a hundred thousand suits of padded army uniforms and much other military matériel, still guarded by the Traffic Police forces the Juntong had posted there; and Du Weiping, since there were sentries at the main gate and the students found it awkward to come and go, asked the Juntong to move the warehouse. After the Juntong was reorganized into the Ministry of National Defense's Bureau of Confidential Investigation in 1946, the army clothing and other military matériel it had taken over were, on Chiang Kai-shek's instructions, handed over to the Supply Department of the Combined Service Forces Headquarters. I was then director of the bureau's management office, in charge of management business, and accompanied the men sent by the Combined Service Forces Headquarters' Supply Department to Shanghai to take delivery; but that department, because the puppet Revenue Guard's uniforms were all black and unsuited for use as army uniforms, delayed and would not take them over. It dragged on until the winter of 1947, when Du Yuesheng sent Guo Lanxin and Lin Yaomin to Nanjing to talk it over with Mao Renfeng and to deal with me; and only after I had several times pressed the Supply Department chiefs Chen Liang and Fu Zhongfang to take the goods over and move them out promptly did they, in 1948, finally cart the matériel away. Afterward the school, because of the tension of the times, simply closed down.

### III. Du's Relations with the Juntong After Dai Li's Death

Dai Li died in a plane crash on March 17, 1946. Du Yuesheng was greatly grieved, and had the Tongji Company present a gift of two billion yuan in fabi as condolence money; Lin Yaomin took the check to Mao Renfeng, deputy director of the Juntong, and Mao, holding that this sum ought to go to Dai's family, told Lin to take it back to Shanghai and hand it to Dai's son, Dai Cangyi.

In January or February 1948, Du Yuesheng went from Shanghai to Nanjing and stayed at the home of Hong Lanyou (then deputy minister of the Ministry of Social Affairs); on one occasion he went together with Hong to the Linggu Temple to pay his respects at Dai Li's grave. I heard afterward from the men who tended the grave that when Du reached the graveside his eyes brimmed with tears, that he made three deep bows, and that he looked long about the grave before leaving. From this one may see how deep the relation between Du and Dai was.

After Dai Li's death, Du Yuesheng still kept up relations with the Juntong (the Ministry of National Defense's Bureau of Confidential Investigation included); but Du's relations with Zheng Jiemin, Mao Renfeng and the others were not so intimate as with Dai. Until Du left Shanghai for Hong Kong in January 1949, Wang Xinheng handled the liaison between the two sides throughout. The facts of Du's dealings with the Juntong and the Bureau of Confidential Investigation during this period (1946 to 1949), so far as I saw and heard, I set out as follows.

(i) In June 1946, Chiang Kai-shek, under pressure from the whole people's demand for democracy and the abolition of fascist military dictatorship, had no choice but to dissolve the Military Affairs Commission; but at the same time he merged the commission's former organs of military command, military training, logistics and the like with the Ministry of Military Administration into a "Ministry of National Defense," in effect only removing the signboard of the Military Affairs Commission. And because the people had called for the abolition of the secret-service organs, he secretly instructed Dai Li to break the Juntong up into scattered pieces. Of the plan to break the Juntong up, I have already given an account in my article "The Merging of the American and Chiang Secret Services and Dai Li's Collusion with Du Nuomai, Hurley, Cooke and Others," in volume 28 of the national CPPCC's Selected Historical and Cultural Materials. This plan had already been approved by Chiang in Dai Li's lifetime and had begun to be carried out. Only the question of the name and subordination of the secret-service headquarters — the Juntong after its reorganization — remained unresolved. As the Finance Department of the Combined Service Forces Headquarters held that the "Ministry of National Defense" had no establishment or budget for the Juntong, it could not issue funds, advancing only a fraction as maintenance money, so that the secret-service funds ran into difficulty. At this time the Juntong's director, Zheng Jiemin, was serving in Beiping as the Nationalists' executive member on the Military Mediation Executive Headquarters, and Tang Zong was acting director; Tang did not concern himself with funds, and internal affairs were the responsibility of the deputy director, Mao Renfeng. I was then deputy director of the bureau's management office and bore the actual responsibility of that office; and having reported the funding difficulty to Mao, Mao ordered me to come to Shanghai and borrow three hundred million yuan in fabi from Du Yuesheng to keep things going. I came to Shanghai in June; and as I did not know Du's address, I got Wang Xinheng (then head of the Juntong's Shanghai station and concurrently head of the investigation office of the Shanghai municipal government) to take me to the "Eighteen-Storey Building" (that is, the Maoming Apartments) to see Du. Du agreed at once, and told his secretary Hu Xuwu to make out a check for three hundred million and give it to me. I asked Du where the money should be repaid in future, and Du said only that it should be handed to the Commercial Bank in Nanjing. At the beginning of the next month, when I went to that bank to repay the loan, the man in charge, Tong Yiping, said Du had given word that no interest was to be taken. Thereafter, in July, August and September, I came to Shanghai once each month and borrowed three hundred million from Du, always repaying it in Nanjing at the start of the following month, and no interest was ever taken. By October, after the Juntong was reorganized into the "Ministry of National Defense's Bureau of Confidential Investigation," its establishment and budget were fixed and its funds provided for, and only then did the borrowing from Du cease.

In 1949, Du Yuesheng went from Shanghai to Hong Kong and took up residence at No. 18 Kennedy Town; that October, when Guangzhou was liberated, I too reached Hong Kong. Because Mao Renfeng had telegraphed me from Taiwan to issue funds to the Hong Kong station-group, and the funds had not yet been remitted while the station-group urgently needed money, I again sought out Du to borrow ten thousand Hong Kong dollars for the time being, and Du at once had Gu Jiatang fetch ten thousand Hong Kong dollars in cash and give it to me. A few days later I repaid it; this was the last time I ever met Du. Not long after, I left Hong Kong for Chongqing, and whether Du went on giving the Bureau of Confidential Investigation financial support thereafter I do not know.

(ii) In the winter of 1946, the Juntong chief Xu Liang (that is, Xu Weibin), seeing that the power of the Green and Red Gangs everywhere was very great, while the National People's Mobilization Committee under the Juntong's leadership had already been wound up, proposed to the director and deputy director of the Bureau of Confidential Investigation, Zheng Jiemin and Mao Renfeng — in order to go on controlling and using this force, to make it a fringe body of the Ministry of National Defense's Bureau of Confidential Investigation, and as his own political capital — the setting up of a "China New Social Enterprise Construction Association" (the "New Construction Association" for short) as a central organ to unite the gangs everywhere; and Zheng and the others adopted his proposal and passed it up to Chiang Kai-shek for approval. In the spring of 1947 the association set up its head office in Shanghai, with Xu Liang as chairman of its council and Du Yuesheng pulled in as a standing councillor, and had branch offices in the provinces and cities of the Chiang-controlled area, its methods and aims the same as the National People's Mobilization Committee's. Xu pulled Du in as a standing councillor of the association still in order to use him to call the gangs everywhere — and above all the Shanghai Green and Red Gang elements — to take part; but all real power in the association rested in Xu's hands.

(iii) After Dai Li's death, Zheng Jiemin and Mao Renfeng served respectively as director and deputy director of the Juntong; and after the Juntong was reorganized into the Ministry of National Defense's Bureau of Confidential Investigation, Zheng and Mao served respectively as director and deputy director, Mao acting in Zheng's place until Zheng returned to the bureau. Neither Tang Zong nor Mao Renfeng was in the gangs, and the two of them had no direct dealings with Du. Zheng Jiemin had once joined the Green Gang and was of the "Tong" generation, ranking above Du. Zheng had had some contact with Du, but their relation was nothing like so deep as Du's with Dai Li; Zheng's attitude toward Du was different from Dai's — when he needed to use Du he would go to him, and when he did not, he would not go near him. During his tenure as director of the Juntong and of the Bureau of Confidential Investigation, Zheng went to see Du only once, in the summer of 1947, when he came from Beiping to Shanghai; and after Du sought him out to attend a board meeting of the Tongji Company, he never went to see Du again. After the Tongji board meeting, Zheng said to me in Nanjing that Du Yuesheng smoked opium and led a dissolute life, taking day for night and night for day; that when one went to see Du in the evening Du would be in fine spirits, but he himself would by then be wanting to rest; and that Du would draw him into long talks his own spirits could not stand, so that he was very reluctant to go and see Du. This was the reason Zheng himself gave for being unwilling to go and see Du. In truth the biggest reason Zheng did not often have contact with Du was that Zheng was afraid of his wife: Zheng's wife, Ke Shufang, kept a very tight rein on him, fearing Du would lead him astray. On one occasion in the summer of 1947, when I went with the deputy director of the Bureau of Confidential Investigation's general affairs office, Cheng Xichao, to Zheng's house on Beiping Road in Nanjing, Ke said to us something to this effect: Shanghai is a thoroughly bad place — opium, gambling, whoring, party-girls, everything to be had. Du Yuesheng has so many wiles that whenever Zheng Jiemin goes to Shanghai she always goes with him, for fear he should fall in with Du and be led astray. When Zheng went to see Du she would go along with him too; and when Du asked Zheng to a party she could not well go, but she would telephone to hurry Zheng home.

(iv) From February 1946 on, Wang Xinheng served as head of the Shanghai station of the Juntong and the Bureau of Confidential Investigation and concurrently head of the investigation office of the Shanghai municipal government, and throughout handled the liaison between Dai Li, Zheng Jiemin, Mao Renfeng and the rest and Du Yuesheng, whom he treated as an honored guest. Not only was Wang on close terms with Du himself; the womenfolk of the two households also came and went very closely. From 1946 to 1948, every time I went on official business from Nanjing to Shanghai, I would be sure to call on Wang at No. 1 Route de Say Zoong (today's Changshu Road), and several times I ran into Yao Yulan and Du's eldest daughter-in-law and others playing cards and dining at Wang's; once or twice Wang even invited me to play mahjong with them, and sometimes Wang's mother-in-law and Wang and his wife would sit in too, and every time we would break up only after dinner at Wang's. When the women played cards at Wang's they would all telephone home; and when Du Yuesheng heard they were playing at Wang's, he was at ease.

In the spring of 1948, the Chiang regime elected legislators to the Legislative Yuan, and Wang stood for election in Shanghai, with Du coming forward to support him and canvass votes for him. At that time the so-called elections of National Assembly delegates, legislators and control commissioners under the Nationalist government were all manipulated by the CC clique, Chiang Ching-kuo and the like, who put forward the lists of candidates for Chiang Kai-shek to mark off before the poll. Because Wang was on excellent personal terms with Chiang Ching-kuo, having been a fellow student at Sun Yat-sen University in Moscow in the Soviet Union, he was, on Chiang Ching-kuo's proposal, marked off by Chiang Kai-shek as one of the candidates for legislator to be elected for Shanghai. As the candidates put forward outnumbered the fixed quota, each had to canvass votes and campaign for himself; and Wang, although he had been many years in Shanghai, had all along done Juntong secret-service work, so that from the standpoint of running an election his outside connections were not enough. So Du Yuesheng not only drew connections for him but spent his own money to give dinners and canvass votes for Wang. With Du's backing, Wang was elected legislator. In the autumn of 1948, when Wang came to Nanjing for a session of the puppet Legislative Yuan, he said to me: "In standing for legislator I spent little of my own money; it was entirely thanks to Du Yuesheng's help. Du helped very warmly, drawing connections for me in every quarter in person, and even paying out of his own pocket to give people dinners; he spent no little energy and money on my affair." This may be said to be a return Du Yuesheng paid Wang for his years of serving as the link in the mutual collusion and exploitation between Du and Dai Li and the Juntong.
