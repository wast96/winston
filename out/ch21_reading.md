## Chapter Twenty-One. The Hour of the Rabbit, Second Half (6 a.m.)

The two of them slid down through the empty air, cringing and fearful, making for the city wall.

To see how close they clung, they might truly have been a pair of lovebirds soaring across the edge of heaven.

The third year of Tianbao, the fifteenth day of the first month; the hour of the Rabbit, second half.

Chang'an; the Xingqing Palace.

The thing that stood beside the chiwen was a stone-carved image of a warrior. Shaped like a vajra-guardian, this mighty figure had a curling, knotted beard and a half-bared body, with only half a lion's pelt flung across one shoulder; on his head he wore a ruffled, close-fitting crown, its two sides adorned with a pair of wings. His right hand was raised high, the five fingers spread stiff as halberd-blades, and his left gripped a great club; he seemed lost in the rapture of slaughter, his lust for battle awful to behold.

Though the Son of Heaven did not know its provenance, he could see at the least that the thing was nothing of the Central Lands in style; it must have come out of the Sasanian Persian country, and carried, besides, a trace of the Sogdian manner.

The statue was not tall—by a little under a chi shorter than the chiwen. Its place had been chosen with the greatest cunning: fore and aft it was screened by the chiwen and the flying eaves, so that unless one came up close and looked level across the roof-slope one could never find it at all—and in all the city of Chang'an, how many places were there from which a man might look level upon the roof-slope of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower?

The Son of Heaven's face grew blacker still. Day after day he had lingered in this tower, and never once known that so uncanny a contrivance hung above his head. Were someone to work sorcery and curses by it, what then?

Xiao Gui said with a smile: 'Let Your Majesty not be troubled. This god is called Yaluoshan, a war-god of the Persian lands. When this tower was first built, there must have been Persian artisans among the workmen, who secretly raised a lodging-place here for the deity they worshipped.'

The craftsmen of the great Tang were highly skilled in their own right, yet they were by no means loath to take in the techniques and styles of the lands beyond the borders. In a great royal building such as the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, the broad design followed the fashion of the Central Lands, but the details were shot through with the traits of the Türks, the Persians, the Tibetans—even of Goguryeo, of Pyu, of Linyi. That foreign artisans should have had a hand in the building, then, was nothing strange. And it was common enough for such workmen to tuck a little private cargo away in some inconspicuous corner, to leave a name or a few words behind.

But to set a foreign god in secret upon the eaves of a royal hall—that was a thing most rare, and how it had ever passed the overseers and the inspection, none could say. Whoever had supervised the work deserved nothing less than the loss of his head.

But what the Son of Heaven pondered now was another question altogether: how did the aphids mean to make their escape?

A foreign god it was, true enough; but it sat upon the flying eaves, and on every side there was still no road of escape—unless this war-god should suddenly work a wonder and carry them all down upon its back?

Xiao Gui had the others come over beside the Yaluoshan, patted the shoulder of the carving, then took hold gently of its right hand and, with a slight exertion of force, the whole statue tipped clattering over to one side. Everyone saw that beneath the carving there had opened a great square hole, matching exactly the shape of the statue's base, so that it looked as though a hole had been punched clean through this stretch of the flying eaves.

This hole was a passage the craftsmen had used, while raising the flying eaves, to hoist up mud and tiles and materials. The workmen would mix their stuff below on the ground, set it in buckets, and pass a rope through the opening, so that it could be let up and down along the eaves with great convenience. It seemed these Persian artisans, once the work was done, had not sealed it up as the rules required, but had instead capped it over with the statue of the Yaluoshan.

'How came you to know this?' The Son of Heaven glared at Xiao Gui; his pride could not endure that the Qinzheng Wuben Tower should be riddled with such gaps.

Xiao Gui spoke on with a touch of feeling: 'How shall I put it... This statue of the Yaluoshan was the very first reason I ever thought to seek audience with Your Majesty. Many years ago, when I was a hunted outlaw, my heart full of hatred and no notion of how to repay it, I could only wander here and there. That year, out in the Western Regions, I chanced to fall in with an old Persian artisan, a native of Jiling, who had already retired to his ease. Once, deep in his cups, he boasted that he had built a tower for the Son of Heaven, and had secretly enshrined a war-god upon the very roof of the emperor's palace. The old man meant no harm, of course—he wished only that the Yaluoshan might have some small place within the royal halls of the Central Lands. But this news, falling upon my ears, meant something quite different.'

Hearing this, the Son of Heaven's shoulders trembled faintly with rage.

'I poured a few cups into him, and he spilled out every particular: where the god's image stood, what it looked like, how it was opened, and the rest—laid it all out plain as day. I questioned him again and again, and when there was nothing new to be had, I killed him then and there—which you can surely understand? Had he told it to another, that would not have done at all.' Xiao Gui spoke lightly, as of some trifling everyday matter. 'From that time on I racked my brains without cease over how this secret might be turned against Your Majesty. It began as a rough notion, and then, revised and revised, perfected and perfected, it took shape at last as a flawless plan. Were it not for this Yaluoshan, neither you nor I would have come to where we stand today.'

Xiao Gui patted the statue, his voice full of feeling. For a long while the Son of Heaven could not speak. The unwitting act of an old artisan ten-odd years before had grown, of all things, into a catastrophe. The strange workings of fate and fortune—words could scarcely convey one part in ten thousand of it.

As he spoke, Xiao Gui took a coil of rope from his waist; the other aphids loosed theirs as well, and soon they had strung the ropes together into one long line. Yet everyone, Taizhen included, could see that even this length was not enough to reach the ground.

'That length will reach no further than the third floor—do you mean to leap down from such a height?' the Son of Heaven said in derision. 'Even should you chance not to die of it, the ground below is already thick with the imperial guards; you have no road of escape.'

'That need not trouble Your Majesty,' said Xiao Gui coolly.

They tied one end of the rope to the tail of the chiwen and slowly let the other end fall. Just as the Son of Heaven had reckoned, the rope reached no further than the third floor before it came to its end. And the third floor was the Wind-Wooing Hall, open on all four sides, so that, unlike the other floors, it had no flying eaves jutting out, and no safe place to set one's feet.

The Son of Heaven mocked no more; he was keen to see what fresh trick these accursed aphids could yet contrive, now that it had come to this.

Xiao Gui gave the rope a few tugs to be sure it was tied fast enough, then charged the other five aphids to keep close watch on the hostages, and himself took hold of the rope and slid down it little by little.

By now the Qinzheng Wuben Tower was all in chaos. The guards of the several commands had already come up, and floor by floor they were rescuing people, hunting for the culprits, beating out the fires, the shouts and the footfalls rising and falling all about. The sky was black as ever, and not one of them thought—not one of them saw—that the cunning aphids hung upon a slender rope some several zhang out beyond the eastern face of the tower, sliding slowly down.

As he was about to reach the height of the third floor, Xiao Gui began to swing his body, setting the rope to arcing widely back and forth. After several passes to and fro, at the moment he once more reached the topmost point on the eastern side, he moved all at once, hauled on the rope, and leapt across onto the blue-grey city wall that faced the third floor far off across the gap.

The Qinzheng Wuben Tower stood at the middle of the southern wall of the Xingqing Palace, so that a stretch of wall joined onto it at either end, east and west. The height of the wall was level with the third floor, the Wind-Wooing Hall, and the two were very near; but for safety's sake the floor and the wall were not made to connect, and a gap of some three zhang in breadth had been deliberately left between them.

A little while before, when Zhang Xiaojing had slid down from the top of the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower, he had meant to come down upon the wall, only to be flung into the Wind-Wooing Hall on the third floor when it collapsed. Now Xiao Gui was, as it were, playing the same old trick over again.

This stretch of wall was more ornament than fortification, its every part given over to beauty and grandeur. The battlements stood tall and straight, and the road along the wall-top was wide enough to gallop a horse. Xiao Gui swiftly made the rope fast to the socket of a war-banner's flagstaff, then gave three measured tugs.

The sky was too dark, and Xiao Gui could raise no torch, so that those above could tell only from the shuddering of the rope that he had come safely to ground. Then the aphids fell to work: they had in hand two hostages and one comrade who could not stir, and each of these must be bound to one man, two to a pair, and let down slowly.

The aphids need not fear that the hostages would resist: with one's life hanging by a thread between heaven and earth, no one would choose that moment to make trouble. But there was one difficulty that had to be settled at once. Taizhen, seeing that she was to leap down from so great a height, went limp on the spot and wept aloud, and no threat the aphids could make availed anything.

At last one aphid could bear it no longer and made to go over and knock her senseless. The Son of Heaven cried out in anger: 'You shall not touch her!' The aphid turned his head and said with vicious menace: 'If she does not shut her mouth this instant and brings the guards down on us, we will throw her straight off the wall!'

'Let me speak with her.' The Son of Heaven drew himself upright. The aphids hesitated a moment, then let go his arms. Treading among the black tiles, the Son of Heaven came to Taizhen's side, crouched down, and tenderly lifted the disordered hair from her brow: 'Taizhen, do you still remember the words I spoke to you?'

'Mm?' Taizhen went on sobbing.

'In heaven, may we be birds that share a single wing; on earth, two branches twined into one.' The Son of Heaven took her hand and softly recited these two lines of verse, as though returned to the Chenxiang Pavilion beside the Longchi. Taizhen, hesitating, raised her head; down her fair cheeks ran two fresh runnels of tears.

She remembered now. These two lines came out of a strange dream of the Son of Heaven's. He had told her that in the dream he had seen a man surnamed Bai, kneeling below the vermilion steps, who wished to present to the Son of Heaven and his precious consort a poem to set down in words their love. The fellow had murmured on and on a great while, and when the Son of Heaven woke he remembered only the two lines. Afterward he told the tale to Taizhen, and she, feigning pique, said that she was only a female Daoist, and no precious consort at all. The Son of Heaven gathered her into his arms and promised that within one year he would surely give her a title and a name. At that her pique turned to joy, and once more they shared the delight of fishes in water.

'See now—we can rise into the sky this very moment, like birds that share a wing; is that not a fine thing? We have promised you: We will never leave you, and never let you come to harm.' So the Son of Heaven comforted her, drawing her into his arms. Taizhen buried her head against him and made no sound. These two lines of verse were a little secret between her and the Son of Heaven, and no one else knew of them.

The Son of Heaven rose to his feet and, fixing his eyes on the aphids, said: 'Let Us be the one bound to Taizhen for the descent.'

The aphids were taken aback for a moment; with Xiao Gui gone, they did not know what to make of so unlooked-for a request. At this Zhang Xiaojing spoke: 'Let it be so. There are men watching at both ends, above and below—where could they run to?'

The aphids stood where they were and did not move. Zhang Xiaojing's face darkened: 'If you doubt whether Zhang Xiaojing's word is to be heeded, you may go and ask Xiao Gui.' Long a buliang chief, he carried great force of presence, and the aphids, knowing too his standing with their chief, were readily cowed into submission.

No one marked how, at the sound of Zhang Xiaojing's name, Taizhen's eyes lit up all at once.

In a bustle of many hands the aphids bound the Son of Heaven and Taizhen together, and strung a belt as well along the rope, lest the Son of Heaven, old and failing, should lose his grip on it for a moment.

By now Zhang Xiaojing had recovered a little of his strength, and said that he would come and check the rope. The Son of Heaven was a personage of high worth; to take extra care was only fitting. Bearing down against the fierce ache in his muscles, Zhang Xiaojing stepped up close, took hold of the rope with one hand, and murmured low: 'Your Majesty, I have come to save you.'

The Son of Heaven gave a snort of derision through his nostrils—at such a moment as this, and still playing at such tricks. But Taizhen blinked her lovely great eyes and said in a small voice: 'I know you. You are Tanqi's sweetheart.'

Zhang Xiaojing started—where on earth had this sprung from?

Tanqi, to win Taizhen over at the time, had falsely claimed that she and Zhang Xiaojing were sweethearts, their affections mutual. Such an embarrassing detail she had naturally been too shy to mention when she recounted the matter to him. With the case now so urgent, Zhang Xiaojing could not well press the point. He tightened the head of the rope once more and murmured: 'True or false, Your Majesty will know before long. Only I beg you—watch for the moment, and act as it serves.' Then he stepped away.

Taizhen shut her eyes tight and clasped both arms about the Son of Heaven with all her strength. The Son of Heaven took hold of the rope, glanced once downward, and hastily drew his gaze back again, his face gone pale. An emperor of the great Tang must pass through perils of every kind in his life, yet a peril such as this day's he met for the very first time.

He had, after all, weathered great storms in his time; setting his teeth, he gripped the rope fast, let the weight of the two of them bear down upon it, and then slid slowly down through the opening.

The two of them slid down through the empty air, cringing and fearful, making for the city wall. To see how close they clung, they might truly have been a pair of lovebirds soaring across the edge of heaven. Their pace was very slow, and several times along the way there were moments of peril. Happily the Son of Heaven was much given to polo in his ordinary days, and was tended with the greatest care besides, so that his frame and his reflexes were far better than a common old man's; and in the end he came down safe upon the city wall after all the alarm.

The moment Xiao Gui saw the Son of Heaven touch down, he came forward at once and had him fast. Taizhen there was no special need to mind; she was already half fainting with fright.

Right after, an aphid slid down without mishap, and Zhang Xiaojing was bound tight to his body. Zhang Xiaojing's strength had come back a little, and his two hands could grip the rope fast and share the strain, so that these two came down more smoothly, in fact, than the pairing of the Son of Heaven and Taizhen.

But when the next aphid slid down, the mishap came.

He had slid but halfway when the rope, as though it could not bear the load, snapped and flew apart with a sharp crack. A black shape, with no time even to scream, fell heavily from mid-air onto the top of the wall; his spine came down squarely upon a jutting merlon, and his whole body was snapped in that instant into two halves. The upper part swung violently down again, the skull burst open, and turbid brains smeared all across the face of the wall.

It was fortunate that Taizhen was in a daze and did not notice the grisly sight, or she would surely have shrieked aloud and brought a killing calamity down on them all. The Son of Heaven, holding Taizhen up, saw this ghastly thing, arched a brow, and could not help casting an extra glance at Zhang Xiaojing.

Xiao Gui stood rooted where he was, a look of blank astonishment on his face. The blood streaming from his wounded eye had smeared over half his face, giving him a look of peculiar ferocity.

This was more than the trouble of losing a single man. There was but one rope, and now that it had parted, the way back for the three people up above was utterly cut off. Of Xiao Gui's men there now remained, besides the half-crippled Zhang Xiaojing, but one alone.

That rope was twisted from the split fibers of hemp-goat vine and horsetail hair, shrunk in cold water and then steeped in oil, tough beyond compare; by rights it could not have broken so soon. Before he came down, Xiao Gui had checked it inch by inch, and had felt no hidden flaw in it at all. How could it have snapped for no reason?

While Xiao Gui sank into his doubts, Zhang Xiaojing, without a sound, drew his hand together and slipped into his sleeve an ivory-handled folding knife that was not his own. He had filched it a little before, in the scuffle with the Son of Heaven.

When Zhang Xiaojing had gripped the rope, the folding knife was already hidden in his palm, its point pinched between two fingers. As he slid down, the point had quietly cut into the rope. The force and the angle, of course, had to be judged to a nicety, and a part of the load-bearing strength kept back, or else the rope would part before the man reached ground—which would be no different from suicide.

Zhang Xiaojing had used ropes of this kind before and knew their nature to the bone. As he cut, he raised the blade a hair, so as to pick apart only the outer ring of vine-fiber. The vine-fiber bore the pull, and the horsetail hair the bending. Once the fiber was severed, the horsetail hair could keep the rope stiff still, but could no longer bear any weight.

'Let us go.'

Xiao Gui had but gazed off once, then quickly turned about and spoke it without expression. Those three aphids trapped on the tower-top were past all saving; when a thing must be cut off, cut it off.

'And where do you think to go?' The Son of Heaven kept still his tone of mockery.

Even had these aphids, for all their endless schemes, at last brought themselves down onto the southern wall—what of it? The Son of Heaven knew this quarter all too well. Along the wall, every fifty paces there stood a sentry post, one man each within and without, seen and hidden; and every three posts had a wall-officer set over them. They were still within a net spread across heaven and earth, with nowhere to flee.

Xiao Gui said coldly: 'Our escape just now we owed to the private whim of an old Persian artisan; for the road ahead, we must thank the bounty of Your Majesty.'

'Mm?' The Son of Heaven felt on the instant that something was wrong.

'Take the walled corridor.' Xiao Gui bit the words out.

Yao Runeng huddled in his cell, cold in body and in heart.

He remembered still the scene of his arrest atop the great watchtower: purple lantern in hand, he had signaled to Zhang Xiaojing for all he was worth—'Do not come back, do not come back, do not come back.' The Jing'an Bureau was no longer what it had been. Then vicious, brutal guards had rushed upon him, dragged him down from the great watchtower, and flung him into the freezing gaol.

Yao Runeng did not know that Wen Ran had been taken almost at the same moment; still less did he know how great an effect that message, once passed along, would have upon the whole course of things.

As to what would befall him next, Yao Runeng knew full well. Tomorrow Ji Wen and Yuan Zai would surely fasten some false charge upon him, and his family's good name would be shamed by it. But he did not repent of it in the least, for it was a right thing he had done; however the world outside might blacken him, his own heart would render a just verdict—and, more than this, he feared whether the Que-le Huo-duo had truly been stopped or no.

'If only Commander Zhang were here, all would surely be well.' So Yao Runeng thought, in his daze.

He knew not how long had passed when the lock of the cell door rattled, as though someone had opened it. Yao Runeng raised his head and saw a familiar figure standing in the doorway, hands clasped behind his back.

'Deputy Director Li?!'

Yao Runeng, overcome with glad surprise, scrambled up at once from the straw. He would have gone to meet him, but seeing how stern Li Bi's face was, he forced down his excitement and made only a simple hand-clasp bow.

'I know you have a bellyful of questions and grievances, but now is no time for weeping them out.' Li Bi wasted not a word, and came straight to the point. 'Go back at once to the great watchtower, and get the watchtowers working again as fast as you can. I want every city gate sealed on the instant, the lantern fair halted, the curfew laid on anew.'

Yao Runeng was greatly startled—had things come to so grave a pass as this? He had meant to ask how matters stood with the Que-le Huo-duo, but now could only swallow the words back down in silence.

'How soon can it be mended?' Li Bi asked.

Yao Runeng thought a moment and said that a single mark would suffice. Li Bi was much surprised—so soon as that?

Most of the nodes in the watchtower system were in fact running as they should; only the central hub of the great watchtower needed setting to rights. The work itself was not great; the hard part was finding men who understood the watchtower craft. The reason it had gone so long unmended was that Ji Wen understood none of it, and had moreover driven off a whole company of Hu officials, so that in the matter of manpower it was frost heaped upon snow.

The most pressing thing now was to send word out, so the great watchtower need not be restored to its full working state; a simple power to receive and send was enough, and so he dared to strike his chest and say a single mark would do.

Having heard Yao Runeng out, Li Bi was well pleased: 'Good and quick. Set to it at once; whatever supplies you need, only say the word.'

'Yes, sir.'

Li Bi made a gesture and had someone help Yao Runeng up, and passed him a bowl of hot mutton broth, cooled to just the right warmth, with a few slices of flatbread soaking in it. Cold and hungry, Yao Runeng took it without ceremony and drank in great gulps. Just then Li Bi threw out another question of a sudden: 'There is a traitor within the Jing'an Bureau—did you know it?'

'What? No, I did not.' Yao Runeng was greatly startled, and nearly dropped the bowl on the ground. 'Had I known, I should certainly have reported it long ago.'

Li Bi said: 'From our analysis, we judge that this traitor must have had some dealings with you, and must at some point have let slip a flaw. Think it over carefully; if anything comes to you, tell me at any time.' Then he turned and left.

Yao Runeng nodded gravely, then asked all at once, out of curiosity: 'Was it Recorder Xu who worked it out?'

Li Bi's steps halted a moment, but he said nothing, and walked on ahead. Yao Runeng was somewhat baffled, but this was no fit time to press the question. He drained the mutton broth at a draught, slapped hard at both cheeks, gave a loud shout of the watch-cry, and then set off toward the great watchtower.

Hearing behind him that watch-cry, so full of vigor, Li Bi could not help a sigh, and felt of a sudden a touch of envy for Yao Runeng's ignorance.

Had he known the state Chang'an was in just now, he would hardly have been so easy of heart. But then again—who could grasp the whole of it? Unlooked-for, Li Bi thought once more of Zhang Xiaojing, and wondered where he had been when the lantern-tower blew apart.

The one thing Li Bi could be sure of was that, so long as there was one chance in ten thousand, this fellow would never give up.

Oh—yes, there was Tanqi too. It struck Li Bi as strange that only now, of all times, had he thought to trouble over where she had got to. Since she had gone out with Zhang Xiaojing there had been no word of her. But the girl was clever; surely she would have hidden herself away somewhere safe.

These irrelevant matters flashed through his mind and were gone. Li Bi turned his attention back to the present situation; and just then a runner came hurrying up before him, saying loudly that there had been a discovery, and handed him a roll of paper that the recorders, he said, had only just dug out.

Li Bi unrolled it and saw that it was a hand-declaration. The paper had yellowed and was of no few years' age. It was the deed of contract to a residence in Anye Ward, and the names of buyer and seller alike were strangers to him. The hand-declaration set out the layout of the residence in full—fully six courtyards deep it was, and took in besides a broad garden, with the kinds of trees, the buildings, the dimensions, and other particulars all written out; and there, plain to see, were a Persian pavilion, an underground chamber for the caging of beasts, and a great store of rare and precious plantings.

This layout Li Bi knew at a glance—it was the very residence the aphids had taken him to. He had not thought it would be unearthed so soon.

Anye Ward... Li Bi turned the name over in his mind, his expression a tangle of feeling.

Anye Ward was the fourth ward on the western side of the Vermilion Bird Avenue, one of the finest districts in all Chang'an, where those who dwelt were rich if not noble. But the most famous building in Anye Ward was the Temple of Empress Wu Zhenshun.

Empress Wu Zhenshun had in her life been Consort Wu, the most beloved of the Sage's favorites; she died in the twenty-fifth year of Kaiyuan, and after her death was raised in title to empress, with the posthumous name Zhenshun. Her memory was a most delicate thing in Chang'an. For she had a son named Li Mao, who took to wife Yang Yuhuan—and who was, in the end, robbed of her by his own father.

And between her and the heir apparent, Li Heng, there was a chain of cause and effect as well. To make an opening for Li Mao, Consort Wu had framed the then heir apparent, Li Ying, and driven him to his death. Yet the Son of Heaven, as it fell out, had no mind for Li Mao, and bestowed the title of heir apparent upon Li Heng instead.

So this Anye Ward, whether for Li Mao or for Li Heng, was a place where a welter of feelings met and crossed. Had this woman lived a few years longer, the fates of many, it may be, would have shifted with her.

Setting aside these old affairs of bygone years, Li Bi turned his attention once more to the hand-declaration, and noticed all at once that beside the buyer's name the place of registry was given as Longxi. His gaze shifted; a certain detail came suddenly to mind.

Some years before, the court had promulgated an Edict of Grace on the Bestowal of Residences, which laid down that the residences of the four wards on either side of the Vermilion Bird Avenue might not be bought or sold save by those of the imperial lineage or the titled nobility.

And beside this buyer's name on the hand-declaration there was set down no office and no rank of merit, nor any clan affiliation noted; the man was, to all appearance, a commoner of plain estate. That he could have bought a residence in Anye Ward admitted of but one possibility—that he was in truth some great house's house-born bondsman or trusted slave, buying on his master's behalf.

Such cases were far from rare. Many a man of sensitive standing, wishing to buy a separate residence and yet to keep his identity hidden, would put a household slave of his forward to do it. This kind of arrangement was called 'concealed holding.' This hand-declaration, then, ought to be a sale of the concealed-holding sort.

The buyer's place of registry being Longxi, the master behind him would naturally be some great clan that had come out of Longxi.

Li Bi gave a cold laugh and gave the hand-declaration a shake. The Right Minister, Li Linfu, was the great-grandson of a younger cousin of Emperor Gaozu, and a branch, too, of the Li clan kinsmen of Longxi.

This inference seemed crude and without warrant; but this was no trying of a case, and there was no need of ironclad proof. Let Li Bi but perceive the least thread of connection, and that was enough.

'Muster the Lüben Guards at once; I will lead them in person, and we go to Anye Ward.' Li Bi gave the order in few words. He needed to confirm with his own eyes whether that garden was the one he had been to.

The Deputy Director's order was carried out with the utmost speed. Thirty-odd soldiers of the Lüben Guards mustered swiftly and, with Li Bi at their head, went racing off toward Anye Ward. The observant among the Jing'an Bureau's people noticed that these soldiers bore not only blades and crossbows, but strong bows and iron shields as well.

With so grim an array, as though facing a great foe—were they going to look into a case, or to make war? So they wondered.

From Guangde Ward to Anye Ward was no very great distance, and in under a mark they were there. By that hand-declaration, the residence lay in the northwest of the ward, hard by the Temple of Empress Wu Zhenshun.

The ward was still ablaze with lantern-light, though the lantern-gazers had grown far fewer. It was, after all, already the hour of the Rabbit's second half, and those who had reveled through most of the night were going back in droves to catch up on their sleep. Li Bi and his party came straight to the gate of the residence. Here before the gate there were neither ranged halberds nor a black-lacquered lintel; it looked most plain and unassuming. Yet a costly seven-fragrance carriage stood at this moment before the gate, its sumptuous fittings betraying the uncommon taste of its owner.

'I have you now, old fox!' A thread of a smile showed at the corner of Li Bi's lips.

Two broad-shouldered, thick-waisted soldiers slammed the gate open with a crash, and those behind poured in. Li Bi had charged them especially that on no account were they to be careless or remiss, so they kept to the proper posture of assault—three to a group, advancing apart and striking together, with ten-odd crossbow-bolts trained at every moment on every quarter.

They rushed through the front court and the central courtyard; all about was silence, and along the whole way there was no hindrance of any kind. Doubt stirred in Li Bi's heart, yet still he pressed on. And when he stepped into the rear garden, the first thing to meet his eye was that strangely fashioned Self-Raining Pavilion.

There was no mistaking it—this was the place!

Li Bi clenched his fists. I have come back again!

At this moment, beneath that Self-Raining Pavilion, several people stood. The rest were dressed as pages and servants; only the one in the middle wore a round-collared robe of brocade and a black gauze head-wrap, and stood with his hands clasped behind his back—it was none other than the Right Minister.

The two of them looked upon each other, four eyes meeting, and before either had opened his mouth, all at once the sound of the street drums came flying over the walls from afar and into their ears. It was not one drum sounding, but many drums, taking up the beat together from every side, far and near.

The people of Chang'an knew this drumming better than anything. On an ordinary day, the moment the sun set the street drums would sound, struck three hundred times running, to signal that the curfew was about to begin. Anyone who could not get home before the drums fell silent had better seek lodging than stay in the streets, or he would be beaten with the rod, or even judged to a capital crime.

That the street drums should sound now, at the hour of the Rabbit, meant not only that the lantern fair was halted, but that Chang'an was entering a total lockdown, one that would not be lifted even after sunrise.

The moment Xiao Gui said 'the walled corridor,' both the Son of Heaven and Zhang Xiaojing understood at once.

In the plan of Chang'an, the north was the place of honor. North of the Zhuque Gate, past the Chengtian Gate, stood the Taiji Hall. Emperor Gaozu and Emperor Taizong had both held their councils in this hall, and it was the pivot on which the empire turned. Later Emperor Taizong raised the Yong'an Palace to the east of the Taiji Hall, calling it the 'Eastern Precinct' to distinguish it from the Taiji Hall, the 'Western Precinct'; afterward it was renamed the Daming Palace. When Emperor Gaozong came to reign, he did not care for the geomantic siting of the Taiji Hall, and so removed to the Daming Palace to hold his councils there.

From then on the emperors of each reign attended to affairs in the Daming Palace, enlarging it time and again, until its scale was vast indeed. In the Kaiyuan years, the Son of Heaven struck out on a fresh design of his own, taking the Xingqing Ward to the south of the Daming Palace and rebuilding it into the Xingqing Palace, where he took up his long abode, and which was called the 'Southern Precinct.'

The distance between the Xingqing Palace and the Daming Palace was rather great, and the Son of Heaven's going back and forth between the two was much beset with inconvenience. So, in the sixteenth year of Kaiyuan, the Son of Heaven struck out once more on a fresh design, and from the southern wall of the Daming Palace raised an elevated corridor within a doubled wall. The corridor began at the Wangxian Gate, ran eastward along the southern wall, met the eastern wall of Chang'an's outer rampart, then turned south, passed over the Tonghua Gate, and joined onto the southern wall of the Xingqing Palace.

In this way, whenever the Son of Heaven wished to pass between the two palaces, he could go by this walled corridor and never trouble the common folk. Afterward, finding the device truly excellent, he extended the corridor southward as far as Qujiang, for a whole length of nearly sixteen li. From then on, from the Daming Palace in the north to the Qujiang Pool in the south, the Son of Heaven could roam the whole of Chang'an without once setting foot outside the palace walls.

On so chaotic a night, when every eye was fixed on the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, no one would ever think the aphids might set their minds on the walled corridor. Xiao Gui needed only to hold the Son of Heaven hostage, go down by the stairs near the southern wall into the corridor, and then, following the empty and deserted passage, flee straight south to the Qujiang Pool—getting out of the city would be as easy as turning his hand.

Small wonder he had called this escape route 'a gift bestowed by the Son of Heaven'—the words were not the least bit wrong. The Son of Heaven's face was iron-grey; he thought the fellow an utter blackguard, yet in his eyes there was, more than anything, a wariness.

From the fierce-fire thunder of the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower to the water-driven works that fed the Longchi; from the Yaluoshan idol atop the Qinzheng Wuben Tower to the walled corridor—before ever he set his hand to it, this fellow had truly carried his preparations to the very limit, and studied Chang'an through and through. What close-woven cunning of mind, what vast daring, it must have taken to build up so intricate a plan.

And this plan of his had, of all things, succeeded.

No—to be exact, it had by now drawn infinitely close to success, and wanted but the last step.

Xiao Gui knew well the saying that of a hundred-li journey ninety li is but the halfway mark, and did not let triumph carry him too far away with himself. He had the one remaining aphid help Zhang Xiaojing up, then took his own stand behind the Son of Heaven and Taizhen, and barked at them to move quickly on.

'You have won already; let her go. You have no men to spare in any case.' So the Son of Heaven spoke once again.

Xiao Gui was, in fact, somewhat moved by this suggestion. But Zhang Xiaojing spoke up: 'No. Let her go, and the guards will find out soon enough. Once the drums pass the word along, the corridor will be shut at once, and we shall be turtles in a jar.' Xiao Gui, hearing this, found reason in it, and so pushed Taizhen up onto her feet as well.

'You—'

The Son of Heaven turned a glare of fury on Zhang Xiaojing. Ever since that one aphid had fallen to his death, he had begun to place a little hope in Zhang Xiaojing, and now that hope was gone again. But Zhang Xiaojing feigned not to see it. Taizhen's safety was no concern of his; he cared only to lay a heavier burden on Xiao Gui, for only so would there be a chance to save anyone.

Xiao Gui parceled out in short order the task of guarding the hostages, and led his much-shrunken band onto the road once more. They went east a stretch along the wall, and soon saw, ahead of them, a huge fissure open between the walls—a fissure regular and dead straight, as though some master craftsman had cut it open little by little with a flat chisel, running on into the distance.

A downward path of stone steps reached toward the bottom of the fissure. Down the steps they went, slowly, and felt as though they had tumbled headlong into a wholly different world.

This so-called walled corridor was a narrow way dug out through the middle of the city wall, wide enough for a single carriage to pass; its two sides were built up with blue brick, its floor leveled with river sand and paved over with flagstones. The thickness of the wall being limited, the corridor too could be made only so narrow as this.

At this depth, all the light and clamor of the outside were shut away, making a deep gloom out of nothing. The brick walls to either side rose high and close, their slope leaning faintly inward, like two great mountains bearing in toward the middle. A man walking at the bottom felt like a toad squatting at the foot of a well: raise his head, and all he could see was a single thread of night sky overhead.

There were no patrolling guards within the corridor, and it was exceedingly still. Walking inside it, they could hear even one another's breathing plain as day. In such a place, every one of them fell into a kind of daze, as though the confusion of interlacing light and shadow just past had been only a gorgeous dream.

One could not but admire the Son of Heaven's imagination, that he could think to break, between the city walls, a hushed and sealed-off road like this. Walking here, one need fear no common folk spying, and could go with a light carriage and scant train. In daylight, how pleasant it must be.

After walking about a mark, they saw the road ahead come to its end. This must be the end of the southern wall of the Xingqing Palace; ahead lay the eastern wall of Chang'an's outer rampart. Here a fork branched off, reaching out in two directions, north and south.

'Xiao Gui, which way do you mean to take?' Zhang Xiaojing asked.

The road to the north led straight into the Daming Palace—as good as walking into the net oneself; the road to the south led to the Qujiang Pool, which was a fine enough goal, only the way was long, ten li at the very least. In the state this party was in, without horses they would be worn to collapse before ever they reached Qujiang.

Xiao Gui seemed to have his reckoning laid long since; he stretched out a hand, pointing south: 'To Qujiang.'

Zhang Xiaojing did not ask why; Xiao Gui was sure to have arranged it all beforehand. The fellow had prepared far too thoroughly; even if he were to conjure a horse out of his pocket now, Zhang Xiaojing would feel no surprise.

The party turned south and walked a long stretch more. All at once Taizhen dropped down sitting on the ground, pleading that she truly could go no further. Clad in brocade and fed on jade, carried by carriage wherever she went—when had she ever walked so far on foot? The Son of Heaven bent down and asked after her with concern; aggrieved, she slipped off her cloud-toed brocade slippers and gently rubbed her own ankle. Even in the black of night, that skin white enough to shame frost and outdo snow stood out with singular brightness.

His face dark, Xiao Gui barked at her to keep moving. The Son of Heaven straightened up and set himself in front of Taizhen, insisting they rest a while. Xiao Gui said with a cold laugh: 'Every extra finger-snap we linger is one more measure of danger that the guards will cut us off. Should I be driven to the end of my road, you both shall come to no good end either.'

Hearing this naked threat, the Son of Heaven, helpless, could only go and help Taizhen draw her cloud-toed slippers back on. Taizhen's moth-brows knit faintly, and she seemed on the verge of tears. Aching with tenderness, the Son of Heaven stroked her powdered back and soothed her low, and with great difficulty brought her weeping by degrees to a stop.

At this Zhang Xiaojing spoke up: 'I have rested near enough; I can just manage to walk on my own. Better let me be the one to guard Taizhen.'

Xiao Gui thought it over: paired this way, it was in fact better. Taizhen was too frail to stand against a breeze, and in his present state Zhang Xiaojing could keep watch over her well enough, which freed up one aphid's hands to guard the Son of Heaven with undivided care.

So the party made a small rearrangement: they bound the hands of both the Son of Heaven and Taizhen once more, and pressed on. This time Zhang Xiaojing walked behind Taizhen; the one dainty and the other weak, neither could go fast, and they trailed far at the very rear of the band. Taizhen walked stumbling, murmuring complaints without cease, while Zhang Xiaojing kept his silence throughout.

This corridor was no unchanging straight line. Every two hundred paces the road would suddenly broaden for a span, opening out a ring of clear ground to either side, which was called a passing-bay. Thus, when the Son of Heaven's carriage drove through, the patrol-soldiers and menials along the way would have a place to step aside and make their bows, and other vehicles could pass one another with ease. Were one to look down from the sky upon the whole dead-straight corridor, one would find a string of passing-bays set along it, like many knots tied along a single rope.

This little band had walked they knew not how long when another passing-bay appeared ahead. Xiao Gui waved a hand to signal a halt, saying they would rest a while. That said, he went on ahead alone, and was soon lost in the dark.

Taizhen, past all care for decorum, plumped down on the ground, panting daintily and without cease. The Son of Heaven would have come over to comfort her, but an aphid barred his way. Xiao Gui had left word before he went that these two were not to be let near each other. The Son of Heaven, having grasped his own situation, did not waste his breath in loud rebukes; he cast a sullen glare at Zhang Xiaojing, walked to the other end of the passing-bay, and gazed up, hands behind his back, at that single thread of pitch-black sky.

Zhang Xiaojing stood beside Taizhen, his body leaning against the stone wall, his eyes lightly closed. Over one whole day his strength had been spent too far, and now he could barely walk at all. He had to seize every moment to recover his vigor as fast as he could, against the fierce fight that might yet come.

All at once a woman's low voice slipped into his ear: 'Zhang Xiaojing, you are a good man, really—you will save us, will you not?' Zhang Xiaojing's heart tightened; he opened his single eye and saw Taizhen tilting up her round face in curiosity, the tracks of tears still beneath her eyes. Her right hand went on rubbing her ankle. An aphid glanced over their way, but felt no suspicion.

'What makes you say so?' Zhang Xiaojing asked back, lowering his voice.

'I trust Tanqi.'

Zhang Xiaojing started, then gave a slight nod of his head: 'She is a girl clever as ice and snow, that one—but what has your trusting her to do with me?'

Taizhen said, with a look between a smile and none: 'The man Tanqi loves could not be a bad man.'

'Uh...'

'But I can see there is nothing, really, between you and Tanqi. Women in love, and men in love—I have seen far too many of both. She is one; you are not.'

Zhang Xiaojing was somewhat at a loss—what a moment for it, and here this woman was, taking up such a topic with lively interest. Seeing this fierce, forbidding fellow wear so awkward a look, Taizhen could not help pursing her lips in a little smile.

'I knew it—there must have been some other purpose in what you did.'

'So all that you put on just now was only playacting, to make the aphids let down their guard?' Zhang Xiaojing asked back.

'No—when I was sliding down from the roof of the hall, I truly was near to breaking apart. But set against the life of wealth and rank I am about to lose, I would sooner slide down ten times more.' Taizhen gave a self-mocking laugh. 'I am a female Daoist who has forsaken her husband; were I to lose the Son of Heaven's favor as well, I should be nothing at all. So I must seize on every chance to keep both the Son of Heaven and myself alive.'

Taizhen turned her neck slowly, her two eyes on the darkness ahead: 'Tanqi begged my help before, and saved your life; now I can only look to you to repay the debt.' As she said this, a look of firm resolve rose in her face, and she seemed a different person altogether from the pampered, feeble woman of a moment before. Zhang Xiaojing's single eye watched her, and his gaze grew earnest.

'Very well—you have guessed right. I am here to save people.' Zhang Xiaojing owned it at last.

Taizhen let out a breath and wiped away the tear-tracks with her fingers: 'That is very good indeed. Were the Sage to learn that he has so loyal a subject, it would gladden his heart.'

'A loyal subject?' Zhang Xiaojing gave a snort of a laugh. 'I am no loyal subject, and it is not out of loyalty to the Son of Heaven that I came. I have no interest in such things.'

This answer took Taizhen aback—not out of loyalty to the emperor? Then why, after all, was he doing all this? But just then an aphid happened to come strolling over, and the two of them both closed their mouths and turned their faces away.

The aphid cast a glance at the two of them and turned back again. The Son of Heaven paced anxiously, his hands clasped behind him, and Xiao Gui had not yet returned. The pity of it was that even against this one aphid alone Zhang Xiaojing was no match; his strength now could barely keep him talking and walking, and no more.

Faced with Taizhen's unexpected words, Zhang Xiaojing found he must amend his plan somewhat. He had taken her at first for a mere ornamental vase, good only to make more trouble for Xiao Gui; but she was far cooler-headed than he had supposed, and might, for all he knew, be of help to him.

He glanced ahead, turned his head once more to Taizhen, and said in a lowered voice: 'Next, there is one thing I need you to do.'

'I have no strength for fighting, mind—that is the thing I am worst at...' Taizhen said.

'No need for that. What I want you to do is the thing you like least.'

Before long, Xiao Gui came back out of the dark, a look of gladness on his face. He made a gesture to signal the party onward, and so they went on southward once more along the walled corridor.

This time they had not gone far before Xiao Gui halted the band. Ahead was another passing-bay, but here, on the left side, there was besides a brick stairway that climbed upward. Needless to say, the stairs must lead to the eastern wall of the outer rampart.

The corridor could not possibly be sealed off from end to end; it left here and there a set of stairs up and down the wall, for moving supplies or meeting emergencies. Xiao Gui's going on ahead just before had been to scout whether this stairway was guarded by anyone.

By rights these stairway entrances all had guards in ordinary times, to keep idlers and strays from entering the corridor. But today they had all been drawn off by the upheaval at the Xingqing Palace, and here there was, of all things, not a soul.

Xiao Gui waved a hand, and all of them left the corridor and climbed slowly up this stairway to the top of the city wall. The moment they reached the wall-head, the world about them turned clamorous and lively again, jerking them all at once back into the mortal Chang'an.

Zhang Xiaojing looked about him. The tall rampart split Chang'an into two worlds as sharply divided as the Jing and the Wei: within the wall it was still ablaze with lantern-light, but without lay a pitch-black darkness like a sea of ink. He narrowed his eyes and saw, far off to the south, a tall gate-tower—that must be the Yanxing Gate. Reckoning the distance from it, they were now upon the stretch of wall level with Jinggong Ward.

Jinggong Ward... a faint bitter smile rose on Zhang Xiaojing's face. From this height he could see, within the ward, a broad stretch of darkness: the polo ground. Months before, he had stood at the center of that field, holding Prince Yong under threat; then he had cast down his weapon and become a condemned prisoner, walking toward his own end—or toward another beginning.

Who would have thought that today, after coming full circle, he was back at the origin of it all. It was as though Zhang Xiaojing could see the wheel of creation turning in the unseen dark, grinding round and round like the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower itself.

'We go down from here.'

Xiao Gui's voice broke off Zhang Xiaojing's musings. He had walked to the outer side of the wall and patted a wooden frame beside him, something like the windlass over a wellhead. This wooden contrivance was much sturdier than a common windlass; ten-odd coils of thick hemp rope were wound about it, and its cross-arm reached out a span beyond the wall, from which hung a rattan basket, suspended in the air. Nearby, hard against the edge of the wall, a signal-flag was planted as well; but as there was little wind, the flag drooped upon its staff.

The laws of Chang'an were severe: at nightfall the gates were shut, and without an imperial warrant they would not open. If some urgent matter arose in the night that made it needful to enter or leave the city, the garrison had a way around it: they would rig a lowering-frame on the wall, tie on a great rattan basket, and a man or a horse would stand inside while they hoisted them up or down by the windlass.

This was the last step of Xiao Gui's plan—to use the lowering-frame to let everyone down outside the city. It was just now the darkest stretch of time before the dawn, and with the city in such disorder, no one would mark this inconspicuous piece of wall-head. The aphids could slip free of Chang'an's bonds at their leisure, and then go wherever they wished.

Seeing that but the last step now stood between him and success, even Xiao Gui could scarcely hold his composure. He said to the Son of Heaven with a smile: 'Your Majesty, take one more look at your Chang'an while you may; hereafter, I fear, you will have no chance to see it.' The Son of Heaven gave a cold snort and, hands clasped behind him, said not a word. He knew that with a vicious, brutal scoundrel like this, whatever he said would only bring him more humiliation.

The two hostages were kept, the one by Xiao Gui and the other by Zhang Xiaojing. The one surviving aphid set about loosing the ropes on the lowering-frame. He unwound the rope coil by coil, then hooked it onto the top of the great rattan basket.

A lowering-frame was required to hoist a man and a horse, and so this rattan basket was woven exceedingly strong. To keep its balance and not tip over, a rope was hung from each of the basket's four sides, gathered into one strand at the top and joined there to the windlass's hauling-line. To get these several ropes untangled and rightly joined was a matter of skill, or else the basket might well tilt askew halfway down—and that could cost a life.

The aphid worked at it a while, sweating from his whole head with the effort, and got the basket balanced at last. Once the windlass was let loose, they could begin lowering people down.

The next problem was hands.

For the basket to descend slowly, at least two men were needed to work the windlass—and two men with strength in them, at that. If Xiao Gui and the aphid took the windlass, then only the weakened Zhang Xiaojing would be left to guard the two hostages.

Xiao Gui did not hesitate long; he stepped up to the Son of Heaven and, all at once, chopped out with the edge of his hand and struck him at the neck. This lord of the highest majesty rolled up both eyes, toppled on the instant, and lay senseless. He had not struck the Son of Heaven unconscious before, because to get clear of the tangled environs of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower it was easier to let him walk on his own; now that it looked as though they would soon be out of the city, there was no more need to spare him.

Taizhen, thinking the Son of Heaven had been killed, let out a scream in spite of herself, crouched down, and trembled violently. Xiao Gui cast her a cold glance and ordered the aphid: 'Knock her out as well.'

He knew Zhang Xiaojing's body was now utterly spent and could hardly judge the force of a blow, so he set the aphid to it. The aphid gave a grunt and went over to lay hands on Taizhen. At this Zhang Xiaojing said: 'Throw her into the basket first, then knock her out.' The aphid started a moment, then took his meaning.

It was a good suggestion, and would save a measure of strength in the hauling. So the aphid seized Taizhen by the arm, dragged her roughly all the way to the edge of the wall, and flung her into the rattan basket. Taizhen huddled at the bottom of the basket, panting without cease, the jade pin in her hair trembling.

The aphid too stepped into the basket and reached out a hand to pinch her neck, thinking to himself whether this tender, slender pink neck might be severed with a single chop. But Taizhen, at the sight of his reaching hand, took fright and shrank hastily aside. The basket hung suspended in mid-air, and at this movement of hers the whole of it swayed unsteadily.

The aphid could scarcely keep his feet, and grabbed hold of the basket's rim, roaring: 'Do you want to die?'

This shout of rebuke worked the opposite effect: Taizhen dodged all the more wildly, swaying this way and that while the tears streamed down her face. The aphid perceived that she seemed to be doing it on purpose, and flew into a rage; he rose and pressed close, meaning to teach this vile wench a good lesson.

As he pressed forward like this, the basket swung more violently still. To dodge the aphid's assault, Taizhen leaned back behind her with all her might. Suddenly a scream burst from Taizhen's mouth. She seemed for an instant to lose her balance, her right arm flung high, as though she were about to topple out over the side.

In his alarm the aphid reached out to catch Taizhen's sleeve, hoping to haul her back. But in the instant his palm clutched the sleeve, he found something was wrong.

Though Taizhen was a female Daoist by station, she cultivated the Way within the palace after all, and dressed unlike the common run of Daoists. Today, being the Shangyuan Festival, she wore over her Daoist robe a plain-colored silk stole. This stole passed about her neck and spread across her two shoulders and the crooks of her arms, its ends pinched between her fingers, giving an air at once understated and noble.

A moment before, Taizhen had quietly wound the stole anew: not about her neck, but with the whole long scarf looped loosely over her right arm, its two ends slack and untied, so that it was easily mistaken for a sleeve. This way of wearing it was called a 'false drape,' and was used, as a rule, in private for the receiving of a close friend among the women's chambers.

How was the aphid to know the ways of these noblewomen? He thought he had caught the sleeve, when in truth he had caught the stole looped loosely over her arm. The moment the stole took the strain, it slipped straight off her arm. The aphid had gathered his full force, meaning to use the advantage of his weight to drag her back, and now all at once clutched nothing; his whole body pitched violently backward, toppling out over the side of the basket.

Luckily the aphid too was a skilled hand from the army, quick of eye and quick of hand; though his body had fallen out, his two hands had gripped the basket's rim. His fright not yet settled, he was just about to heave himself back over when all at once he felt a stab of fierce pain in his fingers.

It turned out that Taizhen, finding courage from who knew where, had drawn from the breast of her gown an ivory-handled folding knife and, eyes shut, stabbed at him with all her might. This folding knife had been the Son of Heaven's own; later Zhang Xiaojing had taken it, and now it had come into her hands.

The aphid dared not let go, and could not strike back either; he could only cling to the outer rim of the basket and dodge for his life. A veteran soldier stripped of his armor and a rare beauty of the palace—so it was that, inside and outside a rattan basket swaying in mid-air, the two of them joined in a strange duel.

Taizhen, after all, had no experience of fighting; she did not know what a vital point was, and only stabbed wildly on. The upshot was that though the aphid took many wounds on his body, none of them was mortal. The aphid himself realized this, knew there was yet hope of striking back, and so, bearing down against the fierce pain, groped and clawed. By chance he caught hold of Taizhen's fallen loose hair, and with no thought to spare for tenderness toward the fair, he hauled at it hard. Taizhen felt only a burst of raw pain across her scalp as her whole body was dragged over, and the aphid raised his hand and smashed it down all at once, striking her square on the temple.

Never had Taizhen borne such suffering; with a cry of pain she crumpled limply to the bottom of the basket and fainted away.

Snarling with fury, the aphid climbed back into the basket, meaning to give this wench a heavy lesson. But just then, from overhead, came a faint clicking sound of something breaking; he raised his head and saw that one of the ropes holding up the basket had, of all things, snapped—it must be that Taizhen, flailing wildly a moment before, had cut through a suspending rope by mistake.

The aphid's face changed; he sped hand and foot to clamber inside, but it was too late. The basket, robbed of a quarter of its support, tipped abruptly toward the other side. The aphid gave a wail; his two hands could hold on no longer, and his whole body fell out just so.

The wail had not yet faded when, in mid-air, another crisp sound of breaking was heard.

It turned out that the grappling just past had tangled the suspending ropes near the basket into a snarl of hemp thread. As the aphid fell, his neck happened to slip into one of the rope loops. That crisp crack was the sound of his neck-bone being wrenched apart by the sudden plunge of his body.

The basket still swung of itself, Taizhen slumped senseless at its bottom. Below the basket, the last aphid hung with his head lolling and his two eyes bulging, his body left to swing by the ropes in mid-air, creaking back and forth against the wall in the dark of night.

All this happened too fast; standing by the windlass, Xiao Gui could not take it in at all. Only when the aphid gave his last wail did he grasp that something was wrong, and in two strides for three he reached the edge of the wall and looked into the basket.

Seeing his last man too hanged dead, Xiao Gui flew into a great rage. A fierce light broke from his eyes; he looked toward Taizhen at the bottom of the basket, and at the very first glance marked the small ivory-handled folding knife gripped tight in her hand.

Xiao Gui's pupils contracted sharply. He remembered now: this ivory-handled folding knife was the one worn at the Son of Heaven's waist, taken by Zhang Xiaojing within the Star-Plucking Hall, and now it had fallen into Taizhen's hands. What that meant went without saying.

An unnatural stirring of the air swept past behind Xiao Gui's ear. He whipped his head around, only to see a black shape charging at him with every ounce of its strength, ramming him hard toward the outer side of the wall. In his alarm Xiao Gui could only shift his body barely in time, setting his back against the staff of that signal-flag near the lowering-frame, taking it for what poor support it could give.

In that instant barely bought, Xiao Gui saw clearly. The one charging at him was none other than his old comrade-in-arms of years gone by, Zhang Big-Head.

'Big-Head, you—' Xiao Gui cried out. But the other kept his face dark and said nothing. He had no strength left to grapple, and so, fixed on the resolve to perish together, had charged with his own body for a weapon—it was his only choice.

The flagstaff held out for less than a finger-snap before it snapped with a crack. The two of them, and that one signal-flag, leapt from the top of Chang'an's eastern wall out into the air. The great flag caught a gust of wind all at once, spread open in a flash, and, wrapping the two of them, fell away toward the far country outside the city—just as it had in years gone by.

At that very moment, on the eastern horizon there appeared the first faint stroke of dawn. The dim morning light cast itself toward Chang'an, and lit, just so, the silhouettes of those two figures fallen out beyond the wall into the curtain of night.

Within the city of Chang'an the street drums began to boom, sounding out across the whole city.
