### A Vicious Manhunt (Part 1)

### Agents Sent to Hunt Down Chen Geng

After Gu Shunzhang betrayed the Party and went over to the enemy, he served his new masters with a whole heart. The Kuomintang's secret-service apparatus made him chief of a Zhongtong action squad, and he threw himself into wrecking the Communist Party for them, hunting down and killing Communists and progressives with a vicious cruelty. He gathered a band of traitors and defectors into a new action squad, trained them, and set them to destroying our underground organization. Yang Zhihua, then working in the Party Center as a member of the Central Women's Work Committee, wrote under the alias Du Ning that Gu Shunzhang directed the Kuomintang agents to "use every captured renegade, spread them across the streets, above all at the busy crossroads, the tram stops, even the vegetable markets: seize the important ones, shadow the rest. The wrecking of the Central Military Commission in 1933 and the arrest of Comrade Chen Geng came about because the renegades spotted Chen Geng's wife at a vegetable market and shadowed her. And his wife was taken because Gu knew of her ties to her own family home; she went there, and there she was caught."

Chen Geng was seized on March 24, 1933, by agents Gu Shunzhang sent. Afterward Gu even came to the prison where Chen Geng was held to press him to surrender, and sent his own agents into the courtroom to testify against him. Arrested about the same time were Luo Dengxian, Liao Chengzhi, Tan Guofu, and others. Lin Jinsheng, the bodyguard Xu Enzeng had assigned to Gu Shunzhang, later recalled that after his defection Gu opened the first Zhongtong "agent training class" in Shuangtang Lane in the southern city of Nanjing, with only four Whampoa graduates under instruction. On finishing, Shi Jimei (alias Lü Keqin, Ma Shaowu) was sent to Shanghai as head of the action district there, where he set about "wrecking the Communist Party's underground on a great scale, luring, seizing, and kidnapping Communists and progressives; the most famous of these were the March 1933 arrests of Chen Geng, Liao Chengzhi, Luo Dengxian, Yu Wenhua, and the rest, which shook the whole country at the time." The arrests of Ding Ling, Pan Zinian, and other literary figures also fell within this same stretch. In the latter half of 1933 Shi Jimei was himself eliminated by the Communist underground. That same year the deputy district chief, the renegade Chen Weiru, narrowly escaped the same fate, which threw a great scare into the traitors' ranks. Through all this frenzied Zhongtong campaign against the Party's Shanghai underground, Gu Shunzhang often came to Shanghai in person to plan and direct it. Turning to Chen Geng's arrest, Lin Jinsheng went on: the strong-arm men who did the action work for Gu Shunzhang were chiefly the renegades he had brought over from the Communist Special Section: Tong Guozhong; Cai Fei, nicknamed "Old White-Face"; and Chen Liansheng, nicknamed "Little White-Face" (aliases Zhang Alin and Zhang Wennong, Gu Shunzhang's own protégé). These were Gu's trusted men, devils who killed without a blink, desperadoes of the cruelest stripe. The arrest of the great Chen Geng in March 1933 came of Chen Liansheng's shadowing. Chen Liansheng had known Chen Geng back in his Special Section days; spotting him one day on the street, he followed him right into a cinema. He meant to make a secret snatch after the show at a suitable spot, but Chen Geng saw him, and rather than wait for the film to end walked briskly out, meaning to shake the renegade off. Chen Liansheng clung to him, and at last the two grappled in the street. Left no choice, Chen Liansheng blew his whistle, and the police came running from all around and took Chen Geng on the spot to the old Laozha station in the British Concession; only after negotiation was he handed over to the Kuomintang's Shanghai Public Security Bureau. After the arrest Gu Shunzhang spoke with him several times. "I went along each time," Lin Jinsheng said. "I remember Chen Geng then in his Western suit, a tall man, close-cropped."

As related earlier, after Gu Shunzhang's defection Chen Geng had been sent by the Central Special Section to work in Tianjin. In September 1931 the Party Center dispatched him to the Fourth Front Army of the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army. He reached the E-Yu-Wan Soviet in early October and served in turn as commander of the 38th Regiment of the Red 13th Division, commander of the Red 12th Division, and chief of staff of the Fourth Front Army. In the third and fourth counter-encirclement campaigns in the E-Yu-Wan Soviet, Chen Geng led his men in valiant fighting and won repeated victories. On September 5, 1932, in the battle of Hushanzhai northwest of Xinji, he took a severe wound to the right knee. Unwilling to leave the firing line, he went on directing his troops from a stretcher.

On October 12 that year the main force of the Fourth Front Army left the E-Yu-Wan base and pushed west across the Beijing-Hankou railway. His wound was worsening by the day, and the organization decided to send him to Shanghai for treatment.

At the end of October, when the troops had reached the country between Nanyang and Xinye in Henan, Chen Geng disguised himself as a merchant and, limping on his right leg, slipped away from the army. He came through great hardship on the road and reached Shanghai in early November, finding the Party organization the very day he arrived. By then the Party Center had moved into the central red region, and the Party's leading body in Shanghai was the Shanghai Central Bureau. He reported to the Bureau on conditions in the E-Yu-Wan Soviet, then went to the Niu Huilin Orthopedic Hospital on Avenue Edward VII (now the corner of Yan'an and Chengdu Roads) to have his leg treated. Chen Geng had been a patient there in the winter of 1927 and knew both Dr. Niu Huilin and his brother Niu Huisheng well. Niu Huilin received him warmly, set him up in the best ward, and under the brothers' careful care the wound soon healed.

Coming out of the hospital, Chen Geng lodged with Shao Dafu, who had also come from the E-Yu-Wan Soviet; the two had done secret work together in Shanghai before and knew a good many people. The White Terror was then at its fiercest, and Chen Geng was a man who could not sit still, fond of moving about; fearing trouble, the Party organization found the two of them a place to lie low.

In March 1933 the Party Center decided to send Chen Geng to the Central Soviet in Jiangxi. On the eve of his departure, March 24, he went to the Beijing Theatre on Guizhou Road (later renamed the Lido Theatre) to see a film starring Li Lili, daughter of Qian Zhuangfei; as ill luck had it he was seated beside a renegade named "Alian" (nicknamed "Little White-Face"). Chen Geng later learned that his Shanghai lodging had been under enemy watch for several months, so the "chance meeting" in the cinema was likely no chance at all. The renegade's real name was Chen Liansheng, now going by "Zhang Alin"; once an apprentice at the Sincere Company in Shanghai, he had worked in the secret-service section of our Military Commission in Wuhan, then moved to Shanghai and into our Central Special Section. It was there he had come to know Chen Geng, before following Gu Shunzhang into betrayal. Spotting Chen Geng, the renegade feigned that he had never turned, and drew him into talk. Chen Geng at once left the cinema; the renegade dogged his heels, clinging fast. Chen Geng walked briskly on, meaning to lose him, but his newly healed leg would not let him run, and the renegade held him back until the two grappled in the street. Chen Geng struck out and knocked him down with a fierce blow. The renegade lay on the ground blowing his whistle, and the police came running from all sides, ringed Chen Geng in, and arrested him on the spot.

After his arrest Chen Geng was taken to the Laozha police station. Not long after, Liao Chengzhi and Luo Dengxian were shut in there too; they had been taken on March 28.

Arrested in Shanghai the same day as Chen Geng was a woman comrade calling herself his sister, Chen Zaoying (also given as Chen Xiuying or Chen Shuzhen), who was brought to the same station. According to the Shanghai daily Shen Bao of March 31 that year, the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights, in a declaration issued specially on the case, set down briefly how they had been taken: "On March 28, three men, Luo Dengxian, Yu Wenhua, and Liao Chengzhi, were arrested on an informer's report. By the newspapers' account the charge was joining the Communist Party and the trade-union movement; Luo and Yu, the two of them, were taken at their lodgings, while Liao, calling by chance a few hours later, was detained along with them. On March 24, one Chen Geng (the writer's note: the papers at the time gave it wrongly as Chen Geng) was also arrested on a spy's report, and at the same time a Miss Chen (said to be his sister), coming by chance to Chen's rooms after his arrest, was likewise held. Chen Geng was charged as 'commander of the 14th Army of the Jiangxi Communist forces.' Yet by report there was not the slightest evidence for his arrest. Chen Geng's rooms and the five places he had lodged before were all searched, and nothing found."

The woman Communist then passing as Chen Geng's sister Chen Zaoying was in truth named Tan Guofu. She too was from Xiangxiang in Hunan, a young fellow-townswoman of Chen Geng's. Her grandfather was the noted modern democratic revolutionary Tan Renfeng (1860–1920), a veteran of the Tongmenghui, who had taken part in the Zhennanguan Rising, the Hekou Rising in Yunnan, and the Battle of Huanghuagang. Later, with Song Jiaoren and others, he had drawn together the revolutionary forces of the Yangtze provinces in readiness for revolt, keeping in touch with Huang Xing. In the 1911 Revolution he served for a time in the military command at Wuchang; in the "Second Revolution" he returned to Hunan to raise a campaign against Yuan Shikai, and after its failure went into exile in Japan, returning in 1916 to live in Shanghai. Tan Guofu had been a student at the Changsha Girls' Normal School and joined the revolution young; her family was rich and well known. Their house in Changsha was very grand, and the upper floor where she lived had once sheltered the Party organization's secret work. Later she came to Shanghai to work in the Central apparatus. She knew Chen Geng well, and after her arrest gave the name Chen Zaoying and claimed to be his sister. By her account, Chen Geng's Shanghai lodging had long been watched by the station's agents, though as it happened he was taken while out at the cinema. On later analysis, the enemy had known his lodging all along and had held off arresting him, meaning to use the place as bait to "fish"—to reach further in and wreck the Party's apparatus.

Because from 1928 to 1931, in his Special Section work, Chen Geng had often dealt with the concession police and with certain Kuomintang agents and detectives, when the enemy now hauled him into the station the police took one look and blanched. "What?" they said in astonishment. "You're Chen Geng! Aren't you Mr. Wang?" Lampson, the British chief of the Political Section and inspector for the International Settlement's Municipal Council, was badly shaken too. There was at the Laozha station an inspector sympathetic to the revolution, once in touch with the Central Special Section, who had met Chen Geng before; seeing that it was him, he quietly learned the circumstances of the arrest and at once passed word of it to the Party organization.

In the western cells of the station (the British called them the "flogging rooms") Chen Geng was beaten savagely, blow upon blow, and then put to the inhuman electric torture to make him give up the names of Communists and the Party's secrets. He bribed a few of the warders to slip him cigarettes, and under torture he chewed and swallowed them, hoping they might dull the pain a little. However the enemy racked him, they could squeeze nothing from his mouth. At length they announced that if there was still no confession they would use the electric torture again. Just then Soong Ching-ling, head of the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights, and Yang Quan, its executive member and secretary-general, brought a crowd of reporters to see Chen Geng, Luo Dengxian, Liao Chengzhi, and the others, and only then were the enemy forced to stop this inhuman cruelty.

Seeing that hard torture could not break Chen Geng, the enemy put on another face and tried soft deceit instead. Chen Geng never bent. Noticing the change in their methods, he seized the moments when their guard slackened to talk to his fellow prisoners and the warders, telling them the reasons of the revolution and stories of the Red Army's brave fighting. He had a gift for speech, and his stories often moved his listeners to sighs; even a seasoned old British agent like Lampson listened with relish and grew "friendly" toward him. For all their change of tack, their hope of softening him with the carrot in place of the stick, they got not one word of confession. Chen Geng kept his high revolutionary honor and gave up nothing of the Party's secrets. In prison he went on spreading the revolution's cause, organizing his fellows to resist ill-treatment, and steadily heartening and teaching the comrades within to keep up the struggle.

On March 31, 1933, the Shanghai Second Special District Court held a "trial." Questioned the same afternoon, along with him, were four others: Luo Dengxian, Liao Chengzhi, and two more. Among the witnesses that day was the renegade Wang Yuncheng.

When this trial-farce got up by the Kuomintang opened, Soong Ching-ling and Cai Yuanpei had engaged the noted Shanghai lawyer Dr. Wu Kaisheng and others to appear and see to the defendants' defense. The two lawyers brought in to defend Chen Geng were Lü Kunfan and Ni Gang. A reporter sent by the Shanghai journal China Forum set down the scene in the courtroom:

The court was packed with workers, police stood guard on every side; a tall Englishman stood to one side, watching these Chinese heads with his blue, contemptuous eyes; a hulking Chinese wore an S.M.P. uniform, and the other officials all bore the Kuomintang's white sun on a blue sky on their caps. Two court police stood at the door, barring the dozens of workers pressing in from the passage outside.

On the benches, thin bodies in dirty work clothes glared with hatred and loathing; a few old yellow faces must have been past thirty. On the high dais five plump judges sat in black robes. Behind them stood two tall English detectives, and by the witness stand too lounged a handsome Englishman.

First to be questioned were Luo Dengxian, Liao Chengzhi, and Yu Wenhua. Heedless of the three defendants' and their lawyers' righteous rebuttals and protests, the court went on nothing but the informer's bare word, and "there was no document or material proof to bear that word out. The Municipal Council's lawyer sat still and said nothing. The judges rose and went in. Five minutes later they came out again and announced that the 'criminals' were handed to the representative of the Shanghai Public Security Bureau," who bore them off into a waiting car, for all the world like a man seizing stolen goods.

Next came the case of Chen Geng and his "sister":

"After a wait, when the case of Chen Geng and Chen Zaoying opened, the judges, the Party representative, and the station's lawyer took their old seats again, and none said a word. Chen Geng said he had come back from the Three Eastern Provinces not long before and was looking for work. Then another well-known informer, one Zhang Alin (the writer's note: this was the very agent 'Alian,' 'Little White-Face,' under another alias), was called to the witness stand and said he knew Chen Geng and had worked with him in the Communist Party in 1926. Without any written proof at all, this spy named Chen Geng commander of the Jiangxi Red Army."

The defense lawyer pointed out that there was no proof of any tie between Chen Zaoying and Chen Geng, and none against Chen Geng himself; the judges, the Party representative, and the station's lawyer listened on in dead silence. Another recess, another announcement, and the Public Security Bureau bore its victims away once more.

In this "trial" the imperialists and Chiang Kai-shek's country-selling government were in collusion, and it was ruled that the Shanghai International Settlement's Municipal Police would hand Chen Geng and his four fellows over to the Nanjing government. On the day after this trial-farce, Soong Ching-ling, in bitter anger, laid bare without mercy the shameless crimes Chiang Kai-shek and his kind had committed in league with their foreign masters: "On March 31 the Shanghai Second Special District Court 'tried' five prisoners and ruled that the Shanghai Municipal Police would hand them to the Chinese authorities. This affair is a plain instance of the Chinese government and the imperialists in wolfish collusion, oppressing the anti-imperialist, anti-Japanese fighters of the Chinese people.

"These arrests of patriotic 'criminals' are but one more instance of the policy Chiang Kai-shek's government pursues, a policy that has now brought China to the edge of total dismemberment, sunk to a dependency of imperialism. So grave is the state of the nation that I feel it my duty to call once more upon the broad masses of China to rise and struggle."

Soong Ching-ling pointed out sharply that this "trial" "laid bare that the Chinese and foreign authorities had settled it all beforehand, and only brought the arrested out to be tried in the form of a trial to deceive the public; whatever evidence and reasons the defense offered, whatever articles of law it cited, could never change the court's decision. In truth the verdict on the arrested had been fixed before this trial-farce began; that much is plain. At the questioning no charge was laid against them. The judges merely suffered the lawyers' defense, while the Municipal Council's lawyer sat silent in his seat without a word. The defendants wholly refuted the informer's slander. Yet the Kuomintang representative in court was armed to the teeth, proclaimed that he stood for the Public Security Bureau as well, and, it is said, told people before the sitting that everything had been arranged." When this ugly farce was over, the judges, for form's sake, left the bench a few minutes and then came back and read out the extradition order.

In the enemy's "mixed court" Chen Geng, Luo Dengxian, Liao Chengzhi, and the other comrades made a great uproar, treating the majesty of the enemy's court with utter scorn and flinging their challenge in the face of the armed foe. They raised their voices in the "Internationale" and spoke out with passion, defending the great Chinese Communist Party and the shining cause they served, denouncing the Kuomintang reactionaries' every country-selling crime of surrendering sovereignty and shaming the nation. In the face of the enemy's savage threats and cruel torture, Chen Geng and the rest showed a fearless revolutionary heroism. They turned the enemy's court into a platform for the reasons of revolution. Their forthright arguments and unyielding bearing showed them fully for what they were: China's anti-imperialist fighters, every one a model the Chinese people should take pride in.

With the "trial" over, Chen Geng and his fellows were "handed" to the Shanghai Public Security Bureau, passing from the imperialists' western cells into the clutches of the Kuomintang reactionaries. As the enemy loaded them onto the truck they shouted revolutionary slogans the whole way. Moved from the International Settlement to the "Chinese city," Chen Geng was shut in the detention house of the Public Security Headquarters on Jing'ansi Road. Fearing he would escape, the enemy at one point chained him to an iron post in the cell.

After Gu Shunzhang's agents seized Chen Geng, the shameless renegade came again and again to the prison to "urge surrender," and each time drew a scathing rebuke. The famous American reporter Edgar Snow wrote:

A few days later Gu Shunzhang, who had put Chen Geng in prison, came to try to persuade him to leave the Communist Party and join the Kuomintang. Gu said the revolution had failed, that Communist principles would not work in China, that many Communists had received the Generalissimo's clemency. "Mr. Chiang has sent many telegrams about you," Gu said, "asking that you be given special treatment." Chen Geng denounced Gu Shunzhang as a traitor and said he would never be a man like him. Gu later sent him some fruit and gifts, but Chen Geng would not so much as glance at them.

The next day, April 1, Chen Geng was taken under guard to Nanjing, for he was a man famous for his knack of escaping. As they set out from Shanghai the enemy brought an iron chain and shackled him to Tan Guofu, put them into a barred prison car, and watched them closely the whole way. Before the train left Shanghai the two of them raised the "Internationale" together, and so at every station along the line, all the way to Nanjing. Their mournful, stirring song passed through the carriage walls and out the barred windows, heartening the passengers around them beyond measure and throwing the agents escorting them into a fright. When the train reached Nanjing, the Kuomintang gendarme commander Gu Zhenglun came in person to the station to "meet" Chen Geng, pointedly bearing a telegram from Chiang Kai-shek. This telegram, sent purposely for Chen Geng to see, said that in view of his record in Guangdong and during the Northern Expedition he was to be given every "comfort" and "encouragement," so as to bring him to "repent" and join the Kuomintang; if he would "reform and submit," it said, he would be granted high military rank and a fine "future." When Chen Geng was brought into the gendarme headquarters on Zhanyuan Road, Gu Zhenglun did indeed "favor" him with the best room. It was all, of course, wasted effort. Gu Zhenglun, a devil who killed without a blink, for all his shows of courtesy never heard a single pleasant word from Chen Geng's lips. He soon dropped the disguise and showed his true colors, throwing Chen Geng into a cell in the gendarme headquarters detention house.

No sooner was Chen Geng shut in the cell than the enemy came again with harsh "interrogation," pressing him to admit past "errors" and to give in full detail the Communists' revolutionary activity inside the Kuomintang and its army. The chief interrogator was the renegade Gu Shunzhang. Again and again Gu shamelessly "urged surrender," going on with his old refrain that "communism can never be realized in China," and even showed Chen Geng several renegades' confessions. Chen Geng flung the whole lot on the floor, cursed Gu Shunzhang for the shameless traitor he was, told him to get out, and left him in a wretched fix. Still Gu would not give up, and kept sending people to "persuade" him, all driven off by Chen Geng. After this Chen Geng was taken into a small foreign-style house, where the enemy sent two agents to plead with him: you are a patriot, they said, you have taken the wrong road. Chen Geng scorned these creatures and flatly refused to speak with them. Just then he met one of our own people, who quietly told him: "Deng Wenyi, Chiang Kai-shek's aide-de-camp, wants to find you; they are going to move you to Nanchang, so be prepared."

At this point the enemy tried a fresh trick. They sent several of Chen Geng's Whampoa "classmates" and "students" to visit him. These base, low creatures wore gold-braided general's uniforms and gleaming leather on purpose, to show off their high rank. They told Chen Geng that Chiang Kai-shek "could not bear" to kill Whampoa officers; that Chiang would welcome him if he would "come round"; and they put a jeering question, asking whether he could still endure the Red Army's hard life. Chen Geng despised these upstarts with their hands steeped in the people's blood, and with boundless loathing, in the sharpest, most cutting words, he ran through the Kuomintang reactionaries' country-selling crimes and told how the Workers' and Peasants' Red Army had beaten the Kuomintang armies to a pulp without lifting a hand—how he himself had lately led his men to take Zhao Guanying, commander of the 69th Division, and how the E-Yu-Wan Red Army had taken alive Yue Weijun, commander of the 34th Division, and cut to pieces Chiang's Tang Enbo's 2nd Division. Before Chen Geng, so righteous and unafraid, this pack felt their own smallness. Beaten down by his words to the end of their arguments, they lost their tempers and said angrily, "You still dare spread propaganda here?" With a light, cold smile Chen Geng answered wryly: "The questions are yours; I have only answered them truthfully." The Fascist die-hards slunk off one by one, heads hanging.

On April 4, Chen Geng and several other prisoners were each moved into a better single cell and given new underclothes. At first Chen Geng could not make out why; the next day, when Soong Ching-ling, Yang Quan, and others of the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights came with foreign friends to see them, it dawned on him why these "favors" had come so suddenly, and he was filled with fury at the Kuomintang reactionaries' trickery.

The League had all along been working hard to rescue Chen Geng and his fellows. On the day after they were taken to Nanjing, April 2, the League's national executive committee and its Shanghai branch held a joint session at 331 Avenue du Roi Albert in Shanghai. On the spot they set up a committee to rescue political prisoners and chose four representatives—Soong Ching-ling, Yang Quan, Shen Junru, and Harold Isaacs—to go to Nanjing and press on with the effort to save Chen Geng and the rest. That day Soong Ching-ling and Cai Yuanpei jointly wired Wang Jingwei, president of the Executive Yuan of the Nanjing Kuomintang government, and Luo Wengan, minister of justice, demanding the release of Chen Geng, Luo Dengxian, and the others. On April 5 Soong Ching-ling reached Nanjing. For Chen Geng's sake she went to Chiang Kai-shek himself and said: "Chen Geng was a Whampoa cadet; through the whole Eastern River campaign he fought at your side, and when you lost a battle it was Chen Geng who saved your life—but for him you would not have lived to this day. Now you would kill him: that is sheer ingratitude. Where is all the propriety, righteousness, honor, and shame you preach day after day?" Chiang bowed his head under her rebuke with nothing to say, and though he dared not have Chen Geng killed, he would not let him go. In Nanjing Soong Ching-ling also went to Wang Jingwei and Luo Wengan, demanded to their faces the immediate release of Chen Geng and the rest, and put four demands in writing: first, the immediate release of all political prisoners; second, the abolition of arbitrary torture; third, freedom for political prisoners to read newspapers and books, an end to shackles, and better prison conditions; fourth, the severe punishment of jailers who extorted from and preyed upon the prisoners and who took bribes.

On the evening of April 5, Soong Ching-ling and the others visited Chen Geng and his fellows in prison. The China Forum reporter who came with the League's delegation set down the scene of their visit to that prison shrouded in White Terror:

At the mouth of every courtyard, dozens of guards, bayonets flashing on their rifles, ranged on both sides. Behind every gate a screen was painted with the Kuomintang's white sun on a blue sky. Everywhere were plainclothesmen with the Party badge. Trim officers in crisp uniforms wore gleaming yellow belts. Everywhere were rifles, bayonets, and Mauser pistols—the emblems of Kuomintang rule. Looking down from the wall of a side room upon the commandant's office was a portrait of Sun Yat-sen.

The delegation was led by five officers and a squad of armed soldiers through gate after barred gate, down a long, winding, maze-like corridor. Some doors along the way were shut hurriedly as we drew near. At times one could glimpse people moving inside. We came to a block of sealed cells: along a corridor two feet wide and forty feet long, seven cells on each side, the passage lit by two electric lamps.

Solid wooden doors closed off each cell, each door with a small opening seven inches square, and no lock. Peering in, at first one saw only gray and black shadows, dim shapes. As the eyes grew used to the light—to the dark—we made out a square hole, five feet wide, seven feet long, perhaps eight feet high, no window, only an iron-grated ceiling that let a little light in from the corridor. An iron bedstead stood against one wall, taking up eighty percent of the cell. In the space that was left, two people could just turn or stand. In one corner was an uncovered toilet bucket. The damp air stank.

The young men pressed their faces to the little square holes in the doors. A few were so young and slight that they could put their heads out to look at us. And that hole ringed their necks like the blade of a guillotine, or like a cangue.

Armed guards packed the corridor. Officers watched over every representative who spoke with a prisoner. How, then, could one ask the prisoners whether they were tortured in secret? We could go out—heavy at heart—back into the fresh night air. But these young men had to stay, with these officers and guards. They all shook their heads, or murmured, "No!" Chen Geng was alone in a cell, a growth of beard on his face. A guard's face watched over our shoulders. … His dark eyes flashed light out through the hole.

Chen Geng let slip no chance to expose the enemy and strike at them. Now, to Soong Ching-ling and the others, he spoke of the prison's brutal darkness, of the beatings and ill-treatment he had borne, and laid it bare on the spot: it was only for the sake of this visit that they had been given this bit of special treatment. On this visit Soong Ching-ling deftly passed Chen Geng a slip of paper for the Party organization in secret.

In the cell next to Chen Geng's was held Chen Zaoying—that is, Tan Guofu—who also exposed to Soong Ching-ling and the others the savage cruelty of the British station and the Kuomintang agents:

They opened the door of the next cell, and the girl, Chen Zaoying, came to the doorway to welcome them. She was no more than a teenager, five feet and a bit tall. The color that should have shone in her cheeks had been stripped away. Her cropped hair was uncombed.

"They beat me in Shanghai," she said. "The British station beat me, and the Public Security Bureau beat me too. When they took us there, they shut us in a small room, packed so tight I could not move. It was too crowded—give us more room! The moment I cried out, they dragged me off and whipped me with a bamboo cane. But I am not afraid!" She folded her arms, held them tight, and looking at me, "I am not afraid!" she said again.

Because Chen Geng and the others exposed it on the spot, the China Forum reporter came to understand the truth, and noted in the report that "when they first came, they were not here. Seven or eight of them were crammed in a small, damp hole; only the day before had they been moved here." Chen Geng's pitiless exposure put the prison authorities to shame on the spot and left them badly embarrassed; the warders were furious but could do nothing. As soon as the visitors left, the enemy at once threw Chen Geng into a filthy, dark cell that gave off a grim, moldy stench; stepping in, one saw nothing but blackness. When the light was switched on, there was a heap of human bones on the floor and blades and guns hung on the wall. A Kuomintang agent held a gun to his nose and said over and over: "Confess! Confess!"

Taken under guard from Shanghai to Nanjing with Chen Geng, besides Tan Guofu, were Luo Dengxian and Yu Wenhua. The women political prisoners then held in the women's block of the gendarme headquarters detention house—Qian Ying, Xia Zhixu, He Baozhen, Xiong Tianjing, Huang Li, and others—were nearly all known to Chen Geng. Xia Zhixu had been arrested that March, a few days after him. By prison rule the women could talk to one another during exercise, but the men could not. Chen Geng paid no heed to any of that: at every exercise period he shouted aloud in the yard, saying things on purpose for the comrades to hear.

Chen Geng never bent in prison, and the enemy could do nothing with him. To their lackeys he was hard as iron. Whenever he wanted to come out and stir about, whether or not it was the exercise hour, he would bellow at the jailer: "I want to come out for a walk—open the door, quick!" Chen Geng was a famously difficult prisoner, and the jailer dared not defy him and had to let him out. Each time he came out to move about, he would find some way to see his fellow prisoners and hearten their fighting spirit.

While heartening his fellows to keep up the struggle, Chen Geng also worked on the guards, teaching them and winning them to help us. In the gendarme detention house was a twenty-two-year-old warder-sergeant, Zhang Liangcheng. An orphan, he had been driven by hardship into serving as an orderly in a Kuomintang office and had come at last to the detention house. Zhang Liangcheng had a patriotic heart and a sympathy for revolutionaries, and Chen Geng, Xia Zhixu, and others found chances to draw near and teach him, so that his thinking grew steadily. In secret Zhang Liangcheng passed messages and notes for the political prisoners and told them of certain people's betrayals. A woman political prisoner, Huang Haiming, had with her a baby girl only four months old, and when Zhang Liangcheng made his rounds he would often carry the child from cell to cell to look in. The prisoners used the chance of holding the baby to tuck notes into its clothes and pass word. Later, when Chen Geng left, he entrusted Zhang Liangcheng with a note and five yuan for Huang Li. As ill luck had it a renegade informed on them, and Zhang Liangcheng was arrested for it. When the Nanjing gendarme commander Gu Zhenglun heard of the affair, he was appalled and had Zhang Liangcheng shot. With Zhang Liangcheng there had been a clerk, Xiao Jin, a young man in his twenties, also of an upright mind, who had seen here the heroic deeds of many Communists and, won over and taught by Chen Geng and the rest, had worked with Zhang Liangcheng in secret to do the political prisoners much good. When Xia Zhixu was sentenced and moved from the gendarme detention house to the Jiangsu No. 1 Model Prison at Laohuqiao in Nanjing, he even went, under the name of Xia Zhixu's brother "Xia Cao," to visit her, and told her through his tears how heroically Zhang Liangcheng had met his death. Xiao Jin too was later arrested, and when Xia Zhixu and the others were moved on to the Xiaozhuang "reformatory," Xiao Jin was sent there as well. Only in 1937, when Zhou Enlai came to Nanjing to negotiate and demand that the Kuomintang release political prisoners, was he freed along with the others and went to Yan'an to study at the Anti-Japanese Military and Political University.

Chen Geng's case was known to nearly everyone in the prison, and he himself thought there was no hope left. It was because Soong Ching-ling and the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights kept up the effort to save him, coming to Nanjing to see him several times running, that the Kuomintang government dared not do him sudden harm. After Chen Geng and the others were handed by the concession authorities to the Nanjing government, Soong Ching-ling at once put out her hand in support and told the whole country: "The five men arrested and handed to the Kuomintang yesterday, like all the comrades who have met the same fate, are no criminals but the noblest representatives of the Chinese people. I therefore call upon the whole people of China to rise and demand their release, to demand that they not be subjected to torture and death. If we allow these revolutionary fighters to be arrested, imprisoned, even killed, we allow the loathsome reactionary forces to blight the very root of the Chinese nation's life. To free them, to free the thousands like them, is to free the unconquerable force of the Chinese nation's revolutionary spirit. Without that force, China cannot go on living as a country and a nation."

At this time the Kuomintang's fourth "encirclement" campaign against the Central Soviet was still under way, with Chiang Kai-shek directing it from Nanchang. Chiang resolved to lure Chen Geng into surrender with his own hands, hoping through him to "influence" the Whampoa men in the Red Army, and ordered him brought by steamer from Nanjing through Jiujiang to Nanchang.

Chiang did this not only out of his habitual cunning but also to hoodwink people and win their hearts. He knew that nearly everyone who had joined the Second Eastern Expedition against the warlord Chen Jiongming in Guangdong in 1925 knew Chen Geng had saved his life—and that for that debt his repayment was to ruin Chen Geng's family: his father, Chen Shaochun, twice thrown into prison on charges of "failing to raise his son strictly" and "kin of a red bandit," and his fifth brother arrested in the same net. With the cruel political persecution, the family's property was extorted away to nothing, driven to utter bankruptcy; even the few broken rooms and few acres of land that remained were mortgaged and sold off clean. With no way out, the whole family had to move to a Buddhist retreat on a hilltop near their home village to keep body and soul together. … And now he put on again a hypocritical face of "gratitude."

As the enemy took Chen Geng under guard from Nanjing toward Nanchang, all along the way—on the steamer, in the inns—he loudly proclaimed the revolutionary cause of the Communist Party and the Red Army, who loved the country and saved the people, and the crowds who gathered to hear him were deeply moved. Fearing Chen Geng's stirring speeches, the enemy afterward shut him in the cabin and would not let him out. When the boat reached Jiujiang, they put him aboard a special car on the Nanchang-Jiujiang railway, on which Chiang Kai-shek's confidant Wu Zhongxin and others had come specially to meet him.

In Nanchang the enemy lodged him at the Jiangxi Grand Hotel by Ximachi in the city center—the very site of the headquarters of the Nanchang Uprising in its day—a hotel wholly overrun by Chiang's agents. After two days Chiang sent Deng Wenyi, who came to the hotel to talk with Chen Geng, bringing many things as first-meeting "gifts."

Deng Wenyi was from Liling in Hunan, a graduate of the first Whampoa class. A right-winger even as a cadet, on graduating he "was sent as a specially chosen student of the Kuomintang Central Committee to study at Sun Yat-sen University in Russia." In the memoir he wrote after returning home, Ten Thousand Li Through Russia, he called himself "the first man educated in Russia to turn and oppose the Soviet Union." Back from Moscow, he returned to work at the Whampoa Academy, and in the anti-Communist high tide after the "April 12" coup of 1927 he headed the academy's "Party-purge investigation committee." He then served for years as Chiang Kai-shek's aide-de-camp and won Chiang's deep trust. In 1932, when Chiang, taking Whampoa graduates for his core and modeling himself on the Fascist dictatorship of Hitler's National Socialist Party in Germany, founded in Nanjing the "Chinese Renaissance Society" (the "Renaissance Society" for short, also called the "Blue Shirts"), Deng Wenyi was a director of this secret organization's central council and had served as its secretary. When Chen Geng was brought to Nanchang, Deng Wenyi was "gloriously" serving as Chiang's aide-de-camp and head of the investigation section of the Nanchang field headquarters. Chen Geng and he were "fellow-townsmen" and had been "schoolmates"; Chen Geng knew Deng Wenyi's reactionary record well, knew him for Chiang's faithful running dog, an executioner with both hands steeped in Communists' blood. Neither the raging tide of the Guangzhou Great Revolution nor the revolutionary flood of Moscow had washed the fawning servility from Deng Wenyi or shaken his reactionary stand a hair's breadth. In his heart Chen Geng loathed and despised the man, while Deng Wenyi went on prattling his pleas for surrender.

Deng Wenyi repeated the string of nonsense Gu Shunzhang had spoken—that "the Chinese revolution has failed, China needs unity and strong leadership, democracy is not practical in China"—and lavishly praised Chiang Kai-shek's "benevolent rule," saying "the Gentleman" (meaning Chiang) was setting reforms in motion, that a certain high officer had been disciplined for whoring, a certain high official shot for corruption, many new roads built, and some railways under construction. Chen Geng answered that Chiang built roads and railways not for the people's convenience but to fight the Communists. Deng Wenyi argued hard that Chiang was truly for resisting Japan, "preparing to fight Japan in 1936." He boasted, too, of Chiang's military strength: with only three thousand graduates of the Baoding Military Academy the Northern warlords had ruled China for more than thirty years; the more than ten thousand Whampoa graduates would surely rule China for eighty years. "Mr. Chiang's wish is to win back every Whampoa officer …" Chen Geng rebutted Deng Wenyi's shameless talk with righteous force, demanding why the Kuomintang government worked so hard to oppose the Communists, wage civil war, and crush the people, while meeting Japanese imperialism's seizure of the Northeast and attack on Shanghai with a policy of non-resistance. Deng Wenyi stammered, unable to make his own case hold together, and could only quibble at last: "Not fighting Japan is husbanding our strength," dressing up surrender and compromise toward Japan as "Lenin too signed the Treaty of Brest." His sophistry laid Chiang Kai-shek bare for the country-selling, tyrant-and-traitor face it was.

Deng Wenyi's "talks" went on for two days to no result. As he left that day he urged Chen Geng to think it over well, and told him Chiang would see him the next day. The next morning Deng Wenyi came again to press him to surrender. He brought a copy of an order Chiang had signed, granting a great sum of money to a renegade's family. That renegade had been a Whampoa graduate, once a Communist, who after betraying the revolution and going over to the Kuomintang had been killed by the Red Army during the fourth "encirclement" of the Central Soviet. Deng Wenyi's move was a study in calculation, but Chen Geng showed only utter disgust and contempt. Deng Wenyi then produced a "statement of repentance" prepared in advance and told Chen Geng that he need only sign his name to it, and it could even be kept private. He added that this way Chen Geng could have command of any division he wished in the national army—whichever division he wanted to command, he could command; or else he could be chief of staff to the national army now "encircling" the E-Yu-Wan region—that is, Deng Wenyi said, chief of staff to Wang Jun, commander of the 3rd Army, who was then leading a dozen-odd divisions in "encircling" the Fourth Front Army. To all these threats and inducements Chen Geng gave a stern refusal and a scathing rebuttal.

That afternoon Chen Geng was taken to see Chiang Kai-shek. Chiang was then lodged at the Scientific Instruments Hall on Baihuazhou in Nanchang—the "field headquarters" from which he directed the Kuomintang armies "encircling" the Central Red Region. Chen Geng was famous for his "beard"; after so long in prison his face was thick with it and his clothes were in tatters. Chiang, well knowing Chen Geng's firmness and stubbornness, put on a show all the same, sending Deng Wenyi to bring a silk shirt, a fine long gown, a hat, and shoes, and to have Chen Geng shave, bathe, and change. "You are going to see the Gentleman," Deng Wenyi said. "To go dressed like that would be too discourteous." Chen Geng rebuked him on the spot: "You lock me up, reduce me to this state—and you talk to me of courtesy?" Deng Wenyi was left with nothing to say. However Deng Wenyi "pleaded," Chen Geng paid him no mind.

Chen Geng was brought into a spacious drawing room, guards packed within and without. He had not been seated long when he heard the creak of leather shoes on the stairs. To keep up his "dignity," Chiang deliberately called out loudly from the stairs in his heavy Zhejiang accent: "Where's Chen Geng? Where's Chen Geng?"—meaning to announce his coming, so that Chen Geng would rise to greet him with respect. At the sound Chen Geng reached for a newspaper and took no notice of Chiang at all.

Chiang came down the stairs, feeling foolish, but still struck a pose and said, with the air of a cat crying over a mouse:

"You are Chen Geng, you are Chen Geng, you are the Headmaster's fine student. Though you have made a political mistake, I can forgive you."

Chen Geng laid down the newspaper in his hands, turned his face the other way, and answered coldly: "I have no need at all of your forgiveness."

Chiang covered his awkwardness by making small talk, greeted Chen Geng again, and asked where he had been, whether he liked it here, how he had come to be here.

Chen Geng answered that he had been arrested by Chiang Kai-shek and sent here.

Chiang said slyly: how could he put it so—they were all old Whampoa comrades. Whampoa men should all unite to save the country. The Headmaster of Whampoa would never kill Whampoa men.

Seeing that Chen Geng ignored him, Chiang made more small talk, saying he knew Chen Geng had just come from E-Yu-Wan, and asked how things were there.

Chen Geng said coldly: "So-so."

Chiang paced the room and was a long while bringing out another word: "Don't take it so hard. Only come over to this side, and if you are willing to command troops, you may pick any division you please."

Chen Geng answered with a cold laugh: "I'll not be an official of yours. A Communist is not like you—not like you, wringing the people's blood and sweat for his own enjoyment, still less turning running dog for the imperialists. Today I am in your hands: beat me if you will, kill me if you will, but harbor no illusions about me!"

Chiang went on with his playacting: "The country is in such a bad way now, more than three hundred thousand dead in the bandit-suppression—China cannot be sacrificed like this …"

Chen Geng said: "That the country is in such a bad way is a thing you yourself must answer for. It is you who betrayed the revolution and set off this counter-revolutionary civil war; we Communists bear no responsibility for it at all."

His voice rose, stern in look and tone, and the guards all over the yard could hear the words, which made this tyrant-traitor lose face badly. Chiang went livid with rage and stammered: "This attitude of yours, this attitude! You ought to repent, you ought to repent! …"

Chen Geng sat on the sofa without stirring, and his upright bearing left Chiang badly embarrassed, with no way to climb down. Chen Geng made ready to be thrown out at any moment. After a long silence, as it happened Yu Xuezhong arrived, and Chiang, seizing the way out, said hastily to Deng Wenyi: "You have a good talk with him. This won't do, this won't do."

Chen Geng rose, wearing the awe-inspiring air of a hero not to be violated, and said flatly: "I will never betray our Party!"

As Chiang left he said: "From now on Deng Wenyi will speak with you for me."

Deng Wenyi brought Chen Geng back to the hotel and asked what he meant to do.

Chen Geng gave no answer.

Deng Wenyi said: "If we release you, please go back to the Red Army and tell the Whampoa students: so long as they turn back, the Headmaster will not kill them. How is that?"

Chen Geng shook his head firmly and answered that he would never betray the Communist Party, never forsake Marxism, never join the Kuomintang!

At this the enemy took Chen Geng back to Nanjing, shut him in a cramped cell, and put him through another month's torment, still pressing him to "repent." …

After Chen Geng's arrest the Party organization had done everything it could to save him, and Soong Ching-ling too kept up the effort. Before long Chen Geng was moved to another house, and though four or five guards watched over him he was now allowed out into the street, and had come by a certain limited "freedom." Seeing that things here were looser than before, he began to think of getting in touch with the Party organization and of some way to escape; only now did he shave off the full beard on his face and change into clean clothes. A Communist known as the "Cantonese Pockmark" appeared one day, all at once, in Chen Geng's room. Chen Geng had known him back in his Shanghai days. Following the Party's instructions, the "Cantonese Pockmark" did all he could to help Chen Geng escape, and that very night two comrades helped him out of the house. Sadly the "Cantonese Pockmark" was killed by Chiang Kai-shek in 1936. His death caused Chen Geng boundless grief. Twenty years later, while serving as president of the Military Engineering Institute of the Chinese People's Liberation Army, when he had finished telling this passage of his life to an old professor, he said with deep sorrow: "My life was bought with a comrade's life!"

At the end of May 1933 Chen Geng escaped from Nanjing to Shanghai, found the Party, and was soon sent again to work in the Central Soviet in Jiangxi.

### The Claw Reaches into Wang Genying's Family Home

Chen Geng's wife, Wang Genying, was seized in December 1933 by agents under the renegade Gu Shunzhang, taken from her parents' home.

As related earlier, in September 1931 Wang Genying had come back to Shanghai from Tianjin with Chen Geng, bringing their small son Zhifei. In October, when Chen Geng was ordered to the E-Yu-Wan revolutionary base, Wang Genying stayed on in Shanghai with the boy to carry on the struggle. At that time Japanese imperialism had brazenly sent in its troops and seized the three provinces of the Northeast. The Chinese Communist Party called on the masses to take up arms and resist Japanese imperialism, and the national resistance and salvation movement rose higher by the day. The Party sent Shuai Mengqi to Shanghai to prepare the founding of a Workers' Anti-Imperialist League. In the Hudong district she found Wang Genying, passed on to her the Party Center's line of uniting people of every class for resistance and national salvation, and put her in charge of the work of the Hudong branch of the Anti-Imperialist League. From then on Wang Genying threw herself into hard work, organizing the masses and spreading the call to resist Japan, on the move day and night without rest.

On January 28, 1932, Japanese troops again attacked Shanghai, and the 19th Route Army rose to fight back. The Shanghai Party organization moved into emergency action, throwing all its strength into mobilizing the city's soldiers and people for war. Wang Genying organized a Hudong workers' first-aid team that went straight to the front, rescuing and carrying the wounded under fire. She and her comrades hurried about day and night, working to the point of forgetting food and sleep. Each evening she made her way over to Huxi to report to Shuai Mengqi.

After the Shanghai fighting ended Wang Genying went back to factory work, laboring in turn at a cigarette factory and a rubber-shoe factory and carrying on mass work. At this time the All-China Federation of Trade Unions set up a women workers' department, and Wang Genying was made its head, with Xia Zhixu, just back from the Soviet Union, as its secretary. Wang Genying lived with her child and Xia Zhixu in an attic room in the British Concession; with no time to look after her only son, she was at the factories all day, leading and organizing the workers' struggles. That autumn she and Xia Zhixu went to work at a training class run by the Federation, training trade-union cadres. Wang Genying was loyal to the Party, honest with people, warm in her dealings, and plain in her life, and left everyone a deep impression. Around this time she fell gravely ill, and just as she recovered the organization sent her to work at another training class. That October Chen Geng, wounded in the right leg in the fighting in the E-Yu-Wan Soviet, left the army for Shanghai to have the leg treated, and he and Wang Genying were together again. In late March 1933 Chen Geng's wound had healed, and the organization had decided that Wang Genying would go with him to the Central Soviet. Unexpectedly, on March 24, Chen Geng was suddenly arrested by agents Gu Shunzhang sent, and Wang Genying was forced to move to the countryside to hide. In truth both her own home and her parents' had all along been under the watch of Gu Shunzhang's agents.

In June or July 1933 Wang Genying came back from the countryside to the family home on Tongbei Road in the city, and in December she was arrested there. The renegades who sold her directly were Zhang Xiaomei and her husband Chen Yuexian. Zhang Xiaomei was also a woman worker at the Hengfeng Cotton Mill and had once been an officer of the mill's Communist Party branch. Her husband, Chen Yuexian, turned first and told her to watch Wang Genying's movements at all times. Seeing Wang Genying come back now, she passed word to Gu Shunzhang's agents. The day after she informed on her, as Wang Genying and her family were about to eat breakfast at home, a lean, tall agent in a long gown came, with several thugs standing guard outside. He ordered Wang Genying in a bullying tone to "come along" with him. At the agent's insolence, Wang Genying's younger brother and the rest of the family were furious and wanted to beat him to death. But she, mindful that she must not drag her brother and the others down, signaled them not to make a rash move. In the end Wang Genying was taken away. Only after much asking around did the family learn she was held in Tilanqiao prison, though relatives were not allowed to visit.

Wang Genying was sentenced by the mixed court in Shanghai to eight years, then handed to the Shanghai Public Security Bureau, taken at once under guard to the Nanjing gendarme headquarters, and moved on to the Jiangsu No. 1 Model Prison at Laohuqiao. By then Shuai Mengqi, arrested in Shanghai as early as 1932, and Xia Zhixu and others, arrested in Shanghai in March 1933, had been moved in July 1933 from the gendarme detention house to this prison. In the women's block were held twenty-odd women political prisoners, all Communists but for one or two revolutionary masses or family members from the Party's outer bodies. In cell 3 at the time were Shuai Mengqi and Xia Zhixu, both sentenced to life, and He Baozhen, sentenced to fifteen years, together with Geng Jianhua, sentenced to ten; in the neighboring cell 2 were Qian Ying, Ji Jun, Xiao Min, and others. Wang Genying was put in cell 8 with Xiong Tianjing and the rest. In the wall between cells 2 and 3 was a small hole, fitted with a little lamp, through which word could be passed. The women in cell 3 took matters to Qian Ying. In this way, with these two cells at the center, there formed of itself a leading core around Qian Ying, Shuai Mengqi, and Xia Zhixu, while in the other cells there was at least one backbone member each (Wang Genying being one), through whom, when anything came up, the others' views were gathered; after the comrades in cell 3 and Qian Ying had talked it over, the decision was passed to all. It was so that the several hunger strikes in the prison were set going.

At this time Hilaire Noulens and his wife—the secretary of the Pan-Pacific Trade Union Secretariat's Shanghai office and a Comintern worker in China, Poles—had been arrested by the Kuomintang and brought to the Model Prison. Again and again the two lodged protests with the Kuomintang authorities, and people in society kept up their support and world opinion pressed hard for their release, but the Kuomintang paid no heed, and the Noulenses declared a hunger strike in prison. The backbone members in the cells, learning of it, talked it over and agreed with one voice to support them by a hunger strike of their own. The demands they put to the prison authorities were: first, better food, meat once a week; second, that relatives be allowed to bring books and food into the prison; third, that the Noulenses be released without charge. Then they began the strike. At first the prison paid no heed, and even dragged several of the lifers out for a beating; then the warden, Niu Chuanqi, worked on the rest to provoke and divide them. But they held firm and united, not one of them wavering. By the seventh day the prison authorities had lost their nerve and were forced to grant the demands. The struggle was won, and the Noulenses, near the end from the early days of their strike, were sent by the prison to the hospital for treatment and released once they recovered. Not long after, a second hunger strike was won as well. The ordinary prisoners in the women's block were deeply moved, and greatly admired the political prisoners' strength of will; some warders, too, showed them sympathy, quietly lengthening the after-supper dishwashing time so that they could use it to talk and go about among one another.

Wang Genying took an active part in the hunger strikes, showing herself firm and brave and taking the lead in her own cell. She went without food herself, and explained the reasons for the strike to the ordinary prisoners, winning the sympathy of most. In daily life too she helped her fellow prisoners of her own accord and got on well with the comrades.

Communists have always taken prison for a kind of school, using their time behind bars to study politics and culture. So it was with the women political prisoners at the Model Prison. Wang Genying worked very hard and did well (all of it snatched in the moments of emptying the slop bucket, before and after meals, and at exercise). In prison she even learned the crafts of embroidery and cross-stitch.

In the autumn of 1936 the Kuomintang authorities transferred the women political prisoners out of the Model Prison to the "Capital Reformatory" at Xiaozhuang near Nanjing. When they first reached the "reformatory" they were housed two to a room, but because a renegade informed that the women prisoners were carrying on political activity, they were shifted to "solitary cells," each in a room alone, cut off, forbidden contact with the other prisoners; even at ordinary times—for lessons, meals, washing—each squad moved as a body, watched at every moment, forbidden to talk with one another. …

Through her imprisonment, facing the enemy's cruel tortures and the torment of prison life, Wang Genying showed to the end the heroic character of a Communist, loyal to the Party heart and soul, unbending, waging with her fellow prisoners a brave struggle.

Wang Genying was held more than three years in all, and had still not been freed when the War of Resistance broke out.

In August 1937, Zhou Enlai, as representative of the Party Center, went with Zhu De and Ye Jianying to Nanjing to take part in a national-defense conference called by Chiang Kai-shek. When Zhou Enlai and Zhu De met students returned from study in the Soviet Union, a younger comrade of Wang Genying's from the Ewo Cotton Mill said that Wang Genying was still held in the "Capital Reformatory" at Xiaozhuang. Zhou Enlai said at once: never mind, I will get her out.

Since the peaceful settlement of the Xi'an Incident the political situation within the country had changed sharply, and conditions at the "reformatory" where Wang Genying was held had greatly changed too; the Kuomintang could no longer seal off news from outside. And after the War of Resistance broke out, the warders here had loosened their grip on the political prisoners somewhat, so that Wang Genying and the others heard beforehand that Zhou Enlai was coming to see them. Only, the news came so suddenly that when they first heard it they "could not believe their own ears."

On the morning of August 19, Zhou Enlai, Ye Jianying, and others came to the Xiaozhuang "reformatory." Looking over the full list of political prisoners, he pointed to the names of Wang Genying, Xia Zhixu, and Zhang Qinqiu (the other comrades had changed their names after arrest) and said to the head of the "reformatory": "Today we wish to see them." The head agreed readily and at once sent to fetch them.

Catching sight of Zhou Enlai and Ye Jianying, Wang Genying and the others were struck dumb by the unlooked-for meeting. After a spell of silence it was Ye Jianying who spoke first: "What, don't you know us?" Only then did they wake as if from a dream, and came forward overjoyed, telling Zhou Enlai and Ye Jianying one by one the true names of the other comrades held there.

Zhou Enlai exchanged a few pleasantries with them, greeted them warmly, and then turned to the head of the reformatory standing to one side: "Today we will take them away first." The man agreed at once, but an agent standing by put in: "Zhang Qinqiu is a prisoner of war; to take her out you must have Gu Zhutong's order." At this Xia Zhixu suggested that Xiong Tianjing, who was ill, be let out first in Zhang Qinqiu's place. The head nodded his assent. But the agent raised another point: to take her out, there had to be a guarantor. Zhou Enlai said to the head with humor: "Then General Ye Jianying and I will stand guarantee—will that do?" The head said hurriedly: "It will, it will! If Mr. Zhou will stand guarantee, how could it not?"

So the guarantee was filled out on the spot. Zhou Enlai then asked to meet everyone, and the head at once had all the political prisoners held there gather in the hall, where Zhou Enlai gave a report on the situation, explaining that after negotiations the Kuomintang and the Communist Party had halted the civil war and joined hands again to resist Japan together; he said too that the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army would be reorganized as the Eighth Route Army of the National Revolutionary Army, and told them all: before long you will be able to go to the anti-Japanese front.

Back at his lodging Zhou Enlai sent a car to bring the three of them — Wang Genying and two others — to No. 1 Fuhougang in Nanjing—the Eighth Route Army's Nanjing office. The Red Army was then being reorganized as the Eighth Route Army of the National Revolutionary Army in the Sanyuan area of Shaanxi, and Chen Geng, made commander of the 386th Brigade of the 129th Division, was about to lead his troops to the front of the War of Resistance. So, not long after her release, Zhou Enlai took Wang Genying to Xi'an. On August 26 Wang Genying came by way of Xi'an to the General Political Department of the Eighth Route Army. Chen Geng, having taken a telephone call from the Department, hurried to Yunyang town in Sanyuan county and was reunited with Wang Genying, who had suffered so much in the enemy's prisons. This couple, who had come through so much, were beside themselves at their reunion after long parting. On August 28 Wang Genying went with Chen Geng to the headquarters of the Red Army's 31st Army. She stayed there until the reorganization of the troops was complete and Chen Geng took up his post as commander of the 386th Brigade of the 129th Division of the Eighth Route Army. On September 15 Wang Genying left the 386th Brigade and returned to Yunyang. The next day, even as Chen Geng led his troops out to the war against Japan, she left the General Political Department and set out for the revolutionary holy ground she had long yearned for, Yan'an, seat of the Party Center.

After reaching Yan'an Wang Genying rested briefly and then entered the Party School of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia border region to study. When she finished the next year she asked to go to the anti-Japanese front to fight the Japanese. In the autumn of 1938 the organization sent her to work in the 129th Division in the Shanxi-Hebei-Shandong-Henan border region. Leaving Yan'an, she came to the Taihang Mountains base and was assigned as political instructor at the school for finance-and-economics cadres run by the division's supply department. In wartime conditions this school had no fixed site and moved often with the troops. It had a women's company and a men's company, and the students marched and studied at once, a life of great hardship. Wang Genying lived among the students, thorough and painstaking in her work, warm and caring toward them, sincere with people, and won the praise of the unit's leaders and everyone else. A woman student at the school, recalling it more than forty years after Wang Genying's death, said: "From October 1938 to February 1939 I was with Comrade Wang Genying. I was a student at the finance-and-economics cadre school of the 129th Division's supply department, and Wang Genying was our instructor. She wore an army uniform and lived among us. She was especially sincere with the comrades and lived very hard, coming at night to talk heart to heart with us, learning our thoughts and helping us solve our troubles. I had just joined the Party then, and Wang Genying taught me how to be a Communist, and spoke too of the difference between the true Three Principles of the People and the false. First she had me read, then had me tell what I thought. We all held our instructor Wang Genying in great respect. At the finance school we women were one squad, and there was a men's squad too; Wang Genying was instructor for the whole school. She was just like us, eating in the country people's homes, with no privilege at all. Once she received a bundle of war trophies, which she said her husband had sent her as a keepsake. At the time we did not know who her husband was. The school was of the marching kind (the writer's note: meaning it had no fixed site and moved often with the troops), and we marched and studied at once. The organization gave Wang Genying a horse, which she often did not ride, giving it over to the sick or to comrades who had fallen behind, to carry their packs. When the Japanese converged in a pincer attack, we cleared the ground and stood fast. Some days we had to march a hundred and several tens of li."

In December 1938 the 129th Division's commander Liu Bocheng and political commissar Deng Xiaoping led the main force of the 386th Brigade across the Beijing-Hankou line and into the southern Hebei region. The finance-and-economics cadre school moved east with them. In January 1939, as the fighting grew fiercer by the day, the Japanese threw more than thirty thousand troops in an eleven-pronged, large-scale "mopping-up" of southern Hebei. Our main force, joined by local armed units, at once launched a counter-sweep that struck a heavy blow at the enemy's arrogant spirit. In March, with the counter-sweep in southern Hebei nearing its end, the finance-and-economics cadre school decided to follow the main force west across the Beijing-Hankou line and move on to the Taihang region. One day, near Nangong, the school ran suddenly into Japanese troops, and part of the student body was scattered; heedless of her own safety, Wang Genying took charge of gathering up the sick and wounded and brought them to the 129th Division's supply department.

On March 8 Wang Genying was stationed with the division's supply department at Qianhou Wangjia, southeast of Nangong county. Then the enemy struck suddenly and surrounded the supply department. At this desperate moment Wang Genying led the horse assigned for her own use over to the medical team for the wounded to ride, urged them to go on ahead, and broke out on foot with the guard unit. Rushing out of the village, she caught her breath, and feeling for it found that the satchel holding the documents and public funds had not been brought out. "This is bad—there's public money not brought out!" she said urgently, and turned resolutely back alone toward the village; try as they might, no one could stop her. Wang Genying got the satchel from the quarters, but as she left the village, alas, she met the enemy troops. When the comrades formed a party and came to her aid, they found Comrade Wang Genying already fallen beneath the enemy's bayonets, heroically dead.

At this time Chen Geng was leading the 386th Brigade west across the Beijing-Hankou line, and learning that same day of the grievous news he was stricken with grief. In his diary for March 8 he wrote but a single line: "March 8—a day I can never forget, and the most bitter day of my life."

The Party organization and the comrades all grieved deeply at the martyr Wang Genying's death, and paid the highest respect to her long years of struggle in the White areas, where she risked death time and again against the enemy, and above all to the contribution she made in throwing her whole strength behind Chen Geng in the Central Special Section's fight. In January 1946 the Party organization found Zhifei, the orphan who had been left destitute in Shanghai to suffer every hardship, and sent him by stages to the Shanxi-Hebei-Shandong-Henan Liberated Area. The first thing Chen Geng said to him when they met was: "At the Seventh Party Congress, when the revolutionary martyrs were mourned, your mother's picture was hung up!"
