## Chapter 21

On December 13 I at last had a telephone call from Mrs. Stephen; her voice was as serene as ever. In Shanghai at that time it was very hard to speak over the telephone, for the Japanese had sent men to eavesdrop at the telephone company, and at the least suspicion something would befall. So I asked nothing, and she said nothing, but only inquired, graceful and calm:

"Is it Xu?"

"Yes." I said. "It is a long time since we met."

"It is a long time since we met," she said. "Will you come to my house to dinner tonight?"

"Very well," I said.

At half past six I went to Mrs. Stephen's; she received me in that milk-white room touched with yellow and green. She was somewhat thinner, but still in good spirits, without a trace of unease or alarm. The air of the room was still bright and fresh, the flowers in the vase most gorgeous, the pot of slender bamboo-leaf a lush green too, the leaves still marked with the trace of a fresh watering. She rose, and two russet-brown dogs stood up from before her feet and came to sniff at my clothes and shoes; Mrs. Stephen waved them out, and receiving me, sat me facing her, and said:

"You have come to see me several times."

"Yes." I said. "And how is Stephen?"

"In an internment camp," she said; "quite well, thank you." Her eyes cast down, she seemed not to wish to speak of the matter.

"What have you been busy with these days?"

"Inquiring after Stephen's whereabouts." She sighed faintly.

"Is there nothing in which I can help?"

"I have now found out," she said; "he is in an internment camp over on the Pudong side."

"May one visit him?"

"Once a week, but only immediate family."

"Have you been?"

"I went yesterday." she said gloomily.

"How was it?"

"Only that I took him a few things." She sighed with a proud faint breath.

"Is there hope of his getting out?"

"None," she said; "save at an exchange of prisoners."

She fell silent and sorrowful, and, as if to avoid the grief on her face, rose and turned quietly away from me toward the round table. Though there was in my heart a sympathy past telling, I could find no words to comfort her; I lit a cigarette and gazed in silence at her grave back, my mind neither pondering the problem that lay across her heart nor weighing within what bounds we might help Stephen; I only waved, empty and vague, my sympathy and my anxiety.

The sky darkened; she switched on the lamp, went to the window, gazed out, drew the curtains, and then turned her head and said:

"It is time for dinner, is it not?"

She went downstairs with me, the two russet-brown dogs following us. In the dining-room we sat facing each other, a great vase of large-leaved marguerites set between us, so that we could easily avoid each other's gaze; several times she seemed to have something to say to me, but somehow never said it; and I too seemed to have many things I wished to speak of, yet knew not, in the end, what to say. It was very still; but for the sound of the knives and forks, and now and then a car outside the court, this still air dissolved away our words, and we kept silence, silence.

After dinner neither of us broke that silence, nor did we rise, but only sat in silence before the dregs in the coffee-cups; at last I said:

"It is late; you should rest early."

"No." she said. "I have still something to say to you."

So she led me, following the two russet-brown dogs, upstairs into the milk-white room, and sent the two dogs out of the door. She shut the door and sat down; I browsed aimlessly before the bookshelves, and she said:

"Will you sit down and talk with me?" I came back and sat facing her. Suddenly, with a calm, grave look, she said:

"If it may be," she rose, went to the bookshelf, took out a Bible, and said solemnly: "I hope you will consent to swear upon the Bible."

"You mean to keep a secret?" I stood up and asked.

"Yes." she said. "If our friendship will let you promise me not to speak of tonight's talk, then swear it, please."

"I will." I said, and boldly laid my left hand on the Bible and raised my right, and said: "I swear to keep the secret."

"That of all Mrs. Stephen's talk with me tonight, I will speak to no one," said Mrs. Stephen.

"I swear that I will not speak to others of tonight's talk between Mrs. Stephen and me," I went on with the oath.

Then she put away the Bible, set it back in its place on the shelf, and came over gravely, and said in a clean voice:

"Now I have a thing to ask you."

"Ask what you will," I said, and leaned back with a smile on the pale-yellow sofa.

"Will you answer faithfully?"

"Whatever I am willing to answer, I will answer faithfully."

"Then," she smiled: "suppose I ask you—are you an intelligence agent appointed by the government?"

This was indeed a question beyond my reckoning; I was much startled—that a man so fond of abstract questions of philosophy as I should be the object of so strange a surmise from Mrs. Stephen! I could not help laughing. I said:

"How did your thoughts come to such a place?"

"Do you suppose this is a surmise of mine alone?" still she said gravely. "Now only answer me 'yes' or 'no.'"

"No," I said.

"Truly?"

"Did I not promise you I would not lie?"

"If the government sent you to do a little intelligence work," her eyes fixed on mine, so cold now that I could not believe it was Mrs. Stephen, she said: "would you be willing to undertake it?"

"They would not send me," I said lightly. "An intelligence agent, I should think, must be a quick and capable talent."

"No," she said. "Suppose there were some kind of work for which someone thought you the most fit—would you be willing to undertake it?"

"No one would think me suited to work of that kind," I said. "I am neither quick nor capable."

"But you have a cool head and a nimble mind."

"You flatter me," I said. "But even so, that is only a thinker's talent."

"When the whole nation is mobilized to save the race, talents of that kind are conscripted to serve as agents," she said. "I ask you only this: if someone appointed you, would you accept?"

"If it be truly for love and for the light, I accept."

She turned her eyes from mine, rose lightly, came quietly over, and sat down near me, and said gently:

"Now, when this Pacific war has begun, we stand, surely and certainly, on one battle-line."

"Naturally," I said; "and for that reason too, over and above friendship, I feel a particular concern for Stephen."

"Now," she said, dropping her voice, "our intelligence work is already under way, and we much hope you will consent to work along with us."

This truly startled me; that a lady so stately and splendid as Mrs. Stephen should be an intelligence agent! A strange feeling rose suddenly in my heart, and I asked in wonder:

"You are…?"

"We are all workers of the United States Navy in the Far East." She lowered her head coolly.

"…"

"Do you need money?"

"I know that you people have money." I smiled mockingly.

She walked off as though she had not heard, went to the window, and said with a cold laugh: "Because it is too dangerous?"

"…"

She came quietly over again, and said, cold and yet kindly:

"I give you five minutes to consider." So saying, she went quietly out. Alone in the room, I felt rise in my heart at that moment a bewilderment past telling—that a person like Mrs. Stephen should be an intelligence agent! Then why should it not be possible for me? Ever since the incident of July 7, I had been forever entranced by the philosophical problems I studied, yet my harvest fell far short of my first ideal; time and again, through the shifting of the times and the disquiet of my mind, I had been unable to give myself to the work. Several times over I had thought of going to the rear to find some practical, solid, timely work, but had shelved it always because my book was unfinished. Now that my mind was after all unfit for the study of philosophy, and here was such an opportunity, and, by Mrs. Stephen's manner, I seemed to be of some convenience to the carrying-on of their work—then why should I not consent to her?

The door sounded; Mrs. Stephen came in, and with questioning eyes looked at me, standing silent before me, awaiting my answer. I said:

"Very well; I will undertake such work as lies within my power."

She smiled, and put out her hand and clasped mine warmly. At last she sat down. She seemed about to say something, but I asked first:

"May I know the particulars of your situation?"

"Even I do not know that," she said. "We know only our own work."

The servant brought in black tea, the big russet-brown dog following at his heels. So Mrs. Stephen poured the tea for me, bade the servant take the dog out, and began:

"Strange—we all took you for a Chinese agent."

"Are my ways so furtive?"

"Many, many pieces of proof," she said. "What I saw was that your life and your manner did not agree."

"How do you mean?"

"On the one hand you have a very strong feeling for your nation; on the other you seem quite indifferent to the war. On the one hand you much loathe the flourishing city; on the other you are steeped in the city's splendour."

"And are these the marks of an agent?"

"They mean that your contrary conduct is all a pretence, and that your whole life is but the instrument of your work."

"That may perhaps be Stephen, not I," I said. "I am no more than a bundle of anguish and contradiction."

She smiled and said nothing; I asked:

"Do you think I can be equal to my duties?"

"Naturally," she said. "I think your duties will not lie beyond your powers."

"Then what is my work?"

"That I do not know either," she said. "Tomorrow afternoon at five o'clock, go to Dr. Philip's clinic."

"Very well," I said.

"When you go in, you may say you have a nervous complaint."

"Very well," I said. "Then I shall go now."

I took my leave and came out, my heart seemingly all excitement, feeling that in this grey, humdrum life there might now come a novel turn, that from vexed and dreary problems I might turn to plain, clear work. What a happy thing it was. And what for years I had wished to do—to undertake a little work directly bound up with the nation's war of resistance—was now of a sudden brought to pass. What a life this was. Home again, I could not sleep in peace; I meant to put in order the manuscript I was at work on, but in the ordering of it I found many books and materials I was consulting, and I saw that once they were laid aside, to take them up again would cost a whole fresh labour, and I should need another month before I could bring it to a resting-point. But this was not to be. There being no help for it, I could only lay it aside, neither setting it in order nor troubling myself over it. My mind was overspread with a tangled anxiety. I opened the window and stood at it, breathing the cold air outside. On the horizon countless petals of cloud were driving; the pale moon showed and hid by turns, until at last it was sealed close by the cloud-banks, and the clouds below gathered again, and, as though weaving cloth, wove very swiftly another layer, and so layer upon layer they wove; the sky came slowly down; there was a wind, and then the raindrops came whistling down, with a rustle of sleet between, striking now and then upon my face with a bitter, biting feel. I knew not what I was thinking of, but this feeling was good to me; it was as though it had cast off all the stale heat of the past, and I drank in a fresh moisture.

At two o'clock I felt tired and began to make ready for bed. Calling to mind the mission Mrs. Stephen had spoken of that evening, I grew excited; I had contradictory thoughts and a strange feeling; whether I could be equal to my new mission I had, myself, no assurance at all. But I had confidence in my power to learn, and I seemed of a sudden to have grown strong, to have grown quick, and capable too; I saw in the darkness a light, one small point, glinting everywhere, glinting, creeping, creeping, gathering into a mass, piecing into a sheet, melting into one body, letting through a radiance, brightening, brightening, lighting up the exquisite immensity of the world—round crystals, square, hexagonal, rhomboid, of every shape and kind, casting back a five-coloured light; my flesh seemed to grow translucent; something throbbed at my heart—it was light; it throbbed higher and higher, higher and higher, up out of my breast.

I seemed to have lost myself; I was giving off light, giving off light among a host of light-giving things, like a swarm of fireflies each giving off its own light in the open field.

All that radiance was laughter.
