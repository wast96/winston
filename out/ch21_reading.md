## Chapter 1. Back in Shanghai, Our Might Restored

### A Great Counter-Ploy: the Dead Past Testimony, and Forever an Unsolved Riddle

This was the third year of the War of Resistance against Japan.

As the tide of the war turned against us, the Japanese warlords and Wang Jingwei set up their sham apparatus at Nanjing, breaking the ranks of the resistance. Our tasks came thick and unbroken, one upon another, growing heavier and more numerous by the day, and carrying us into a realm intricate and delicate, beset on every side with peril; and the many mishaps that arose in that time came, likewise, without end.

In the early days of August in the twenty-eighth year of the Republic, the writer was ordered out on assignment. From Chongqing I took a China National Aviation flight to Hong Kong, stayed but a single night, and the next day changed to a French mail steamer for Shanghai—the Shanghai that had, by then, fallen to the enemy in the War of Resistance.

The ship reached the mouth of the Wusong, where, as usual, it had to stop and wait for the pilot to come aboard and guide it into the Huangpu. Ordinarily half an hour would have served; this time, for some reason I could not tell, we waited an age and still the ship did not move—vexing beyond words. After some four or five hours of anxious waiting the anchor came up at last, and just as the wharf came into view but not yet within reach, the ship stopped again. And what was this for?

The Huangpu ought to count as fallen territory, being already under Japanese occupation. Along the west bank ran a line of wharves, part of them belonging to the "leased ground"; on the east bank, all dust and clamor, lay Pudong, thick with factories. Japanese military launches shuttled to and fro across the water; and though one knew well enough that they were not yet in a position to board a ship openly and seize its passengers—nor had any such thing happened before—still the sight of them could not but fill one with the bitterest loathing.

The engines throbbed, the hull swayed, and we were about to make fast. From the great glass window of the dining saloon I searched the shore carefully, and sure enough there was that warmhearted friend who had once escorted me by the same ship from Tianjin to Shanghai—Third Master Hu, come again. His tall frame stood out in the crowd like a crane among fowls, and the moment I set eyes on him I felt, without knowing how, a measure of safety added to me. But how came he to be there? Steadying my gaze, I saw that the one standing beside Third Master Hu was—plainly, was that not my own wife? What could this mean, and who had told them? Ah, I understood: it must be that the "Hong Kong District," by one channel and another, had notified the "Shanghai District," and the "Shanghai District," having no one who knew me by sight, had passed word to the Chief Inspector stationed in Shanghai, Brother Mao Wanli; but Wanli, hindered by how tight the air had grown, could ill afford to show his face before a crowd, and so could do no better than send word to my wife; and my wife, altogether at a loss to account for my sudden coming and not knowing for the moment what to do, in her extremity hit upon the idea of dragging Third Master Hu along—which not only lent her courage but meant that, should anything go wrong, he could help her, in matters great or small, to make up her mind. If I judged rightly, Third Master Hu, ever so generous toward me, had of course felt himself bound in honor to come.

The instant I set foot on land I met with two utterly different receptions. Third Master Hu—Brother Yongquan—gripped my hand and shook it without stopping, his whole face wreathed in smiles of welcome; my wife, her face grave, hurried over and told me in a low voice, "Kangzi and Yinzi come round often—you cannot go home." I took her meaning at once, and though I had things I wanted to ask, I thought it best to wait a while.

Brother Yongquan, perhaps seeing how it was, pulled me along outside, and together we got into a car made ready beforehand, driving first to the Hu residence at the Carlton Apartments to find a footing, and to reckon what to do next once I had gained some understanding of the state of things.

Here I must break in with a word of explanation to make the matter plain. The "Kangzi and Yinzi" my wife had mentioned just now on the wharf were the two daughters of Brother Wang Tianmu. Wang Kangzi, also called Chanhong, was the elder; Wang Yinzi, also called Chanlü, was the second—and from the names alone one may see that these two young ladies were no ordinary pair. The sisters were but a year apart, and were then either eighteen or nineteen, or else seventeen or eighteen. In the twenty-third year of the Republic, when Brother Tianmu was sentenced and jailed, and in the twenty-fourth, when the writer suffered for a breach of discipline, the families were made to bear it too—by what article of law I do not know—and my wife and the Wang sisters were shut up together. Just because they had shared a season of hardship, they became the closest of friends, so close that they set aside all difference of generation, which is really rather comical: ordinarily I called Brother Tianmu "elder brother," yet his two daughters called my wife "little elder sister." Is that not a droll thing? Their meeting again now in Shanghai, going back and forth and talking over old times, was only what feeling and reason would have. It is a pity only that our "line of work" is unlike others, and there is no telling how many bonds and friendships it has thinned and cheapened.

At the end of the twenty-fifth year, after the Xi'an Incident, Brother Tianmu was released and Kangzi and Yinzi regained their freedom; they lodged for a time at Nanjing. Whether by chance or by someone's deliberate matchmaking I cannot say, but the two sisters, Kangzi and Yinzi, had both in turn been friends with Dai Li's son Cangyi, and there was even talk that the Dai and Wang families were soon to be joined in marriage. That it never came to pass owed, I fear, not to any change of heart in the younger generation, but for the most part to the influence of the political climate.

As for the attachment between Brother Wang Luqiao and the second young lady, Yinzi, that developed only later. In the ordinary way of things, love between man and woman is one of the fair things of this world; who would have thought it should be defiled by the ugly face of hard reality—a thing to sigh over in the telling. And as for how Brother Wang Luqiao was lured and taken, I have already set it out clearly in the Second Part of this book, "The Whole Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi," and shall not dwell on it further here.

Though I believed the two Wang sisters would never of themselves do a thing to wrong a friend, still, warned by the overturned cart ahead, I could not but be on my guard, and dared not take it lightly; and now that they knew where my wife was staying, it was best I not go back. So it is that in our trade one is often driven, in matters of human feeling, to hard necessities there is no helping. I therefore asked my wife, "When was the last time they came to our home?" She thought a while and said, "From the time Luqiao met his trouble until today, they have not come again—not so much as a telephone call." I reckoned up the days: Luqiao had been taken on the fourteenth of July, more than twenty days before. Was it not plain enough? Had their affair no bearing whatever on Luqiao's, how could this be? There was no room for hesitation; look at it how one might, I had to move house at once.

Third Master Hu, standing by, had heard all my wife and I said, and without waiting for me to open my mouth, most handsomely pressed me to stay a few days at his house until I could find a place and move there, and so spare myself the troubles of a hotel. This way was both easy of mind and safe, and of course I could ask for nothing better. He at once bade my wife make contact with Brother Mao Wanli, and arrange a time and place for us to meet and talk.

Brother Mao Wanli and I had not seen each other for over a year, since we parted at Beiping in the spring of the twenty-seventh, after our attempt to sanction Wang Kemin came to nothing. He was the same as ever, sanguine in all things, as though only good times lay ahead; and now, with Shanghai growing tenser by the day and mishaps liable to happen anywhere and at any moment, he did not care a straw, but held firmly to the faith that ill would turn to good—a very lucky star, forever keeping company with fortune. My own head was full of alarms, seeing an enemy in every bush and tree; but caught something of his temper, my mind grew a good deal brighter for it.

My meeting with Brother Wanli was of a purely private character. When I set out from Chongqing my superiors had given me no instruction that I must contact him, nor had Wanli on his side received any word. Still, Wanli and I had a deep private friendship, and even where the general working rules forbade us to have any lateral dealings, we would have contrived the chance ourselves. Now we not only met at his office, but I told him, without the least reserve, the whole of my errand—though in truth, had I not said it, he could have guessed it. And just because of that, we talked on freely, without a scruple.

As to the internal troubles that had lately befallen the "Shanghai District" one after another, and the many disadvantages they had brought on, Brother Wanli had happened upon the very change of things; and though not the first to bear the brunt, he too had had his share of alarms. Having smarted through it, he understood it, of course, most thoroughly, and his very first words were to say to me, flat and clean as a knife: "Your task can no longer be carried out!" And straightway he set out an analysis:

"Mr. Dai hopes you will use fair words to persuade Brother Tianmu to come back. I can tell you for certain that, since he has gone this far, he has surely prepared for it in full, and not out of any passing gust of feeling; and there are many signs, moreover, that he is not acting on mere pique. In such a case, can three or two sentences turn his heart around?

"Does Mr. Dai not grasp so plain a truth as this? So you should understand that in sending you here, Mr. Dai does not care whether you can talk him over, but is laying down a chess piece first, to master him with. This much only can I say; a step further, and you must feel your own way to it.

"To speak of the plainest problem: with mutual trust already lost, every matter will of course be turned to the worst; wrong this way, wrong that way, there is no doing it well however you try. For instance—if he names a place and asks you to meet him, dare you go? And turn it round: if you ask him to meet, dare he come?

"Your task and mine in Shanghai are of a special kind, and for the present our one course, besides keeping in touch with the 'Shanghai District' and awaiting instructions from above, is that whether we advance or draw back is not for you or me to choose. What matters is to steady our footing first, hold off and watch a while; and I believe that in these next few days Mr. Dai is sure to send a telegram."

Having only just come to Shanghai, unfamiliar with my surroundings, and worse, none too clear on the present state of affairs—still less with any grip on it—I had, save for the hints I gained under Brother Wanli's guidance, no view of my own at all. Since Brother Wan held that one should plant one's feet firm and fight steadily, then I would simply wait quietly for further instructions from above.

The foregoing was a private talk between Brother Wanli and me. It seems commonplace enough now, but in those days it was reckoned very bold.

Above all so were those two sentences of Wanli's that he bade me feel my own way to: that it lay not in "talking" Tianmu over, but in "mastering" him.

We had been long parted, over a year, and had feelings past telling to unfold; yet talk as we would, it came back always to the internal troubles on the Shanghai side. Though this was but the defection of two or three men, upon us it was a record-breaking calamity—one no one had lived through, no one had ever handled. What lay before our eyes was a tangled, involved situation, one to bewilder a man; and Wanli and I discussed it, in truth, too much and too deeply. We even guessed at this—might it not be a planned and far-sighted "grand detour," with Brother Tianmu cast as the greatest unfortunate in it?

Those who reasoned so at the time were Wanli and I, we two alone. With one whose friendship was not deep enough, we dared not speak of it; among the general run of working comrades it was not fit to be spoken of; and among the senior cadres of our own Bureau everyone alike shunned any mention of the matter. Were it not that more than forty years have passed, and the thing long over and gone, I fear I could not even write it down, let alone publish it openly. That Brother Wanli and I should have such thoughts was surely no wild fancy, for there were many appearances that showed the thing to be by no means so simple as it looked on the surface; and in certain stretches of time thereafter came several matters more baffling still. But those who made them are long since withered away, and the chance of a three-way confrontation exists no more, so it can only be left as a riddle—a thing one may say, and no more.

Our talk stopped there. Wanli invited me, to my face, to stay a while with him. My heart was willing, but it was against the rules; and in fact for us all to gather in one place was no perfect plan either, and so we fixed on meeting again next time. On the way back to the Hu house I mused in silence: my errand here had been, precisely, to handle the "Shanghai internal personnel problem," and now I could not lay hold of a single thread of it—so how was I to set about it at all? How vexing!

I imposed two days on my private friend Brother Hu Yongquan, and he had already borrowed a floor of a house for me; though he called it a sublet in so many words, that was but a courtesy to set my mind at ease. The family was named Hua, Ningbo people, honest folk who did honest business—that is to say, not like us, who so often make business a pretext when in truth it is nothing of the kind. What they lent me to live in was one large room on the third floor and a small one on the mezzanine half-floor (at the turn of the stairs)—the sort of place Shanghainese all call a "tingzijian." The third floor had a toilet and bath; the kitchen was on the ground floor, shared by all. Both households were small in number, and with a little mutual courtesy we felt no inconvenience. For my part these terms were more than satisfactory; the most important thing was that a very respectable family should, without knowing it, serve me as "cover."

Third Master Hu and Mr. Hua were fellow townsmen, and once the thing was agreed, it of course held good, and thus I had a place to rest my head. The address was on Route Doumer in the French Concession, in a residential quarter—the surroundings quiet, the passersby few—very well suited to my comings and goings. The third floor where I lived had a small oblong balcony, from which one could at leisure lean on the rail and gaze into the distance, and at the same time look out to left and right—all of which were advantages. Across the way stood a great household, from which one seldom saw anyone come or go; the one or two who now and then stirred did so quietly, and looked to be servants—the master, most likely, was away? Later my landlord, Mr. Hua, told me, and only then did I learn that this was the famous "Du Mansion" of Shanghai—the residence of Du Yuesheng.

Good as the lodging was, I could not simply go into hiding there and let it rest at that. Just as I was at my wits' end, there came all of a sudden a telegram from Mr. Dai, its purport being to appoint me chief of the "Shanghai District." What the wording was I no longer clearly recall—it was but a copied-out slip of paper; yet make no light of that little slip, folded and creased: it had the same force as a formal document stamped with seal and warrant. When I received the appointment I felt neither fear and trembling nor mingled alarm and joy—not that I am a blockhead, but that our word of command is as a mountain: bidden to do a thing, one must do it, with no room for second thoughts. Only, all at once I recalled how, a few days before, Wanli had judged rightly: sure enough, Mr. Dai had sent a telegram.

Thinking of it now, in appointing me chief of the "Shanghai District," my superiors had not chosen a very fitting man. For one, I was a stranger to both the place and its people, could not tell east from west, south from north when I went out, and could not find my own door when I came home; for another, I did not know the tongue—could neither speak it nor well understand it; and as for personal and social connections, the less said the better. There was yet one thing more: with the comrades of the "Shanghai District" I had no working roots at all, and though it did not come to being wholly out of joint with them, still there could not but be some strangeness. All that I have set down are places where I fell short of the mark; the reason they bade me fill the gap was, most likely, that I happened to be at hand? And yet—if my being a stranger to Shanghai was a defect, then Shanghai's being a stranger to me was no less an advantage.

Here I would take the chance to lay stress on one fact: that in the more than two years I served as District Chief in Shanghai, lacking the conditions for the work, I was yet able to exercise my authority smoothly and to carry out in earnest the tasks my superiors laid on me—which should be called a full working-out of "the function of organization." And this was precisely a stern test of "the strength of organization." Pardon me if for a moment I cannot hold back a word that may give offense: in the ordinary way we forever have the two words "organization" on our lips, yet those who have truly, in reality, felt how great a strength "organization" can bring forth, and how firm its uses are, are, I fear, not so very many. Take the writer himself: what I can write out now makes it seem I grasp well the marvel of "organization"; but had I, in those days, said anything of the sort—how I wielded the function of organization to bring forth its strength—that would have been sheer humbug.

Let us leave theory aside and speak only of fact. The inner and field units under the "Shanghai District," great and small, numbered five or six score, and the working comrades came to about a thousand. I, being District Chief, had had direct contact with only a very few field-unit heads, such as the intelligence-group leaders and the action-group leaders; the great majority of group and squad leaders I had never so much as met. Yet a single order could be carried through to the end, unabated by a hair, and never once did there occur any such thing as feigned obedience or a shifting-off on some pretext. This, I hold, is the concrete showing of "having organization." Of course, "organization" must be held together by a spiritual force suited to the background of the age; and in the War of Resistance, that was the national consciousness of resisting outside insult and the shared hatred of the foe—as well, one must own, as the prestige the Juntong districts had by then built up.

This is no empty high talk. In the doings I am about to set down, all are true instances, with proof and warrant. Yet once victory came in the War of Resistance, "the function of organization" grew dull; "the strength of organization" grew weak; and a small part of the elements making up the organization slipped their tracks and drifted, turning to the pursuit of goods and consumption.

### Amid Crisis on Every Side, Standing Firm to Weather the Peril

The "District" spoken of here is not the same as the "district" of an administrative division; it is only one form of establishment among the field-work units of the "Juntong Bureau," ranked as of the first grade, directly under the Bureau headquarters, and reckoned the topmost tier of the organization. Below the "District" there are further the "Station," the "Group," the "Squad," "directly-subordinate members," and the like. Districts, too, differ in size, according to the needs of the work; comparing the "Shanghai District" with the "Nanjing District," although their standing is wholly equal, yet in personnel, establishment, and funds they are far apart—so much so that a single "Group" under the "Shanghai District" outnumbered the whole "Nanjing District."

The "Shanghai District" had been set up long before, back in the twenty-first year of the Republic, in the days of the "Special Services Department," the forerunner of the "Juntong Bureau." So far as the writer knows, the first District Chief was Mr. Weng Guanghui, whom I did not know well; whether I ever chanced to meet him once, my memory is now too dim to say. He left office not long after taking it, gossip having it that he did not get on with Mr. Dai. Afterward he was said to hold a civil post in some field administration, and did not withdraw to Taiwan. The one who succeeded Mr. Weng Guanghui as chief of the "Shanghai District" was an old elder brother of the first class of the Military Academy, Mr. Wu Naixian. Naixian, styled Jinfu, was a Guangdong man; we once trained in the same class at Nanjing, and I made mention of him in my humble work "Rooting Out Traitors in the North." His doings are many, the most storied being the "Deng Yanda affair" and the "Two Guangs affair." Later he was elected a delegate to the National Assembly, and passed away some years ago, at the ripe age of over eighty. The third chief of the "Shanghai District," Mr. Zhou Weilong, styled Daosan, of the fourth class of the Military Academy, a Hunan man, had an old tie with Mr. Dai Yunong—that is, before ever Mr. Zhou Weilong entered the special-service work, he and Mr. Dai were already friends. Mr. Zhou and I trained together at the Central Training Corps at Futuguan in Chongqing in June of the twenty-eighth, and formed a private friendship, though we never worked together. In the winter of the twenty-seventh, Mr. Zhou was taken by the French police-house in Shanghai; rescued and got free, he was transferred back to Chongqing, and the post of District Chief was for a time filled in an acting capacity by the head of the "Shanghai District" Action Corps, Mr. Zhao Lijun. Mr. Zhao Lijun, of the sixth class of the Military Academy, a Sichuan man, held the acting chiefship a very short while, for Mr. Dai then reappointed Mr. Wang Tianmu as chief, while Mr. Zhao, in the capacity of "Deputy District Chief," went on holding the Action Corps command as well. This was the first time a field unit of the "Juntong Bureau" had a deputy head—one might as well say it was a post created to fit the man. Mr. Wang Tianmu, a graduate of the Baoding Military Academy and of the Japanese officers' school, a Jilin man, had early on carried on activities together with Mr. Dai Yunong, and the two were deep in friendship. In the case of the sanction of Zhang Jingyao at the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lit in Beiping in the twenty-second year, he had shown his hand to great effect, yet he always counted it a regret that he had never been able to give full play to his ambition.

The work under each successive leadership of the "Shanghai District" had its achievements, more or less, and among them were several historic events that shook the times. It is a pity my own knowledge is very limited, and I dare not speak at random. Here, then, let me sum up the annals of the "Shanghai District" in the eight words "extraordinary in performance, dogged by mishap and disaster," and leave it at that.

After Mr. Wang took charge of the "Shanghai District," perhaps too much in a hurry, he did not, as the practice was, seek instruction from above, but of his own hand made sweeping changes in personnel, moving the District secretary, Brother Zheng Xiuyuan, out to the field, and letting the assistant secretary, Chen Dirong (Mingchu), take over the secretary's business. At the same time, in a number of measures, he failed to secure the cooperation of the Deputy District Chief, Mr. Zhao Lijun, and of most of the field-unit heads, so that internal disputes broke out one after another, and he even met with the boycott of some working comrades. When Mr. Dai received the reports, he wired Mr. Wang Tianmu to Hong Kong; one hears only that they had a great quarrel, the upshot of which was that Wang Tianmu was relieved of his chiefship of the "Shanghai District" and sent back once more to Tianjin as a "directly-subordinate member," in direct contact with Mr. Dai, all his telegraphy to be relayed through the "Tianjin District." And as for how the thing really stood within—no one knew!

After Mr. Wang Tianmu went to Tianjin, he met with rough going at every turn, and nothing fell out to his liking; it was in that same stretch of time—when the writer was acting head of the Third Section at the Bureau headquarters in Chongqing—that in one of Wang's incoming telegrams there actually appeared the words of a great cursing, "you sons of bitches." From then on Wang's movements were fitful; he came and went among Tianjin, Shanghai, and Hong Kong, and what exactly he was up to, and whether he bore some secret mission laid on him from above, there was no way to know. By the middle of July in the twenty-eighth, there fell out in the French Concession of Shanghai that affair of Wang Luqiao's being lured and taken, the course of which I have already told in the last chapter of "The Whole Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi," and shall not repeat here. In sum, it was no very ordinary matter. On that very day, fourteen offices belonging to the "Shanghai District" were searched, and alarms came thick; intelligence confirmed that the scouts of the enemy and the puppets were out on every side, bent on taking the acting chief Zhao Lijun and would not rest until they had him. For a while it was all crane-cries and wind-rustlings, so that the work of the "Shanghai District" was all but brought to a standstill. From this, too, one may understand why my superiors did not appoint Zhao Lijun to take charge of the "Shanghai District."

At that moment the "Shanghai District" was as a host of dragons without a head, and could only lean on the District secretary, Brother Zheng Xiuyuan, alone, to see to inner and outer both and bear up the whole by himself. I may put it thus: had he once come to grief, the whole "Shanghai District" would surely have fallen past all mending. Below I quote three passages from Brother Zheng Xiuyuan's own account, "A True Record of Three Perils on the Shanghai Shore," to show the state and the plight of the "Shanghai District" at the time.

"The Shanghai office of the Loyal and Patriotic Army was set up at No. 277 Route de Sieyès in the French Concession. One day in the winter of the twentieth year of the Republic, a certain comrade Shen Zelin, who had charge of personnel at the office, was, through his implication in the arrest of another field operative, taken into custody by the French police-house. I got the news at about forty minutes past two that afternoon, and at once telephoned the residence of Zhou, the concurrent director; it being his siesta hour, his wife answered. I asked her then to wake Mr. Zhou, tell him the situation, and have him deal with it at speed. Because it was not Zhou himself who took the call, I, to be safe, hung up and hastened straightaway to the office, telling comrade Mao Zhongxin, who had charge of confidential matters there. At the same time I drafted a written report of what I knew of the affair and left it for Mr. Zhou; then, the hour of my appointment with another field comrade being come, I hurried off again. Who would have thought that no more than five or six minutes after I left, Mr. Zhou too arrived at the office. He sat down to peruse the written report I had left, and in less than ten minutes a great body of French police detectives swarmed in, turned up firearms, cipher codes, and some documents, and took the whole company there into custody (among them an old Mrs. Xu, who lived in the office as cover). Afterward it cost great trouble; first we managed to have the arrested comrades held within the French police-house, and not handed over to the International Settlement police-house, lest they fall by degrees into the hands of the Japanese invaders. In the end it took Mr. Dai's exerting of his utmost power to have District Chief Zhou got safely off by way of deportation. Had I lingered in the office a dozen minutes more, or had I been less quick—had I written the three-sheet foreign-paper report but a little slower—I too should assuredly have been taken. And were it so, the gravity of the consequence would not bear thinking of. For in the Shanghai Special District, the only one besides the District Chief who understood the whole secret of the inner and field organization and personnel, and who kept constant contact with the responsible comrades of the fifteen field units, was myself alone. Had I once lost my liberty, the whole organization of the Shanghai Special District must have fallen into the plight of dragons without a head, and all its inner and outer work been forced to a total stop. Happily, at that hair's-breadth moment, I left the office in time, and was able to preserve the safety of the District organization; and moreover to keep the work going as usual until Mr. Dai should send a successor District Chief to take charge of District affairs."

The above is Brother Xiuyuan's own text. Besides it, there are things Brother Xiuyuan told me by word of mouth that were not set down in the foregoing, together with some the writer would add by way of explanation:

One. The chief of the "Shanghai District," Zhou Weilong, was concurrently director of the "Shanghai office of the Loyal and Patriotic Army."

Two. When Brother Xiuyuan telephoned the Zhou residence, Mr. Zhou was at his siesta, and his wife, on taking the call, dared not wake him. By Brother Xiuyuan's own account, it was because Mr. Zhou was hot-tempered and his wife feared a scolding.

Three. Where the text says, "first we managed to have the arrested comrades held within the French police-house, and not handed over to the International Settlement police-house, lest they fall by degrees into the hands of the Japanese invaders"—beyond this, the writer surmises that the arrested comrades very likely had, besides, a record kept in the International Settlement police-house too, the so-called "case file."

Four. As to Mr. Dai's exerting his utmost power to get District Chief Zhou Weilong, in his concurrent post, out of the country, the writer asked Brother Xiuyuan whose power it was, in the end, and was told it was through a personage of ministerial rank, who made an informal representation to the French authorities—a minister surnamed Song. Writing this, the writer is put in mind of a related tale. From the reorganization of the Juntong Bureau out of the Special Services Department down to today, a rescue of arrested comrades by such means as this was managed but this once. Why was Mr. Dai willing to do it? As said above, Zhou Weilong had an old tie with Mr. Dai, deep in kindness and warmth of feeling, which must have been the greatest cause prompting the deed. For in the year when the warlord Tang Shengzhi raised troops in revolt in Henan, Zhou Weilong was a gendarme battalion commander under Tang's forces, while Mr. Dai, in the capacity of a staff officer of the Military Affairs Commission, was engaged in intelligence work, and was, all unlooked-for, seized by Zhou's subordinates. Zhou, so far from turning him in to claim the credit, actually threw up his post without a backward glance and came away in Mr. Dai's train—which was truly no small thing: unless a man be of a large and lofty spirit, clear-sighted in telling right course from wrong, it is by no means what the common run can do.

Below I excerpt another passage from Mr. Zheng Xiuyuan's "A True Record of Three Perils on the Shanghai Shore."

"The Fourteenth of July Affair: a Fright, but No Harm"—

"On the fourteenth of July in the twenty-eighth year of the Republic, being ill at ease over suspicious persons found two days before loitering and peering about near No. 2 Aitang New Village, the District headquarters, and it being besides the time when the acting chief Brother Zhao Lijun was on sick leave in hospital, I had again become the actual head of the Shanghai Special District. My duty lying so, I dared not use my illness as a pretext for ease. When I reached No. 71 Maiyang Road (I was regularly stationed at No. 2 Aitang New Village, the District headquarters, but on its alarm had shifted for the time to Maiyang Road to work), and was just going through the documents and telegrams received that day, there came all at once a telephone call from Brother Mao Wanli, the Chief Inspector in Shanghai. In veiled words he told me that Brother Wang Luqiao had been taken by someone, and bade me contrive at speed to rescue him. I at once telephoned the leader of the First Intelligence Group, Zhu Xiaogu, and asked him to consult Brothers Liu Junqing and Liu Shaokui without delay, to find out by whose hand Luqiao had been seized, and by every possible means to rescue him—by all means not to let the enemy have him by extradition. Since Brother Luqiao had been sent to Shanghai by Mr. Dai's order to bear a temporary and important mission, taking his instructions directly from the Chief Inspector Brother Mao Wanli alone, and was not entered in the Shanghai Special District organization, it was then needful only to work at the rescue of Brother Luqiao as an individual, without fear for any effect on the Shanghai District organization. But when I had finished going through the District documents and telegrams, at two in the afternoon I got a telephone call from group-leader Zhu, reporting that he had passed word of the rescue of Brother Luqiao to the two comrades Liu. Besides, Brother Liu Junqing let me know that the Settlement authorities would that afternoon make searches at several places within the two concessions, and bade me take heed. As I did not then know the addresses and door-numbers to be searched, I could only, by telephone, instruct the more important inner-work units to be on their guard. When at fifty minutes past two I hurried to the home of Brother Liu Junqing at No. 24 Pubo Lane, Route Vallon, he had just come back in haste from the International Settlement police-house, having excused himself on the plea of taking medicine for illness, and had secretly copied out the list of districts and street-names that the Japanese wanted the two police-houses to search jointly. He showed it to me, and it threw me into great alarm. The fourteen street-names on the list were exactly the offices of the inner departments under the Shanghai Special District. (The Japanese, being cunning, had deliberately omitted the door-numbers, to guard against anyone's slipping away to give warning.) I felt then that the matter was of the gravest kind. There in Brother Junqing's house, I first instructed the comrades at each unit that had a telephone to stop work at once and hide their documents, codes, and wireless sets and the like within the secret depots. The working staff might all go out; only one or two lesser members were to stay behind to keep watch. When I had done telephoning, I joined group-leader Zhu (who by then, also warned by Brother Junqing, had hurried to the Liu house), and he drove his own car to the units that had no telephone, to have the comrades take safety measures at once. I of course could go no more to the District headquarters or to No. 71 Maiyang Road. At that tense hour, near the Cathay Theater on Avenue Joffre, I chanced to meet the Chief Wireless Inspector Brother Yang Zhenyi (who had once held an important post in the Ministry of Judicial Administration); having told him the above, he at once returned to the wireless central and took emergency measures. I loitered on Avenue Joffre a good while, then went into the Guanle Cantonese restaurant on Rue du Consulat, opposite Route Vallon, ordered a bowl of shredded-chicken noodle soup, and used the telephone they had set in a little inner room to call No. 2 Aitang New Village, the headquarters. It was the Hunanese cook, old Xiao, who answered. I asked him whether any 'guests' ('guests' being the veiled word for police-house men) had come that afternoon. He said several 'guests' had come, and had looked everywhere too, but had carried nothing off. Afterward we learned that all fourteen of the office sites had been visited by them. Besides the detectives of the International Settlement and French police-houses, there were Japanese-invader personnel among those searching jointly. But no material evidence was turned up, nor was anyone arrested. That we came through so great a storm without harm at the time was owing to the following reasons:

1. Happily the two Brothers Junqing and Shaokui got the news beforehand and gave timely warning, so that I could make my dispositions and take precautions with speed.

2. The French chief of police of the French police-house did us, all unseen, a very great turn. For when the Japanese, together with the International Settlement detectives, reached the central police-house of the French Concession, the Japanese produced a list of six so-called 'Blue Shirt Society' principals for this arrest-hunt: first on it was the then acting chief of the Shanghai Special District, Zhao Lijun; second, Brother Mao Wanli; third, the writer, Zheng Xiuyuan; fourth, the leader of the Second Intelligence Group, Liu Jian; fifth, the leader of the First Intelligence Group, Zhu Xiaogu; sixth, the woman courier, comrade Xiao Jieying. But all on the list were the several persons' cover names (of the six-person list the Japanese produced against our side, I now recall only three of the cover names: the acting chief Zhao was Ling Qiuyun, group-leader Zhu was called Wang Dakui, and I was Tan Yunxiang; those of Brother Wanli and of the two comrades Liu and Xiao I no longer remember). The French chief of police at once directed the detectives sent to take part in the search that, on reaching the fourteen sites given by the Japanese, if they did not find the persons named on the above list, they were not to search or arrest, and so forth. Moreover, because the fourteenth of July was the French National Day, when the French police-house staff might have had their holiday, the Japanese request for a joint search (note: the fourteen sites above were all within the French Concession, so a joint search with the French police-house was unavoidable) delayed their holiday, and they were inwardly most put out. Toward the search work they showed not the least zeal; and because, too, the Chinese detectives of the French police-house were for the most part patriotic countrymen—the War of Resistance being under way, and hatred of the foe shared by all—they naturally bore, in their hearts, a resentment against the snarling, clawing Japanese military police.

Had it not been for all the above reasons, the outcome of this affair would have been beyond bearing to think of." Brother Xiuyuan's account of this passage is fairly detailed; there are several points I would explain further:

One. Mr. Mao Wanli then held in Shanghai the post of "Chief Inspector," but this was a title only; his real task was to carry out the project of sanctioning Wang Jingwei.

Two. As to Brother Wang Luqiao's being taken in Shanghai, the writer has already given an account in "The Whole Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi." What Brother Xiuyuan records is the same affair, only he was in touch with another side of it.

Three. That the Japanese side could produce a detailed list of fourteen work sites without a single error was plainly by no means got through any line of reconnaissance; it cannot be gainsaid that something had gone wrong within the "Shanghai District."

Four. The Japanese who joined the police detectives in searching the fourteen sites were Japanese gendarmes in plain clothes. During the search they only stood by and "watched," because at that time the Shanghai Settlement authorities would not yet allow the Japanese to "lay hands" directly, and did their utmost to guard their own rights.

Five. Mr. Yang Zhenyi, the Chief Wireless Inspector of Shanghai mentioned in the text, held in Taiwan the posts of head of the Fifth Section of the Intelligence Bureau and deputy director of the Bureau of Investigation, and died more than a dozen years ago.

Six. In safeguarding its own security, the "Shanghai District" owed much to the two gentlemen Liu Junqing and Liu Shaokui, of whom there is more below in the Third Part. As of now, Brother Liu Junqing died long since, while Brother Liu Shaokui is alive and well at the great age of eighty.

Seven. By a happy chance—writing to this point, I suddenly recall that Mr. Liu Shaokui has a draft of a memoir here with me; taking it out to look, I find in it a passage, too, on the matter of the Japanese side's demand on the fourteenth of July in the twenty-eighth year that the police-houses of the two concessions jointly search the fourteen offices of the "Shanghai District"—too precious to lose. At the time, Mr. Liu Shaokui's post was that of judicial superintendent of the Laozha police-house of the International Settlement police, and he was soon promoted to Chief Inspector. In our Bureau he was listed as a communications man of the "Shanghai District First Group." Here I record his own text: "At eleven in the morning on the fourteenth of July in the twenty-eighth year of the Republic, the Japanese Special Services Department (the writer notes: possibly the Special Higher Section of the Japanese Shanghai Gendarmerie) led gendarmes to the office of Kleighton (the writer notes: Kleighton, an Englishman, had already been recruited to help our side's work), chief of the Eighth Section (the writer notes: a section newly set up by the International Settlement police-house expressly to handle cases of this kind), to request help in hunting down and arresting Chongqing special-service personnel. Kleighton, for his part, telephoned to summon me to his office, declaring loudly that men would be sent at once to help make arrests; and as he spoke, he gave me a secret sign that there was something in the left-hand drawer of his desk. Taking the chance while Kleighton talked with the others, I got from the drawer a little roll of paper; and when I got back to my office and opened it, it was a detailed set of addresses with a rough sketch attached, but no door-numbers. I at once telephoned to call Liu Junqing to notify Brother Zheng Xiuyuan quickly, and copied out one set of the sketch-addresses for Junqing to take out and make contact. In this way, an hour was lost in the doing. When at last they set out to act, they searched fourteen places by the sketch-addresses, and the upshot was that all drew a blank, and not one person was taken. At several of the places the food and dishes stood on the tables, not yet eaten. Seeing this, the Japanese fell into a towering rage, and thereafter the special-service commander (the writer notes: possibly the head of the Special Higher Section of the Japanese Gendarmerie), Yokoyama Shūma, together with Iwai Eiichi of the Kōain, sent a letter to the Municipal Council of the International Settlement declaring that there were senior men within the police who were in secret communication with Chongqing, and demanding severe punishment. The case being brought up, the Eighth Section chief Kleighton offered his explanation, saying that from first to last he had handled it himself in person—where was there any communicating with Chongqing elements? It was purely the Japanese side making trouble without cause, with some ulterior aim, and so on. And so it came to nothing."

Eight. In Brother Xiuyuan's account there is one point very hard for a man to understand: since the Japanese meant to go into the French Concession to hunt down "Chongqing elements," why did they still have to notify the International Settlement, run by the British and Americans? The writer, for this, wrote to Brother Xiuyuan for enlightenment; and Brother Xiuyuan, to be safe, in turn inquired by telephone of Mr. Liu Shaokui, who had served in those days in the International Settlement police-house, and was told: "Because the Shanghai region is vast and the departments in charge of public order many, the two police-houses had once agreed that, for any case handled within the French Concession and the International Settlement, it must be done jointly by the detectives of the two police-houses. The constables of the International Settlement police included not only men of British, American, and other nationalities, but also Japanese in middle and upper posts. And within the International Settlement police-house a Eighth Section had been set up, charged expressly with such matters." Things of this kind are really very hard to explain clearly; told now, they can only be looked on as old lore.

Next I excerpt a still more stirring passage from Brother Zheng Xiuyuan's same account.

"A Perilous Scene inside the Weierdeng Dance Hall"—

"That evening I naturally dared not go back to lodge at a place already searched, and at the same time had to find the chief accountant Bai Shengzu and get him to disburse funds, so as to take over another house and set up the inner-work offices anew. Then again, because the various sites had been searched that afternoon, I dared not put up at a hotel either, and made ready to muddle through a night in a dance hall (there were then several dance halls in Shanghai open all night through), and to reckon further the next day. With me at the time was the District headquarters accountant Zhang Huang; and when the two of us went to the Jazz-lu dance hall on Route de Tibet, we met again the leader of the Fifth Intelligence Group, Zhu Cenlou. In seeking out the accountant Bai Shengzu, we left the Jazz-lu, and found him at last at the Yunshang dance hall, just opened that very night on the fifth floor of the Sun Sun Company. He promised to disburse me three thousand yuan the next day, for the taking-over of another office house. About one o'clock past midnight, the three of us moved on again to the great Lido dance hall on Medhurst Road. We had not sat long, for the Lido closed at two past midnight, and so at about half past one we hurried to the Yvonteh dance hall on Yuyuan Road (a place of extra-settlement roads, within the reach of the enemy-and-puppet power). I walked ahead; and on entering the hall I felt the lights rather dim and the air not too good, and turned back at once to the Weierdeng dance hall near Jessfield Park close by. This place was more spacious; inside the main door, to the left, was an open floor, and to the right a corridor, some twenty feet long, at the end of which three stone steps led up into the inner hall, oblong in shape. The band sat at the rear, facing the front door of the hall. The three of us sat down at a little table on the band's left, by the dance floor; and not long after we were seated, I saw of a sudden comrade Chen Dirong (in charge of personnel at the District headquarters) come into the hall. As it chanced just then that Brother Zhu Cenlou had risen to go to the toilet, I hailed Chen to sit in the empty place on my right. His first words were to ask me: 'How is Mr. Ling?' (Ling Qiuyun was the cover name of the acting chief Zhao Lijun, who, on sick leave in hospital, had not come to the District headquarters for many days.) I answered him: 'Very well!' He asked me again: 'Would you care to go and see Mr. Wang? He is at the Lido dance hall now.' I answered him at once: 'There is nothing to see him about; I do not wish to see him. When there is need, I will get in touch with you.'

I raised my left wrist and looked at my watch: the hour hand pointed exactly at two. I said: 'I too came out from the Lido just now; how was it I did not see Mr. Wang?' He hummed and hawed; and marking his manner, a little unnatural, and that it was already past midnight—how came he to be idling about a dance hall on the extra-settlement roads?—I outwardly showed nothing, but inwardly harbored some misgiving. After he rose and left, I gave a kick with my right foot under the table at Zhang Huang's toe, and said: 'Mingchu' (Mingchu being Chen Dirong's cover name) 'is a bit suspicious; go out quick and take a look.' But because the band was loud, Zhang Huang did not catch it and did not rise, so I hurried out myself. First, inside the little door where they played 'bingge,' I turned side-on and craned my neck to peer out, and saw all at once Chen Mingchu standing in the corridor outside the hall, facing the main door, his back to the hall, so that he could not spy me watching him from behind. Standing opposite him was a burly big fellow, in a white short shirt above and black glossy-silk long trousers below—altogether got up as a Shanghai baixiangren idler. As the spot where I stood was still five or six paces from them, and Mingchu was talking with the big fellow head to head, I could not hear what they said; but I felt on the instant that the matter was grave enough, and that they might have some design on me. Not daring to turn back into the hall, I hurried into the room where they played 'bingge' and mingled into the crowd. The moment I saw Mingchu come into the hall with the big fellow, I slipped out through the corridor, ran quickly to the silver car-hire across the way, got into a car, ducked my head down, and bade the driver make for Route de Tibet; and as the car sped south along Bubbling Well Road, I looked back—and there, no great distance off, was a black sedan, as if in pursuit. I said to the driver, feigning: 'My head aches terribly; please drive a little faster.'"

Luckily, when our car turned from Bubbling Well Road into Route de Tibet, I looked behind again, and that car had not turned to follow; only then did I know that no one was tailing me, and felt somewhat easier. Reaching Route de Tibet, I got out near the Great China Hotel; and after the silver car I had ridden in had gone far off, I walked into the Great China Hotel. I caught my breath for some twenty minutes, and when my fright had a little settled, telephoned Mrs. Zhang Huang (she too was a woman courier), lodging for the time at the Far East Hotel, to inquire; Brother Zhang Huang had already got back there, and I said to Zhang: 'It seems to me Mingchu has something the matter with him. How did things go after I left?'

Brother Zhang Huang answered me on the telephone: 'Not long after you left, Mingchu brought a big hulk of a man into the hall and hunted everywhere for you—I thought it none too right either.'

I arranged with Brother Zhang Huang to meet the next afternoon at a comrade's lodging in the French Concession, and learned the following:

1. Mingchu first brought the big fellow in white shirt and black trousers into the hall, and not finding me, went out; a few minutes later he came again with three more men of the hired-thug sort, and once more went through the hall, and behind the band, and even the toilet, and still not seeing a trace of me, went off in a huff.

2. Early the next morning, Mrs. Zhang Huang, passing on some errand near the Bund by Wumalu, met Chen Mingchu and a party of four or five; the moment he spied Mrs. Zhang he asked her: 'Where has Mr. Zheng gone?' Mrs. Zhang answered: 'I have no idea; my own husband is hunting everywhere for him too.'

On the strength of these two circumstances, together with the suspicious doings of the night before, and taking with them the joint search of the fourteen work offices of the District by the Japanese invaders and the police-houses, it may be all but proved that Chen Mingchu had turned traitor and gone over to the puppets!"

Brother Xiuyuan's account of this passage is unfinished; I have excerpted it thus far, and add here some further explanation:

1. Chen Dirong, whose cover name was Chen Mingchu, was originally the assistant secretary of the "Shanghai District," in charge of personnel. After Mr. Wang Tianmu became District Chief, without so much as a report to his superiors he moved the incumbent secretary, Brother Zheng Xiuyuan, out to the field, and let Chen Mingchu fill the secretary's post in an acting capacity. This was not necessarily an ambush laid beforehand, but it sowed a seed of calamity.

2. Brother Xiuyuan's meeting Chen Mingchu at the dance hall was wholly a coincidence—what the common saying calls "foes meeting on a narrow road." Had it been a planned operation, dispositions should have been made beforehand; otherwise Brother Xiuyuan could hardly have got clear.

3. In the text, the "Mr. Wang" spoken of in Chen Mingchu's mouth was the former chief of the "Shanghai District," Mr. Wang Tianmu; his whereabouts were by then unknown, and what exactly was the truth of it could not yet be judged.

4. After the fourteen offices of the "Shanghai District" were searched, ordinary work was greatly affected; from Brother Xiuyuan's description it is not hard to see that the "Shanghai District" at that moment had not so much as a safe office to work in, and that many of its inner staff were even living in hotels. One may say that the whole heavy trust of the "Shanghai District" rested solely on Brother Xiuyuan; and had it not been for his cleverness and quick wit, and his spirit of braving danger and hardship, then to talk grandly of restoring the working machinery—how could that have been a thing to do in a short span? This is no wild puffing or flattery: at the time, the one who could truly grasp in fact how perilous the plight was, was, I believe, myself alone, and so it is that I dare to say it so surely.

Brother Xiuyuan's "A True Record of Three Perils on the Shanghai Shore" falls into eight passages in all; the three quoted above are of things that happened before the writer reached Shanghai. After I took over the "Shanghai District," although many mishaps were still ones Brother Xiuyuan lived through in person, I had by then come to some understanding of them, and shall record them separately from another angle.

The writer only vaguely recalls having taken over as chief of the "Shanghai District" on the twelfth of August in the twenty-eighth year; but whether that is exact I can no longer trace out. As to what month and day is set down on the personnel card at the "Juntong Bureau," I do not know; and in truth none of this matters. What is still fresh in memory, rather, is the manner of the taking-over—and what do you suppose it was? Merely that Brother Zheng Xiuyuan and I shook hands and laughed a ha-ha, and the handover was reckoned done—not only no seal and warrant, but not even a wooden stamp. As for the former acting chief, Mr. Zhao Lijun, he was not present—very likely he did not even know of it.

### Our Behind-the-Lines Command Center, a Thing of Its Own Kind

Many past matters, written out now, look as if they were all done in due order and reason; but in truth, at the time, they were by no means every one a planned act. That is to say: for one thing, I had not yet the power to command the whole and see far ahead; for another, in the basic principles of the work one must always follow the guidance of one's superiors; and yet again, some of the measures could hardly help meeting a little check or interference from the personnel side. One may say that my more than two years at the post of District Chief were passed amid just such conditions. In that time I had, too, thoughts and doings that reached beyond, or broke through; and though I of course held them to be all gain and no loss to the work, our Mr. Dai would not allow them. It was his habit to give little reason: no was simply no, and who would dare not heed?

A few idle words disposed of, let me go on with what I was recording of things after I took over as chief of the "Shanghai District."

The first great matter at that moment was that I had to plant my feet firm and see how to meet this state of things, with the storm about to break. Since all the inner offices had been searched, there was of course no guaranteeing the enemy would not come again, and so it brooked not a moment's delay to move them, one and all, at once. This was not so simple as merely finding houses and moving; for safety's sake there had of necessity to be many technical niceties too, above all the guarding against being traced and tailed along a line, or else it would be cleverness turned to blunder.

My idea was, within the shortest span, to build up step by step a nimble and pliable command center—that is, a duplex District headquarters of the "Shanghai District." To avoid the fault whereby a single alarm would affect the handling of business, we did not fix the office in one place; the staff living at the offices had to keep the mobility to shift on the instant; the documents that must be stored were cut down to the least degree, so that they could be destroyed at any time and also carried on the person as one ran. As for whether it was by then possible to shrink a great many documents, by photographic technique, into a film as small as a postage stamp or even a comma, I do not know whether that age had yet brought it off. In any case, in those days we had not learned this craft, nor had the state any such equipment, still less any speaking of how to apply it.

To establish a new work command center was in itself an urgent matter; then why not get it done at a stroke, rather than go about it step by step? The greatest reason was the limit set by funds. Had it been that one might draw as much as one wished, and the keeper of the purse pay out as much as one drew, some things would have been far easier to manage. And besides, the various outlays of this stretch of days, added up, were no small sum. To go a layer deeper, there were here, too, some unavoidable matters of formality and procedure: on the "Shanghai District" side, as regards the whole removal of the District headquarters' several offices, including the liaison stations and the courier department, and the setting-up besides of several reserve sites, all these were needful, and so, without seeking instruction from above, one might handle them as expediency required; but to pay out a fairly large sum of money—the chief accountant the Bureau headquarters posted to Shanghai (Bai Shengzu at the time) could not necessarily decide it, and even were he glad to support it, could not necessarily produce such a sum at once. This is one of the hindrances that arise from each department in charge going its own way; and should there be added to it, into the bargain, even a little touch of human factors, then it grows more troublesome still.

To build the work command center anew was for the sake of fighting steadily from firm ground; and of equal importance with it was that one must exactly examine one's own lines of attack and defense. The truth of the "Chen Mingchu affair" could by now be made out; the motive of his turning traitor and going over, whether it was of a crazed and darkened mind and a wit blinded by gain, or else of an ignorant folly worked on by incitement, was assuredly not like the play-acting made real of one who walks a roundabout road. This affair was to be handled as a special case, of which there is a detailed account below. At the time, the inner effect it gave the "Shanghai District" was to lower the degree of mutual trust; and recording it here, I cannot name names, but there was indeed for a spell all manner of rumor going—"this one is not to be relied on, that one has something wrong with him." I, being the responsible head, was in no way yet abreast of the situation; and what was to be done?

I could not, on hearing a word or two of gossip and hearsay, put a question to the person concerned. Only think: if there really were some unspeakable thing, who would own to it? And if there were no such thing at all, would it not deal the other a mighty blow? No one, I believe, could bear it. To go about it by hint and side-stroke was no method either; handled amiss, it would breed unease in all, and even mutual suspicion—and for a body to lose its inner unity and concord, what an end that would be! Say so, then—was one to let it simply take its course? Before I had gained a full understanding of the "Shanghai District" personnel as a whole, this was truly a great problem, most vexing. I remember that I once went carefully with Brother Xiuyuan over the present standing of every man, weighing and appraising; and in the end we held every one of them loyal, true, and patriotic, with not one worth doubting. Thereupon I made a decision: that from that time, should any like affair arise again, so long as it was fixed who the man was, then, where there was no way to verify it, the only course was to order him recalled to the rear within a set day and the matter reconsidered. To do this was itself wholly out of helplessness: if he obeyed the order and went, that of itself proved his innocence; but if he defied the order and would not go, then he had surely something wrong, and that too was easy to handle. As to whether, once recalled, he would meet with punishment, that turned on the details of the case; and in the personnel-transfer report we would be sure to offer our views to our superiors. Speaking generally, I have never favored punishing subordinates harshly: let the truth only be made clear, and then reassign the work or give retraining.

After the "Shanghai District" had passed through a fairly tense spell, it was happily quiet and without event—for about a month only—and then the general working spirits steadied down. Otherwise, moving house and changing places day by day, and, if there were trouble within, it would be as carrying bacteria about with one: there would still be no health and peace.

There is a saying: the spirit of special-service work lies in unceasing attack. When we had planted our feet firm and looked over our array, it followed in due course that we should go forward to meet the foe and make ready to fight on three fronts. The first thing to be weighed was the disposition of personnel and the deployment of fighting strength. The incumbent secretary of the "Shanghai District" (like a chief of staff on the military side, a secretary-general on the civil), Brother Zheng Xiuyuan, had already had his identity exposed, and had become the second man the enemy and the puppets were bent on having (the first being the former acting chief, Brother Lijun, of the cover name Ling Qiuyun); yet he was the very pillar of the present "Shanghai District," and to lose his support—there was truly no upholding this tottering situation—so I meant to ask him to stay on. But Brother Xiuyuan was heavy with a sense of duty; he had the fine virtue of seeking after perfection and leaving no cause for regret, and, though he well knew crisis lay hidden on every side, still braved the danger to go everywhere, keep every appointment. And Brother Xiuyuan's nature was, as it happened, restless besides; were one to counsel him to bear safety in mind, and that for lesser matters he might well assign an assistant to act, he would still see to everything in person and would go himself and none other. This bred in me a kind of inner contradiction: by right reason, Brother Xiuyuan ought indeed to leave Shanghai; yet, out of a little selfishness too, I still hoped to keep him as my right arm. I remember I once talked this mind of mine over with Brother Xiuyuan face to face, and had already got his nod to it.

Another plan was to invite Brother Mao Wanli to take part in the work of the "Shanghai District." Because we had a deep friendship, I made this wish plainly known to Brother Wanli; he would not, I thought, refuse. Properly speaking, his post was that of "Chief Inspector," representing the Bureau headquarters and stationed in the Shanghai region to superintend the work as a whole; in working standing he seemed to stand half a head above the chief of the "Shanghai District," and what was more, when I was station chief at Tianjin, he was already acting chief of the "Beiping District." Sure enough, he showed his glad assent. The way we jointly proposed was this: that Wanli, under his title of "Chief Inspector," should concurrently be inspector of the "Shanghai District," a separate inspector's office to be set up by the "Shanghai District"; while in fact we would, as the work advanced and opened out, divide off a part of the business and ask Brother Wanli to take charge of it—the intent being that, the "Shanghai District" being so large and its work so much, could we truly attain a division of labor and joint effort, would it not be well for all? Afterward, I telegraphed under my own name to Mr. Dai Yunong for instruction; and not many days later Mr. Dai wired back a refusal—the tone of it firm, with no room to turn about.

Writing this, I cannot but laugh in spite of myself. I who had always opposed the "inspectorate system" in special-service work, this once actually asked, of my own motion, for an inspector—and could not get one. The affairs of this world are past all reckoning! Thereafter the "Shanghai District" never did set up an inspector; besides Wanli, I was of course not glad to add a needless post. After Wanli left Shanghai, Mr. Dai appointed him director of the "Southeast Office," concurrently head of the Second Section of the Third War Zone Commander's headquarters, stationed at Shangrao and Yanshan in Jiangxi, the work very heavy—and this, perhaps, was Mr. Dai's reason for not agreeing to keep him in Shanghai.

Failing to keep Wanli, there was no help for it but to gird up my spirit and set to work with the strength I had at present.

At this point the District headquarters of the "Shanghai District" had already been split for the time into two, removed to new premises to work.

To speak of our office work—it was, again, unlike an ordinary government office, above all where, within the Shanghai concessions, we took private dwellings for cover; there one could the less be bound by forms. As to the District Chief, he commonly had a fixed seat, though not necessarily a desk for office use; the desktop would not be piled with stationery, and there was never a many-colored array of files. Still, two things were not to be dispensed with: first, paper, blank or written on; second, a fountain pen of one's own.

Documents were not left lying on the table all day; only when the District Chief came would the secretary or assistant secretary take them out and hand them to him to deal with. Commonly one looked first at the incoming telegrams and dispatches from above; the incoming telegrams meant those wired from Chongqing to the wireless station on duty, which the District headquarters' couriers (mostly women) fetched back at regular intervals from the several station sites, and handed to the code clerks to render, by the marked ciphers, into plain text. After the assistant secretary in charge had seen to the registration formalities, they went first to the District secretary; and if the "confidentiality grade" of that telegram was marked "self-decipher"—for instance "for Guzhong's own deciphering," "Guzhong" being the District Chief's code name, that is, marked to be rendered by the District Chief himself from cipher into plain text. But in fact those truly deciphered by the District Chief himself were next to none. By custom, the District Chief mostly authorized some one code clerk that, meeting a self-decipher telegram, he need not seek instruction, but render it straightway into plain text, put it in an envelope marked "to be opened in person," and leave it for the District Chief. Telegrams of this kind mainly did but show that their confidential nature was especially high, and it was not necessarily insisted on whether they were truly self-deciphered. For instance, by the middle years of the eight-year War of Resistance, the cipher codes we used had, to guard against decipherment, grown fairly complex, so that even doing it oneself, without careful pointers, one might well not render it out.

As to who was entitled to hold a code book for personal use—the Bureau headquarters had no rule for it; it turned on Mr. Dai's own command and discretion: he would issue one to whom he would. On the whole it was mostly the heads and deputy heads of field units and their chief assistants, or directly-subordinate personnel bearing some particular mission. Take myself, for example: in the more than ten years I was in the work, he never once issued me a code book for communicating with him alone. The odd thing is that my secretary or assistant nearly all had one—such as Zeng Che of the "Tianjin Station," Fang Bingxi of the "Hanoi temporary establishment," and so on. Why should this be? Very cleverly, I guess he did not give me a private code book because he knew I had no such need; and that he gave one to my deputies was not necessarily to guard against me, though I did now and then do some things that went askew. Of course the use of a code book is by no means so simple; I do but mention it here in passing.

Next, to speak of the "code clerk." "Code clerk" is a name of office, found in every field unit that has a wireless station; where no code clerk is set, the code work is by custom done concurrently by the secretary or assistant secretary. Of the District headquarters code clerks of the "Shanghai District," at the most there were four or five at once, one of whom was like a foreman—that was comrade Wang Shaoqian, quick of hand and fine of mind, of no little ability; the more important telegrams to and fro were all rendered by him, and he never bungled. Others who did code work, first and last, included the comrades Xu Jiahe, Hua Nianxiong, Ling Feng (a woman), and Zhou Jinshu. Though there were so many, in busy times it often happened that, working overtime till dawn, there was still a backlog left over—which shows how many telegrams there were in a single day.

The telegrams rendered into plain text were sorted by kind and distributed to the several assistant secretaries or compiler-reviewers, who, after annotating them, gathered them for the District secretary to handle. Ordinary routine incoming telegrams the District secretary dealt with straightway; only the needful ones did the secretary annotate, with or without an opinion, and pass to the District Chief to see. Because I was newly come, and wished to gain more understanding, I looked at rather more; and once I was abreast of the situation, I authorized them to see to it, for I have no love of meddling in fussy matters.

Among the dispatches from above, besides telegrams, there were also official documents, all conveyed by special men sent on from the Bureau headquarters at Chongqing, relayed hand to hand. Most were of larger bulk, with more text and of no time-urgency, and now and then there were also credentials and letters in the sender's own hand. Of these, what the comrades set store by were the letters in Mr. Dai Yunong's own hand: to whomever he wrote, it was given to that man, and what the content was, each to his own measure, none asked; even those he wrote to my subordinates, I would not ask a word of what was written. Yet such things were not common; and there were times they roused envy, and could stir suspicion too.

Now to speak of the "compiler-reviewer." The compiler-reviewer's working standing is but next below the assistant secretary, his task being the handling of intelligence solely. Strictly speaking, "intelligence handling" is a specialized learning; with pains bent to the study of it, it could well be gathered into a great work—but a pity it is too specialized, and much said of it here would pall, so I stop. In the early days of my taking over the "Shanghai District," there were two intelligence compiler-reviewers, Wang Shiying and Liu Yuanshen. Comrade Wang Shiying had been promoted from code clerk; comrade Liu Yuanshen had had special training—both were very able. This intelligence compiler-reviewer, comrade Liu Yuanshen, is the very Brother Liu Yuanshen who now revises the whole of "Nameless Heroes" for the writer; and, to speak of it, we have been together over forty years. At the time, the intelligence material the "Shanghai District" got in daily from the field was fairly large in amount, and its quality uneven; two or three men handling it could not get through it from morning to night. The matters our superiors sent down to investigate were beyond count too, and those on which a full answer could be got were but two or three in ten—which ratio is no very low one; and in fact those that forever came to no result and turned into pending cases or lost their importance were of common occurrence. To think that one had but to wish to gather a thing, and could get it—would that not be too marvelous by half? No intelligence organization in the world, of whatever form, could do it, I am sure.

The "Shanghai District" headquarters appointed one assistant secretary to have charge of intelligence—that is, to superintend the intelligence work of the whole District. In system and procedure, the compiler-reviewers should answer to him; but by custom there was at most a distinction of upper hand and lower, and when other work pressed and manpower fell short of the sharing, the assistant-secretary tier bore too much, and matters of intelligence handling were then borne by the compiler-reviewers alone. Still, the intelligence sent out had all to have the District secretary's sanction, and those of importance the District Chief too must weigh and review. This was a work of the everyday sort, the whole three hundred and sixty days of the year, every day so, never breaking off; even amid a din of frequent alarms, one had to contrive by every means to get out what was to be sent.

The comrades who served as assistant secretaries of the "Shanghai District" were many; when I took over, there were Yi Zhen, Hu Shangwu, Xu Wenqi, and others. Not long after, comrade Yi Zhen was transferred elsewhere, and my superiors sent besides Gui Difei, Zheng Hailiang, and the promotion of comrade Liu Yuanshen, and so on—first and last there were all of six or seven; the exact times of their coming to and leaving the post I can no longer clearly recall. Each assistant secretary had charge of a part of the business, divided broadly into intelligence handling, action guidance, personnel management, liaison and conveyance. The assistant secretaries of the "Shanghai District" were of a working standing equal to the group and squad leaders of the field units, and were at times transferred out to the field too. Because the "Shanghai District" headquarters did not work concentrated in one place, the assistant secretaries too were stationed in several different places, and, as it seems, were never all together at one time.

The "Shanghai District" had besides an accounting department, having sole charge of finance; though it was but one man, with a courier attached, yet it worked apart in a place of its own, the greatest reason being that it had to keep in custody, by the boxful, the house-rental contracts, the various receipt-and-payment vouchers, the rosters, account books, and the like. Rack one's brains as one might to dress them up and hide them, one could never make what was into what is not, leaving no trace at all. This was no small matter; discuss it as we did without cease, we never had a good method for it. The former District accountant, comrade Zhang Huang, could think of no way; the new District accountant, comrade Chen Xianrong, could still put forward nothing new; in sum, the keeper of the purse could not, say what one would, do without keeping accounts. In my memory, the things the "Shanghai District" accounting department kept in custody came, at the most, to as many as six great leather trunks—a very plague!

The District headquarters of the "Shanghai District" in principle never had any direct dealing with the field units, and it was for this that the liaison hub—the "courier station"—was set up. The "courier station" was as our lifeline: once cut, it would at once affect the whole activity, and the least disturbance would hinder the normal running. The "courier stations," too, were of inner and outer kinds: the liaison between the District headquarters and the several groups and squads was the affair of the "inner courier station," and the liaison between the several groups and squads and their own subordinates was the affair of the "outer courier station." To do it thus, though it slowed the timing, yet safeguarded security. Throughout the whole "Shanghai District," inner and outer, the total of all the couriers, added up, was no fewer than several score; and as for the District headquarters' couriers alone, those whose names I can remember were, first and last, Xiao Jieying, Hu Man, Qiu Shuying, Yang Zheng, Shen Huiying, Ling Feng, Fu Meiying, Li Jiongzhu, Wang Qiufang, He Minxin, besides a Miss Wu something-xin whose name I cannot clearly recall, and one called by habit A-Ling. All the above were women, which also shows that women are best suited to courier work. More than half of them had had special training, and were fit not only to bear this work, but, given weighty use, could alike hold a front on their own. At the "Shanghai District's" fullest, the inner and outer courier-liaison sites came to twenty-two units in all; not to speak of aught else, merely to set up so many places took no small provision of conditions, plus a round of management to bring it off.

The two or three offices that served as District headquarters all took the family style—that is, took a household for cover; from the outside they looked much like a family "with a little money," and in the Shanghai concessions, so long as one was not "come-down poor," one had already the advantage. So we too had a great cook who cooked the meals and an amah who kept the house; but they were mostly trained folk, or else kin of working comrades, further "schooled up"—we would never go to a servants'-broker and hire a servant at random. Whether or not the neighbors to left and right marked it, I do not know, but our "home" had, too, a spot where it was not quite like one: that was, too few old folk. For this reason we specially encouraged the inner-work comrades to fetch their aged ones to live with them. Mao Jialin, who had once been a great cook at the District headquarters, and Amah Zhao, Cai'ai, and others who served as maidservants, were all Jiangshan people, each with a reliable origin in the personnel connection. (Editor's note: Mr. Dai was himself a man of Jiangshan, Zhejiang.)

The one long passage above has already introduced, in outline and in their differing guises, the inner array of the "Shanghai District"; let me try a simple reckoning further. The District's office sites, the wireless stations included, were commonly between ten and twelve at any time; the total of working staff, from District Chief down to office-hands, was kept regularly at about thirty or forty. Of conveyance we had, besides bicycles, not a single motorcar—not that we could not afford to buy one, but purely so as to leave no clue, for in the concessions there were car-hires everywhere, serving day and night, come at a call, most convenient; to have a car might be but an encumbrance. The wireless transmitters and receivers counted as the hardest great objects to hide (the wireless sets of those days were all fairly bulky); in daily use there were three sets, and spare parts besides. There were always some documents that could not be wholly destroyed, great and small, now more, now fewer, for which the assistant secretaries were held answerable, each keeping the part in his own charge; and if the District Chief wished to find a thing, he would not know where it was kept. Of code books there were a dozen or more sets, which had of necessity to be kept: the "base cipher" was a little booklet printed and bound by ourselves, much like an ordinary plain-code book; and there was besides a set of "under-cipher," which every code clerk could hold firm in memory, with, within it too, many transformations of adding, subtracting, multiplying, dividing—troublesome indeed, yet one could not grudge the trouble. Apart from this, the inner units had no arms; those would not only be of no use but might breed trouble. All that the District headquarters had was but so much. As for money—what belonged to the state was all on the accountant's side, and where there was a payment, he would disburse it by rule; what was private was all in our pockets, though I know that, on an ordinary day, add up all the money of every inner-work comrade, and it would not stretch to stand three tables of wine.

Why so shabby? A courier's pay was but thirty-eight yuan of fabi a month.

### Silhouettes of the Ever-Present Fighters on the Invisible Firing Line

Of the structure and make-up of the inner side of the "Shanghai District," an outline has been sketched in the last section—not perfectly clear, but a rough shape at least. Next it should be the turn of the field side; and the writer really cannot clearly recall the several static and dynamic conditions of all the field units belonging to the District, so that to give an account of them item by item and in full detail is truly a hard problem. In recent years, taking any chance that offered to inquire of, or verify with, the old comrades of the "Shanghai District," I have, in the course of talk, corrected not a few "errors" and added much "new knowledge"; yet there are still a good many questions to which I can get no answer that all agree on. Even Brother Zheng Xiuyuan—who once served as secretary of the "Shanghai District" and later as head of the personnel section of the Bureau headquarters, and with whom I often meet and talk of everything—and Brother Liu Yuanshen—who once served as assistant secretary of the "Shanghai District" and was afterward transferred to be an action-brigade leader—have alike some matters they cannot recall or cannot make out. Small wonder, after all: it is over forty years; privately there is no written record to consult, and in the state archives one may not rummage at will; relying on bare memory alone, one can hardly help a hair's-breadth of error.

There is yet another layer: the frequent changes among the field staff, and the constant filling-in of new men, are all reasons why they are not easily kept in mind; and next, the chances of contact between inner and field were few, above all for the writer himself. In the more than two years, of the several score field units, those whose heads and deputy heads I met, and talked with, made up but a third, and mostly only once or twice at that—so how could the impression be deep? For this reason, what is recorded below is what I have gone over and checked against with the two Brothers Zheng and Liu.

The field is the real executant of all the work: it takes the van, breaks the first line, and stands forever on the front rank; the dangers it braves far exceed those of the inner side. In not three years of the War of Resistance, it had already lost heroes and brave men past counting; yet on the "Roll of Heroes" they have no name.

The field units, including the intelligence groups, the action squads, and the directly-subordinate members, were not fixed at one man or a few. In Shanghai, there was once a unit of nineteen men taken together in one case, with a record of several deaths—this should be ranked the most glorious of units.

Among the field units there is a small part of the working comrades who are sunk on the mainland, their life or death unknown to this day; if any of them is mentioned in the text, no name will be given, to spare him harm.

Below, then, is a silhouette of the intelligence departments of the field units of the "Shanghai District."

"First Intelligence Group": the group leader was Zhu Xiaogu, a university graduate, good in both Chinese and English, small and wiry, full of resource, and of no little ability. He kept up several high-level intelligence lines, one of them being a puppet provincial government's commissioner of education. The inner connections with the International Settlement and French Concession police-houses were also developed and kept up by this group; among them, besides Chinese police officers, were two Englishmen and two Frenchmen, all police officers holding first-grade posts, of nerve and willing to shoulder responsibility, who did great service in safeguarding the security of our work. The writer met with group-leader Zhu Xiaogu once or twice; because the enemy-and-puppet hunt grew daily more pressing and it was hard for him to keep a footing in Shanghai, before long he was transferred to be director of the political department of the "Loyal and Patriotic Army." The group-leader's post he left behind was held concurrently by the leader of the newly established "New Group One," comrade Bi Gaokui.

"Second Intelligence Group": my impression of this group is rather faint. The group leader, Liu Jian, a Hunan man, a senior comrade, shrewd and capable and rich in work experience—we seem never to have met—was transferred away less than half a year after I became District Chief. The one who succeeded him as leader of the Second Group was the District headquarters assistant secretary, comrade Hu Shangwu. This group had as its main work the gathering of political intelligence; its members were mostly natives of Hunan province, and it had inner lines planted within the puppet apparatus. At the same time the group also gathered intelligence bearing on the Communist Party, and had good line-connections not only within the Communists' "Shanghai Special Committee," but which could at times reach even the "Chinese Communist Central."

"Third Intelligence Group": the group leader was Zhang Shengcai, the deputy leader Ge Yuexi. After Zhang Shengcai was transferred elsewhere, Ge Yuexi succeeded as leader; this group seemed to be, one and all, Fujian men. Brother Wu Nianzhong, who in the twenty-ninth year was puppet mayor of Hangzhou, was also one of the members of this group—he too was a Fujian man. In Shanghai, the writer never met either Zhang Shengcai or Ge Yuexi; it was only in the fortieth year of the Republic that I came to know Ge Yuexi, in Hong Kong. He was a National Assembly delegate who "would not return to the ranks"—why, I do not know. We had a spell of dealings in Hong Kong over the arranging of "mainland work." I have a certain impression of this group: I always feel they were of a shape all their own, and if one used "bureaucrat-politicians" to describe them, though not quite apt, it would yet come near.

"Fourth Intelligence Group": the special mark of this group lay in the gathering of "social intelligence." "Social intelligence," though inclined to local trifles, has yet its not-to-be-slighted importance for action work and for the verifying of general activity. The group leader Sheng Zhicheng was a woman, of no little accomplishment—a pity I never met her.

"Fifth Intelligence Group": it had changed leaders several times over; earlier it was Shi Shouzhang, and afterward there were Zhu Cenlou and others; who else was there in between? For the moment I really cannot recall—perhaps writing on and on will suddenly hook a memory back, one cannot say. Besides gathering intelligence on the enemy and the puppets, this group had one particular task: to gather the facts and the developing situation of the Communist Party's and the puppet apparatus'—of the Communist Party's and the Japanese invaders' colluding with each other. This is no fabrication out of whole cloth: the Communists, stopping at no means, doing every evil, in order to swell their own power and drag down the National Government that led the whole people's resistance, did indeed have facts of wolfish partnership with both the enemy and the puppets—iron proof piled like a mountain, past all crafty denial!

It is a shame to own it, but who would have thought that within this very group there had long been Communist elements lying hidden; at the time we never noticed, and it was only some years later, on a chance occasion, that it was found out and the thing confirmed.

The above is the general shape of the five intelligence groups belonging to the "Shanghai District." Besides these, there were, bearing intelligence tasks but not brought into the group establishment, the "directly-subordinate communications men," together with individual intelligence lines not bound by the "organization," and the like.

Below is the action side, comprising eight brigades, each brigade divided into several squads, the whole brigade running from about thirty to fifty men.

"First Action Brigade": this brigade had no substantive brigade leader; in name it was held concurrently by the former acting chief Zhao Lijun, but in fact the three squads under it were led directly by the District headquarters. At the end of the twenty-ninth year, comrade Liu Yuanshen acted as brigade leader.

The first squad of this brigade was the main strength of the whole; the squad leader, Xiang Qiangwei, a Sheng County man, of a rough-and-ready origin, none too lettered, was a man of honor who set store by his word: once he had nodded his head to a thing, it was truly "one word and no more." Just for this, I laid tasks on him to his face many times, and every one he did with dash and courage.

The second squad leader, Liu Quande, had in his early years knocked about a spell as a "Little Red Devil" in Jiangxi, and later mended his ways and joined our ranks; skilled with a gun, brave to the death, he was an outstanding man of action. During my chiefship I never met him; later I used him as a personal bodyguard, and in the end he brought down on himself the great disaster of killing a man and fled.

The third squad leader, Zhou Xiyuan—we never met from first to last; what impressed me most was that he brought calamity on the First Action Squad.

Though these three squads belonged to one and the same brigade, there was no linking between them—that is, no lateral relations.

"Second Action Brigade": the whole brigade had fifty or sixty men, with, when needed, a latent strength besides to draw on for supply. The brigade leader, Ji Zhencang, cover name Zhao Sheng—this big fellow I had met by appointment—was of imposing looks and awe-inspiring bearing, and stand where he might, he had the air of one who props up heaven and stands firm on earth.

This brigade's making-up had, they say, no small backing: it was, so the tale goes, a piece of cooperation between our Mr. Dai and the Shanghai notable Mr. Du Yuesheng—also a kind of support Mr. Du gave to Mr. Dai. And so the general chief cadres of this brigade were either Mr. Du's "students" or of his "following."

As regards the command system of the "Shanghai District," the "Second Brigade" was an action squad belonging to the "Shanghai District," treated on the same footing as all the other squads, drawing its "living and activity funds" by the same rule, and with, likewise, absolute personnel and administrative authority; never was the "background" of that brigade taken into account; and ordinarily few ever brought the matter up.

The "Second Brigade" had a squad leader named Chen Mo, whose name I remember because, in my time in office, his work showed well, though I did not know him. Later, when I had trouble and fled to Pudong, I chanced to throw myself on the guerrilla corps he commanded. After the victory of the War of Resistance he cut quite a figure for a spell in Shanghai; but good times last not long, and over a very worthless matter he met with ill fortune full in the face, and died at the hands of the law. Had he not just then happened to be caught in a whirlwind of purges, he need not have died.

"Third Action Brigade": the brigade leader, Jiang Anhua, a classmate of the Military Academy of the classes near mine, a Guizhou man, had, one may say, already struck root in Shanghai. The whole brigade was over fifty strong, of a fairly even strength, and up to the political mark; formed and established two or three years since, it had achievement after achievement in the work. After I took office I strengthened and enlarged it further, using it as the mainstay of the whole District. And because there was, into the bargain, a tie of being classmates, my personal contacts with brigade-leader Jiang were rather more too: not only did we often meet, but at times I would even put work problems to him face to face for joint discussion—which I never had with the heads of the other field units. What I would make plain is that I had no thought of fostering a personal power; for at that time I did not yet understand that sort of thing. Later, as I gradually came to a knowledge of it, I learned that for one in special-service work there is fundamentally no such thing as a personal power to speak of.

"Fourth Action Brigade": who was the leader of this brigade? At the start of my taking over, being not yet familiar with the personnel, I can hardly say for certain. As I remember, Wan Lilang was the deputy leader, and the leader's post was held by another; but by Brother Zheng Xiuyuan's account, Wan Lilang was the very leader of that brigade.

In October of the twenty-eighth year Wan Lilang had already turned traitor and gone over to the enemy, though not until the middle of November was the situation made out; the course of it is seen in outline in a passage of Brother Zheng Xiuyuan's "A True Record of Three Perils on the Shanghai Shore."

After Wan Lilang turned traitor, he went into the puppet "Special Operations Headquarters"—that is, "No. 76"—headed by Ding Mocun and Li Shiqun, as its First Section chief, given wholly to making an enemy of us who worked behind the lines, and in the end became a scourge.

Now, on the question of whether Wan Lilang was leader or deputy leader of the Fourth Brigade, I inquired of Brother Liu Yuanshen, who said he distinctly remembered Wan Lilang was the deputy leader, the leader being one Liu Shiyong. The two, cooperating ill, were forever at odds; at the time I had many times read reports of the two accusing each other over work problems, and was just about to deal with it, when Wan of a sudden was heard to have changed sides.

In the twenty-ninth year my superiors sent four men who had completed their training—Xu Wanfeng, Cheng Qinghe, Wang Yuanying, and Feng Qiceng—and after a trial, Xu Wanfeng and Feng Qiceng were chosen as leader and deputy leader, to re-establish the "Fourth Action Brigade."

"Fifth Action Brigade": with this brigade I was really too much "cut off"; I recall only that the earlier leader was Wang Fuqian, cover name Wang Yang, with whom we never had any contact from first to last. Later Wang Fuqian came to grief and was taken; in the interval there was one reorganization, and who was the leader after it? I mean to leave it in reserve for the present, till I have inquired and made it clear, to add later—for those I have already asked are all unsure, and I must wait for an old comrade who has gone overseas to come back.

"Sixth Action Brigade": this too was a very strong brigade, which in the records of the enemy and the puppets had many a "case" to its name. The brigade leader, Pan Shaoyue, should of course be ranked among the outstanding; of his doings I once saw an account, a dozen years ago, in a magazine, and had put that magazine by, but now for the moment cannot find it, and must leave it to be recorded further on together with his action cases.

"Seventh Action Brigade": the brigade leader, Zhang Bingquan, was in poor health and often on sick leave, so that the brigade's affairs were at times handled in his stead by the brigade secretary, Dong Jiarui. For this reason, this brigade's performance too was poorer than the others'.

In the twenty-ninth year my superiors sent Li Liang and one other, two men in all, to Shanghai, who, it seemed, were to be attached to the Seventh Brigade; but, as Brother Yuanshen has pointed out, they were for the time formed into a directly-subordinate intelligence group, in direct liaison with the inner courier station.

"Eighth Action Brigade": this was a brigade newly formed after the writer took office. The brigade leader, Xiao Zhangquan, was the younger brother of the inner courier station's head, Miss Xiao Jieying; he was the youngest of the eight brigade leaders, then no more than twenty-four or twenty-five. Bold and fierce, he was truly a newborn calf. Because I greatly admired this stout fellow's fine and upstanding bearing, after gaining my superiors' approval, and further asking the Third Brigade leader Jiang Anhua to lend strong support in the matters of its personnel and establishment, I formed the brigade.

Once, comrade Xiao Zhangquan was sent on a reconnaissance mission to the Nanjing–Shanghai–Suzhou region; who could have foreseen that he should go and never return, all trace of him lost? After a stretch of time it was confirmed that he had died a cruel death at the demon hands of the Japanese gendarmerie at Suzhou—alas, the grief of it!

To speak of the Xiao family, it may be called a whole house of loyal martyrs, a match for the Wang Wen family of Baodi County told of in "Rooting Out Traitors in the North." Old Mr. Xiao Huanwen, with two sons, three daughters, and a son-in-law, all took part in our work; his eldest son-in-law and youngest son both laid down their lives for the country; and there was much besides in the way of self-sacrifice for the safeguarding of the organization's security. There is to be a separate record of this below in the book.

In view of the want of flexibility in past personnel usage, a new conception was formed on the side of establishment; and, instruction sought and approval had, we by degrees made intelligence and action one, hoping to bring out an all-round effect. The "New Group One" was born just so.

"New Group One": also called "Directly-Subordinate Newly Formed First Group," it took a mixed establishment of intelligence and action, further divided into several small units. The group leader, Bi Gaokui—written also Bi Haokui, both his true name—a Zhejiang man, was a France-trained mechanical and electrical engineer, well versed in both the English and French tongues, of a blazing revolutionary ardor and a fine, precise scientific head. After graduating from our Bureau's foreign-affairs training class, he was sent to Shanghai, his instructions given him in person by Mr. Dai.

The deputy leader, Huang Zhiyuan, a Shanghai man, thick of brow and large of eye, a man of honor who set store by his word—a hero indeed. He directed the action work within the "New Group One," and showed to fine effect.

The whole body of basic group members had all had strict training, including the "Linfeng," "Qianyang," "foreign-affairs," and "staff-training" classes; all were men of excellence, and all had great achievements.

The whole group came to about fifty or sixty; many should be ranked as high-level lines. The martyr Chen Sancai, who in "The Whole Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi" died for the country over the case of sanctioning Wang Jingwei, was liaised through this very group. And that White Russian Ivanov, who had sold out the martyr Chen Sancai, was likewise sanctioned by this group, in requital for the removal of a traitor, in September of the thirtieth year.

Besides this, there was a patriotic youth body not originally within the establishment of the "Shanghai District," namely the "Anti-Japanese Traitor-Killing Corps," formed at Tianjin in the twenty-sixth year and abbreviated the "Kang Corps," with which the "Shanghai District" kept close liaison.

"Anti-Japanese Traitor-Killing Corps": from the end of the twenty-eighth to the beginning of the twenty-ninth year, our work stations and groups at both Beiping and Tianjin were destroyed one after another by the Japanese invaders, and the cadre comrades sorely trampled. At the same time the "Kang Corps" too was caught up in it, and most of its leading cadres all but sacrificed to a man; those who got clear of the danger zone, and were bent on killing the foe to requite the country, came one after another to Shanghai.

The Shanghai "Kang Corps" was led by comrade Sun Dacheng, a core cadre among the original founders of the Tianjin "Kang Corps." Comrade Sun was sent from Chongqing by Mr. Dai's introduction to Shanghai, and handed to the "Shanghai District" to be responsible for liaison, support, and guidance. Thereafter, in killing the foe and rooting out traitors, and in carrying on anti-Japanese propaganda, it did great things; its achievements yielded to no work unit within the establishment.

Sun Dacheng is a cover name, which may also serve as a code name for the "Kang Corps," because the man himself is even now (the seventy-second year) still in service and not retired, and it is not yet convenient to use his true name. What is worthy of praise and reverence is that, in the course of an important action work at the Bund Park in Shanghai, an explosive device went out of control and blew off his own elbow, so that he became a one-armed great hero who killed the foe. During the War of Resistance, throughout the fallen zone, there were anti-Japanese activities everywhere, present at every turn. Such activity, apart from the spontaneous kind, was "behind-the-lines work." This work, sorted by kind, had each its own charge; and of it, the "action" and "sabotage" that struck at the enemy and the puppets and stirred up men's hearts, our military and political authorities laid, as a heavy charge, upon the "Juntong Bureau."

The actual head of the "Juntong Bureau," the deputy director General Dai Li, to accomplish this mission, once spurred on his subordinates, saying: "We must enlarge our action, and make our authority felt, so as to chill the enemy's gall." And we who were staff of the "Juntong Bureau" naturally held ourselves bound, for the resisting of Japan and the rooting-out of traitors, to strive our utmost, reckless of the shedding of blood or the sacrifice of life. So it was that, ever since the War of Resistance against Japan, we had unceasingly dealt the enemy and the puppets heavy blows.

By the autumn of the twenty-eighth year, unhappily, our two great organizations at Beiping and Tianjin were wrecked one after another by the Japanese invaders, the cadres of every level who died gloriously, first and last, being no fewer than several score or a hundred; and at the same time the "Shanghai District," of the most formidable striking power, was likewise, through internal troubles, beset with mishap upon mishap, the effect reaching so far that the whole work was all but brought to a stop. After reorganizing and refitting, though it drew by degrees toward normal, yet already for several months it had had no showing in the work.

At this juncture, fierce fighting was in progress on every battlefront; and for the sake of strategic or policy coordination, all behind-the-lines work was to move forward in step together, on no account suffered to fall out of joint or lag behind, and so my superiors were exceedingly concerned for the progress of the "Shanghai District's" work.

The "Shanghai District," reorganized, though it completed by the middle of October its first action case of a deterrent effect, was yet far from the demands of the work. What was beyond all looking-for was that word came—the deputy leader of the Fourth Brigade who carried out the case, one Wan, had actually betrayed the organization and gone over to the Wang puppets!

Examining the causes of it, the greater part lay in personnel problems; the disasters at the two places, Beiping and Tianjin, and the not-long-before "Chen Dirong affair," were none of them otherwise. From this one may see that personnel and funds are, for behind-the-lines work, by no means trifles of the tail-end.
