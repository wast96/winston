## The Code of the Corps

"Hijikata Toshizō.—So we meet at last,"

{j} said Shichiri Kennosuke, rubbing his back against the base-board of the crossroads lantern. As he spoke, slowly, he was sinking his point into the low guard.

"Hijikata,"

{j} Shichiri seemed to be enjoying himself.

"the assistant master of a potato-patch dōjō in Bushū is now the vice-commander of the Shinsengumi, here in the flower of the capital. A grand rise in the world, troubled times or no."

"………"

Toshizō held the high guard.

"Just because you've risen in the world, don't go throwing over this Shichiri, mind."

"That's why I'm taking you on."

"Fine, fine. And is Kondō-san keeping well? I mean to pay him my respects one of these days."

"He's well."

{j} Toshizō said it as if spitting it out.

"That's good to hear. I'd like to say I've missed you—by ordinary reckoning our tie runs deep enough that I'd want to say, let's go and have a cup somewhere; but a tie it may be, and yet, my friend, a cursed crossed one."

"A crossed tie, it is."

"I'd rather not haul the muddy, mud-stinking brawls of South Tama in Bushū all the way to the flower of the capital and warm them over again; but you and I, it seems, are made never to get on."

"I hear you've been loafing about the Chōshū residence on Kawaramachi."

"My mother's people were foot-guards of the Chōshū clan on permanent posting in Edo. I've all manner of ties with Chōshū. I came up here thinking I'd die a more manly death than brawling with a loach-stinking fellow out in the Bushū sticks—and here that same loach-stink has come following up the road after me."

"Forgive me for breaking off your talk,"

{j} said Toshizō,

"but do you know the Lady Osae?"

Shichiri fell silent.

He knows her, Toshizō judged. Shichiri must have some line of connection with Osae, and it was that which had set him following Toshizō today.

"I don't know her."

"You've gone strangely flat all of a sudden. Honest, past expectation, it seems."

For answer, Shichiri brought his blade back to the middle guard. In that instant Toshizō's blade dropped swiftly from the high guard.

But Shichiri was no longer there.

With a rip, Toshizō's point split the base-board of the crossroads lantern. Wrenching it free, he raised his foot high and kicked the lantern over.

From beyond the lantern Shichiri sprang out.

"Just gave you a little teasing, that's all,"

{j} Shichiri laughed.

Meanwhile one of the men had circled behind Toshizō and struck out at him in a flash. He sprang clear by a hair, but his hakama was cut.

(Something is off.)

His blade would not gather its spring. In a brawl the one with the spring wins. It must be that the tangled impression of Osae was weighing his heart down.

At times like this, one throws off all appearances and pulls out. That is what makes a man good in a fight. All this Toshizō knew a hundred times over. The man he had been, brawling in the muddy paddies of Bushū, would have fled without a second thought. But he was another man now. He was the vice-commander of the Shinsengumi. Even a fight has its face to keep. Let it be said he had run, and what evil name might be scattered about Kyoto?

(Just as Osae said—even to a place like this, I have changed utterly.)

Toshizō, holding his sword aloft in his right hand, deftly slipped half out of his haori. It was not that he wanted his haori off. The haori was Toshizō's cunning lure.

Sure enough, aiming at the opening as he stood half out of it, the man on the right struck down from the high guard.

(I was waiting for it.)

The body of the man, carried away with himself, Toshizō scooped up and cut open from below with the one hand.

"That fool's strength, the same as ever,"

{j} Shichiri clicked his tongue from the shadows. A man of Shichiri's parts knew it: with a one-handed stroke, a man cannot be cut unless the strength behind it is great indeed.

Toshizō got clear of his haori at last.

"Shichiri, come a little closer."

"I'll not. There's no fool who'd take on a fellow gone oddly hot and worked up to his pitch."

This man too was no common swordsman. He knew the vital points of a brawl. Seeing Toshizō's fighting spirit begin to fill to an uncanny fullness, he drew his blade back, walked lightly along the shadows, and,

{j} "Fall back,"

{j} gave the order.

They scattered all at once.

Toshizō did not pursue.

(Shichiri, too—he's grown stout in himself.)

Among the many rōnin gathered in Kyoto were men of great parts. Even a man like Shichiri, rubbing shoulders with such fellows and forever debating some affair of state or other, had come to give a rather different impression from the Hachiōji cudgel-bully of old.

(A man is a strange thing.)

Such a change of substance, like a caterpillar turning to a butterfly, seems now and then to happen.

That December the shogunate issued its edict for the control of rōnin. Unquiet rōnin flowing into the Kyoto-Osaka country were to be seized and put to death on sight.

The reason: the Shogun Iemochi was shortly to enter the capital. The peace of Kyoto must be held down by force of arms.

"Such is the situation,"

{j} said Kondō, gathering the whole corps together.

"By the might and majesty of the great shogunate, we shall sweep away the drifting rabble and—awesome though it is to say it—guard the tranquility of the Forbidden Interior. From this day on, understand that the great streets and small of the imperial city are the Shinsengumi's field of battle."

It was from this time that the Shinsengumi began, in the literal sense, to work like fiends. Day after day they made a rain of blood fall on Kyoto.

Their number, a hundred men in the rough.

They were not, of course, all swordsmen of the first rank. There were the unripe, and there were cowards. A man who flinched on the field of battle was, afterward, punished without fail. And "punishment" was nothing so lukewarm as the confinement or house-arrest of the old warrior society. All of it was death. Death for the first, death for the second. To the eyes of the samurai of that day, who for three hundred years had settled everything by mutual accommodation, it was a penal code to make one shudder.

For the corpsman it was one or the other: cut down by the enemy in the wild play of blades, or cut down within the corps once he had withdrawn—so their daily life was a life resolved on death.

"Is it not a little too harsh?"

{j} so, one day, when as many as three men in a single day had been made to suffer decapitation or seppuku, Yamanami Keisuke once said, before Kondō and Toshizō.

The order of things runs ahead of itself here, but a little before this, just after Serizawa Kamo and his connections were swept away, Yamanami Keisuke's standing in the corps had changed. Where until then he had been vice-commander, the same as Toshizō, he was now made,

{j} "General Secretary."

{j} He was promoted. In order of rank it came to this: commander Kondō Isami, general secretary Yamanami Keisuke, vice-commander Hijikata Toshizō.

This promotion was a thing Toshizō had urged upon Kondō.

—Yamanami, by all means.

{j} When he said it, Kondō, for this once, was delighted. That Toshizō did not care for Yamanami had been a seed of grief to Kondō. And here was that same Toshizō making a special office called "General Secretary" for Yamanami's sake, and setting him above himself.

—Toshi, it's going to rain.

{j} He said as much.

—It won't rain.

{j} Toshizō said, expressionless. The post of "General Secretary" had a fine ring to its name, but in substance it was a thing like Kondō's personal adviser, counselor, staff officer, consultant—without authority. Or, more important still, this fine-ringing office carried no power of command over the corpsmen. Command ran from commander to vice-commander to jokin to the rank and file. In the language of today, General Secretary Yamanami Keisuke was Kondō's staff, and not his line.

Toshizō had shelved Yamanami neatly. Made an ornamental Daruma of him. Yamanami too was pleased at first, but as the true nature of the office came clear to him he came to hate Toshizō more than before. And not only that: he begged Kondō,

—Put me back to vice-commander, as I was.

{j} and Kondō, half won over, consulted Toshizō.

—Toshi, shan't we bump him back down?

—No, leave him as he is.

{j} And he drew a curious comparison.

As a boy, Toshizō, when the family's Ishida Powder was to have its raw stuffs gathered and the medicine compounded—work that fell in the summer slack season of the farming year, so that the whole village was set to it—had directed the labor from the age of twelve or thirteen. From that experience he remembered how, each time his eldest and second brothers came bumbling up to put in a word, the work's efficiency dropped. For then commands came from two quarters, or three.

—Two vice-commanders, and that is what you get. Kondō-san, unless a command out of your mouth rings at once to the vice-commander, is passed to the jokin, and moves the corpsmen like a lightning-flash, the Shinsengumi will go dull. An organization is the same as swordsmanship. It must be quick to feel. And for that, one vice-commander is enough.

This was Toshizō's own invention. The system of the shogunate and the clans was such that, as the Edo town magistracy for instance was staffed by two men, every office was filled by several men serving one duty. It was a thing that all the foreign envoys then coming to Japan marked with wonder. That mean custom the Shinsengumi broke without the least trouble.

—It is to make the corps tough and strong. And in exchange, I have decked Yamanami-san out in a place of honor.

{j} said Toshizō.

That is a digression.

"Is not the penal law too harsh?"

{j} When Yamanami Keisuke, now general secretary, gave Kondō this counsel, Toshizō turned a cold white eye on Yamanami.

"Yamanami-sensei,"

{j} he said.

"I would not have thought it of Yamanami-sensei. Do you wish to make the corps weak?"

"Who said such a thing?"

{j} Yamanami colored. Without so much as a smile, Toshizō,

{j} "To my ears, that is how it sounds,"

{j} answered quietly.

A hateful fellow, Yamanami must have felt, his belly boiling to the bottom.

"Yamanami-san, I'll tell you—I hold that every samurai in all Japan is a spineless thing. Samurai, samurai they say, but it is nothing to boast of; the truth of it I have seen with these eyes many a time. The heritable stipend and three hundred years of peace have made them so. But the Shinsengumi alone I will not let go that way. I will forge them into true samurai."

"And what is a true samurai?"

"Not the samurai of now. The samurai of old."

"Of old?"

"A Bandō warrior, or a warring-provinces warrior of the Genki and Tenshō years—well, I can't put it neatly, but a thing of that kind."

"You are innocent, Hijikata-san, past all expectation."

Childish, he must have wished to spit. In its place, Yamanami let an open sneer rise to his cheek.

Toshizō sat gazing steadily at that cheek. When once, long since, he had held the "debate on the warrior's code" with Serizawa Kamo, a sneer of the very same kind had risen to Serizawa's cheek; and it clung now to Yamanami's.

—A jumped-up peasant.

That, in fact, was how Yamanami felt. Yet in the depths of Toshizō's heart, too, there was a thing that wanted to cry out. An ideal—is it not, at bottom, a childish thing?

"Come, let it be; let's have some wine,"

{j} Kondō smoothed it over. Kondō held Toshizō for a man beyond compare, yet a possessor of scholarly talent like Yamanami Keisuke was also hard to lose. The official documents sent to the Protector of Kyoto, to the Shoshidai, to the palace's officers for affairs of state, to the captains of the Mimawarigumi—nearly all of them Yamanami drafted. And when they conferred with the clans' officers of public business, Yamanami went along. Brave men the corps had in plenty, but the only one who could hold forth boldly and with dignity in a place of ceremony was Yamanami Keisuke, the rōnin who had fled the Sendai clan.

Having had a page bring wine, Kondō looked from Yamanami's face to Toshizō's and back, and said:

"I am a fortunate man. I hold the two wheels of a cart together—the wit of Yamanami-kun and the valor of Hijikata-kun."

But was Toshizō a vessel of valor merely?

How far even Kondō had seen through to this talent of Toshizō's is a question. Yamanami's wit was knowledge simply; but Toshizō had the power to create.

(Watch. I will make just such a corps.)

That night, in Toshizō's room, the lamp burned late.

As was his way, Okita Sōji came to tease him.

"Haiku again?"

{j} he peered in.

"Ho—the Code of the Corps."

Toshizō was working up a draft.

The corps's law it was. On the paper at his hand were written, close-packed in this man's usual fine script, some fifty articles or so. Okita picked them out one by one with his eye and,

"A great labor,"

{j} burst out laughing.

"Hijikata-san, do you mean to have the corpsmen keep every one of these?"

"I do."

"There are fifty-some items here."

"It isn't finished yet."

"Insufferable—more than this, still?"

"No, from here I'll pare them down. I'll narrow it to five articles. The law is enough in three."

"Ah, I've heard that—at a variety hall, though. Which of the Tang generals said it, mind, you'd have to ask Yamanami-san to know."

"Hold your noise."

{j} With a firm stroke he blotted out one article in ink.

Working into the dead of night, five articles were made.

One: Thou shalt not go against the warrior's code.

One: Leaving the corps shall not be permitted.

For both, the penalty is seppuku. The third article: "None shall raise money on his own account." The fourth: "None shall take up a lawsuit (an outside one) on his own account."

The fifth article: "Private fighting shall not be permitted." Whosoever transgresses the foregoing articles shall be commanded to commit seppuku.

Further, he made detailed rules attached to these five articles.

Among them is one curious clause. This clause above all, Toshizō believed, would put steel into the corpsmen of the Shinsengumi.

"If a corpsman, not on official duty, quarrels in the town with a man from outside the corps,"

{j} so it ran.

"and crosses blades with the foe, and wounds the foe, and yet, failing to finish him, lets him escape—"

"In that case, what becomes of him?"

"Seppuku,"

{j} said Toshizō.

Okita laughed.

"That is cruel. Merely to have wounded the enemy is a feat in itself, surely. There will be times one lets a man escape. To make him cut his belly for letting one escape is too cruel."

"So he will come to fight for dear life."

"But—for all the pains of your handiwork—it may raise a snake from the grass. To the corpsman it will come to this: rather than cut the foe and let him escape, better not to cut, and to run oneself."

"That too is seppuku."

"Eh?"

"Article One: Thou shalt not go against the warrior's code."

"I see."

For the corpsman, then, once he had drawn naked steel, there was no course but to press in and in, without a glance aside, and by any means strike the enemy down.

"And if he balks at that?"

"Seppuku."

"A coward will grow afraid of the corps and want to run away."

"That, too, by Article Two, is seppuku."

This was made public.

The young hot-blooded corpsmen, reading it, seemed rather to feel a fierce grandeur, as of a waterfall beating on the bare skin; but among the older headquarters men who had joined only lately a quiet agitation could be seen. They had grown afraid.

Toshizō watched the effect with a careful eye. Sure enough, a deserter appeared.

It was the jokin Sakai Hyōgo.

An Osaka rōnin. A Shintō priest's son, he had, rare in the corps, a grounding in the National Learning, and was skilled at the classical waka.

He deserted.

Toshizō put the whole strength of the inspectorate to it, and had them search as far as Kyoto, Osaka, Sakai, and Nara.

Before long it came out that he was being sheltered at a certain shrine-house of the Sumiyoshi Myōjin in Osaka.

"Yamanami-kun, what shall we do?"

{j} Kondō consulted him.

Yamanami spoke for sparing his life. Yamanami was, in the ordinary way, on terms with Sakai Hyōgo such that he would ask him to correct his own poems.

Kondō wished to cut him down. Sakai, as a jokin, had shared in the corps's inner councils, and so knew its secrets. Let them leak to the world, and the harm would reach not only the Shinsengumi but the Protector of Kyoto himself.

"Toshi, how say you?"

"This is no matter to debate—poems this, secrets that. It would not do for the commander and the general secretary themselves to forget the Code of the Corps."

"We cut him, then?"

"Of course."

At once Okita Sōji, Harada Sanosuke, and Tōdō Heisuke, the three of them, went down to Osaka.

They called on Sakai Hyōgo at the Sumiyoshi shrine-house.

Sakai, it seems, resigned himself and drew to meet them.

Harada struck that blade down, and, avoiding a fight within the precincts, walked Sakai as far as a bamboo thicket along the Abiko road, and there handed him back his sword afresh.

In a few passes, he fell fighting.

From that time the corps grew still and grave. It was from this point that the Code of the Corps began to live within the bodies of the corpsmen.

Before long, the year changed.
