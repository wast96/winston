## Chapter 35. At a Single Stroke the Traitor Is Rooted Out, the Geese Return to Rank

Yashu's body ached as though it were being torn apart, and a terror she had never known before moved through her three souls and six spirits. Her thoughts drifted and swayed, struggling with all their strength somewhere between the three realms, unable to break free of the snare; she felt the footsteps of the "God of Death" drawing nearer and nearer...

Her end was upon her, and she was thinking: the book she had bought for him, had he read it?

Surely A-Chu had seen her heart by now?

She could, after all, have died in his place!

Yashu understood it at last: the one she loved most was A-Chu, and for the one she loved most she could die. For so long, it seemed, her love had been veiled and cheated and driven on by what she called her worldliness, her vanity, forced down countless crooked paths in its search; and only today, with death upon her, could Yashu see clearly that her love for A-Chu did not carry a single mote of dust.

Her heart's song not finished, her heart's longing not ended, her thoughts not run out, her heart's tide rising and falling, her very soul grown faint, she thought that she and A-Chu might never meet again in this life, never share the vow of a hundred years, and that this would remain a grief in the world of men.

Suddenly Yashu felt her body, suspended in midair, touched lightly by someone, and it curled and convulsed as if to defend itself. Her eyes were bound with a black cloth and her mouth stopped with a towel, so she could not resist the intrusion at all, nor could she tell whether the newcomer was friend or foe.

"It is Yang Muci," A-Ci said softly.

In that instant Yashu's heart grew calm.

Muci moved the desk beneath Yashu's feet to spare her strength, and indeed, once her feet were on solid ground, she herself grew a good deal calmer.

"Sister-in-law, do not be afraid; you will be all right..." No sooner were the words out than Muci fell silent, for he heard clearly the "tick, tick" of a timer, and knew at last why Yashu had convulsed with terror.

"Do not be tense; it is all right..." Muci soothed her in a low voice, and with a blade cut lightly through her cheongsam. He saw a timed bomb bound to her left leg; the minute hand of the timer told him there were ten minutes left before it went off.

Muci's nerves drew taut in an instant. Close upon that, he heard, coming in broken snatches from the bedroom, the sound of prayer; he heard Yang Yuhua's voice, something of "Father, Mother, Son, and Holy Spirit... amen."

Yang Yuhua was no Christian; plainly he was clutching at Buddha's feet in his need. His intent was already plain: he wanted to die but had not the courage to kill himself, and so had taken to another extreme. He had bound the explosive to Yashu's body and hung her in the middle of the study. The study was only fifteen meters from the bedroom, and the bomb's blast reached more than twenty meters, so the bedroom lay within its ruin as well; and so Yang Yuhua had chosen to hide in the bedroom and make his last confession and prayer to the divine, praying that he might rise to heaven as Yashu was blown to ash and smoke.

To put it plainly, he was using the destruction of Yashu's body to accomplish his own suicide.

The very deed of a coward!

"It is all right; there is still half an hour before it goes off. First I will take the cloth from your mouth. Steady yourself now. Good, do not cry out. Good, well done. Take a deep breath. Good, that is very good." Muci encouraged Yashu, and she did all he asked. To spare them both awkwardness, Muci did not remove the blindfold. He studied the bomb's structure with the greatest care; happily, it was a very ordinary one, three fuse-wires joined together. With great caution Muci chose a wire, and then cut it... and the timer stopped. Only now did a little cold sweat seep out at his temples and the tip of his nose. He set Yashu down on the ground, untied her blindfold, took off his own coat and wrapped it around her waist, and said, "Let us go." Then he pushed her out.

Given back her life, Yashu ran outward, limping.

She ran in wild disorder, stumbling and lurching forward; and in the very instant she neared the courtyard gate she still cast an anxious glance back over her shoulder, so that her foot caught on a stone, her balance failed, and she pitched into the two gate-leaves and burst out through them.

Yashu was like a butterfly just flown out of a gorge, its wings spread wide; her frail limbs came down hard in the dust.

Her arm had, it seemed, been hurt in the fall, and blood soaked through her sleeve. In the very moment her whole body throbbed with pain, she saw many pairs of shoes come rushing toward her; and soon she saw A-Chu's shoes, and caught the scent of her own man.

Swiftly A-Chu lifted Yashu up, and her tears, like brittle cold ice that thaws at the touch of spring, poured freely into his embrace.

"It is over now, it is over now."

"A-Ci... A-Ci saved me... he is still inside... danger..." Yashu said in broken snatches.

"Good, I know; do not worry."

Yashu's sight went black, and the wind sang in her ears.

"A-Ci is inside," A-Chu said.

"See to the Second Master's safety," Han Zhengqi ordered his men.

"Mr. Yang, it is Gao Lei."

"Squad Leader Gao, how do you do."

"Do you need help?" Gao Lei asked.

"A household matter, no more."

"Open the car door... open the car door..." someone was calling.

Yashu felt herself carried into the back seat of a car, and she went wholly slack. She knew that in this life and this world her heart had its love, her person its longing, her love its home.

There was nothing left to regret.

Yang Yuhua crouched in a dark corner, his face panic-stricken, trembling without cease. Death, for him, was now only a single step away.

He was waiting, waiting for the bomb to go off; three minutes more...

Yang Yuhua was sweating. He was very much afraid, afraid to set out alone and desolate on the road to the Yellow Springs.

He would make Yang Muchu pay a price, a bitter price; since he had resolved to end his own life, he would drag someone down with him in death.

And this woman he dragged down would, with her shattered body, sound out the music that bore him to the kingdom of heaven!

Fifty seconds, forty seconds, twenty seconds... Yang Yuhua's heart shook in time with the second hand. He felt all at once the hand of death touch the crown of his head, the very hairs of it; his chest heaved without stopping, and he murmured, "Angel, come, take me to see God."

Three seconds, two, one! With a "bang" the bedroom door was flung open. Yang Yuhua's instinctive motion was to throw up both hands to shield his head; he thought the bomb had gone off! A second later, something dawned on him: the bomb had not gone off, and he himself was still, in his misery, alive. A fear caught up with him then, and in that very second he felt how precious life was, how fragile, how worth clinging to.

He raised his eyelids, and in the blur he saw A-Ci, his son.

"God is the Lord who created the universe and all things, the very image of all that is almighty, righteous, holy, and loving. Tell me: could He receive into heaven a criminal steeped in blood, who has slain his own kin and killed his own brother?" Muci said.

"The Holy Spirit can make a man know his sin, and repent, and be born again..." Yang Yuhua muttered, his gaze wooden and dull.

"Do you know your sin, Father?" Muci's words were cold.

Yang Yuhua was silent a while, then said, "You hate me, do you not, child?"

"Yes. I hate you. Hatred, pain, resentment, all of it dammed up in my chest, do you understand? I do not even know whether I should call you Uncle, or call you Father," Muci said.

"You have called me Father for more than twenty years now; better to go on calling me Father," Yang Yuhua said.

"Father, from first to last I have never understood why you did all those things that killed off every last shred of conscience. My own father was your elder brother, my own mother your elder brother's wife; how could you, for the sake of what you called wealth and glory, kill your brother's wife and his nephew, harm your brother and burn down his house, turn traitor for a salary, and sell out your country to the enemy?"

"Child, from first to last I have loved you." Yang Yuhua answered beside the point. "Do you know, child, that dreadful night has haunted the depths of my heart, never to be driven out. Nightmares, nightmares that follow me like a shadow; every night I toss and turn and cannot sleep. I thought perhaps time could wash everything away, the sense of guilt included. Over and over I refused the memory. Toward you I was tender as... as toward a child of my own blood. I thought, my son, that so long as you lived on in health, the Yang line would have an heir, and that might lighten my sin by a share. I wanted to redeem my own soul; I wanted to wash the blood from myself."

"Your sin can neither be washed away nor redeemed," Muci said calmly.

"There was a time I thought of killing you. But every time I gave it up, the hunt for your brother included."

"You blew up his clinic."

"That was that vile creature's doing."

"But you carried out her order."

"Yes. In truth, it was our greatest blunder!"

"Why?"

"Because he is too formidable. We set up a formidable enemy against ourselves with our own hands."

"You had not reckoned on my brother's power."

"No. Never in our wildest thoughts did we imagine that a man born a lowly house-slave could be so resolute, so shrewd, and possessed of such power to strike and to kill."

"You regret it?"

"Yes," Yang Yuhua said. "And so I thought of death. Death is the best of painkillers."

"You wanted to kill yourself, and you chose to bind a bomb to an innocent woman. How can a man be shameless to such a depth?"

"When both sides have hacked each other down to the last man, no one cares whether anyone is innocent, child."

"You admit you are guilty, yet you will not repent?"

"I have nowhere to run, child."

"You could choose to give yourself up, to bear the guilt, to lay bare before the whole of society the truth of how the Yang house was destroyed and burned twenty years ago, and to drag the Japanese ambition of conquest out into the light of day. Even if it cost you your life, your soul could yet rest, and those wronged and murdered souls could sleep in peace beneath the Nine Springs."

"Impossible." Yang Yuhua's face was ashen. "Impossible, A-Ci. The 'truth' is a thing I can never face. Child, you must save me, save me, child. These twenty years I have not treated you badly, child. Can you bear to watch, with your own eyes, while I go to my ruin?"

"Impossible." Yang Muci said it with great firmness. "Impossible, Father. You must face it, face the crimes you have committed; you owe a reckoning to my murdered father, to my mother who took her own life, to my sister who was blown apart, to all the souls burned to death."

"I raised you for more than twenty years; we have been father and son for more than twenty years, A-Ci..."

"Father!" A-Ci said gravely, word by measured word. "If my own father and mother were still living, they too would have raised me, nurtured me, loved me, cherished me. It was you who stripped them of the right and the duty to love me, you who cruelly severed the bond of nature between us. Were they here, I believe they would have done it better than you."

Muci's unbending stand left Yang Yuhua in utter despair.

"Child, you know that a man in the end fears death. Only half an hour ago I gathered up my courage to go into the arms of death, and you ruined it for me... The truth is, ever since Yuzhen died I have been sunk in gloom. Your mother was very beautiful, I mean your own mother by blood; she was one of the rarest beauties in the world. However finely Eiko was carved and painted, in the end she was a 'forgery.' Tell me: after I die, could I see her again?"

Yang Yuhua's meaning was plain: he was preparing to kill himself.

"Child, help me," Yang Yuhua said.

"How am I to help?"

"Shoot me dead."

"Would you fire on me?" Muci asked in turn.

"No."

"That is my answer too," Muci said.

"Or else, give me the gun." Yang Yuhua's manner was wholly sincere.

Muci looked into Yang Yuhua's eyes, one second, two seconds, three seconds; then he drew the pistol from his person, turned his back, and held it out behind him.

Time seemed to stand still. Muci used his ears in place of his eyes, listening closely to Yang Yuhua's ragged breathing.

Three seconds, two, one!

"Muci," Yang Yuhua said. "I am sorry."

Yang Yuhua had indeed gone back on his word.

Yang Muci turned his head to look, and the black muzzle was leveled at his own chest. Muci was very much disappointed.

"My brother is right outside," Muci said. "Once the shot is fired, have you thought about your own end?"

"I did not mean to kill you, son; but you have given me a chance at a new life. Yang Muchu will not let you die at my gun. With you as my chip, perhaps I still have one road out alive."

"Yang Muchu would use even his own woman as bait; would he care about a man who has taken a thief for his father these twenty years?"

"He would. Brothers are as one's own hands and feet, a wife is like a garment; he will save you."

"Twenty years ago, was it not for the sake of one garment that you hacked off your own hands and feet with your own hands?"

Yang Yuhua's hand began to shake.

"You hold no chip at all. Hear one word of counsel from me: either come with me and give yourself up, or make an end of yourself. Beyond these there is no other road. I promise you, out of the 'kindness' of your twenty years' rearing, that whichever road you choose I will respect it; when you are dead I will wear mourning for you and bear your coffin."

"Both of those roads are roads to death!"

"From the moment a man is born, he walks a road to death. Only do not walk it too ugly."

"No, I do not want to die now!"

"That is no longer for you to decide!" Muci did not fall back but pressed forward, and with a sudden lunge his right hand gripped the hand that held the gun. Yang Yuhua paled with terror and, sweating all over, pulled the trigger.

There was no bullet in the gun at all.

Yang Yuhua's face was panic-stricken to the utmost.

Yang Muci opened his left palm, and five bullets slid from it.

"Our bond as father and son is at an end."

"A-Ci, hear me out..."

A-Ci turned and walked away, walked forward without a thought in his head; and in the same moment a crowd of men brushed past him and rushed in, and behind him came Yang Yuhua's hoarse, wrenching wail: "A-Ci, look after your sister..." "I beg you, A..." A volley of gunfire rang out. A-Ci shuddered from head to foot. Under the sunlight the whole courtyard seemed quiet and serene. A-Chu, the shattered light of the setting sun scattered over him, came walking toward him; and even from several meters off, A-Ci could feel the killing air about A-Chu.

A-Ci walked up to A-Chu, and his body went soft; he crumpled to the ground, and A-Chu caught him.

A-Ci was burning all over, and his face had no color in it.

"Spare my sister." It was the sentence A-Chu least wanted to hear, and it was the last A-Ci spoke before he lost consciousness.

The lotus lamp hanging from the ceiling gave a soft light; the whole room's fragrance of plum blossom, mingled with the smell of Chinese medicine, filled it with the warmth of a household sickroom.

Muci opened his eyes. His body felt soft and weak all over; his high fever must just have broken. He raised his head to look at the furnishings around him, and knew he was back in A-Chu's residence on Changle Road. Bracing himself, he leaned against the head of the bed, glanced at the nightstand, and there, of all things, stood a crystal iceberg.

Muci threw back the quilt and sat up in alarm.

This crystal iceberg had been a birthday present from his sister, Yang Sitong, in the year he turned fifteen. It had always sat in his own bedroom; how could it suddenly appear here in A-Chu's house?

Next he saw, laid out neatly on the nightstand, a stack of newspapers, and he reached out and took them to read. They were of many kinds: the Shen Bao, the Shanghai News, the Shen Bao Monthly, the Eastern Miscellany, the Strange Tales Gazette, the News Monthly, and more...

Muci understood that A-Chu was telling him, in another way, of the great events that had befallen Shanghai these two days. Bold, arresting headlines, one exaggerated exclamation mark after another, came crowding into his eyes.

"Truth Behind the Shanghai Financial Magnate Yang Yubo's Killing of His Wife Laid Bare"; "The Yang Bank Changes Hands, Suspected 'Palace Coup'"; "Yang Yubo Shot Dead on the Spot Resisting Arrest"; "Brothers Yang Yubo and Yang Yuhua"; "The Riddle of the Yang Family's Old Mansion Burned Twenty Years Ago"; "Japanese Spy Momokawa Keiko Takes Her Own Life in Prison"; "The New Master of the Yang House: An Inquiry"; "Whereabouts of the Yang Heiress Yang Sitong a Mystery"...

Muci's nerves drew tight. Hurriedly he pulled on his shoes and got up to go out; his body felt light and floating, his steps unsteady. The instant he pushed the door open, he heard bursts of merry laughter rising from the drawing room below.

The drawing room was ablaze with light. A-Chu was chatting away with the three of them, Young Tang, Yuechun, and Han Yu; the four of them were all wreathed in smiles, and had likely just finished supper and were talking on at their ease after the meal, with no subject at all, only circling round and round and coming always to rest on A-Chu, each with his sparkling quips, drawing out one "colorful" topic after another, so that laughter rang out on every side.

Standing on the stairs, Muci suddenly caught sight of a graceful figure in a plain-flowered cheongsam, half hidden beside a pillar. There was no need to say it; he knew it was Yashu. Yashu's own faint fragrance had steeped into the stairway, and the stairway itself had taken on an air of quiet, uncommon sweetness.

"A-Chu has swept away the murk and taken the reins again; he sweeps all before him in the stock market, the silver houses, in commerce and manufacture. His future has no limit one can set," Young Tang said.

"And it is not only in the marketplace that A-Chu is doing well; in the field of love he has done well too," Yuechun said.

"How so?" Han Yu asked.

"A-Chu has decided to take a wife," Yuechun said.

"Who?" Young Tang asked, though he knew.

"Ah, now that is a long story," Yuechun said. "That charming, well-favored gege has had her entanglements with Young Tang as well."

"He Yashu," Han Yu answered.

"A-Chu, you have always upheld the Confucian tradition. As the Confucians would have it, one marries a wife for her virtue and takes a concubine for her looks. So, A-Chu, is it virtue you are marrying, or looks?" Young Tang's question was a barbed one.

"By your reckoning, then, no woman of virtue has any beauty at all?" A-Chu protested. "You are quoting out of context."

"Young Tang, do not let him change the subject on you halfway. Ask him only this: 'a friend's wife is not to be trifled with,' is she?" Xia Yuechun prompted.

"Just so. Ordinarily he wears the face of a feudal guardian of morals; when it is his own turn, that is quite another matter," Young Tang said.

"What a monstrous wrong. And has Young Tang ever taken a wife by proper rites?" A-Chu would not have it.

"My house sent the betrothal money, her house received the wedding gifts," Young Tang laughed.

"You snatched a love away at swordpoint; that does not accord with tradition," Yuechun said.

"The Confucian tradition is for one's own self-discipline." Cornered, A-Chu made a feint of a thrust.

"Everyone heard that: he disciplines himself but does not restrain himself," Yuechun chimed in, egging things on for all he was worth. "You have not looked deep enough into his heart. Everything he said before was against his real feelings. Now, there is only one way to deal with such a man, who says one thing and means another: we invite Miss Yashu down, and make him declare himself to her face, and go down on his knees to propose."

"You have watched too much of that modern stage-play, have you not?" A-Chu laughed, giving Yuechun a shove.

"To root out the strong and prop up the weak is a duty we cannot shirk," Young Tang said, backing Yuechun's proposal.

"Just so; if Young Tang is not to press the matter, we cannot do without asking Miss Yashu down to tell us the history of your free courtship," Han Yu put in from the side.

"You are simply 'banding together to attack the odd man out,'" A-Chu cried out on purpose. "Have a care, or I shall have my revenge!"

"Ha! You still dare talk of revenge? You are a crossbow bolt at the end of its flight, and still you swagger?" Yuechun said.

"Yuechun, today of all days it is you making the waves and stirring the wind," A-Chu said.

"That is what you say? Have a care, or I shall tell a story or two..."

"There are stories to be heard?" Young Tang's interest was piqued.

"Stories in plenty: exotic foreign romance, a flight into the rainy night, love left with a jade bracelet, a chance meeting in the ballroom..."

"Director Xia, Director Xia, Master Xia, Lord Xia." A-Chu called out the titles one after another, rising with a laugh and making his bow.

"We will have none of it; Miss Yashu must come down to save you," Yuechun laughed.

"Yashu is thin-skinned; Lord Xia, be so good as to spare her," A-Chu said.

"I should say A-Chu is sure to be a hen-pecked husband one day," Young Tang cackled.

"It is not that he fears his wife; only that, loving deep and feeling strong, even steel tempered a hundred times will melt into a thread that winds round a finger," Yuechun said.

Hearing this, Muci looked silently toward Yashu.

She stood with one corner of a silk handkerchief bitten between her lips, both hands tugging at its two corners, a faint smile on her face, an endless happiness spilling from her brows and the corners of her eyes; a wisp of her spring-soul wound tenderly about the handkerchief, brimming with a deep and clinging devotion.

"Have you set a wedding date?" Han Yu asked.

"The sixth of next month," A-Chu answered.

"A-Chu, before you marry, I should like to offer you one last choice," Han Yu said.

"What do you mean?" A-Chu asked.

"A-Conghui has written from France." Han Yu drew a letter from his pocket.

A-Chu was faintly taken aback.

"New love or old, let us see how you choose," Yuechun said.

The three of them watched A-Chu's face in silence.

"A-Conghui's letter was not addressed to me, so there is no need for me to read it," A-Chu said.

"A-Conghui's letter was indeed addressed to me, but she asked me to pass it on to you. As they say: charged with another's trust, one sees the matter through."

"Charged with another's trust, one sees the matter through. Very well, give it here." A-Chu took the letter from Han Yu. "Trouble you, Young Tang, for a lighter."

As he handed over the lighter, Young Tang said, "You had best be sure. One is a fairy blossom from the Jade Pool, the other a fallen petal of the red dust."

"I am but a common man." A-Chu struck the lighter and burned the letter, and in an instant a sheet of scented notepaper turned to smoke and ash.

From the drawing room came, of all things, a scattering of applause.

"So the gentleman's mind is made up after all," Young Tang said.

"Rather say: the gentleman's heart was iron all along," Yuechun added.

"I have lost," Han Yu said, hanging his head in defeat.

"I told you not to stake so much on the bet," Yuechun said.

"Pay up, pay up." Young Tang pressed Han Yu for the money.

"Well, then! Of all things, you go and bet on this?" A-Chu, the light dawning on him, began to protest loudly. "No wonder that today one of you played the sly contrarian, one echoed and abetted, and one conjured a thing out of nothing." He flung the paper ash at Han Yu, who dodged, laughing.

Young Tang laughed until his sides ached.

"Let me put in a fair word. The letter may be false, but the heart is true. It seems Miss Yashu really is the woman fated for A-Chu," Yuechun said. "As the line goes: past loves lie in the dust and smoke..."

"May you cherish the one before your eyes," Young Tang took up the line.

"But you must treasure the jade held to your breast..." Han Yu said, pointing at A-Chu.

"I have said I am a common man, so I will cap it with the most common line of all: fair flowers, a full moon, a hall brim-full of spring." When A-Chu had done, the three of them cheered.

At that moment a hundred feelings crowded in on Yashu. To her ear, every word of A-Chu's line was long with love. From this hour, this instant, she had won not only A-Chu's love but his heart.

Love, from now, would flow in no other channel; the heart was one whole and undivided heart.

From this day, a whole life of love, never to fail each other!

Musing and musing, Yashu fell into a reverie; and as she turned her gaze slowly back, she suddenly found Muci's eyes upon her, and, her whole face flushing red, she turned and went.

"A-Chu, when do you mean to move back home properly?" Young Tang asked.

"When A-Ci is a little better; these two days his fever has been fierce," A-Chu said.

"And Rong'er? Why does he not come out?" Young Tang, ever mindful of his student, asked.

"I have sent him abroad," A-Chu said.

"When did he leave?" Young Tang was much surprised.

"The day before yesterday. I thought going abroad would do him good, to ease his mind. The boy is deep in his schemes; of all things, he had hidden a girl away in some Yunxiang Pavilion or other." A-Chu's words carried a hint of displeasure with Young Tang.

"What business is that of mine?" Young Tang was not pleased.

"I asked you to teach him something of a gentleman's bearing, and instead you taught him nothing but the boundless charms of wind and moon," A-Chu said.

"He has gone; and what of Yang Sitong?" Young Tang asked.

Muci's whole attention gathered in that one instant.

"What has Yang Sitong to do with us?" A-Chu said.

"She is Muci's sister, after all," Yuechun said. "Count it a good deed."

"Just so; she is half out of her wits. Surely you will not really pack her off to an asylum?" Young Tang said. "Be large-hearted, take her in; you will get a good name from it too."

"My thanks to you all, but I have no taste for chasing a cheap reputation," A-Chu said.

"Now that sounds like him," Young Tang said. "It is just this stubborn streak of A-Chu's that people find so tiresome."

"Where is my sister?"

All at once the drawing room fell silent, and every gaze fixed on Muci. Slowly he came down the stairs; his body was plainly still weak, and his face was ghastly.

"Where is my sister?" Muci said the same words again.

Not a sound stirred in the drawing room.

"To whom are you speaking?" A-Chu said with authority. "Do not tell me that, grown as big as this, you were never taught by Yang Yuhua who stands above and who below."

The three of them watched the two brothers' faces. Muci's lips were cracked and dry; unconsciously he pressed them together, while A-Chu deliberately settled himself in his seat and fixed his eyes on Muci.

"I said we would first be friends," Muci said.

"Friends? What kind of friend? A friend who breaks faith and forsakes honor? Or a friend to be made use of?" A-Chu asked.

"I want to know where my sister is now. Is there anything wrong in that?" Muci's voice rose; his anxiety had made him agitated.

"Who is your sister?" A-Chu's voice was low and deep.

"Take it as my begging you," Muci said.

"I could not presume," A-Chu said.

From childhood on, Muci had never borne such treatment, and least of all before Young Tang, whom he despised most. Without another word he turned to go, only to hear A-Chu's low, stern rebuke. "Where do you think you are going?"

"Home," Muci said.

No sooner had Muci said the two words "going home" than Young Tang's mocking laugh came from behind his back.

"I forgot to tell you: I have bought the Yang mansion," A-Chu said, very evenly. "It is being redecorated just now; go there, and you would not get in the door."

Muci halted, and did not go on.

The scene had reached an utter deadlock. Young Tang, presuming on his old acquaintance with Muci, came over to smooth things down. "Brothers are like geese in flight; whatever there is to say, sit down and say it properly. Do not go point-of-needle against tip-of-wheat-awl... You, as the younger brother, ought to know that an elder brother is as a father. And besides, you are penniless now; you have just lost a rich father, and here comes a millionaire elder brother, so you are a lucky man. Would you really refuse to be the young master ready-made, and go and be a beggar instead?" It was truly a needle wrapped in silk.

Muci laughed coldly. "A beggar is a hundred times better than an addict of the pipe."

As they say: strike a man but not his face, curse a man but do not tear open his sore. Young Tang loathed being called an "addict of the pipe," the more so when he was mocked before his own friends.

"What did you say?" Young Tang roared in fury. "You think yourself so fine? Taking a thief for a father..."

"Let it go, Young Tang." Han Yu was pleading. "The man is with the Detective Division; he has his privileges."

"The affairs of my Yang house are no matter for you to screech like owls and howl like wolves over!" Muci said.

"Who is the owl? Who is the wolf?" A-Chu demanded coldly.

Muci said nothing.

"I am asking you a question. Who is the owl? Who is the wolf?" A-Chu waited quietly. "Point them out to me."

His fever only just broken, anxious at heart and cold in his body, hounded by A-Chu's cool, unhurried rebukes, Muci flushed red to the roots of his ears with rage; his knees felt weak beneath him, and a cold sweat he could not stop poured out of him.

"A-Chu, let it go." Yuechun spoke up. "Muci is only bound close to his sister; why press him until it grieves him?"

"It is not that I would deny you face, Yuechun," A-Chu said. "Every person in this room is a guest I, Yang Muchu, have invited, yourself included. A-Ci is my brother; he may show no respect to me, but he cannot fail to respect my friends." A-Chu turned to Muci and said, "Now I will tell you: of the four people present here, myself included, three of them once performed surgery on you and saved your life, and one of them has taken in the very 'sister,' Yang Sitong, of whom you speak. After your house fell, every friend your sister had shrank from the sight of her; only Young Tang drove out to bring her to stay at the Tang house, where she now lives together with Young Tang's own sister."

The heart that had been lodged in Muci's throat dropped, at last, in an instant.

"Now I give you two choices. First: apologize at once. Second: leave my house, and from this day be as a stranger to me."

Muci was greatly embarrassed. Still, with Sitong accounted for, his mind was set at ease; and looking round, he saw that everyone seated there had some tie of his own with him, so why should he stubbornly pit himself against A-Chu, who was, after all, his own elder brother? And so he turned back and stepped up to Young Tang.

"I am sorry, Young Tang," Muci said.

Young Tang gave a "hmph," which passed for an answer.

"Come." Yuechun came over of his own accord and pulled Muci by the arm, taking up a teacup from the tea table and putting it into his hand. "Two brothers after all; Young Tang has the right of it, brothers are like geese in flight. Come, offer your elder brother a cup of tea and call him big brother; where is the harm?"

Muci was all but pushed before A-Chu by Yuechun. Mechanically he held out the teacup; he said nothing, and A-Chu made no move to take it, and the scene turned suddenly cold.

As though with a fishbone caught in his throat, Muci said, most awkwardly, "Big brother, drink your tea."

A-Chu had never been a man who lorded it over others; seeing Muci hold himself so humbly before his friends, he felt, on the contrary, a pang in his heart. He said nothing, but with a gentle motion took the teacup from Muci's hand, and let the matter come to its end.

"There now; from this day let the brothers be at peace and harbor no grudge," Yuechun said.

"The gongs and drums were plainly beaten for a 'martial scene,' and Doctor Xia has turned it into a 'civil' one, cheating us of a fine show," Young Tang said.

The company caught his meaning, and all broke into smiles.

The spring light broke through the ice, and all things came back to life.

On the flowered street of Yuyuan Road in Shanghai, the newly repaired Yang mansion threw open its gates in due form. The servants stood in a neat row, drawn up to welcome the coming of the new master.

When the Western clock in the foreign-style house struck nine, six black cars, nose to tail in orderly line, drove slowly through the mansion gate.

At that moment a fine, unbroken rain began to fall from the sky, its silken threads and gusts brushing over the pear-blossom courtyard, making it all the more secluded and still.

The servants came forward one after another to open the car doors and hold umbrellas over the masters. Yang Muchu and Yang Muci, in new black Western suits, stepped down from the cars with solemn faces.

Close behind came the Han father and son, Deputy Chief of the Shanghai Police Bureau Han Zhengqi and Chief of the Anti-Smuggling Office of the Shanghai Customs Han Yu; the Chief of the Detective Division of the Central Shanghai Garrison Command, Du Luning, and his intelligence-section head Yu Xiaojiang; the director of Chunhe Hospital, Xia Yuechun; the senior detective of the French Concession police and chief of the Hongmen of the rivers and lakes, Huang Sanyuan; and the Tang brother and sister of a well-known Shanghai family.

Several black umbrellas covered these figures of note from both the lawful and the underworld sides of the Shanghai waterfront as they moved slowly toward the Yang garden.

The pear trees of the Yang garden were all in flower, the lawn washed by rain, the air especially fresh.

Beneath the pear trees lay two spades bound with black silk. Yang Muchu and Yang Muci, one at the left and one at the right, plied the spades and began to loosen and turn the earth. The fine rain sprinkled over the faces of the two men, and the soil the spades flung aside scattered onto the fresh green grass; and in less than two hours, from the loosened earth there appeared white bones, stark and pale...

The wronged spirit whose grievance had gone unavenged for twenty years saw the light of day again.

Tang Shaoqi, then a reporter for the Shanghai News, raised the camera he had made ready and caught this instant.

In 1933, on the ninth day of the first month, the Shanghai News ran a great headline, "The Wrong Done to Yang Yubo Redressed," with photographs of the two Yang brothers raising the grave and bearing the remains at Ciyun Temple and in the Yang garden.

In 1933, on the sixth day of the second month, the Yang mansion was hung with lanterns and colored silks, all festive joy, as Yang Muchu held a grand wedding banquet at the Park Hotel in Shanghai and was joined to Yashu in a union of a hundred years.

That same month, the cover of the Eastern Miscellany carried the wedding photograph of Yang Muchu and He Yashu.

People change all too fast.

So Rong Sheng thought.

He set down the copy of the Eastern Miscellany in his hands. On its cover He Yashu wore a look of sweet, contented happiness, and A-Chu's bearing grew ever more refined and lordly, a heroic spirit springing from between his brows, the old gentleness and humility swept clean away, and a certain worldly depth added in its place.

Perhaps this was A-Chu's true face all along.

Rong Sheng had lived his days steeped in the fragrance of books. Since A-Chu's departure, First Madam had sent the maidservant Hong'er to Rong Sheng's side; and though Hong'er served him with all her heart, she could hardly hold much conversation with a young master. Rong Sheng's life had been a fine and delicate thing, but now in the study the tea was not quite tea and the ink not quite ink, and not one thing was as he wished it; he felt his joys few and his sorrows many. Yet his was a gloomy nature, slow to break out, and mostly he swallowed things down, so that as the days and months went by no small store of discontent lay banked within him.

First Madam had set her heart on finding her son a new wife. Rong Sheng had at first been against it, but of late his mind had shifted; he thought that perhaps, with a companion beside him who knew books and understood reason, his life need not be so dreary and monotonous.

And so, among a stack of matchmaking photographs First Madam brought him, he chose one: a pure, fresh girl he had once met by chance in a bookstore, Mingxuan.

In Rong Sheng's dim and blurry memory, there hovered about Mingxuan the faint shadow of his late wife; those feelings slowly fading from him were stirred now by a single ripple of spring across his ashen heart.

When First Madam and Third Madam saw the photograph Rong Sheng had chosen, they were both a little astonished.

"She seems rather young in years, and rather frail of body," First Madam said.

"Yes; this girl is too thin and small..." Third Madam echoed.

"That is just what I said," Lishui said. "But my cousin says..."

"What does Sheng'er say?" First Madam asked.

"He says: she is simple," Lishui answered.

"Simple?" First Madam smiled. "Then let it be her."

"But Aunt, though this girl is of a good family, she is a child of a concubine. They say her mother's name is Chen, and she came from a family of flower-sellers. Would she truly be a match for my cousin..."

"What of it that she is a concubine's child? What of it?" Third Madam would hear no more. "I tell you, Miss Lishui, and it is no idle boast: a concubine's daughter can be a clever thing indeed."

Lishui's face grew hot.

Just then Hong'er came in with a small bundle. "First Madam, the young master asked me to hand you this; he says it is to be sent over together with the betrothal gifts."

First Madam opened it and looked: a blue embroidered book-bag and an English book. Somewhat puzzled, First Madam asked Lishui what book this was.

Lishui said, "It is Alice's Adventures in Wonderland."

"Alice, Alice?" First Madam did not understand.

"No wonder the eldest young master went and chose himself a schoolgirl of all people; so there is a story behind it," Third Madam laughed.

In September 1933, the Shanghai News carried the news that "Young Master Rong, heir to the Rong Pharmaceutical Company, weds Miss Mingxuan, daughter of the Chen family flower-house."

That same month, Yang Muci sent Sitong to Edo in Japan for treatment.

In December of the same year, He Yashu was safely delivered of a pair of twin boys at Chunhe Hospital. A-Chu was overjoyed beyond measure, and the children were named Aizhong and Aihua.

Four years later...

On August 13, 1937, Japanese forces launched a great assault on Shanghai, boasting that they would "conquer China in three months."

On August 14 that same year, the National Government issued its "Statement of Self-Defense and Resistance."

On September 22 that same year, the Central News Agency of the Nationalist Party issued the "Declaration of the Central Committee of the Chinese Communist Party on the Announcement of Cooperation Between the Nationalists and Communists." The Second United Front had formally begun.

On November 12, 1937, the great city of Shanghai fell beneath the iron heel of the Japanese invader.

"The soldiers everywhere, hearing the call of righteousness, hasten to the peril; an order given at dawn is answered by dusk. At the front they build ramparts with bodies of flesh and blood, holding with death and never retreating; their positions turn to ash, yet the army's will stays firm as iron and stone. The valor of those who storm the breach, the fierceness of those who die in the cause, are enough indeed to show forth the spirit of a nation's independence, and to lay the foundation of China's revival."

Yang Muchu and Yang Muci listened in silence to the wireless broadcast, while the shrill of sirens and the roar of aircraft mingled with the sound of bombs and tore the whole city apart.

Yang Muci stood at the window, his face stern. Fierce battles yet to come, a battlefield with no smoke of powder, a shifting of fortunes past all fathoming, were about to draw back their curtain...

(The End)
