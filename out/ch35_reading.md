## Chapter 35

I suddenly felt that the human heart is perhaps a snob's: in any gathering, the one who wins out always gains the support of many. The world's winners may still often meet with people's envy, but that only proves the winner has not yet wholly won; once the victory is complete, those who most envied the winner all become the winner's most fervent supporters.

Tonight Bai Ping was truly blazing with radiance: brilliance beyond all compare heaped upon her face, checks and cash beyond all counting heaped before her, gazes of every kind fixed upon her—and in these gazes was admiration and respect, with no envy or hatred that I could see; yet people went on giving her money.

I stood coolly at the side and observed; Bai Ping's face truly glittered with every kind of splendor. This splendor was not the least bit pride, nor complacency; it was a kind of victory, a strange beauty, a gladness, an unspeakable sweetness. This splendor called forth in everyone respect and love for her, as though all were willing to kneel before her. People's talk seemed to count it a glory to have lost the most to Bai Ping, though on her face there was still a look of vexation. This air made me feel that my not having offered money to Bai Ping was a disgrace, and I took out money and said:

"Bai Ping, now it's my turn to make you an offering."

I laid the money down, and Bai Ping returned me a smile. Mrs. Manfield was now arranging Bai Ping's notes for her and managing the payments; she said:

"Tonight's Bai Ping is not one you can set yourself against."

Sure enough, I lost; but this did not add to the splendor on Bai Ping's face, and when her strangely shining eyes looked at me once, they made me feel instead an unspeakable menace, as though she had seen through me as Mei Yingzi's helper, setting myself against her tonight. This made me think of the beautiful figure of Mei Yingzi I had just seen in the garden, and how pitiful a contrast it made with Bai Ping.

Everyone in the whole room seemed to exist for Bai Ping, all the light in the whole room seemed to blaze for Bai Ping, all the furnishings of the whole room seemed to be arranged for Bai Ping; and the people in the whole room, and the money the people in the whole room held in keeping, all seemed to be under Bai Ping's command; while Mei Yingzi, wandering in the bleak, dim garden, was for all the world a person cast off by the world, with not a living creature heeding her—only I, from a hidden corner of the window, stealing a look at her. Was it then Helen's not coming that had brought this about? And this was the failure of my work. Needless to say, Bai Ping was our enemy, and Mei Yingzi my comrade. Even by my temperament, which forever pities the weak, there seemed for an instant to rise in my breast a kind of hatred—as though my winning at the gaming table could somehow redeem the failure of our work. Calmly I laid a still larger stake; but again I lost! I lost again and again!

Suddenly I remembered that I was here to wait for Miko and Mei Yingzi, and that Mei Yingzi's coming up would be an appearance the more eclipsed: in this instant Bai Ping had become fierce sunlight, and Mei Yingzi's appearance would be the pale moon at dusk, with no one to heed her any longer—for I saw Umetake behind Bai Ping, waiting only for a single glance from her to count it an honor. I could be sure that when Mei Yingzi came up, even he would neglect her to the point of discourtesy; so now it seemed there was only I, and I ought to stop her early from coming up. Just as I was about to break off gambling and go downstairs, or to the door, to meet Mei Yingzi, someone at my side suddenly said:

"Well? Won't you dance with me?"

It was Miko, whose coy, guileless manner lightened the burden on my soul; but I grew anxious at once that Mei Yingzi might be behind her, and I looked back through the crowd and found she was not there, and my heart was much eased. I said:

"You see—not finding you has cost me a good deal of money."

So saying, I squeezed out of the crowd, and we hurried downstairs. Mei Yingzi was already on the arm of a young Japanese officer, who seemed not well acquainted with her and danced with great gravity and courtesy. As I danced with Miko, I stole glances at Mei Yingzi's look: it was cool and resolute, without the anxious, doubting, uneasy air of before; she did not smile, did not talk, and when she saw me she did not greet me, but only danced serenely—as though she had a plan fully formed, and as though her mind were elsewhere.

The music stopped, and only then did Mei Yingzi greet me, saying very coolly:

"Did you win?"

"Lost." So saying I walked at her side; she went straight toward the sofa on which her handbag lay, and said:

"What time is it?"

I looked at my watch and said:

"Twenty to three."

"…" She let out a faint, weary sigh, and said nothing.

Walking to the sofa and sitting down, she looked at the trolley table by the distant window and said: "Would you get me a glass of wine?"

"Kolisa?"

"Brandy," she said.

So I walked back again, poured a small glass at the trolley table by the window, and returned.

Mei Yingzi took the wine, drank a mouthful, leaned lightly against the sofa, and sighed faintly again. I said:

"Tired?"

"…" She nodded; then suddenly the music struck up and people all rose to dance. She looked and saw no one near, braced herself, and said in a steady, low tone:

"Now, all the responsibility rests on you."

"You mean I can be of help to you?" I sat down beside her.

"Yes, you must bear this responsibility." She did not look at me, and said gravely: "The procedure is exactly the same as last time. It is now in Bai Ping's handbag, I think. Contrive to see her home, and you must get hold of it in the car."

"In the car?" I asked, after a moment's thought.

"You'll have no other chance."

"But—"

"By and by, when the wine is being drunk, you must make her vomit, and then take the chance to see her home." So saying she took her handbag from behind her, drew out a pale-violet handkerchief, and dabbed her nose; I caught her peculiar fragrance. Then she laid her hand down, just beside mine, and said:

"This is the medicine."

The back of my hand touched her soft handkerchief, and without a thought I turned my palm to receive it—but what I received was a paper packet, and my heart shuddered suddenly. With a keen instinct I thought this was poison, and felt, bewilderingly, an unspeakable terror. I strove hard to master my feelings, and asked calmly:

"Medicine?"

"To make her vomit."

But somehow I could not wholly believe these words of Mei Yingzi's. If the work required it, I believed Mei Yingzi would indeed use so deadly a hand; and since I did not understand her work, there was no way to prove this might not be something the work required.

She sat there like a stone statue, her eyes gazing at emptiness, not a trace of expression on her face; and this made me think of the heroine of Ghost Love. I suddenly saw that in that gaze there lurked a killing intent, as if it were shown to me that, even were it not the work's requirement, Mei Yingzi would use this deadly hand out of jealousy of Bai Ping. Holding that paper packet, my hand began to tremble; and so I gripped it hard and said resolutely:

"Is it that you fear I'll be afraid, and so say it is only to make her vomit?"

"Do you think I want to make a puppet of you?"

"Mei Yingzi," I said, "apart from the work, we are friends; in all the work you give me, I hope to understand its meaning and its effect."

"Trust me," she said. "This is no time for me to discuss philosophy with you."

"It's reliable?" I asked.

"Set your mind at ease," she said. "For criminal business I have no need of you."

"May I look into your eyes?"

She turned her head, and in her resolute gaze I saw weariness and tenderness; she lowered her eyes and said quietly:

"After many others have drunk with her, then you go and toast her."

"And then, when she vomits, I see her home."

"Yes," she said. "You and Mrs. Manfield, the two of you, would be best—so that no Japanese officer wants to go along too."

Having said this she stood up, and said serenely:

"Will you dance with me?"

I did not answer, but stood up, put the medicine packet in my pocket, and danced with her in silence.

"Are you a coward?" she said.

"Yes—I am afraid this is more than vomiting."

"If I am lying," she said, "you can betray me at any time."

"I ought to trust you, Mei Yingzi," I said. "Because I am forever loyally obedient to loyalty."

Between us was silence again, and when the music stopped she said:

"Once you have the thing, go at once to the ballroom of the Standford and wait for me; now dance me upstairs."

And so, with a beating heart, I walked out of the ballroom with her and up the stairs. The clamor of voices, the blaze of light in the gaming room, now reached my ears and eyes again, and my heart seemed to shudder the more.

Pushing in through the glass door, I saw Bai Ping standing on the table, glass in hand, everyone crowded round her raising their glasses and cheering. The moment Mei Yingzi went in she left me; someone came up at once to tell her Bai Ping had won a great victory. She took two glasses of wine from the drinks table and squeezed to the edge of the table; someone helped her onto a chair, and she said:

"Bai Ping, please accept this glass from me."

Bai Ping took her glass, and Mei Yingzi said:

"Today let us all name Bai Ping our Queen."

Everyone cheered and drained their glasses, and I drained mine too. Just then someone cried:

"Long live our Queen."

Everyone cried it out, and at that very moment I filled a glass at the drinks table, with one hand tore off the corner of the paper packet in my pocket, and, pretending to hold the glass in both hands, cast the powder into it; then I took up another glass besides. I felt my heart pounding, my cheeks twitching, my hands trembling; but still I forced it all down, and walked to the table's edge. Bai Ping was just about to come down from the table, and I said calmly:

"Wait a moment."

Bai Ping raised her head and looked at me. I said again:

"For your victory, Bai Ping, I hope I may share a little of your glory; I drink your health."

Bai Ping took my glass with a smile like a lily just opening, and this truly filled me with shame and remorse. My heart no longer pounded, my heart no longer trembled; for an instant I hated my hand—and I could no longer take it back. She raised the glass, clinked it with mine, and said:

"I would give all today's glory in exchange for your blessing."

I dared not look her in the eye; I screened my sight with my glass, and drained it. I saw her hand the empty glass to someone, and then she came down from my arm. I had to attend upon the change in her, so I did not leave her, and I said:

"You're too excited! You need to rest."

She said nothing, seeming a little dizzy in the head, and leaned on me to a sofa. I said:

"You're a little drunk."

Still she said nothing, but went straight for the sofa, and at last sat down without a sound; I sat down beside her. Just then Arita brought her handbag over and set it beside her; Bai Ping very naturally moved it onto her own person. Arita asked:

"Tired?"

"Dizzy," said Bai Ping with a smile. But my heart was shaken as though by an electric shock; before my eyes rose Mei Yingzi's stone-statue expression, her eyes gazing at emptiness, her glinting look full of killing intent. Could Bai Ping already be poisoned? And the one who had administered the poison was I.

Bai Ping bore up smiling, but somewhat rigidly, and I was gripped by a nameless dread. I looked across at Mei Yingzi; she was over there laughing uproariously with the soldiers, seeming not to see my anxiety at all. In an instant all my vexation and resentment turned into little deer, leaping and darting about in my heart, and I mastered myself. When I turned to tend Bai Ping again, her face had gone white, and she leaned on the sofa unwilling to stir. Arita comforted her at her side, but Bai Ping said:

"Please let me rest quietly for a while." And then she directed me: "Pour me a glass of water."

When I came back with cold boiled water, Arita had already walked off. Bai Ping sat there as still as if half asleep, but I saw a faint twitching in her fingers. Anxious and afraid, I hurriedly brought the cold water to her lips, and she drank it off at a draught; I set down the glass and took her twitching hand—and in an instant I almost cried out, for the hand was damp and cold and clammy, like two blocks of melting ice. I gripped it hard, using my reason to hold down a parched anguish that left me unable to breathe, and only then did I find my words. I said:

"Bai Ping, I'm afraid a serious illness is coming on—quick, to the hospital."

"…" A slight nod; her eyes closed.

Her hand seemed to run with cold sweat all the while, and for an instant I could not help bowing my head to look—but I saw my own hand, the hand that had given her the poison, and in my remorse a hidden hatred rose at once toward Mei Yingzi! In so perilous a situation, Mei Yingzi had already taken Bai Ping's place, carousing among that crowd of soldiers; laughter and cheers commanded the whole air. Now, in Bai Ping, I felt the desolation of the Lady of the Camellias: fifteen minutes before, how many people had cheered her; now, the moment Bai Ping could no longer furnish her gladness and smiles for others' amusement, people had put her entirely out of mind. My tears could not be held back and fell; but the tears dropping on my hand could not wash the sin from it. With my guilty hand I wiped my tears dry, and the anger within me gathered in my two eyes, and I called out toward the crowd across the room:

"Mrs. Manfield."

Mrs. Manfield came out of the crowd, and Mei Yingzi too came over, feigning surprise. People began to quiet, turning their attention to our part of the room, seeming concerned, and seeming too to blame me for cutting short their high spirits. Someone asked:

"What is it?"

"She must have got drunk." Mei Yingzi pushed forward, went to Bai Ping's side, and, feigning comfort, took her hand and felt her forehead, then said to me: "You had better see her home quickly."

Mrs. Manfield was a warm-hearted soul, and by now she too had come to Bai Ping's side; so I asked her:

"Will you help me see her home?"

"Yes, yes," she said.

Not one Japanese soldier came to pay court to her—this ought to have been our victory, but I felt hatred, for I saw clearly now what this crowd's usual ardor was worth. All the countless flatteries, the lavish praise, were entirely because of Bai Ping's youth and beauty, her wit and her gaiety; and in this instant, when Bai Ping withered like a flower in the fire, not one person came to cherish her any longer. Arita came over with feigned solicitude and said to me:

"Get her off to rest early, quick."

I paid him no heed, but supported Bai Ping toward the door. At the door Umetake shook my hand, and patted Bai Ping's shoulder:

"I'm so sorry, so sorry," he said.

"Let us drink a glass to wish our Queen good night." Mei Yingzi called out again from behind. I did not so much as turn my head; Mrs. Manfield answered for me:

"Good night, everyone."

So she helped support Bai Ping down the stairs, and Umetake accompanied us to the cloakroom to fetch our coats, and saw us all the way to our door.

"Good night," he said politely.

"Good night, and thank you for your hospitality."

"I'm so sorry."

"Good night."

"Good night."
