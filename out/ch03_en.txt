Chapter 3. Giving Myself to the Revolution
Doted on by my parents from childhood, how did I come suddenly to leave home and give myself to revolution? At the time I had as yet no very clear understanding of the great principles of revolution; my motive was simply to oppose the marriage my family had arranged. Loving as my parents were to me, they still could not shake off the force of old habit. Misled by a matchmaker's words, they had betrothed me while I was still a child to a pampered, hot-tempered young master four years my elder, who had not yet finished university and had already taken to smoking opium. I had heard of this while I was studying at the Zhounan Girls' School. When I got home I protested to my parents that I would not marry that man. But my parents felt that, on the one hand, for the matchmaker's sake, and on the other, because the man's parents had had friendly dealings with our family, the betrothal once made was awkward to break off. So they could only console me again and again and have me marry into the family first and see. Unable to disobey my parents, in 1923 I went through the wedding ceremony with him and was formally married. Because it was the eldest daughter of railway section head Huang who was marrying, a whole railway carriage was specially reserved, and my parents prepared me a rich dowry. On the surface it looked most splendid, but after I married in I truly could not bear his bad habits: he not only smoked opium but beat and cursed the maidservants at will. Though he did not yet dare to beat or curse me, I felt that, married to such a husband, would not my whole life be ruined? So I was wretched, gloomy and unhappy. Later, taking the chance when my father came to visit me, I secretly wrote a little note and slipped it to him, begging him to take me home whatever else he did. In 1925 my father, on the pretext that my mother was ill, had my uncle bring me home. From then on I never went back to that family. Just at this time came the high tide of the great revolution of the Northern Expedition, with its advocacy of women's liberation. My father deeply revered Qiu Jin, that martyr of the women's revolution—Qiu Jin too, out of dissatisfaction with an arranged marriage, had gone alone to study in Japan, joined revolutionary bodies such as the Tongmenghui and the Guangfuhui, and thrown herself into the bourgeois-democratic revolution against the Qing. With such a young and promising martyr as my model, I rose up to resist the feudal marriage my family had arranged. Fortunately my parents were enlightened and supported my walking out of the family to join the revolution.
In the spring of 1926, Guangdong was already preparing to wage the Northern Expedition, and the Canton–Hankou Railway union too gave the Expedition its support and backing. My cousin Pan Bingwen often brought to our home the articles, leaflets, and bulletins with which the railway union publicized the Kuomintang's Second National Congress and the preparations for the Northern Expedition. From them I learned that the workers' movement in Guangzhou was in full blaze, and that a National Peasant Movement Institute had been opened there to train the backbone leaders of the peasant movement in various places; the women's movement in Guangzhou, in particular, led by Song Qingling and by Liao Zhongkai's wife He Xiangning, deeply inspired and encouraged me. My father and I studied together Mr. Sun Yat-sen's doctrine of the New Three Principles of the People and his "Testament": "I know deeply that, to attain the goal of the revolution, one must rouse the masses and unite with those nations of the world that treat us as equals, to struggle in common." Mr. Sun Yat-sen's famous three great policies—"alliance with Russia, alliance with the Communists, assistance to the peasants and workers"—played a huge part in driving the rising high tide of the great revolution of the Northern Expedition. My father held that Mr. Sun Yat-sen's course, from leading the old-democratic revolution in the days of the Tongmenghui to putting forward the new Three Principles of the People in his last years and realizing Kuomintang–Communist cooperation, was a great historical advance. He warmly welcomed and supported the Northern Expedition, believing it could realize Mr. Sun Yat-sen's ideal of a national revolution.
My thirst for knowledge was intense, and I was eager, through taking part in the revolutionary movement, to throw off the bondage of clan power and husband power that the feudal arranged marriage laid on me, and to win my own liberation. Just then, learning from a railway-union leaflet that the women workers of the British-American Tobacco Company in Hankou were on a great strike, I crossed the Yangtze from Wuchang, with my father's tacit consent and support and a little money, to go to the British-American Tobacco Company in Hankou and support the women workers in their strike.
With much difficulty I found the union people. They asked who I was; I said I was a student and now also a housewife, and I changed my own name to Mulan. I said that, having read their leaflet, I thought the demands and terms the tobacco company's women workers had put forward entirely reasonable, and was resolved to walk out of my family to support their strike. In the midst of this talk I met Comrade Li Zifen, who was leading the strike there, head of the organization department of the Hubei Provincial Committee of the Chinese Communist Youth League. He received me and talked with me, asking about my background and history, all of which I told him truthfully. He said: "Welcome to the revolution. Let me introduce you to a leader of the women's movement, Comrade Du Yunzhang" (Comrade Zhang Yun, who after the Third Plenary Session of the Party's 11th Central Committee served as deputy secretary of the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection). Du Yunzhang was then a Party member and head of the women's department of the Wuhan Special Municipal Party Headquarters of the Chinese Kuomintang. When she received me she said: "Welcome to the revolution. First cut off that long hair of yours!" I was delighted—was this not just like those who joined the 1911 Revolution first cutting off their queues?—and hurriedly said, "Yes!" and went at once to a barber's to cut off my long hair. When I got home I excitedly reported it all to my parents, and they too showed support for what I had done, saying I had met a fine woman leader, which was a rare piece of luck. And so I left home and moved into the guesthouse shared by the women's department and the general labor union, living in a collective dormitory with the comrades.
Before long—about late June—Comrade Li Zifen talked with me several times, had me read the constitution of the Chinese Communist Youth League, and asked whether I wished to join it. I said that, having only just entered revolutionary work, I very much needed the help of leadership and organization, which would be of decisive significance in raising my ideological understanding and political consciousness, and that I was very willing to join the League. And so, on Comrade Li Zifen's recommendation, I joined. It was then the high tide of the great revolution, and not I alone but a good many revolutionary young men and women, like me, joined the Youth League around this time. We had all become comrades, closer, it seemed, than blood brothers and sisters, and I felt intensely happy. The leap forward in my revolutionary consciousness was inseparable from the enlightenment and guidance that Comrades Du Yunzhang and Li Zifen gave me. After joining the League, I became Comrade Du Yunzhang's secretary, and she introduced me to Comrade Wan Xiyan, head of the propaganda department of the Special Municipal Party Headquarters. Before the Northern Expedition army reached Wuhan, Wan Xiyan, together with Comrade Dong Biwu, ran the *Chuguang Daily* and guided other progressive bodies in the propaganda and organizational work of the national revolution. Comrade Dong Biwu was then the Chinese Communist Party's representative to the Kuomintang Central at Wuhan and concurrently head of the *Republican Daily*—a respected revolutionary pioneer and a kindly, approachable leader. Wan Xiyan was the paper's editor-in-chief and concurrently head of the municipal party's propaganda department. Like Comrades Li Zifen and Du Yunzhang, he gave me great help.
On 9 July the Northern Expedition formally began. At the time the warlord Wu Peifu held Hubei and Hunan, and the warlord Sun Chuanfang was entrenched across Jiangxi, Fujian, Anhui, Zhejiang, and Jiangsu. The National Revolutionary Army, about one hundred thousand strong, set out from Guangdong in three columns: one column, the Fourth, Seventh, and Eighth Armies, attacked Hunan and Hubei, with the Fourth Army's Ye Ting Independent Regiment as the advance guard, entering Changsha in July, routing Wu Peifu's main force in August in the two battles of Tingsiqiao and Heshengqiao in Xianning, Hubei, and taking Wuchang on 10 October; the second column, the Second, Third, and Sixth Armies, attacked Jiangxi, occupying Nanchang and Jiujiang in November and wiping out Sun Chuanfang's main force; the third column, the First Army, marched into Fujian and Zhejiang, and, the enemy being split from within, occupied both provinces in December.
As the Northern Expedition army pressed nearer and nearer to Wuhan, many leaders of the Chinese Communist Party and of the Kuomintang left wing came to Wuhan one after another, and Wuhan was about to replace Guangzhou as the center of the revolution. Chen Tanqiu and Dong Biwu, who had taken part in the Party's First Congress in 1921, had both earlier been teachers at a women's normal school in Hubei, and many of their students had been inspired and educated by them to join the revolution. One of them was Comrade Yuan Puzhi, who died in 1994; she was then head of the women's department of the Kuomintang's Hubei Provincial Party Headquarters (the Hubei Provincial Headquarters and the Wuhan Special Municipal Headquarters ranked side by side, both directly under the provisional Kuomintang–Communist Central leadership), and had taken part in the Kuomintang's First National Congress held in Guangzhou in 1924, where she came to know Comrades Wan Xiyan and He Chang. Elder Sister Yuan lived in the home of Elder Sister Deng Yingchao. Deng was then secretary to He Xiangning, head of the Kuomintang Central's women's department. He Xiangning was the wife of the Kuomintang left-wing leader Mr. Liao Zhongkai; people generally addressed her respectfully as Teacher Liao's Wife, or Madame Liao, while we women cadres all called her "Department Head He." He Xiangning, together with Liao Zhongkai, firmly supported the national revolution led by Mr. Sun Yat-sen, and put great effort into helping Mr. Sun raise funds for revolutionary activities and set up the Whampoa Military Academy. Mr. Liao also served as party representative of the Whampoa Military Academy and general party representative of the National Revolutionary Army, so the Whampoa cadets all addressed He Xiangning respectfully as Teacher Liao's Wife.
While I was secretary in the women's department of the Wuhan Special Municipal Party Headquarters, Du Yunzhang taught me personally, step by step, how to draft slogans and how to draw up the charter of the preparatory committee of the women's association. Having a fair cultural grounding, I picked up these new things at once, and so, following her, I went about the mass movement and the work of preparing to set up the women's association. Because all the preparatory work had been fully done beforehand, the moment the Northern Expedition army reached Wuhan, the women's association, the general labor union, the student union, the teachers' federation, the peasant association, the chamber of commerce, and the other mass bodies all hung out their signboards and declared themselves openly established. In those three or four months I came just in time for all the work of welcoming the Northern Expedition army, and in revolutionary practice this laid the foundation for the raising of my ideological understanding.
In my spare time from work I also often attended League and Party classes, from seven to nine in the evening, two hours each time. Comrade Chen Tanqiu lectured us on Party history and the revolutionary situation, on why Communists and Youth League members should join the Kuomintang and carry out Kuomintang–Communist cooperation. He began from the revolutionary friendship formed between Comrade Li Dazhao and Mr. Sun Yat-sen: at the time, over Mr. Sun's proposed three great policies of "alliance with Russia, alliance with the Communists, assistance to the peasants and workers," a great dispute had arisen over the different wording of a single character. The right wing within the Kuomintang did not favor the wording "ally with" (lian) the Communists and demanded it be changed to "admit" (rong) the Communists. This difference of one character, though a hair's breadth, missed by a thousand li. "Alliance with the Communists" showed that the two parties cooperated as equals, whereas "admitting the Communists" made the Communist Party subordinate to the Kuomintang and stripped it of its independence. Because it bore on the very essence of Kuomintang–Communist cooperation, it was a thing that had to be fought out. As the representative of the Chinese Communist Party, Comrade Li Dazhao of course had to hold to principle and argue his case. Mr. Sun Yat-sen was most enlightened and held that "alliance with the Communists" must still be the wording, and that the wanton change of a single character must not be allowed to affect the equal cooperation of the two parties. To those Kuomintang rightists who stubbornly opposed cooperation with the Communist Party, he once said angrily: "If you all oppose cooperating with the Communist Party, then I, as leader of the Kuomintang, will go and join the Communist Party myself—how would you like that?!" Only thus was the rightists' mistaken position put down. This shows Mr. Sun's great spirit of selflessness. It is a pity he died too early, in 1925—a huge loss to the Chinese revolution. Had Mr. Sun still been alive, there would not have been, in 1927, at the very time of the first Kuomintang–Communist cooperation and the successive victories of the Northern Expedition, the Kuomintang right's "party purge" against the Communists, and the consequent split of the two parties and the failure of the vigorous great revolution. The Kuomintang left, headed by Song Qingling and He Xiangning, always carried on Mr. Sun's bequeathed aims, waged a tit-for-tat struggle against the Kuomintang right that opposed the three great policies, and, sharing weal and woe with the Chinese Communist Party in long and close cooperation, together ushered in the great victory of the Chinese people's revolution. So to this day we commemorate every year Mr. Sun Yat-sen's birth and death, and hang portraits of this great forerunner of the revolution in important places such as Tiananmen Square, in his memory.
Besides Comrade Chen Tanqiu on Party history, we also heard Comrade Su Zhaozheng on the history of the workers' movement: he told not only of the "February Seventh" great strike on the Beijing–Hankou Railway but also of the Canton–Hong Kong strike of the Canton–Kowloon workers—even the city's sanitation workers struck, so that refuse lay everywhere with none to clear it and Hong Kong—the "Fragrant Harbor"—became a "stinking harbor"; and he told how unity is strength, and how the proletarians of the whole world can win their own thorough liberation only by uniting. Comrade Peng Pai, who led the peasant movement in the Hailufeng region of Guangdong and was called the "peasant king," lectured us on the history of the peasant movement.
Before the Northern Expedition army entered Wuhan, while we were secretly preparing to welcome it, everyone learned to sing a song whose words and tune were both very simple but full of revolutionary passion: "Down with the powers, down with the powers, wipe out the warlords, wipe out the warlords! The national revolution succeeds, the national revolution succeeds; let us all sing for joy, let us all sing for joy!" "Down with the powers" meant opposing imperialist aggression and winning national independence and liberation; "wipe out the warlords" meant opposing feudal oppression and winning the people's democratic right to be masters of their own house. These two short, clear lines gave vivid expression both to Mr. Sun Yat-sen's revolutionary Three Principles of the People and to the demands and program of the anti-imperialist, anti-feudal national-democratic revolution led by our Party. All the worker and peasant representatives from around the country who had gone to Guangzhou for meetings learned to sing this song and carried it everywhere, as a powerful means of propaganda to rouse the local worker and peasant masses to welcome the Northern Expedition revolution. So wherever the Expedition army went, old and young, men and women, almost everyone could sing this song—truly on everyone's lips, known to women and children alike. Of course, through study we Party and League members further came to understand clearly that this was only the minimum program of the Chinese revolution led by our Party, and that in its basic goals it accorded with Sun Yat-sen's new Three Principles of the People, which is why there was a first Kuomintang–Communist cooperation; but compared with the Three Principles of the People, our Party's maximum program is the realization of socialism and communism—a higher and more thorough revolutionary goal. In those days, when we joined the Party and gave ourselves to it, our hearts were full of high ambition and ardor; we never reckoned how many twists and dangers lay on the road ahead. Taking the Party oath, we had already resolved to obey the organization all our lives and never to betray it, full of confidence in the light, the ideal, the future. Asking myself today, after a lifetime, I have overcome countless hardships and dangers, and have, after all, stood the test.
It was precisely under the impetus of the first Kuomintang–Communist cooperation that the Northern Expedition army, setting out from Guangzhou, swept forward like a hot knife through butter and soon reached Hunan and Jiangxi. Under the leadership of the Communist underground organization and of the open local Kuomintang headquarters, wherever the Expedition army arrived, organized mass bodies lined up to welcome it warmly and held army–people fraternization meetings, singing the national revolutionary song in one voice. In those days the Expedition army had many Communists and Kuomintang leftists serving as party representatives at every level; discipline was strict, army–people relations harmonious, and the people's response to the Expedition revolution was very good: in the Expedition army the civil officials wanted no money (they were not corrupt) and the military officers did not fear death (its officers and men pressed bravely forward at the front, not fearing bloodshed and sacrifice). Everyone was truly high-spirited and full of fight—a world-shaking scene of flourishing revolution.
When our various preparations in Wuhan for welcoming the Northern Expedition army were basically ready, the women's department sent Elder Sister Zhang Duhe to Changsha to welcome the National Revolutionary Army coming into Hubei from Hunan, and sent Elder Sister Zhao Wanhua to Nanchang to welcome Chiang Kai-shek, commander-in-chief of the National Revolutionary Army leading the central column of the Expedition; while conveying our regards to the officers and men, they were also to learn from the local leaders of the women's movement and then come back and report. When Zhao Wanhua set off for Jiangxi, the advance units of the General Political Department of the Northern Expedition army had already reached Wuhan; the head of the General Political Department was the Kuomintang leftist Deng Yanda, the deputy head Guo Moruo, with Li Minzhi (Li Yimang) as deputy secretary-general and Yuan Wenbin as deputy head of the propaganda section. Pan Hannian (who joined the army at Nanchang in February 1927 as editor-in-chief of the *Revolutionary Army Weekly*, and after the "April Twelfth" coup went to Wuhan as head of the compilation subsection of the propaganda section) had also worked in the propaganda section. At that time it was Yuan Wenbin and Zhao Wanhua who went together to Nanchang to welcome Chiang Kai-shek. Zhao Wanhua had not yet joined the Party then; she and Liang Jianhua, Wang Huazhen, and others were in charge of the liaison group, and Wang Wenqiu was head of the recreation group. Zhang Duhe, who went to Hunan, was received by Geng Dan (styled Zhongzhao), deputy commander of the 15th Army of the Expedition. Zhang Duhe knew Mao Zedong's wife Yang Kaihui and Liu Shaoqi's wife He Baozhen before we did. He Baozhen had earlier done labor-movement work among the miners at Anyuan together with Liu Shaoqi; while she was a teacher at the workers' children's school, Zhang Duhe was head of the work-study school. After the National Revolutionary Army reached Wuhan, our Wuhan women's movement gained fresh forces. It had earlier been led by the women's department head Du Yunzhang, and women's associations had by now been set up in the three towns of Wuchang, Hanyang, and Hankou, with Yang Kaihui as head of the women's association's propaganda group and He Baozhen as head of the women workers' group. As the revolutionary situation rose, with a strong core of leadership, the women's association developed all the faster.
In early September the Northern Expedition army took Hanyang and Hankou one after the other. The assault on the city of Wuchang went through a bloody fight, and only on 10 October 1926 did the army victoriously enter it. Then the three towns of Wuhan held three days and three nights of army–people fraternization meetings; there were songs and slogans everywhere, and open-air speeches and open-air theatrical performances, as army and people together celebrated the great victory of the Northern Expedition revolution, and everyone was overjoyed. I took part in the recreational performance rally by which the masses of all circles in Wuhan celebrated the liberation of Wuchang. This rally was organized jointly by our women's association and Comrade Li Zhilong, deputy head of the art troupe of the General Political Department of the Northern Expedition army (the head being Comrade Yuan Wenbin). Liang Jianhua of our women's association was very talented and could write scripts herself; she wrote a one-act play, performed by three people, promoting the unbinding of women's feet. Wang Huazhen played the wicked mother-in-law and Zhang Duhe the aunt next door—both of them were rather tall and big; while I, small and thin (weighing only eighty-odd catties), played the child bride, both of them a head taller than I. In the performance they put on a fierce look, and it was quite convincing. Though I usually loved to watch spoken drama, this was my first time on stage—and the only time in my life I ever performed. The play showed the wicked mother-in-law abusing the child bride and forcing her to bind her feet, which was a very painful thing; and I, pretending to have my feet bound and pretending to tremble with fear, clenched my teeth and, holding back tears, said nothing. Though on stage I said not a word, like a mute, I acted it very convincingly after all, and won the audience's sympathy and welcome. The persons in charge of the provisional Kuomintang–Communist Central all attended this fraternization meeting, and at it I came to know Comrades Guo Moruo and Shen Yanbing (Mao Dun). Afterward I told them that the *Sorrows of Young Werther* and the literary works in the *Short Story Monthly* had all greatly influenced my ideological progress.
In the high tide of the great revolution of the Northern Expedition, I felt as if I had become another person entirely: not only could I take up the brush and write articles, I could also speak at mass meetings without a script; not only could I do the organizing and mobilizing work of the mass movement, I could even go on stage and act—as if I had all at once grown clever and capable, developing in many directions. For all this progress I must of course be deeply grateful to the Party and League organizations for their nurture and education, and to Comrades Du Yunzhang, Li Zifen, and Wan Xiyan for passing on their knowledge, helping me, and setting me an example in the practice of the work, so that I grew up quickly in the furnace of the great revolution. This I shall remember all my life!
Looking back later, I think that if the marriage my family arranged for me had been to my liking, I could not have walked out of the family so resolutely to join the revolution, nor thrown myself, just at the right moment, into the high tide of the great revolution welcoming the Northern Expedition. In those days many intellectuals, and educated women in particular, mostly walked out of their families and threw themselves into revolution in order to oppose the feudal oppression of arranged marriages; this was the tide of that age. After I joined the Youth League, it was only through attending League and Party classes, studying the Chinese Communist Party's constitution, and receiving revolutionary education in every respect, that my horizons were opened, and I understood that to devote oneself to revolution was not merely to seek one's own liberation but to strive for the liberation of all our women sisters and of all humankind; and that the Communist Party is the vanguard of the proletariat, and that only under the leadership of the Communist Party—with firm ideals and faith, with strict organization and discipline, with correct principles and policies, all of one heart, united, pressing forward wave upon wave, overcoming difficulties—can the victory of the revolution be won and the revolutionary ideal realized. If one does not accept the leadership of the Communist Party and does not join the revolutionary organization, but struggles alone single-handed, that is individual heroism, and can never accomplish anything great.
In November 1926, on the nomination of the Youth League organization, I was transferred to membership in the Chinese Communist Party. On the 11th—the day before the 60th anniversary of Mr. Sun Yat-sen's birth—the Chinese Communist Party fraction decided to convene in Hankou the Second Congress of the Wuhan Special Municipal Party Headquarters of the Chinese Kuomintang, and designated me to attend it as a delegate. At the congress I was elected an executive committee member of the Special Municipal Headquarters. I passed from Youth League member to Communist Party member, and then, in the capacity of a cross-party Communist, was elected an executive of the Kuomintang Special Municipal Headquarters—rather than first joining the Kuomintang and afterward the Communist Party. This point is very important for my own history and must be made clear here. By then the former head of the municipal party's women's department, Comrade Du Yunzhang, had been transferred to do labor-movement work in the industrial district of Qiaokou in Hankou; the wife of Wuhan mayor Liu Wendao, Liao Shixun, became head of the women's department but resigned on grounds of illness. After I was elected an executive committee member, I was chosen by common consent to be promoted to the post of head of the women's department.
On 23 November the China Relief Society held its national congress in Wuhan. The China Relief Society was a mass relief organization led by the Chinese Communist Party, founded in Shanghai on 20 September 1925 by the Communists Yun Daiying, Shen Zemin, and Zhang Wentian together with notables of various circles—Yang Xingfo, Zheng Zhenduo, Yu Youren, and others. Its main task was, through propaganda, fund-raising, and other means, to relieve those revolutionaries and patriots, and their families, who were persecuted or imprisoned by imperialism and by the domestic reactionaries. The head office was in Shanghai, with branches in some provinces and cities. The general director then was Comrade Ruan Zhongyi, and Comrade Pan Hannian was the representative of the Shanghai branch. The Wuhan women's association was a group member, and I was a council member. During the congress a number of report meetings were organized: Comrade Mao Zedong reported on the Hunan peasant movement, Comrade Li Lisan on the workers' movement, Zhang Guotao on the international and domestic political situation, Comrade Li Shuxun on the student movement... Wuhan then was truly a gathering of winds and clouds, drawing together many outstanding revolutionary pioneers from all over the country. To hear in person the teachings of so many leading comrades was one of the great good fortunes of my life.
Since joining the revolution, under the Party's nurture and education, I studied diligently and worked hard, and progressed very fast; my memory in particular was strong, so that I could grasp and take in a leading comrade's speech quickly and go at once among the masses to report and pass it on, carrying out propaganda and agitation. In Wuhan I too was, in people's eyes, a figure at the height of fame for a time. Besides serving as an executive committee member and head of the women's department of the Wuhan Special Municipal Party Headquarters, I was also concurrently supplement editor of the *Republican Daily*, head of preparations for the women's association, chair of the committee for the women's foot-unbinding movement, and held concurrent posts in the Relief Society, the Red Cross, and elsewhere. I often attended on the Kuomintang left-wing leaders Song Qingling and He Xiangning, assisting them in all sorts of concrete work. For example: the mass rally at which the people of all circles warmly welcomed Song Qingling on her arrival in Wuhan was one I chaired; at the mass rally welcoming the Kuomintang elder Yu Youren to Wuhan, Wan Xiyan was chair, Guo Moruo gave the welcoming address, and I too sat on the platform.
In December the provisional Kuomintang–Communist Central in Hankou decided to send me to study in the Soviet Union, at which I was naturally very glad. Though I could manage my work well enough after joining the revolution, I felt deeply in practice that the little cultural knowledge I had, especially in theoretical cultivation, was far from enough; to have the chance to study in the Soviet Union, where I could learn more revolutionary theory and enrich my experience of struggle, was of course a fine thing I could only long for. When my mother heard, she too was very glad, and sewed me a fur coat and got my luggage ready. Though the sisters of the women's association could not bear to see me go, they were all glad for me too, and held a tea party specially to see me off. Comrade Yuan Puzhi, head of the women's department of the Hubei Provincial Party Headquarters, was chosen to study in the Soviet Union at the same time as I, and I went with Elder Sister Yuan by boat to Shanghai, staying at the Dajiangnan Hotel and waiting for the liner to Vladivostok, meaning to cross Siberia from there to Moscow. Those who received us in Shanghai were Comrades Yang Hansheng and Mei Dianlong (Gong Bin), then working under the leadership of the Chinese Communist Party's Shanghai (and Jiangsu–Zhejiang) District Committee. Comrade Yang Hansheng did cultural work, and so, while we waited at the Dajiangnan Hotel, it was through him that I came to know the two brothers Gao Erbai and Gao Ersong. While we were still waiting for the boat, Comrade Dong Biwu sent a telegram from Wuhan to Comrade Qu Qiubai, a member of the Party Central in Shanghai, saying that "for the needs of the work, Huang Mulan should be persuaded to give up going to study in the Soviet Union and to return to Wuhan." On receiving the telegram, Comrade Qu Qiubai came with his wife Yang Zhihua to see me, and took me to their home to talk and do the work of persuading me. At that first talk, because my thinking... In my life I have many times unconditionally obeyed the organization's decisions to transfer my work; this virtue of mine, it should be said, had already taken shape by the winter of 1926 in Wuhan.
