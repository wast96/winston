## Chapter One. No Concealment, No Survival

### 1. Born a Conjuror

Up on a high stage under a few hundred watts of light, before a full house, what is there is made to vanish, and what is not there made to appear. Even so, Chinese conjuring in its modern dress was never quite a foreign import; something of its own always showed through.

Chinese conjuring is old, as old as the Han: small props, close handwork, everything in the cunning of the hands and the play of the body. In the third year of the Yuanfeng era, 108 BC, Emperor Wu of Han called for a grand Festival of the Hundred Entertainments, and its program already included the marvelous "Fish and Dragon." By the Tang and the Song it had ripened into a real art. Its "Dance into the Jar" sent a man down into a jar on the left and up out of another on the right, and by the "Method of the Seven Sages" a corpse severed at the waist was brought back to life before the audience's eyes. The dazzling old conjuring seems to have swept the lanes of Lin'an — today's Hangzhou — in those same years.

But orthodox Chinese conjuring modernized in the end.

"Let those above favor a thing, and those below will carry it further." The modern turn in Chinese conjuring owed much to the patronage of the Western Empress Dowager. In the last years of the Guangxu reign, when the Western circus came east and Chinese audiences, baffled and appalled, damned it as heresy and set upon it, Cixi — long accustomed to her plain gown and her murmured devotions — was suddenly confronted with a whole cabinet of wonders: feats of identification and mind-reading, prophecy, the sixth sense, invulnerability, and a bewildering play of light and sound and electric current, physics and chemistry turned to illusion. She could not help crying out in delight, and her favor was open-handed.

That patronage sped the home-grown art, born and bred in China, through a wholesale remaking. In Shanghai above all the change came overnight — that all-embracing city, the ten-li foreign quarter, was first with every fashion. From the Tower-Beyond-the-Tower and the Sky-Beyond-the-Sky and the Cloud-Beyond-the-Cloud to the New World and the Flower World and the Great World, the men in tailcoats were everywhere: pulling a live white rabbit out of a top hat, or firing three shots into a locked and empty cabinet and then parting the curling smoke to lead out a ravishing half-dressed woman.

And so, with the Yellow River between them, Chinese conjuring split into two, a southern school and a northern one. The profit-minded South looked more and more to the West, ever more modern, ever more in fashion. Its acts grew droll and daring, elegant and unforced; its apparatus grander and more bizarre. The less its performers spoke, the more they worked their faces and sharpened the weird artifice of the act. They flaunted their individuality, too, and reached ever harder for modern technology, chasing effects no one could account for, staging the exaggerated and the sensational. The rule-bound North stayed just as it was, keeping to its own old ways, growing ever more traditional, ever more homespun. It still worked its wonders out of plain skill. Its men still took the stage in the long gown, letting the audience first see the thing top and bottom, front and back, the covering cloth inside and out. They still kept to the old drill of "bind, tie, hide, tuck, tear, carry, pluck, loose" — the first four for making ready backstage, the last four for the show out front. Even as they ran through "The Radish-Ring Offering" and "The Nine Linked Rings" and "The Immortal Plants the Beans," they prized the rapid-fire patter of the *guankou*. The *guankou*, also called the *beikou*, belongs to paired crosstalk; the *guan* in it means done in one breath, all of a piece. And yet, once the show people had transplanted that one-breath patter into every kind of glib mouth-skill, the patter act was no longer the monopoly of crosstalk masters like Ma Sanli. At the very least, "Ghost-Hand" Wang Baohe of Wuqiao in Cangzhou, master of the unrivaled "Three Immortals Return to the Cave," had a stand-up turn that was every bit the equal of "Naming the Dishes" or "The Eight-Panel Screen" or "The Hundred Matters."

If a history of the Central Special Branch begins with stage magic — with how the foreign conjuror's art was made Chinese — it is only because one of the important men the Party planted in Shanghai was himself a magician, a gifted one, self-taught and off the street. He had lived through that particular age, through the death and rebirth of Chinese magic, its passage through the fire. And it was in him so deep that long after he had washed his hands of the trade and left the stage for good, he still could not keep away. Again and again he slipped off to the theaters and the halls, hid in a dark corner, and watched his old rivals at work — that Chinese conjuring which even now was not quite the imported foreign article, neither wholly Western nor wholly native, half new and half old.

He was Gu Shunzhang.

Gu Shunzhang despised his old fellows in the trade. When he watched them work his face was full of scorn, a cold little smile always at the corner of his mouth. He was aloof by nature, and thought a great deal of himself. In all the Chinese magic world of the 1920s, only Zhang Huichong caught his eye.

Zhang Huichong was a legend of the Chinese screen, its first martial-arts star. In 1928 he and his second brother, Zhang Huimin, founded the Huichong Film Company to make swordplay pictures, and made a great name at it. Their seventh brother, Zhang Daimin, often brought his girl, Agen, out to the lot. It was Zhang Daimin who recommended Agen to Zhang Huichong, and Zhang Huichong who gave her her first turn before the camera. From there she rose fast, finding her feet, until at last she "fell upon the Chinese screen like a bomb" — Ruan Lingyu, the tragic queen of the silent era, spoken of in the same breath as Butterfly Wu.

Zhang Huichong was also the founder, the father, of "Shanghai-style magic."

With his large solo shows Zhang Huichong lifted modern Chinese magic onto the legitimate stage. His "Man Through the Sword-Box," his "Horse Through the Wall," his "Ball of Yarn" broke new ground and built to one climax after another — every one of them grand illusion in the purest sense. His grand illusions could send a man vaulting up out of a thicket of cold-gleaming blades, or turn a tall horse behind iron bars into Zhang himself. He could vanish a girl assistant out of a huge searchlight into a still larger ball of yarn, and only when every strand had been wound off that great ball would she reappear, smiling, at its center, before the audience's eyes.

Zhang Huichong won Gu Shunzhang over completely. And still Gu believed: whatever Zhang Huichong could do, he could do too; and what he himself could do, Zhang Huichong could not always match.

Gu Shunzhang dreamed of facing Zhang Huichong in open contest.

Gu Shunzhang dreamed of becoming a second Zhang Huichong.

There was arrogance in Gu Shunzhang, but he was not merely reckless, not a man of idle fancies. Anyone, after all, can do a trick; Gu had the wherewithal for his high reach and his unbroken pride. The blood of a born magician ran in him, and his command of the art was consummate, beyond fathoming. The advertisement in the *Shen Bao* of November 9, 1930, and the report in the same paper on 1 December, do more than confirm that Gu "was skilled in magic, and had established in the Shanghai concessions a 'Hua Guangqi' magic troupe that openly enrolled pupils and gave performances" (Cai Mengjian, 1980). The "removal" of a certain "Qixing Magic Research Society," and the enlargement of that same society — with "Hua Guangqi" as its "director" — from No. 22 Xieqiao Street (today's Wujiang Road) to No. 679 Jing'an Temple Road (today's Nanjing West Road), just left of the Xieqiao Club. All this may fairly stand as proof that "the society's affairs prospered by the day" and that "those enrolling to study were most eager" — and that none of it was mere boasting.

In truth, the embroiderings of Frederic Wakeman, Wang Guangyuan, Mu Xin, Zhang Guotao and the rest run further still than Cai Mengjian's reminiscence. With one voice they inflate Gu Shunzhang's magic into something miraculous, without limit. Let his name come up, and they will tell you he "performed on a regular bill at the roof-garden theater of the Sincere Company" and "stood on the stage impeccably turned out, like a Western gentleman" (Wakeman, 2011). They will tell you he "kept a 'Qixing Magic Society' at No. 22 Xieqiao Road, selling conjuring apparatus, its glass cases full of every kind of outlandish little novelty" (Wang Guangyuan, 1999). They will tell you he "not only loved to perform, but loved to bring friends up to assist him and share the stage; members of the Central Special Branch 'Red Squad' — Tan Zhongyu, Zhang Alian, Zhang Wenhu, Zhang Wenlong and the rest — had all played his supporting parts" (Mu Xin, 2013). They will tell you he "performed many times in Hankou, to a great stir, and that certain rich merchants and men of means there took him for their teacher, several of them on close terms with him" (Zhang Guotao, 1991).

Gu Shunzhang did seem to have all he needed to make a great show of himself, using his public magic as cover for the underground work — underground in a Shanghai that jumbled together every kind of character, honest and crooked, with danger on every side. But an honest man keeps clear of the melon patch and the plum tree, as the ancients warned. And no one was less fit to show his face in a crowd: the former commander-in-chief of the Shanghai Workers' Pickets, a member of the executive of the Citizens' Delegate Assembly, a member of the municipal government.

And so, more often than not, Gu Shunzhang had to vanish without a trace, to lie low and go deep.

And so, in public, Gu Shunzhang had to school himself to patience and bury his own name.

When Gu Shunzhang took the stage in paint and powder, making living people appear and disappear, he went by a stage name: "Hua Guangqi."

When Gu Shunzhang worked underground — building the intelligence network, guarding the Party Center by force of arms — he had another name: "Liming," the Dawn.

"Hua Guangqi" was Gu Shunzhang's public face.

"Liming" was Gu Shunzhang's hidden face.

The public Gu Shunzhang was a magician.

The Gu Shunzhang behind it was a secret agent, a man so deep undercover that he slept with one eye open.

The enemy never sleeps — so said Lenin.

You may be forgiven for not being clever, but never for being careless — so said Yu Zecheng.

### 2. Out of the Deep Woods, a Hero

The Central Special Branch had another man of resource: Chen Geng.

{v} I was born and raised in Xiangxiang, in Hunan, and am now forty-one. My grandfather came out of poverty — he had scarcely clothes enough to cover himself — and made his career and his money as a soldier, known in his day as a fine fighter. My father took over the family holdings and had some small standing in the district; he was sympathetic to the revolution. As a boy, under my grandfather's influence, I often thought of throwing over my books for the army, and before I had finished upper primary school I enlisted without hesitation, a private in the 2nd Battalion of the 6th Regiment under Lu Diping. I was only fourteen or fifteen, and shouldering a German-made sleeve rifle (no bayonet fixed) that stood nearly as tall as I did. From private second class I rose step by step to master sergeant. From the sixth year of the Republic to the ninth, Hunan was at war year after year — the campaign against Zhang Jingyao, the Constitutional Protection War, the Hunan-Hubei fighting — and I was in nearly every action. Seeing that misery year upon year, the battlefields strewn with corpses and the villages emptied house by house, I came to have deep doubts about these warlord wars, and the wish to leave took root. In the tenth year of the Republic I quit the ranks and sat the middle-school entrance examination again. Stirred by the currents of May Fourth, I resolved to give myself to the revolution, and in 1922 I joined the S.Y. (the Socialist Youth League) and took up the anti-imperialist work. In the Changsha "June First massacre" of 1923 — when Japanese marines landed and shot down students spreading propaganda — I took part in person, one of those directly involved, and was wounded.

{v} At the end of that year the Party sent me by way of Shanghai to Guangdong, into the Army Military Academy that Cheng Qian had founded. In 1924 the K.M.T. (the Kuomintang) reorganized and set up the Whampoa Military Academy. I held that revolutionary youth should not scatter their strength — still less be turned to some man's private ends — but should gather to train at Whampoa; I argued hard for merging the military academy into it, and to set the example I was the first to withdraw and sit the Whampoa entrance examination. My schoolfellows came over after me one by one, until the whole school was absorbed into Whampoa and reorganized as its first class. On graduating I served as company commander of the cadet-recruits, deputy leader of the third class, and infantry company commander of the fourth. In the Guangzhou revolutionary movement and the fighting of 1924 to 1926 I took part in most of it: the suppression of the Merchant Corps revolt, the Shakee massacre, the Canton-Hong Kong general strike, the putting-down of the Yang and Liu mutiny, the Second Eastern Expedition, and the rest.

{v} In September 1926 the Party sent me to the Soviet Far East to study mass armed insurrection; I stayed with the Red Army three full months and returned to Shanghai in February 1927. At the Nanchang rupture I went to Wuhan and was posted under Tang Shengzhi as a special-service battalion commander. After the split between the Kuomintang and the Communists I joined the Nanchang Uprising. I was gravely wounded in the fighting at Huichang, and came near to death. After the reverse at Chaoshan I made my way, through every kind of danger, into Shanghai. Once my wounds had healed I took up the secret work there, day after day matching wits with the counter-revolution.

The "secret work in Shanghai" of which "My Autobiography" speaks — in *Chen Geng's Diary* (1982) — was this: under the alias Wang Yong, alongside Gu Shunzhang and the others, and under Zhou Enlai's direction, Chen Geng threw himself into the Party's struggle on the hidden front, in the lights and dissipation of Shanghai. And "day after day matching wits with the counter-revolution" is a vivid glimpse of what the Special Branch man was made of: wit and daring, a quick eye and an iron nerve.

Chen Geng was chief of the Central Special Branch's Second Section, its intelligence section. How wide a net he wove in his four years underground, how many operatives he took on, how many intelligence contacts he built, no one can say for certain. Gu Shunzhang's *Theory and Practice of Secret-Service Work* (1933) reproduces a chart that Gu had Li Haifeng "draw out by hand" for him. On it, large circles link to small ones. "Detective Chief Chen Geng" connects to "a certain news-agency reporter," to "a certain woman detective of the Shanghai Garrison Command," to "the Green Gang hoodlum Yang Anqing," to "a Japanese interpreter," to "a Korean." Small circles link to smaller ones still: more and more sub-agents, more and more eyes and ears, in Shanghai and Nanjing but also in Hong Kong and the Northeast, packed as tight as the threads of a spider's web.

After his defection Gu Shunzhang said as much: "The Second Section's intelligence on national politics and economics and on international affairs was extraordinarily quick and valuable. Above all, if we meant to raid one of their offices, they knew in advance, because every day they put out an intelligence sheet called the *Minjiefei*, so their information was always current. At the same time they worked to disrupt the other side, sometimes planting contradictory reports to set the enemy against himself, so that suspicion spread and his strength was divided." Xu Enzeng — in his time chief of the Party Affairs Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department, director of the Special Affairs Headquarters, and deputy director of the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Kuomintang Central Executive Committee, and a man who boasted of having "fought the Communists" for fourteen years — said the same: "Should anything ever go wrong at one of these offices, they could usually be tipped off by an inside man planted within the police and detective agencies; and since the Communists' intelligence people passed word so fast, every person in the house and every document that mattered would be safely gone within two hours, moved to a fallback address long since prepared, with nothing else to trouble over. The empty house was then left to the very agent who had set it up, to give notice on the lease and return the furniture — formalities to be completed, again, within four hours. So by the time the authorities arrived, the room was a true void, with nothing left to question, and the Communists took not the slightest loss." (Xu Enzeng, 1992)

Dong Jianwu's notes bear out, from another side, the chart in Gu Shunzhang's *Theory and Practice of Secret-Service Work* (1933). Dong Jianwu was a Chinese Communist agent who had joined the Party after the collapse of the Great Revolution and answered directly to Chen Geng; under the open identity of the presiding pastor of St. Peter's Church, he moved through an espionage war in which a whisper could move mountains and a shout change the color of the sky.

St. Peter's, a church of the Chinese Anglican communion, stood in Shanghai at the corner of Avenue Road (today's Beijing West Road) and North Chengdu Road (today's Chengdu North Road). Beneath its pulpit ran a hidden passage, and Zhou Enlai and others often met there; at the first sign of danger they could slip away in safety.

In a note dated July 15, 1969, Dong Jianwu wrote: "In September 1928, Comrade Chen Geng transferred me into the Second Section — the intelligence section — that he led in the Central Special Branch. He set me to building up the early-warning work on the International Settlement police side, and for the time being to also handle a warning line, long since established, that ran through Zhao Zibai, a translator and detective inspector in the French Concession police. Zhao Zibai was a Shaoxing man; his son, Zhao Bingsheng, was a detective sergeant in the French police as well. At that time Ouyang Xin — Comrade Lao Wang, also called 'the Fat One,' 'High Nose,' and 'the Foreigner' — served as liaison between Comrade Chen Geng and me. Comrades Pu Huaren and Liu Zhonghua would also often come with Comrade Chen Geng to St. Peter's on Beijing West Road, to talk over with me the various questions of the warning work."

Dong Jianwu's late-life notebooks were four in all: "Investigation and Research" (the first), "Writing and Memoranda" (the second), and "Jottings from a Rampart of Blood and Tears" (the third and fourth).

For reasons everyone knows, Dong Jianwu was treated unjustly for many years, and through the ten years of turmoil he suffered political persecution of every kind. But he met each blow without flinching, indifferent to favor and disgrace alike, and in his notebooks he pressed the same point again and again: "What is it to stand the test? To stand the test is not to fear the hard boiling of struggle, not to fear the fire. When you are wronged, do not rail at heaven, do not blame others. Getting to the bottom of a matter takes a process, takes time. So be humble, be patient, set down the baggage of your own 'progress,' put off the airs of a man of merit. And once a matter is cleared up, do not exult, still less get above yourself; keep to a careful bearing, and never blow your own horn."

In Dong Jianwu's notes I found that the whole "warning" apparatus was headed by Chen Geng. Below Pu Huaren, five further "lines were developed and set up": Mei Yu (a translator in the police criminal division), Chen Jiayin (the telephone switchboard), Zhou Guisheng (chief detective at the Xinwen Road station), Shang Wu (chief detective at the Haining Road station), and Ge Baohua (a translator in the police political department). The "warning" apparatus as a whole ran along the line of Ouyang Xin (Lao Wang, Wang Zichun, "the Fat One," "High Nose," "the Foreigner"), and took in Lao Chen, Lao Yang, Lao Zhu, and Lao Dong (Dong Jianwu). Below Lao Dong there was, besides, "one little courier to run the police and international contacts." She was Dong Jianwu's second daughter, Dong Huifang.

Dong Huifang once told me: "Long before I became his little courier, Papa was already training me on purpose. He would take me out along the street, and once we were inside a shop he would have me watch, particularly, the reflections of people in the show-window glass, and all the strangers the glass threw back. I did not understand; what was there to look at in that? In truth he was teaching me to be an agent. He wanted me always alert, always minding what stirred around us, always glancing back to see whether some suspicious figure was shadowing us, tailing us. We were lying hidden in the very heart of the enemy, and could not afford a moment's carelessness."

In fact Chen Geng's charges went well beyond the ones Gu Shunzhang and Dong Jianwu had already named. Gu Shunzhang's account is the broad one, Dong Jianwu's the narrow; the first speaks in generalities, the second gives particular names. One of the sharpest things that set the hidden-front struggle apart from ordinary underground work was single-line contact. The ordinary underground Party is built as a pyramid — from the base to the middle rank, from the middle to the top, from the single member to the Party group, the Party branch, and on up to the district, municipal, and provincial committees — layer upon layer from the bottom up, structure heaped on structure, managed straight down the line into one great system. Only so can a single call bring a hundred answers, and one vast mass movement after another be raised across a wide field: the December Ninth, say, or the May Twentieth.

Deep cover, lying under, the hidden front, is the exact opposite. The more silent the contest in the dark, the more sharply everything had to be kept apart and cut clean, the up-and-down chain remade as single sideways links: strictly one to one, each man working alone, dealing alone, with no tie at all to anyone on his own level. The result is that even the largest apparatus — spanning several cities, several provinces, several regions — still answers to one leader, forever on the move, who must deal one-on-one with the dozens, the hundreds, even the several hundred subordinates or contacts under him, with no cross-links, no crossings, wholly sealed. Done this way, of course, a single place may hold several systems at once, out of step with one another, overlapping, wasting effort for half the result. Everyone knows that if one place could have a single unified command, with the gathering of intelligence and material shared out among many, it would take less time and yield more than doing it piecemeal. But the danger of the hidden-front struggle is tens, hundreds of times greater than that of ordinary underground work, and its iron law is that safety comes before everything. Deep cover leaves no room for improvising; the undercover man's cardinal sin is being clever for its own sake. To send "a respectable countrywoman who works with her hands" into the big city to play "wife to a great secret agent" — a television drama can stage it that way — it has to, or there would be nothing to watch: no contradiction, no conflict, no suspense, no climax. Life cannot be staged that way twice. Were the hidden-front struggle in life to unfold like that, what would be thrown away would be far more than the life of one Yu Zecheng.

In short: underground work may do what the hidden front does — infiltrate, steal secrets, turn a man — but the hidden front may not do what underground work does: propaganda, agitation, expansion. Deep cover is the first principle of the hidden front, and its highest reach is the line, "Of the two ranks one soldier left, shouldering his spear, wandering alone." To lie in deep cover is to endure being alone. Grand gestures and loud display are, to the man in deep cover, no better than suicide.

During the ten years of turmoil, when Yang Du's widow came under attack, Zhou Enlai asked only that the relevant quarters protect her from harm; he did not say outright that Yang Du had been a "secret Party member" of ours. Not until October 7, 1975, gravely ill, did Zhou Enlai instruct his secretary to find Wang Yeqiu — then director of the State Cultural Relics Bureau — and have him tell the Shanghai editorial office of the *Cihai* (today the Shanghai Lexicographical Publishing House) that whenever the *Cihai* was next revised, any entry touching on Yang Du must state plainly that he had been a member of the Chinese Communist Party, lest the fact be lost. Zhou Enlai granted that Yang Du had a real stain on his record: he had schemed for Yuan Shikai and beaten the drum for monarchy, one of the "Six Gentlemen of the Chou'an Society." But Zhou also said that Yang Du had joined the Party late in life, through Zhou's own hand, with the formal approval of the Party Center's Politburo. And the Party had welcomed Yang Du, had taken him in, because he had turned back from a lost road, made a clean break with his old self, and worked for the Party in earnest, by deeds. In fact, once Yang Du had remade himself and come out of the dark into the light, he threw himself into the effort to save Li Dazhao when Li was arrested and jailed, and helped Li's family in every way, selling off his own property, giving all he had. Later, when the Shanghai "notable" Du Yuesheng, playing the patron of letters, set out in imitation of "the three thousand retainers at the gate of Lord Chunshen" to invite Yang Du into his household and treat him as an honored guest, Yang Du turned the scheme to his own use: through Chen Geng, in cigar boxes, he sent out a great deal of useful intelligence. (CCP Central Party Literature Research Office, 1997)

Some say Yang Du joined the Party "on Pan Hannian's introduction, with Zhou Enlai's approval"; and that in the autumn of 1929 Pan Hannian brought Shen Duanxian — Xia Yan — to see Yang Du and told him, "In a few days I have to go far away, and there is no telling when I will be back, so from now on this friend will be your single-line contact" (Lin Chengxi and Xu Rongsheng, 1996). But in fact Pan Hannian did not "take charge of the Second, intelligence, Section in the Special Branch" until the turn of spring and summer in 1931. In other words, even if Pan Hannian did bring Xia Yan to see Yang Du in the autumn of 1929, that does not make Yang Du the "secret Party member" whom Pan "kept on single-line contact." Yang Du certainly did not join the Party "on Pan Hannian's introduction." Nor could Pan Hannian have "accompanied" Yang Du to see Zhou Enlai, as Lin Chengxi and Xu Rongsheng have it. Still less could Yang Du have, at every meeting with Pan Hannian or Xia Yan, "always brought a box of cigars, or simply a large envelope sealed with wax — and whether it was that perfectly ordinary box of cigars or the large envelope, inside would be all kinds of important intelligence Yang Du had written out in his own hand."

The man who "kept single-line contact" with Yang Du was Chen Geng — Pan Hannian's predecessor.

The man who introduced Chen Geng to Yang Du was Hu Egong.

Hu Egong — original name Hu Rongming, courtesy name Xinsan, style-name Nanhu, his studio called the Tower of Wind and Rain — was born in 1884, a native of Jiangling in Hubei.

Hu Egong was a bold captain of the 1911 Revolution. In 1909, with Xiong Deshan, Qian Tieru and others, he founded the Baoding branch of the Tongmenghui. In 1910 he organized the Republican Association and served as its managing director. In 1911 he went south to Wuhan to join the uprising, holding such posts as chief of the higher detective section, commander-in-chief of the Hubei army by land and water, and commander-in-chief of the Northern Revolutionary Army. His *True Record of the 1911 Revolution in the North* (1948) was the earliest and fullest account of the revolution in northern China.

Hu Egong was also among the very few in modern China to take up Marxism early. The Marxism Study Society that he founded with Kuang Baohan, a teacher at the Higher Police Officers' School, and with Xiong Deshan and others — the "Association of Comrades of the Chinese Communist Party," also called, from the magazine *Today* in which they put their case, the "Today faction of Communists" — seems even to have won the recognition of the Soviet Far East's military intelligence. Chen Duxiu, too, urged the Party's northern organization to reckon with them and draw them in.

In 1923, on Li Dazhao's introduction, Hu Egong joined the Chinese Communist Party — of which Chen Duxiu was General Secretary — and worked for it under cover of his own standing as a member of parliament and a vice-minister of education.

In January 1924, when Li Dazhao went south to Guangzhou for the Kuomintang's First National Congress, Hu Egong stood in for him at the head of the Party's Northern District Committee. In April 1927, when Li Dazhao met his heroic death on the gallows of the warlord Zhang Zuolin and the smell of blood hung over the city of Beijing, Hu Egong, undaunted in the face of danger, took up the post of propaganda chief of the Communist Party's provisional municipal committee there.

Before long, when the secretary of the Party's Beiping municipal committee was arrested and turned traitor, Hu Egong left his home and went south to live in hiding on Route Pétain (today's Hengshan Road) in the Shanghai French Concession. There he gave himself to the study of agriculture and forestry, joined in turn the League of Left-Wing Social Scientists and the Freedom League, and fell in with Chen Geng. Before long he had introduced Yang Du to him.

Hu Egong said Yang Du had been wrong to champion Yuan Shikai, that he had taken a crooked road — but that even before the May Fourth movement he had gone of his own accord to Shanghai to seek out Sun Yat-sen, to abase himself in apology, to repent to his face. Sun Yat-sen introduced him to Li Dazhao, and Li Dazhao urged him to read the Marxist classics in the original. Then at last he saw the whole truth, and there rose in him a fierce wish: "to chant to the pine as my public duty, and pledge my next life to the plum blossom."

Yang Du held himself aloof. When Du Yuesheng fawned on him and tried to draw him in, making him a present of an elegant little villa, Yang Du turned up his nose at it and chose to pinch and scrape, living by the sale of his calligraphy. But the moment he learned from Chen Geng that the Party wished him to become Du Yuesheng's retainer, and to use Du's close ties with high Kuomintang men to learn the inner truth of the Kuomintang's upper reaches, he did as the Party asked without a word of protest, and out of Du Yuesheng he drew a great deal: the secrets of the gangs and the movements of the Nanjing authorities. Those secrets, those movements, helped the Party grasp in time the vile schemes by which the reactionaries and the underworld, thick as thieves, meant to crush the forces of revolution.

During his time in the Central Special Branch, Chen Geng also ran a training class of his own for the Third Section — the Action Section, the "Red Squad," of which Gu Shunzhang was concurrently chief — teaching above all disguise, surveillance and counter-surveillance, codebreaking, writing letters in invisible ink, and how, once arrested, to slip a pair of handcuffs and get clear of the scene. He arranged boats, too, and took the Red Squad men out to sea, beyond the mouth of the Yangtze, to practice their shooting and learn the ways of every kind of firearm. A graduate of Whampoa's first class, he had the *Infantry Drill Regulations*, the *Musketry Manual*, *Field Service*, and *Weaponry* by heart. He was among the first Chinese Communist agents to be formally trained. He had gone, with Gu Shunzhang and Lu Liu, "to the Soviet Far East, to study mass armed insurrection, and stayed with the Red Army three full months," and what he had taken most to heart there was shooting out lightbulbs: "on a wall fifty meters off there are many bulbs; whichever one lights up, you shoot it out at once with a pistol."

Underground in Shanghai, Chen Geng was here and gone, never long in one place, forever changing his open identity. Now he was in a Western suit and leather shoes, like the comprador of a foreign firm; now in a long gown and riding jacket, like the manager of a native bank; now in a felt hat and satin shoes, like the boss of a gambling den. In 1930, Zhang Kexia — a Communist, then chief of staff of Zhang Zizhong's division in the Northwest Army — saw Chen Geng in Shanghai, and Chen Geng at the time "wore a suit of black satin, jacket and trousers, the trouser-legs bound tight with black satin cord, looking very much like a Shanghai young master." From that same period there is a photograph of Chen Geng. In it he wears a soft cap askew, his face all stubble running up into a mop of tangled hair, so that he looks like the lowest sort of riffraff adrift in the underworld. Chen Geng said: "Got up like that, living in a *tingzijian*, one thin partition from the whores and the sing-song girls, everyone above and below and to either side the same kind of character, the nights too noisy to rest — but very safe."

Underground in Shanghai, dealing all day long with party hacks, politicians, agents, police, concession patrolmen, plainclothes detectives, and street-corner toughs, Chen Geng could play any part to the life. He could pitch his voice a dozen ways and had a few phrases of every dialect, and they all called him "Mr. Wang." They liked his wit, his open hand, his readiness to stand by a friend; anyone would be glad to count "Mr. Wang Yong" a friend. Not one of them ever dreamed that this very man — so easy in talk and laughter, so free with his money — had saved Chiang Kai-shek's life on the Second Eastern Expedition, and outside the walls of Huichang had taken three hilltops in a single rush at the head of one battalion.

His years underground in Shanghai shaped the whole of Chen Geng's life.

I have read the diaries of many of the older generation of proletarian revolutionaries, and I have yet to find one who understood intelligence, and prized it, as Chen Geng did, or who wrote of it and dissected it so intently and so well. In *Chen Geng's Diary* (1982) he writes:

{d} June 10, 1943

{v} Troop reconnaissance is the confirming of intelligence, the true from the false, and reconnaissance carried down into the finest detail.

{v} A situation in which the enemy is strong and we are weak makes reconnaissance all the more needed to set it right.

{v} When the enemy drives a nail deep into our heartland, his "mopping-up" is easy to launch and comes without warning — all the more reason for reconnaissance.

{d} 11 June

{v} Intelligence work at present still cannot keep pace with the needs of the war; we have not yet seized the initiative in the intelligence struggle, and are too often misled by rumor. Today intelligence work still leans on troop reconnaissance and on what comes down from above; espionage work is still not doing what it should.

{v} We must, on the Center's decision for investigation and research, overcome subjectivism in our operations through the Rectification, truly come to know the enemy, grasp the situation, seize the initiative, move the enemy at will, and carry through to victory in the war.

{v} Espionage should turn chiefly on campaign reconnaissance, with tactical reconnaissance second. In peacetime troop reconnaissance should be chiefly at the campaign level; once war has begun, chiefly tactical. Above the subdistrict, campaign reconnaissance leads; at the regiment, campaign and tactical count equally; below the battalion lies the province of tactical reconnaissance. The sending-out of spies is possible only above the subdistrict.

{d} 6 July

{v} The tasks of a command post: to besiege, to cut the enemy's lines, to seize grain, to fight the puppet self-government, to reconnoiter and enforce curfew, to strike at the enemy's raiders, and to carry on combat.

{d} 11 July

{v} Care for the people at every turn: reckon with their sufferings, relieve their hardships, give them comfort, keep them informed of the enemy's movements, and above all strengthen reconnaissance. It is a contest of sheer endurance — with the enemy at Qinyuan.

{d} 13 July

{v} Boldness must be encouraged. Combat intelligence is to be passed on at once; the sharing of it, and the heart it puts into men, count for a great deal.

{d} 16 July

{v} Guard against the enemy's raids, guard against his agents, let the people take no loss — and so do not stay long in one place. Strengthen reconnaissance, strengthen the telephone links (string the wires), and keep the people informed at all times. Intelligence gathered by the government and the people must be passed to the army as well.

In June and July of 1943, Chen Geng was commander of the 386th Brigade of the 129th Division of the Eighteenth Group Army of the National Revolutionary Army, and his unbroken run of victories left a deep mark. The secret of it, as Bo Yibo — who fought at his side in those days — disclosed many years later, was this: "Before every battle he would take pains to get the situation clear, sending out scouts again and again, and sometimes going up to the front himself to look over the lie of the land, learn the enemy's dispositions, and turn every plan over and over"; and it was this: "On sending intelligence teams into enemy-held territory, casting out an intelligence net, and guarding against the enemy's own agents, he offered many good ideas, and later took personal charge of building the intelligence teams and carrying on the military-intelligence work" (Bo Yibo, 2003). And so it is worth asking: without the years underground in Shanghai, without that singular apprenticeship as chief of the Central Special Branch's intelligence section for four years, would even Chen Geng have shown such a thing — "painstaking before the battle, treating the light as heavy; bold and sweeping in command on the field, treating the heavy as light"? Faced with his "poring over the map in deep thought, taking neither food nor drink," faced with the way he set "caring for the people at every turn, reckoning with their sufferings, relieving their hardships, giving them comfort" beside "keeping them constantly informed of the enemy and above all strengthening reconnaissance," and made the two together his own "contest of endurance" with the enemy — how can we not cherish his memory all the more, the memory carried in "In the jumbled hills a lofty scholar lies; out of the deep dense woods a hero comes" (a poem the late Ho Chi Minh, Chairman of the Vietnam Workers' Party, gave to Chen Geng)? How can we not understand, more deeply still, what Luo Qingchang said at the symposium for the making of the television drama *General Chen Geng*: "Chen Geng's history touches the history of our Party and our army. He was an outstanding historical figure of our Party and our army, and he made great contributions on both fronts, the open and the hidden. We must remember this history, and honor forever the heroes of the hidden front."

### 3. Purity Not Soon Forgotten

Chen Geng's years underground began with the failure of the Great Revolution.

When that great, roaring revolution stopped short at the shore of the Eastern Sea, on the banks of the Huangpu, the mad killing came down out of the sky, on a rain of blood and a foul wind. By the Party's own partial count at its Sixth Congress, from March 1927 to the first half of 1928 the Communists and revolutionary masses killed across the country came to as many as 310,000, of whom some 26,000 were Party members.

In truth, without the "better to kill a thousand by mistake than let one man slip the net" of Yang Hu, Chen Qun, He Jian, Wang Dongyuan, and Xu Kexiang and Xia Douyin besides, there would never have been the unnatural disappearance of tens, even hundreds of thousands of Kuomintang men either. The figures bear it out: before the "party purge," the Kuomintang's membership across the country stood above a million; after it, the total fell sharply to a little over 650,000. A figure of 350,000 is no small thing. The vanishing into thin air of 350,000 Kuomintang members drew cries of alarm from some of the party's own local members, who said: "It is the party's misfortune that a purge movement, forced on us by practical need, has given corrupt bullies and opportunist degenerates their chance to rise and strike back at the revolutionary forces; every loyal comrade, framed and broken by them, is all but destroyed alongside the Communists. Now that our comrades are all caught in the degenerates' dragnet, some are beaten, some robbed, some killed, some falsely accused, some hunted down, some thrown into prison." In fact those "beaten," "robbed," "killed," "falsely accused," "hunted down," and "imprisoned" were chiefly the young leftist members and the worker and peasant members at the bottom. No wonder even Chen Lifu was sick at heart, and cried again and again that "in the Shanghai purge the innocent who suffered were beyond counting, and the party's loss of the sympathy of the educated classes and the disappointment of the common people were losses that could never be made good" (Chen Lifu, 1994).

The "party purge" was a movement of reverse selection within the Kuomintang. Even as it slaughtered Communists wholesale, it cut down, wounded, and struck at a great many of the Kuomintang's own finest. These were men of a modern cast of mind, utterly unlike the hidebound old guard of the party. They meant to reform, pressed for the new, prized democracy; with all their hearts they wanted to drive old China's modernization forward by their own radical ideas. And they died, cruelly, alongside still more Communists, in the reactionary authorities' savage killing.

So many fell with high resolve; they dared to bid the sun and moon light a new sky.

The "purge," in all its inhuman horror, could not cow, could not conquer, could not kill off the Chinese Communists who pressed forward over their own fallen. They rose up from the ground, wiped the blood from their bodies, buried their comrades' corpses, raised high the banner of truth, and went forward again, and fought again!

Yesterday's allies had become today's mortal enemies. The Chinese Communists who went forward and fought could only hide themselves, and go to ground.

And yet the Chinese Communists were short on experience of underground struggle.

It is true that the *Manifesto of the Chinese Communist Party* of November 1920 had set down plainly the "aim of China's communists": "to lead the revolutionary proletariat in the fight against the capitalists, and to take political power from the capitalists' hands"; it is true that Lin Xiangqian and Shi Yang, the leaders of the "February Seventh" great strike, gave their lives rather than yield, and fell under the butcher's blade of the warlord Wu Peifu. But in its early years the Party had not shaken the reactionaries' rule by any greater use of force, and so the reactionaries had not turned more force, more mercilessly, against it. Thus at the flood of revolution they could not help outdoing one another in fervor and radicalism; and at its ebb, panic, passivity, flight, even betrayal were anything but rare. Something of this can be glimpsed in Li Yimang's late recollections.

{v} The falling-away from the Party was starkest in Wuhan. In early August the Wuhan Kuomintang issued its "Four Measures for Investigating Communist Party Members," of which the third provided: "Any person suspected of being a Communist shall, within three days, publish in the press a declaration opposing the Communist Party, or issue writings against it." So many even published notices of quitting the Party that the Hankou *Central Daily News* ran a notice of its own: "By order of the Center, no notice in the party press of leaving the Communist Party or declaring oneself no Communist may be published without the examination and seal of the Hankou Special Municipal Party Headquarters Reorganization Committee. This paper will therefore, from today, decline to carry any notice that does not meet the above formality." (Li Yimang, 2001)

Nothing did the Party greater harm than the selling-out by traitors. The defection of a confidential courier, for one, brought the arrest of Chen Yannian — member of the Fifth Central Committee, alternate member of the Politburo, secretary of the Party's Jiangsu Provincial Committee — along with Guo Bohe, the committee's head of organization, and Han Buxian, its secretary-general and propaganda chief. Han Buxian's defection in turn brought the arrest of Zhao Shiyan, member of the Fifth Central Committee and acting secretary of the Party's Jiangsu Provincial Committee.

{v} In the year and a half since the "August Seventh" Conference, under the pressure of the White Terror, the organizations in every province have been broken again and again, cadres have fallen beyond counting, and the giving of oneself up, the informing, the defecting, have spread little by little from south to north across the higher party bodies of the whole country. Thus the Party's proletarian base grows weaker by the day, its organization by the day more cut off from the masses and from society, and the higher party bodies above all have become empty shells, wholly severed from the life of the masses. (Zhou Enlai, 1980)

To beat down the enemy's swagger, and to guard the Party's organizations at every level against the corrosion and injury of the vile traitors, the Chinese Communists could only answer blood with blood and force with force, and answer the counter-revolution in kind, method for method.

On October 18, 1927, the standing committee of the Party's Provisional Central Politburo resolved to convene an emergency conference in Shanghai (later called the enlarged meeting of the Provisional Central Politburo), and through the Southern Bureau and the Guangdong Provincial Committee sent word to Zhou Enlai — far off, recuperating at his lodgings on Canton Road, Yau Ma Tei, in Kowloon — that he "must reach Shanghai before the seventh to attend."

By Li Weihan's recollection, the standing committee of the Provisional Central Politburo then numbered only three: himself, Qu Qiubai, and Su Zhaozheng. Because the situation "had greatly changed," and because in the "autumn uprisings in the four provinces of Hunan, Hubei, Guangdong, and Jiangxi" the Party had "laid its weaknesses fully bare and learned a good many lessons, all of which had to be set right in a proper reckoning," Qu Qiubai, having gained the agreement of Li Weihan and the others, issued the "Notice of the Center Convening an Emergency Conference," announcing that the Center had "resolved to convene an emergency conference in Shanghai on the twelfth of November; all members of the Central Politburo, and such alternate members as the Center designates, must attend, and the important provincial committees or central sub-bureaus designated must also send representatives." Afterward a "Correction by the Center Regarding the Convening of the Emergency Conference" went out, moving the date from 12 November to 8 November.

{v} Comrade Luo Yinong reached Shanghai a few days before the enlarged meeting of the Party Center that November. Enlai came back to Shanghai then too, but a little earlier. Before the enlarged meeting, Qiubai, I, Old Su (Zhaozheng), Bishi, and Zhang Guotao as well were all there. (Li Weihan, 2002)

On November 9 and 10, 1927, Qu Qiubai presided over the enlarged meeting of the Provisional Central Politburo in Shanghai; Su Zhaozheng, Li Weihan, Ren Bishi, Gu Shunzhang, Luo Yinong, Xiang Zhongfa, Zhou Enlai, Zhang Tailei, Li Lisan, Deng Zhongxia, Cai Hesen, Ren Xu and others took part.

The meeting was convened for the Party to "strive to stir up the revolutionary high tide in town and country everywhere, and create the conditions for a general uprising," and the "Resolution on the Present Situation in China and the Party's Tasks" that it discussed and passed set down plainly a "general slogan of armed uprising" and a "general policy of armed uprising" — yet not one word touched the Central Special Branch. Not only that: the "Resolution on Important Tasks in Recent Organizational Questions" — first discussed and passed by the enlarged meeting of the Provisional Central Politburo, then revised and settled by the Central Standing Committee, and finally passed and issued at the standing-committee meeting of the Provisional Central Politburo on November 14, 1927 — even carried a passage like this:

{v} "In the past the Party has sent members into the organizations of our class enemies (the yellow unions, the scab unions, the Kuomintang party offices and the like) to do detective work, but the results borne out by experience have for the most part been very bad. It is therefore now decided, as a general rule, to abandon this policy; only where there is very great necessity and significance may the method be used on occasion, and the members sent must be comrades truly reliable and faithful to communism." (Central Archives, 1989)

This is put too absolutely, too baldly. Such absoluteness, such bluntness, we can only take to mean that the Central leaders of the time held "detective work" in real contempt, and disdained "to use this method." Their "experience" had made them resolve to "abandon" utterly what they themselves had, only shortly before, written into the resolution of the previous emergency conference (the August Seventh Conference) — the "Resolution on the Party's Organizational Questions" — namely, that "the Provisional Central Politburo should establish a nationwide secret courier apparatus, in liaison with the Publications Committee's work of distributing propaganda, charged with transmitting notices and orders and conveying propaganda, and so forth; and, besides, with the special-service work of ferreting out counter-revolutionary leads and every other kind of information about local conditions." They had decided that "special-service work" was, in truth, "very bad."

But on November 14, 1927, the Provisional Central Politburo held a standing-committee meeting and resolved to reshape the central structure, doing away with departments and setting up instead, under the Politburo Standing Committee, an Organization Bureau — which still bore the functions of leading the organization, propaganda, military, special-service, investigation, courier, secretariat, publication-and-distribution, and accounts sections, and the Women's Committee.

But on December 31, 1927, the Party Center issued Circular No. 25, once again raising the necessity and urgency of planting agents inside the enemy for intelligence work, holding that: "In keeping with the resolution, our Party branches should send one or two loyal comrades into the Kuomintang and its most important reactionary organs, to carry on sabotage and intelligence work there." (Victor Usov, 2013)

And so, in that same period — between November and December 1927 — the Central Special Branch was founded, after all, in Shanghai, under Zhou Enlai's command. The biography *Zhou Enlai* (1998) records:

{v} In November and December of 1927, Zhou Enlai overhauled the Party Center's political-security apparatus, turning the former special-service subsection into three sections — general affairs, intelligence, and action — and later adding a radio-communications section, and set every kind of work in motion: securing the safety of the Party's leading organs, gathering and holding intelligence, suppressing traitors, rescuing arrested comrades, and building a secret radio station.

The "former special-service subsection" belonged to the Central Military Commission Special Branch. Li Qiang — a Special Branch veteran, a former member of the Party's Central Advisory Commission and Minister of Foreign Trade — once said: "The Central Military Commission Special Branch was founded in Wuhan in May 1927. The Fifth National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party had just closed. In view of Chiang Kai-shek's betrayal of the revolution, the rampancy of the reactionary forces and their mad suppression of the Communist Party, and the steadily worsening White Terror, and in order to protect the Party's leading organs, consolidate its organization, keep timely track of the enemy's movements, and wage the struggle against him with force, Zhou Enlai presided in Wuhan over the founding of the Central Military Commission Special Branch, with the Central Committee member Gu Shunzhang as its chief. … The Central Military Commission Special Branch had four subsections: one, the protection subsection, charged with the safety of the central leaders and central organs; one, the intelligence subsection, charged with gathering intelligence; one, the special-service subsection, charged with suppressing traitors and enemy agents, and with such other special tasks as the Center assigned; and one, the bandit subsection, charged with liaison with the various gang organizations and with taking in the stragglers of the bandit bands. … In Wuhan the head of the protection subsection was Li Jianru, the head of the intelligence subsection was Fat Dong (perhaps named Dong Xingwu), the head of the bandit subsection was called Whiskers, and I was head of the special-service subsection." (Li Qiang, 2006)

On August 19, 1988, in a letter to Feng Wenbin — then head of the CCP Central Party History Collection Committee — Li Qiang, coming to the Central Special Branch, stressed once more: "As to the name of the Central Special Branch, a way of putting it has lately appeared, namely to call it the Special Affairs Bureau. In my view the name 'Special Affairs Bureau' does not fit the facts. Before the August First Nanchang Uprising, the organization the Party Center built in Wuhan was the Special Branch, not a Special Affairs Bureau. The chief of the Central Special Branch was Gu Shunzhang. The Special Branch had four subsections: the protection subsection, whose head was Li Jianru; the intelligence subsection, whose head was Fat Dong; the bandit subsection, whose head was Whiskers; and the special-service subsection, whose head was me. The Central Military Commission office was at No. 12 Yujili, Youyi Street, a Shanghai-style shikumen lane house of three upper rooms, three lower, and two wings. A slip of red paper was pasted at the upper right of the doorframe, reading 'west wing.' Comrade Zhou Enlai worked in the upstairs parlor; the Special Branch was in the upstairs east wing, the secretariat in the upstairs west wing, the organization section in the downstairs west wing, and the odd-job people in the downstairs east wing. … In 1981, at a party-history conference, a question about a Special Affairs Bureau suddenly came up, with someone insisting it was a Special Affairs Bureau the Central Military Commission had set up, and not a Special-Service Section. I must state that I was a subsection head of the Special-Service Section, not the chief of any Special Affairs Bureau; I worked only in the Special-Service Section, never in any Special Affairs Bureau. If anyone insists on writing a history of a Special Affairs Bureau, please do not write me into it, for I never worked there. I do not know whether the Special Affairs Bureau was some other organization, who its director was, or who the section chiefs under him were. No such bureau ever existed at Yujili. This summer at Beidaihe I spoke of the matter with Comrade Xu Yixin, and he too remembered only a Special-Service Section. Back in Beijing I wrote to ask Comrade Nie Rongzhen, and his secretary telephoned to say that what he remembered was a Special-Service Section as well. Where on earth did this 'Special Affairs Bureau' come from? I asked Comrade Luo Qingchang, and he said Comrade Zhou Enlai had once used the term in a speech. My thought is that, if Comrade Zhou Enlai did indeed say so, he very likely meant the Special Committee above the Special Branch. That Special Committee was set up in Shanghai from 1929 to 1930, made up of Xiang Zhongfa, Zhou Enlai, and Gu Shunzhang; it never held a meeting. It was Gu Shunzhang who told me of its existence."

The Central Military Commission Special Branch lasted only a little over three months, but it was intensely active, did much useful work, and played a part at once important, singular, and irreplaceable. When the head of the Soviet advisory group, Borodin, was forced to leave the country, for instance, the protection subsection drew thirty men to escort him, and once their task was done they stayed on in the Soviet Union to study political security. When the Kuomintang reactionaries in the Wuhan area, for instance, kept picking quarrels and stirring up incidents in the hope of harming the Party and progressive people in every walk of life, the intelligence subsection could always intercept the intelligence, master the movements, and see into the inner workings, giving the Party firm support in exposing and smashing the plots. When the Wang Jingwei clique, for instance, launched the "July Fifteenth" counter-revolutionary coup and conjured out of a clear blue sky the murderous "separation from the Communists," the scattering of already-exposed Party cadres, the safeguarding of the Party Center's organs, the covering of the Party's leading cadres in their move to Shanghai, and the secret carrying of supplies and even arms — none of it could have been done without the quiet devotion and unremitting effort of the Central Military Commission Special Branch. And "beating the dogs" — that is, enforcing Party discipline and suppressing traitors — needed the Central Military Commission Special Branch's iron fist all the more. In August 1927 the Wuhan Kuomintang issued its "Four Measures for Investigating Communist Party Members," sternly ordering "any person suspected of being a Communist" to "publish within three days a declaration in the press opposing the Communist Party, or issue writings against it." The result was that every day the papers carried notices of leaving the Party — one or two at the least, a dozen or so at the most. Some said: "The undersigned once joined the Chinese Communist Party on another's introduction, and now, differing from it in views, declares his withdrawal." Some said: "I was in the past lured into joining the Communist Party, and never knew what communism meant; now, seeing the Center's order, I understand the Communist Party to be a party that betrays the nation and the people, and hereby declare in the press my absolute break with it." And some not only turned their coats — publicly declaring their withdrawal — but did as Lu Xun described, "either sending in letters to inform, or helping the officials seize men," "shouting 'Long live communism' yesterday, and today going everywhere to hunt down the workers of the communist network." All this made it urgent for Li Jianru, Li Qiang, and their like to draw their swords — to draw the sword that smites evil.

Rarest and most precious of all, in the very time it was drawing that sword against evil and making its name across the three towns of Wuhan, the Central Military Commission Special Branch also built, for the first time ever, the first intelligence relationship in the history of our Party's intelligence work. The man was Mei Baoji.

Li Qiang, in "The Central Military Commission Special Branch and the Central Special Branch as I Knew Them," said: "At the time, we ran Japanese intelligence through Mei Baoji, a former parliament member of the Beiyang era (the uncle of Mei Gongbin and the father of Mei Zhonglin); I would go to see him every few days."

Mei Baoji, courtesy name Jingyuan, was born in 1881, a native of Huangmei in Hubei. He studied in his early years at the Huguang Normal School, then went to Japan to study, and under Sun Yat-sen's influence came to hold the ideals of the democratic revolution.

In 1908, when Zhan Dabei founded the *Commerce Gazette* and made wide friendships among the anti-Qing men of honor, Mei Baoji put himself forward and became an editor. In 1911, on Sun Wu's introduction, he joined the Progressive Association and became its secretary. On 9 October that year, when Sun Wu and others were testing explosives at the headquarters in Baoshanli, in the Russian concession of Hankou, a slip of the hand set off an explosion; the Russian concession police, hearing of it, came swarming in and carried off the rolls of the revolutionary party, the proclamations of the uprising, and the rest. The Viceroy of Huguang, in a panic, ordered the four gates shut and a city-wide dragnet. Mei Baoji got away by luck and hurried to the Wuchang military headquarters to raise the alarm. The uprising forces, fighting with their backs to the river, split into three columns and on the night of 10 October took the viceroy's yamen and the neighboring headquarters of the Eighth Division at a single stroke, then liberated Wuchang, Hanyang, and Hankou one after another, and set up the Hubei Military Government.

Under the Hubei Military Government was an Inspectorate, and Mei Baoji was one of its eight chief inspectors. In February 1912 he was made instead director of the Wuchang Telegraph Bureau, and a councillor of the military governor's office besides, receiving the "Fifth Order of Merit" conferred by Yuan Shikai and an inscribed plaque bestowed by Li Yuanhong, ranked among the heroes of the First Uprising who "cared nothing for money and spared not their lives."

Under the Provisional Constitution of the Republic of China, parliamentary elections were held across the country at the end of 1912, and Mei Baoji, carried by popular esteem, was elected an alternate member of the House of Representatives by a high vote. The next year, when the Hubei Military Government sent Beijing a formal list of 685 meritorious persons for commendation, Mei Baoji again stood out alone, third in Grade A.

In 1914, Yuan Shikai ordered parliament dissolved, promulgated the Constitutional Compact of the Republic of China, and changed the cabinet system into a presidential one. Yang Du, Sun Yuyun, Li Xiehe, Hu Ying, Liu Shipei, and Yan Fu, six men, joined their names to form the "Chou'an Society," and set busily about organizing branches in Hunan, Hubei, Jilin, Fengtian (today's Liaoning), Anhui and other provinces, fervently preaching constitutional monarchy. Mei Baoji too, for a time, lost his head and threw in his lot with the doomed venture: he sent up a petition urging Yuan to the throne, even had a court robe and tablet made in advance, and rehearsed the kowtow at the offices of the *Tianmin Bao* in Beijing.

In March 1916, when Yuan Shikai abolished the Hongxian reign title and the restoration failed, Mei Baoji went south with his wings clipped, and bore the full brunt of public reproach.

Chastened, Mei Baoji turned back from the lost road and returned to Sun Yat-sen's banner.

In 1918, when Sun Yat-sen, Liao Zhongkai and others went south to Guangzhou and set up the "Constitutional Protection Military Government," and secretly ordered Sheng Yanqi to Jingzhou and Xiangyang in Hubei to win over Shi Xingchuan, commander of the First Division, and Li Tiancai, commander of the Ninth, to the revolution, for a "Jing-Xiang autonomy," Mei Baoji too rose up with a will and went to western Hubei to raise forces.

In September 1926, the National Revolutionary Army swore its oath and marched north; with Guangdong and Guangxi for its base it sent troops into Hunan, and, sweeping all before it, took Pingjiang and Yueyang one after another, then Tingsi and Hesheng, and moved smoothly into Wuhan, aided from within by Mei Baoji and his comrades.

In April 1927, when Chiang Kai-shek carried out the "party purge" and the Nanjing-Wuhan split came, Mei Baoji stood on Wang Jingwei's side. That summer, when Wang Jingwei followed Chiang Kai-shek into the "separation from the Communists," Mei Baoji struck the table in a rage, hung up his seal and left, retired from office, and opened a school to take pupils.

Lin Chengxi and Xu Rongsheng, in *The CCP Central Special Branch* (1996), say: "Mei Baoji had knocked about officialdom for many years, and his information was very good. After the Special-Service Section came to know him, it gave him a monthly stipend, and he for his part passed on to the Section's liaison men, as promptly as he could, whatever news he came by."

I would say that Lin Chengxi and Xu Rongsheng are wrong to write the Mei Baoji who "cared nothing for money and spared not his life" as a mercenary peddler of intelligence.

Mei Baoji was upright, open-handed, a man of his own backbone. It was not because the Central Military Commission Special Branch "gave him a monthly stipend" that he "passed on to the Section's liaison men, as promptly as he could, whatever news he came by." The espionage war is full of informants who take the money with one hand and hand over the intelligence with the other — like Xie Ruolin, husband of Wanqiu in the television serial *Lurk*. Xie Ruolin believed in survivalism, not in any Three Principles of the People or communism. His famous line was: "If you don't kill me with that one shot, and I live, we can go on doing business — so long as the price is fair." But Mei Baoji was in no way one of those profit-chasers with the reek of money about them. What led him to build an intelligence relationship with the Central Military Commission Special Branch was his fierce disgust at the "anti-Communist" policy of the Chiangs and the Wangs. He had his own measure of right and wrong. He was a fellow-traveler of our Party.

In the same way, Mei Baoji's nephew Mei Gongbin became a model in the history of CCP intelligence not for any petty gain.

Zhan Wei, in *The Iron-Blooded Meis: The Red Spy Mei Gongbin* (unpublished manuscript), writes: "April 3, 1955 was a day of disaster for Mei Gongbin and for those who followed close about him. That evening, while he was in Beijing for a meeting, he was suddenly arrested in secret, and from then on vanished from China's political stage." Zhan Wei also says: "This was the Republic's first great miscarriage of justice." That "miscarriage" "ran on for as long as twenty-seven years" because Mei Gongbin's luck was cruel and he was caught up in the Pan Hannian affair.

I would say it is true enough that Pan Hannian stands behind Mei Gongbin, and Pan Hannian does not deny that Mei Gongbin was his own "direct contact." But when Pan Hannian was cast into an unjust prison, Mei Gongbin was not much caught up in it, and certainly was not "suddenly arrested in secret, and from then on vanished from China's political stage."

Mei Gongbin — courtesy name Dianlong, also called Yixian, alias Zhang Baisheng — was born in 1901, a native of Huangmei in Hubei. He took part in the May Fourth movement in 1919, passed into the Tōa Dōbun Shoin in Shanghai in 1921, and joined the Chinese Kuomintang in 1923, serving as secretary-general and party-caucus secretary of the Kuomintang's Shanghai Special Municipal Party Headquarters. In 1924, on the introduction of He Chang and Shi Cuntong, he joined the Socialist Youth League, founded the Xujiahui League branch, and served as its first secretary. In 1925 he joined the Chinese Communist Party, and as a leader of the National Student Union threw himself into the May Thirtieth movement; with Liu Hua — a member of the first executive committee of the Shanghai General Labor Union and head of its organization section — and others, he was reckoned one of the "Five Tiger Generals."

In 1926, Mei Gongbin marched north with the army as director of the political department of the Twelfth Division of the Fourth Army of the National Revolutionary Army. On August 1, 1927, when the Nanchang Uprising fired the first shot of armed resistance to Kuomintang reactionary rule, Mei Gongbin, together with Guo Moruo, Yang Hansheng, and Li Yimang, and with the help of the railway workers, rode a handcar to Nanchang and joined the uprising forces. Afterward he led his men on to Hailufeng to take part in the peasant uprising under Peng Pai.

In 1928, sent by Zhou Enlai, Mei Gongbin took up the posts of member and propaganda chief of the Party's Zhejiang Provincial Committee, and helped organize and raise the peasant uprisings in eastern and western Zhejiang.

In 1929, Mei Gongbin crossed east to Japan, and through Hu Qiuyuan of the Shenzhou Guoguang Press came to know Chen Mingshu and Li Jishen.

In 1931, on his return, Mei Gongbin joined the Party's hidden-front struggle under Pan Hannian's leadership. In the "January Twenty-Eighth" Shanghai War he worked hard to bring about cooperation between our Party and the Nineteenth Route Army.

In November 1933 came the Fujian Incident. Li Jishen, Chen Mingshu, Jiang Guangnai, Cai Tingkai and other patriotic generals of the Nineteenth Route Army publicly declared their break with the Kuomintang and set up, in Fuzhou, the People's Revolutionary Government of the Republic of China. Mei Gongbin and Chen Xizhou reacted at once, and without pausing to rest braved the danger and entered Fujian, sharing every hardship and danger with Li, Chen, Jiang, and Cai.

Chen Xizhou — original name Chen Zhaoli, also called Chen Haoren — was born in 1907, a native of Fuzhou in Fujian, and likewise a figure of note in both the history of the Party and the history of its hidden front. He is spoken of, together with Mei Gongbin and Xuan Xiafu (original name Yaohuo, style-name Jianhun, a native of Zhuji in Zhejiang, of Whampoa's first class, who joined the Central Special Branch in 1934 and was murdered by Kuomintang agents in Xi'an in 1938), as one of the "Three Heroes of the War of Resistance."

In July 1935, Li Jishen and Chen Mingshu founded the Chinese National Revolutionary League in Hong Kong, with Mei Gongbin as its propaganda chief, promoting with all his might a "united front, armed resistance to Japan" and "the Kuomintang and the Communists joining forces to save the nation." His lodgings in Causeway Bay thus became one of the important organs of our Party's hidden-front struggle in Hong Kong.

In July 1937, full-scale war broke out, and the Kuomintang and the Communists stood together against Japan and shared the nation's peril. The Chinese National Revolutionary League dissolved itself, and Mei Gongbin held in turn professorships at Jinan University, Sun Yat-sen University, and Dade College in Hong Kong, and a seat on the Planning Committee of the Field Party-Government Commission. With the Party's united-front policy, and with a personal charm and a readiness to take good advice, he formed deep friendships with Li Renren, Chen Cisheng, Yang Dongchun, Lin Chongyong, Liu Yazi, He Xiangning, Peng Zemin, Li Zhangda and other patriotic democrats, so that throughout the war they held closely together around the Party's Central Southern Bureau under Zhou Enlai.

In August 1945, the Chinese people won the final victory of the War of Resistance against Japan. When Sun Yat-sen University moved back to Guangzhou, Mei Gongbin went with it, as acting dean of the law school and head of the economics department.

In July 1947, in Hong Kong, Mei Gongbin restored his connection with Pan Hannian after six years apart, and Pan Hannian directed him to help Li Jishen and others found the Revolutionary Committee of the Chinese Kuomintang.

In October 1947, Mao Zedong issued the *Manifesto of the People's Liberation Army*, calling on the great mass of people who loved peace and stood for democracy to "form, broadly, a national united front, overthrow Chiang Kai-shek's dictatorial government, and set up a democratic coalition government." Mei Gongbin, drafting the Manifesto of the Founding of the Revolutionary Committee of the Chinese Kuomintang, answered warmly, and wrote in plainly that "to overthrow Chiang without at the same time uniting with the country's revolutionary democratic parties would not only violate the Director's Three Great Policies, but would make it impossible even to reach the goal of toppling the Chiang dictatorship." Feng Yuxiang, reading it, praised it highly, saying the Manifesto swept over its subject from a commanding height, in a grand style, with much of "the literary manner of Zhuge, Marquis Wu."

In January 1948, the Revolutionary Committee of the Chinese Kuomintang came into being in Hong Kong, with Song Qingling elected honorary chair, Li Jishen elected chair, and Mei Gongbin a member of the Central Executive Committee.

The founding of the Revolutionary Committee stirred a deep hatred in the Chiang Kai-shek clique. The Kuomintang's agent apparatus at once drew up a long blacklist, meaning by the vilest methods of assassination to block the patriotic democrats in Hong Kong — the Revolutionary Committee's chief leaders among them — from going north to join in the great work of building a new China. The Party Center sent urgent cables to Pan Hannian, Liao Chengzhi, Mei Gongbin and others to mount a great rescue and escort with all their strength. Mei Gongbin himself accompanied Li Jishen by way of Dalian to a safe arrival in Shenyang, and there, with the leaders of the other democratic parties and the patriotic democrats streaming into the liberated areas, put his name to a joint "Views on the Current Situation," declaring plainly their support for the leadership of the Chinese Communist Party and for the terms of peace the Party had put forward.

In April 1949, in liberated Beiping, Mei Gongbin met Li Weihan, then head of the Party's Central United Front Work Department. Li Weihan said: "From now on your organizational affiliation passes to the Central United Front Work Department; you are an undisclosed Communist Party member sent by the Central United Front Work Department to work among the democratic parties." (Mei Gongbin, 1994)

New China was born, and Mei Gongbin's true identity stayed hidden. People knew him only as a delegate to the first plenary session of the CPPCC, a member and deputy secretary-general of its second and third national committees, a member of the Central Financial and Economic Commission, a deputy to the first, second, and third National People's Congresses, a member of the standing committee of the second and third, and, in the Revolutionary Committee, a member of the central committee, of its standing committee, and its secretary-general through several terms — and did not know that he was a member of the Chinese Communist Party, one of the heroes in the history of its hidden front.

On August 1, 1975, Mei Gongbin departed this life, aged seventy-five.

On January 18, 1980, Mei Gongbin's memorial service was held in Beijing. Ye Jianying, Deng Xiaoping, Chen Yun, Ulanhu, Deng Yingchao and others sent wreaths; Hu Yaobang, Song Renqiong, Ji Fang, Wang Shoudao, Hu Zi'ang, Li Weihan, Wang Kunlun and others attended. Ulanhu presided, and Liu Ningyi delivered the eulogy. The eulogy not only declared openly that Mei Gongbin was a Communist, but said that he had joined the Party more than half a century before, and was "a long-tested veteran member and cadre" of the Chinese Communist Party.

When the news got out, few of Mei Gongbin's old friends were not struck dumb. Reading the eulogy set there in black and white in the *People's Daily*, not one of them dared believe his own eyes.

"The rain has passed, yet the fragrance lingers; such purity is not soon forgotten." These two lines were written for Mei Gongbin by Ma Man Kei, vice-chairman of the CPPCC and president of the Macau Chinese Chamber of Commerce. They fit every Communist who has given himself to the hidden front. We revere their "purity." We do not soon forget it. Without their "purity," what "fragrance" would there be to "linger" in the clear, bright world when "the rain has passed"?

### 4. Are You the Man of a Thousand Faces?

The prehistory of the Central Special Branch can be traced back to the Central Military Commission Special Branch of the Wuhan days, and back, too, to the Shanghai Workers' Pickets of the "May Thirtieth" period — for the members of the Jiangsu Provincial Committee's "dog-beating squad" had, almost to a man, been the backbone of the Shanghai Workers' Pickets in the "May Thirtieth" days.

On May 15, 1925, the Japanese capitalists announced the shutdown of the Naigai Cotton Company's No. 7 Mill and barred the workers from entering. A worker of the mill, Gu Zhenghong, led the men in forcing their way in, demanding that work resume and wages be paid. The Japanese taipan (in effect the mill manager) fired on them, wounding more than ten. Gu Zhenghong, four bullets in him, died of his wounds, and the rage of the workers at every Naigai mill in Shanghai was roused. On 28 May the Party Center and the Shanghai organization held an emergency meeting and resolved to mount a great anti-imperialist demonstration in the concessions on 30 May, against the four proposals the International Settlement had put forward for the oppression of the Chinese, and in support of the strike. On 30 May, more than two thousand students from Shanghai's colleges and middle schools fanned out along the busy roads of the International Settlement to spread the word, make speeches, and march. The British Settlement police, long on the alert, opened fire without warning, killing thirteen and wounding dozens; in an instant Nanjing Road ran with blood. This was the "May Thirtieth" massacre that shook China and the world.

The "May Thirtieth" massacre touched off a strike on a still greater scale. To keep order in the strike and guard against wrecking by scabs and hoodlums, the factory unions organized workers' pickets one after another. A factory of about a thousand hands would then have a picket corps of anywhere from a few dozen to one or two hundred. Its members were chosen from the union activists, the Party members, and the League members. Beyond keeping order in their own plant's strike, the pickets were charged with mediating the disputes that arose among the workers and the people.

By late June, the strike had run more than twenty days, and imperialism and its running dogs had stepped up their wrecking. On the one hand they played on the hardship into which the strike had thrown some of the workers, sowing discord in the hope of splitting the workers' ranks; on the other they used hoodlums and scabs to attack, in an organized and deliberate way, the strike leaders, the union heads, and the activists. Our Party then picked out from the pickets a few comrades who could shoot, to punish the hoodlums and scabs who were wrecking the strike in broad daylight and would not mend their ways, and to avenge the strike leaders, union heads, and activists whom those hoodlums and scabs had beaten and gravely injured, or savagely killed.

As it turned out, in doing away with those evildoers the workers and the people were behind them, and public sympathy was with them. The pickets' stepping forward not only struck back at the enemy's schemes and made the strike firmer; it also laid the foundation, prepared the cadres, and opened the way for the Party to have an armed force of its own — to overthrow warlord rule at a stroke in the three armed uprisings of the Shanghai workers, and boldly to found the first people's political power in the history of the Chinese revolution. Most of the pickets played the vanguard in those three uprisings, arming themselves with weapons seized from the enemy's hands. It was these very weapons that kept Chiang Kai-shek and Bai Chongxi in ceaseless dread. Xu Meikun said: "The Party decided at that time to form a special branch, with me as its secretary, to deal specially with the scab running dogs and with the traitors and spies within the Communist Party. Once I had the task, I went to many factories to find reliable workers, and organized a special-agent group, with myself as its head and Li Jianru, Zhang Asi, Xiao Asi and others as its members — this was the beginning of our Party's using weapons against the enemy. At the time we had five sharp British hatchets, and later I bought four Italian-made pistols off an Italian arms ship. To act with pistols was dangerous, likely to give us away, so for the time being we stored the four at Shen Yanbing's home. This secret counter-traitor body — the special-agent squad — was the later 'dog-beating squad'; at the time of the three armed uprisings of the Shanghai workers I was deputy chairman of the Shanghai General Labor Union, and left the 'dog-beating squad,' which passed into Gu Shunzhang's charge." (Xu Meikun, 1985)

Xu Meikun, born in 1893, was a native of Xiaoshan in Zhejiang. He joined the Party in 1922 on Chen Duxiu's introduction, the first worker member in the Shanghai area. He attended the Party's Third Congress in 1923 and was elected an alternate member of the Central Committee, and held in turn such posts as secretary of the Party's Shanghai local committee and concurrently of the Jiangsu-Zhejiang district committee, chairman of the Shanghai Printers' Union, head of organization and deputy chairman of the Shanghai General Labor Union, and chairman of the National Printers' Union. After the founding of New China, he served as a counselor to the Supervisory Commission of the Government Administration Council, to the Ministry of Supervision, and to the State Council.

As for picking out some brave and able men from the workers' pickets to form a counter-traitor self-defense force answering directly to the Party's Jiangsu Provincial Committee — to suppress traitors resolutely, and to strike hard at the local despots and evil gentry who had fled to Shanghai with their fortunes to escape the peasant uprisings — Qu Qiubai and Li Weihan approved of that too. The Party did indeed need someone, they held, to deal specially with enemy agents, traitors, and spies within. But they refused excess. They had Deng Zhongxia join his name to theirs in stopping the spread of slogans like "kill the gentry and landlords wholesale." And so Wang Ruofei — standing member of the Party's Jiangsu Provincial Committee and its head of peasant work — appointed a political instructor expressly for the provincial committee's "dog-beating squad," and charged him again and again: "The members of this squad are all Party men, true; but if their ideological training is not gripped tight, then, once they are armed, they may still make trouble, and your task is to do the political and ideological work among them well. As for the actual operations, the squad leader is in charge; you settle it between you, but nothing may be acted on without approval."

In early November 1927, Zhou Enlai came by boat from Sham Shui Po in Kowloon to Shanghai, and soon gathered the comrades from Wuhan (the Central Military Commission Special Branch) and the comrades of Shanghai (the Jiangsu Provincial Committee's "dog-beating squad") together under the name of the Central Special Branch, calling them all the Third Section — the Action Section, also called the "Red Squad" — with the former conjuror Gu Shunzhang as its head, held concurrently.

Gu Shunzhang was a member of the Party's Fifth Central Committee. On August 7, 1927, at the emergency conference of the Party Center, he was unexpectedly elected to the Provisional Politburo. On November 14, 1928, at a standing-committee meeting of the Party's Central Politburo, Xiang Zhongfa (then chairman of the Politburo Standing Committee), Gu Shunzhang (then an alternate member of the Central Politburo), and Zhou Enlai (then a member of the Politburo Standing Committee) were named to form the Central Special Affairs Committee (the Central Special Committee). And Zhou Enlai chose Gu Shunzhang as his first assistant, to lead the Central Special Branch directly and carry the Party's hidden-front struggle forward on every side.

The Russian scholar Victor Usov insists that the founding of the Central Special Committee was a product of the Party's Sixth Congress. Yet the *Selected Documents of the CCP Central Committee* — compiled by the Central Archives and settled by the Central Party Literature Research Office (now the Institute of Party History and Literature) — carries no such resolution, though it does say the congress agreed to found the Central Special Branch, and does say that "on the model of the Soviet State Political Directorate, a counter-espionage committee was set up, made up of Xiang Zhongfa, Zhou Enlai, and Gu Shunzhang" (Victor Usov, 2007). I have found only, in the Sixth Congress "Political Resolution," a call "to press the arming and training of workers and Party members to the utmost, and to build, so far as possible, a secret armed force for the workers' self-defense (pickets and the like)"; found only, in "Appendix 1" of the Sixth Congress "Resolution on the Draft Organizational Question," a mention of "a very secret committee with an armed squad"; found only, in the Sixth Congress "Resolution on Military Work," that "all the military work of the Chinese Communist Party should be concentrated in the Central Military Department of the Chinese Communist Party," and that "the Military Department should work in a very systematic way, and to that end must carry out the counting and assignment of military personnel, and be able, when needed, to concentrate its strength in a few important centers"; and, besides, the "three tasks and one prohibition" that Li Qiang spoke of — an outline bearing on the Central Special Branch that Zhou Enlai brought back. As the first professional intelligence-and-security body in our Party's history, the Central Special Branch's "three tasks" were: to gather intelligence, and above all intelligence bearing on the safety of the central organs; to punish traitors; and to raise funds and carry out other special missions. The "one prohibition" was: no spying on one another within the Party.

"Three tasks and one prohibition" set the Central Special Branch's sharpest features: a sound organization, strict discipline, bold action, a clear division of labor.

The Central Special Branch had four sections in all. The First was the General Affairs Section, charged chiefly with setting up the Party's secret liaison points, deciding how the Party would operate in the White areas, choosing sites for the important meetings the Center convened and renting the houses, arranging the rooms and seeing to the sundries, rescuing arrested comrades, and handling the last rites of the martyrs. The Second was the Intelligence Section, charged chiefly with selecting the ablest Communists to go deep into the tiger's den and take intelligence straight from the enemy's hands, or to build counter-espionage ties inside the enemy's own intelligence organs, and so keep exact hold of his movements. The Third was the Action Section, charged chiefly with punishing traitors, guarding the Party Center by force of arms, and safeguarding the central leaders, the central organs, and the high-level central meetings. The Fourth was the Communications-Liaison Section, charged chiefly with securing, through underground radio stations and other wireless means, the contact between the Party Center and the Party organizations everywhere, the Red Army in the Soviet areas, and the Communist International.

In the documents of the Communist International the Central Special Branch was rendered as the "Special Work Department." The Special Work Department of the Comintern's Executive Committee, in its "Written Report on the State of Secret Work and Special-Service Work of the Communist Parties in the Far Eastern and Near Eastern Countries" (June 3, 1932), said: "Before the middle of 1931, China had a well-organized body that worked within the Party against spies and traitors and carried on sabotage inside the enemy's organizations and army. Under the (CCP) Center there was a so-called Special Work Department, led for four years by Comrade Gu (Shunzhang). Gu had several permanent assistants, whose work Gu directed and which answered to the (CCP Central) Politburo."

Gu Shunzhang's "several permanent assistants" were plainly Hong Yangsheng, Chen Geng, Li Qiang and the like, who headed the Central Special Branch's First (General Affairs), Second (Intelligence), and Fourth (Communications-Liaison) Sections respectively; the Third (Action) Section was headed for a time by Gu Shunzhang himself.

Zhou Enlai's choosing Gu Shunzhang for his deputy, to run the whole Central Special Branch in his stead, was not without reason. His chief reason for prizing Gu Shunzhang was that Gu Shunzhang, Chen Geng, and Lu Liu were among the very first cadres the Party had sent to the Soviet Union to study political security. As Victor Usov has disclosed, the Soviet Union ran a "school for future potential intelligence officers," opened a "special military training class," and gave "military training to the Chinese revolutionaries who came to Moscow through the Communist International and other channels," and this was "at the request of the Chinese Communist Party's leaders (Zhou Enlai above all)." Victor Usov also says: "The military training was organized by the General Staff." The task "facing" the Soviet instructors was "to train Chinese experts 'who could carry on secret military work under Chinese conditions,'" and to teach the students above all "the working techniques of underground work." Yan Karlovich Berzin, head of the Main Intelligence Directorate of the Soviet Red Army General Staff (formerly the Fourth Department of the Soviet Red Army General Staff, and before that its Intelligence Department), once expressly required the head of the Workers' and Peasants' Red Army Political Academy, in "training Chinese experts 'who could carry on secret military work under Chinese conditions,'" to "give due weight to the education of the leaders of underground work, of the leaders of the party committees' military departments, and the like." Berzin also gave a particular reminder: "The theory and tactics of armed uprising, the theory and tactics of peasant guerrilla war, the techniques of underground work, and other such courses, should take an ever more important place in the teaching syllabus."

What was called the "techniques of underground work" was the art of deep cover.

The art of deep cover was a required course for the "future potential intelligence officer."

The "future potential intelligence officer" had to learn disguise. For to lie in deep cover is to go under, to hide oneself within the enemy's camp and show the world a false face.

If learning disguise was the first article of the red agent's creed, then how could the supreme commander of the red agents fail, before a cunning enemy, to change at a word and pass the false for the true?

From the turn of autumn and winter in 1927 to the turn of summer and autumn in 1931, Zhou Enlai worked chiefly in Shanghai. On February 29, 2008, in a speech at the symposium marking the 110th anniversary of Zhou Enlai's birth, Hu Jintao noted that after the Party's Sixth Congress Zhou Enlai was "the leader who in fact presided over the work of the Party Center." Because Zhou Enlai was a known leader of the Communist Party, the prime target of the reactionary forces at home and abroad, hunted and wanted with a vengeance; and because throughout the Great Revolution he had held open leading posts, so that many in the Kuomintang knew him by sight — he had to come without a shadow and go without a trace, forever changing his name. He could only shift his lodgings now and then — ten days or a fortnight at the most, a few hours at the least — and hardly anyone knew for certain, once he had gone out, whether he would come back to the same place for the night. His outings, barring the exceptional, were held strictly to between five and seven in the morning or after seven at night. He never took a public tram or went where the crowds were thick and the eyes many. What he pored over most in print were city maps of every edition. What he studied hardest were the tangled, far-reaching streets and lanes. He planned the best route for each of his outings with care, always avoiding the main roads, taking the alleys instead. He usually got himself up as a big boss or an industrialist. After he had grown a full beard and looked more the warm-hearted man of the greenwood, the comrades in the Party took to calling him, affectionately, "Lord Hu." (CCP Central Party Literature Research Office, 1998)

Every culture has striven to exalt the perfect man who keeps faith, who resists oppression or stands against brute force. Look across history, and countless men of high principle, men of "give me liberty or give me death," held under cruel torture to their ideals, held to their convictions, their will truer than fine gold, their hearts like solid rock. Li Dazhao and Fang Zhimin were such models; Xiang Jingyu and Jiang Zhuyun were such examples too. They trembled with feeling at the injustice done in the world; we revere them for refusing that injustice. The harder it is for us to stand in society as persons in our own right, the more we prize the assertion of an independent self. There is a seven-colored light in the sky, and heroism is not of one kind only. The man who "faced heaven and laughed, the blade at his own throat" was indeed a giant who stood between earth and sky — but so, no less, is the great hero who, lying hidden, going under, daring to disguise himself and skilled at it, "carries a twig in his beak to the end of time in a body but an inch long." Life is a stage writ large, and there must always be someone to play upon it. Daring to disguise oneself, and being skilled at it, may not always bring the effect one hoped for; yet the treacherous, shifting weather of the hidden front makes sure there will always be a reason for the daring and the skill of it. And so a sixteenth-century Latin maxim — *Qui nescit dissimulare, nescit vivere* ("he who does not know how to conceal does not know how to live") — sways to this day the history of intelligence and secret agents, in every age and every land.
