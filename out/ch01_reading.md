## Chapter 1. New Waves

One day near the end of the third month of Tenshō 10 — the year 1582 — was drawing toward its close.

That spring, people said, the cherry trees had bloomed everywhere in Kyoto and beyond with a wild, almost frantic abandon. Under the power of Nobunaga — who sat in his great castle at Azuchi with the whole realm under his eye — the townsfolk had been enjoying a rare run of settled, untroubled days, and out they poured to Ōhara, to Daigo, to Arashiyama, to see the blossoms. One sigh of admiration piled onto the next until the word went round that this year the flowers had opened more beautifully than ever.

Today those same blossoms had already scattered and fallen. From a beggars' hut beneath the Gojō Bridge, a single beggar came crawling out. Hair like dried river-weed, thick with dust, hung down in matted strands, and the face that showed dimly through it was, on its left side, hideously burned and raw. The right side was crusted with grime, but it was the face of a young woman. The good right eye and the puckered, drawn left eye did not match, and both were dull and clouded. Yet look closely and you understood: they had been so wholly given over to a grief and despair too deep to sound that they could take in nothing more of the world.

From under the rags she wore — these too hanging down like river-weed — came two legs like a pair of charred bones stood on end. The left one seemed to be hurt; she dragged it behind her, leaning on a stick, hauling herself along at a slow crawl.

The beggar woman followed the bank of the Kamo River as far as the foot of the Shijō Bridge, then turned off west from Kawaramachi and went along Tako-Yakushi Street.

Just then, threading clean through the dull, heavy evening air, came the strange notes of a bell. Strange, though only in a manner of speaking: for five years now it had been a familiar sound to the people of this quarter of Kyoto. What made it "strange" was that it was utterly unlike the countless temple bells of the city. It was not low and solemn like a temple bell but light and high and quick — a sound that seemed to rejoice, to play, to laugh.

Two young women coming east along the street, on the point of passing the beggar woman, heard it and stopped dead in their tracks.

"The bell for the Angelus," one of them said.

With that they pressed their palms together and turned their faces to the sky, and in one voice they began to recite their orasho, their prayer:

"O saving Victim, who openest the gate of Heaven — because the armies of the enemy press hard upon us, lend us thy power and come to our aid, amen. To the one Lord in whom the Three Persons dwell, be praise without end; and grant us, we beseech thee, life everlasting in Paradise, amen."

Then the two of them walked on past the beggar woman, who had come to a halt as well. She too had stopped and put her hands together — but her lips stayed shut.

To the people passing back and forth, a figure at prayer like this was evidently nothing out of the ordinary; they spared her no more than a glance.

The beggar woman's slow feet reached their destination at last.

Before her stood a gate like a Chinese-style karamon, its black tiled roof sweeping up at the eaves. Beside it ran a row of low shops, their plank roofs weighted down with stones, selling brightly colored Nanban hats and contas — rosaries — and crosses. At the foot of one of the gateposts stood a jar with a mouth about a foot across, and beside it a signboard, lettered in a beautiful hand: "A vessel for the rice given in alms to the poor, for the garça — the grace — of Deus."

Passing through that gate, she came into a forecourt walled off with pale-green plaster, and directly ahead stood a second gate, a size larger than the first.

Just as the beggar woman reached it the bell fell silent, and now a music arose stranger and finer even than the bell. It welled up from a whole tangle of instruments — organ, viola, harp, lute (a thing like a guitar), gravicembalo, forerunner of the piano, rebeca, forerunner of the violin — a complex braid of sound that seemed to drift, to float, to weep, to rejoice, that raked at the breast and sank into it with a strange, unnameable feeling.

The beggar woman crouched in the shadow of the gatepost and covered her ruined face with both hands. Look closely and you saw that the left hand was festered dark-red to the elbow, and every one of its five fingers was barely half its proper length.

To the music was added a chorus of voices so clear and pure they seemed to belong to another world.

It was the boys' choir, innocent of every stain, singing the Ave Maria.

The beggar woman did not, of course, understand the Latin. But she had heard it many times, in halting Japanese, from Urugan-Bateren — Padre Organtino — and so she knew it for a song in praise of Lord Jesus Christ, who saves the poor and the afflicted, and of his mother, the most merciful and gentle Maria. And as she listened, the tears came without stopping, until the rags over her knees were soaked through.

Before long the music drew out its last long chord and died away into the air. The beggar woman got to her feet and came up to the three-storied tower that soared before her.

I keep reaching for the word "strange," and there is no better one: this too was a strange building. White walls, pillars blue as the deep sea, long eaves that jutted out and curled upward — and the ground floor a great chapel that could hold several hundred worshippers.

The second floor was ringed on all four sides by a railed veranda, and inside was divided into six rooms, the priests' living quarters.

Higher still it rose, and from up there one could look out over the whole capital and, far off, the fields beyond it.

And crowning the whole tall building, like the last stroke of the work, a golden cross climbed high from the roof-ridge and pointed at heaven.

This was Kyoto's first Christian church, finished five years earlier. It had gone up on a vermilion-seal warrant from Nobunaga, out of the pooled offerings — money, timber, labor — not only of the Christian lords of the nearby provinces but of Murai Sadakatsu, the shoshidai of Kyoto himself, and of countless believers, high and low.

And because the day Francisco Xavier — the first European priest ever to sail to Japan, thirty-four years before — had landed at Kagoshima happened to be the fifteenth of August, the Feast of the Assumption of the Virgin, the church was named Nossa Senhora da Assunção, Our Lady of the Assumption. But the people of Japan bluntly called it the Nanban-ji, the temple of the southern barbarians.

After Xavier, missionaries came over in a steady stream from Spain, Portugal, and Italy, and by their efforts the faith spread swiftly across the whole country. Like the Ikkō sect before it, it sank first and deepest into the peasants who suffered most — because it was, at bottom, an ideology of human liberation: before God, it held, all people stand equal, with no distinction of class or rank, of high or low, of man or woman.

There was more. Where the Ikkō sect asked only that you chant the nenbutsu, nothing else, the Christian faith arrived furnished with a whole picture of the cosmos that the science of the day had no way to refute — a story of how all things were made, the earth and mankind among them; a vision of the world beyond death; and, at the last, a prophecy of the end of the universe itself.

It carried a stern moral code as well. Strict monogamy, the keeping of the Sabbath, the ban on worshipping other gods, the ban on usury — rules that ran head-on into the ideas and habits of the Japanese of that time, and that got in the way of the teaching's spread.

Hideyoshi, for one, after hearing it all set out in detail by Padre Gaspar Coelho, said: "This teaching is surely finer than the teaching of the bonzes. Everything you have told me of it satisfies me completely, and I would find nothing at all hard in becoming a Christian — save the one commandment that will not let a man keep many wives. Relax me that single point, and I will turn Christian." Nobunaga's eldest son, Nobutada, likewise told the irmão, "While that commandment stands, I cannot become a believer." And it is beyond doubt that this same rule kept many a townsman from the faith.

Yet on the other hand, for a great many people those same rules were exactly the new attraction.

As he was seeing off the worshippers who had finished the evening service, a foreign priest appeared in the doorway. He was tall enough that you looked up to him, and — as an old book called the Raichō Jikki has it — "his head small, his color ruddy, his eyes round and yellow, his nose high, his ears reaching to his shoulders, his mouth wide, his teeth white as snow like a horse's, his nails long like a bear's, his hair and beard the color of a mouse; he seemed about fifty years of age."

When the priest caught sight of the beggar woman, he opened that red mouth of his and said, "Oh — Tamo, so you've come. Why didn't you go on inside?"

Padre Organtino crouched down to talk with the beggar woman more easily, so that his head, its crown shaved round in a tonsure, came down below the level of her eyes.

Into the eyes of the beggar woman he had called Tamo came a shy, childlike smile. It was a fragile thing — a single sharp word would have wiped it out in an instant — but for this one moment, at least, it had the strength to push back the grief and despair that filled her.

Besides this three-storied main hall, the Nanban-ji had five other buildings: a refectory, a school, a guesthouse for honored visitors, an infirmary for the sick.

It was into the school that Organtino led Tamo. "School" here mostly meant teaching the music for the choir; the real school was at Azuchi.

Nobunaga, taken with the rare and wonderful treasures of Europe the missionaries brought over along with their doctrine, meant to use this newly arrived faith to sap the strength of Buddhism. And so in Tenshō 7, at Organtino's petition, he had a broad stretch of paddy filled in — north of the Minami-shindō, below Azuchi Castle — and a three-storied wooden building raised on it, the ground floor a chapel and thirty-four rooms above. This he made a seminário, a training house, and enrolled a crowd of high-born sons, among them retainers of Takayama Ukon. Organtino himself became its rector; the Kyoto Nanban-ji he had entrusted, ever since Eiroku 7 — nineteen years back — to Luís Fróis, who was in Japan, though he shuttled constantly between the two.

This Azuchi seminário turns up as an engraving in a book kept in the archives at Modena, in Italy — the Compendium of the Great Deeds of Pope Gregory XIII — where it appears as a wholly Western three-storied building of stone, complete with a square medieval bell-tower. But that was drawn from imagination by an artist who had never laid eyes on the thing actually built at Azuchi, which was in truth a Japanese temple-style building with only the faintest Western flavor added.

The Christian schooling rose by stages — casa de probação, the preparatory house; noviciado, the novitiate; seminário, the training house; colégio, the college — with the colégio's graduates placed in formal teaching posts. Even before the Azuchi seminário opened, the first seminário and colégio in Japan had been founded at Arima; then came a colégio at Funai in Bungo, and a noviciado at Usuki. Great numbers of students bent diligently over retórica (rhetoric), filosofia (philosophy), lógica (logic), matemática (astronomy and mathematics), cânones (canon law), teologia (theology), Portuguese, Latin, instrumental and vocal music, painting, and copperplate engraving.

In Tenshō 9, in a report the Vice-Provincial, Coelho, sent home about how the Arima seminário was faring, he wrote: "These boys" — the youths who are the seminário's students — "are exceedingly well-mannered. In these two years we have not once had cause for displeasure, nor has one of them uttered an unruly or improper word. They live in perfect peace and concord, and in humility and depth of faith they are reckoned no less than the noviciados themselves. The pupils are so eager at their studies that they far exceed our hopes: in a mere few months they master reading and writing, and it cannot be denied that they surpass the boys we train in Europe. They divide their hours — part of the day for reading and writing the Japanese script, part for Latin — and those with energy to spare, hard as Latin is for them, become good Latin scholars through memory and application, in the same time as our own boys or even less. They have learned, too, to sing to the organ and to play the cravo, and there is already a sizable choir that can sing a formal Mass with ease."

When the Azuchi seminário was finished, Nobunaga came to see it one day. Watching the young men at their eager studies, he praised the new-style school warmly; and when he heard Jerome Itō — son of Itō Yoshimasu, the daimyō of Hyūga — play the clavichord, he showed, they say, great satisfaction.

The rooms of this Nanban-ji, floor on floor, had been laid with tatami given by a certain widow.

It was at the close of the previous year that Organtino had first come upon this beggar woman.

Ask her what he might, she answered not a word and only tried to bolt. Coaxing and cajoling, he had brought her to the temple, had her washed, treated the terrible burn-scars — however had she come by them? — and given her rice and clothes.

But the clothes he gave her were snatched from her by someone, and she went on wearing the same old rags as before.

Only about two months ago had she finally begun, a very little, to speak. By what she said, it seemed she had been caught up in a battle at some fortress high on a mountain, and had come through something dreadful. The place had been put to the torch and wiped out; and as she went down in the flames and smoke, someone had caught her up and held her fast — covering her small body completely with his own, and, burning to death himself, shielding her from the fire. That was how her left hand, her left leg, and half her face came to be burned; she lost consciousness. A cloudburst the next day, it seems, brought her back to her senses. The one who had shielded her with his body — who he was, she keeps her lips sealed and will not say — she buried somewhere, and then, a beggar now, drifted her way into Kyoto.

Everywhere there were castles falling, and with each one a countless multitude — young and old, men and women — died, or were maimed, or starved, or simply vanished.

Organtino — who over long years had preached to numberless souls in Europe, the southern seas, and the East, and who understood the fine workings of the human heart — never once pressed her. He only comforted her gently, and tried, each time, to soak a little deeper into that tightly shut heart.

Out of a true and living faith, the padres had crossed ten thousand leagues of storm-driven sea and come the whole way to Japan, careless of the danger to their lives, to bring the grace of God to peoples they called unenlightened. They wound rope about themselves for a sash, lived in rough plank huts, and preached.

But they had soon learned that nothing came of it unless the rulers were won over first, and so they had made believers of a great many rulers — bearing themselves with ceremony, offering rare treasures for gifts, handing out the profits of trade. For the peasants, meanwhile — "slaves who toil for the samurai and the bonzes and keep for themselves only the very worst," as Vilela's report has it — they healed the sick, gave rice and money, took in the aged and the widowed and the orphaned, and comforted body and soul alike. They turned the side-by-side bonds of the peasant villages to account, too, and organized confraternities: the misericórdia, the brotherhood of charity; the brotherhood of Santa Maria; the sexta, or Friday, societies.

Could it really be that so many of them never grasped that this work of good will was doing the errand-work of their home governments' wars of conquest? For all that they had been shown — as they pushed on through India, Java, Borneo, Indochina, the Philippines — one sickening living example after another of rulers turning a country into a colony, it seems that in truth they never grasped it.

Or rather, they did not read it as colonization for the sake of plunder at all. They believed, no doubt, that they were gathering the peoples of heathen darkness into the ranks of the civilized, Christian nations.

And yet in plain fact these missionaries — who took on fearful hardship of their own free will, and gave up even their lives — were serving the rulers' design: first seize the soul, and then the country.

When Tamo had her rice and her medicine from Organtino, she went trudging off from the Nanban-ji. Barely a chō away the tall roof of a great temple reared up, glaring across at the Nanban-ji's three-storied tower.

A flock of crows swept cawing over the two roofs and made for their roosts on Mount Hiei.

This was Honnō-ji itself, the head temple of the Hokke sect. That a bare three months from now history would swing on its great hinge with this very place for its stage, Tamo of course had no way of guessing.

But before the great thing that was to come at Honnō-ji, history had already set another stage, for another event, away to the east in Kōshū.

Ever since its rout at the battle of Nagashino — where it lost the greater part of its veteran commanders and elder retainers, and an army of more than twenty thousand men — the house of Takeda had been lying low, cast off even by the Hōjō and the Uesugi. But that year, in the first month, its vassal Kiso Yoshimasa of Shinano went secretly over to Nobunaga, and Katsuyori — Shingen's son and heir — moved to bring him to heel. When Yoshimasa appealed to Nobunaga for aid, Nobunaga thought "here is my chance," and poured troops into Shinano with his son Nobutada in the vanguard, while Ieyasu answered the call and pushed on into Suruga.

The castles on Katsuyori's side fell one after another, and when even his kinsman Anayama Baisetsu surrendered, he fell back on Kōfu with a bare forty men left to him. Now every trusted retainer he leaned on turned traitor, and though at the last he tried to shut himself up on Mount Tenmoku, east of Kōfu, there was no holding out. On the eleventh of the third month he took his own life, together with his wife and his kin.

So the house of Takeda came to its end, and its domains passed into Nobunaga's hands.

Sasaki Jōtei and other beaten Takeda commanders fled to Erin-ji, a temple Shingen had held in deep devotion, and its abbot, the priest Kaisen, sheltered them for a time. Enraged at this, Nobunaga drove Kaisen and every last one of the temple's hundred and eighty souls, young and old alike, up into the mountain gate, banked grass and straw and firewood beneath it, set it ablaze, and burned them all to death. It was at that moment that Kaisen spoke the famous verse: "Quench the mind, and the very fire is cool."

On the twenty-first of the fourth month, no sooner had Nobunaga made his triumphal return to Azuchi than he ordered Nobutaka and Akechi Mitsuhide to put down Shikoku, and Mitsuhide went back to Sakamoto to make ready for the field.

On the night of the fourteenth of the fifth month, the people of Kyoto were thrown into an uproar of alarm by a great comet that appeared in the sky. It dragged a long, long tail behind it, and when it had still not faded the next night either, people decided that heaven must have hung the star there to tell them something — though what it meant to tell, they could not make out. "What is it?" "What's going to happen?" they whispered to one another.

Down on the dry bed of the Gojō, gazing at this strange star for the first time in her life, Tamo was carried clean away — ah, what a beautiful thing. Having nothing left in the world to lose, she had, perhaps, no need to fear anything at all. Only — ah, if I could show it to Kashii too, she thought, with a piercing ache.

Kashii — that was the name of the young man who had made a shield of his own body and kept Tamo from the fire.

In the Iga basin, in this spot and that, the two of them had often watched the stars together — sometimes the whole sky a lavish, endless spill of them, sometimes only a lonely star or two coming out red in the early dark.

To Tamo they were beautiful things, shining and wheeling of their own accord in some far, far place that had no bearing on the world of men; but to Kashii they were an important instrument of the shinobi's art.

The arts of evasion are the very heart of ninjutsu, and taken all together they go by the name of the Five Escapes.

The outer five are the escapes by wood, fire, earth, metal, and water; the inner five, the escapes by man, bird, beast, insect, and fish.

Subdivided again under the three heads of heaven, earth, and man, they run:

Heaven's, ten in all — the escapes by sun, moon, star, cloud, mist, thunder, lightning, wind, rain, and snow.

Earth's, to which the outer five belong, broken down into ten — by tree, grass, fire, smoke, earth, house, metal, stone, water, and hot water.

Man's, which are the inner five, broken down into ten — by man, woman, old, young, noble, base, bird, beast, insect, and fish.

It was from his uncle Kasumi that Kashii had had his teaching in these arts. "Now, the art of evasion is the art of turning the false into the true and the true into the false" — that was the first phrase they had made him recite. To make the false look true and the true be mistaken for false, and by that to slip away: the means used in the doing are these several categories.

The art of wood-escape, for instance, means suddenly toppling a stacked pile of timber to drag every eye toward it while you get away, or swarming up a great tree with a hand-hook and hiding in the high branches — that kind of thing. Fire-escape means raking dead leaves together, setting them alight, and bolting the opposite way.

Metal-escape turns the glint or the ring of something metal to account; man-escape means fleeing in the guise of another.

The stories of Nikki Danjō changing into a rat, or Jiraiya working the sorcery of the toad, are inflated fictions spun out of the beast-escape branch of man-escape — the trick of loosing rats or toads to snag the eye and slip off.

Among the ten methods of heaven-escape is the art of star-escape. From of old the stars had been studied with a scientist's eye by the Egyptians and the Arabs, and until the compass was invented they were mankind's one guide across the seas; but they had also been taken for something occult, and had bred a tangled thicket of superstition — China's yin-yang and five-elements lore, the Shinto star-festivals, the divinations of the onmyōji — which had seeped into the way of the mountain ascetics, itself one of the roots of ninjutsu. What served no practical end fell away of its own accord, though, and in the Iga school star-escape had never come to much.

Kashii, too, was a master of the beast arts, but at star-escape he seemed a poor hand; all he had taught her was the courses of the Big Dipper and of Venus, Saturn, and Jupiter, and how to fix one's direction by them.

So to Kashii the stars were a thing there was still study to be done of, and yet no real way to know them — a vexing business. And so, even lying side by side to watch the stars, the two of them were forever thinking at cross-purposes, and their talk never quite met.

Around the middle of the fifth month a rumor went round that Ieyasu, at Nobunaga's invitation, would be coming up to the capital to take in the sights of Kyoto, Ōsaka, Nara, and Sakai, and that the Nanban-ji was to be given over for his Kyoto lodging.

At that time the church had in it the padre Cariam and the irmãos Lourenço and Bertolameu, while Organtino was away at Azuchi.

On the twenty-first, word came that Ieyasu had started up for the capital, and at the Nanban-ji they were at their wits' end over how to receive him. But in the event Ieyasu never lodged with them at all: he put up elsewhere for a couple of days and then went off to see Sakai, and one and all breathed again.

On the twenty-ninth, Nobunaga set out from Azuchi and took up quarters at Honnō-ji — a stone's throw from the Nanban-ji — while his son Nobutada lodged at Myōkaku-ji, over by the Kurama gate.

Just then urgent calls for reinforcement were coming in from Hideyoshi, who had pushed into the Chūgoku country and was flooding out Takamatsu Castle; so Nobunaga had sent off Takayama Ukon and a great many men, and kept only a bare handful of guards about himself.

On the night of the first of the sixth month — the twentieth of June by the solar calendar — the muggy heat that had hung over the town of Kyoto since daylight would not slacken; the city tossed and turned, unable to sleep, and dropped off only as dawn came on.

At the Nanban-ji, Padre Cariam, who had got up while it was still pitch dark to say the dawn Mass of the second, was changing into his vestments.

Then, all at once, someone was hammering at the gate. When the gatekeeper opened it, five or six of the faithful came tumbling in, the color gone from their faces. "It seems something dreadful's broken out at Honnō-ji," they said. "It looks to be a grave matter, so you'd do best to hold off a while on the preparations for Mass."

Straining to listen, one could indeed catch a tumult of noise and voices. Before long came the crack — crack — crack of bursting gunfire, and at last, with a great whoosh, flames leapt up.

The converts who came running up behind were crying, "It's Koretō!" "It's Lord Akechi!"

Mitsuhide — who by Nobunaga's own order should by now have marched off to reinforce the Chūgoku front — had turned traitor without warning and thrown a ring around Honnō-ji.

That anyone would rise against a man whose very name set people trembling was a thing not one soul had dreamed of.

And so all Kyoto stood simply thunderstruck; the cry that wanted out froze in the throat.

The padres went white.

Nobunaga was a cruel man, true enough; but he was, for all that, the protector of the Christian faith. It was only because he had given his leave that the Nanban-ji stood at all, and the Azuchi seminary with it. And he was, besides, the easiest of men to win over — weak, utterly weak, for anything rare and strange.

Take, for instance, the visit the year before, in the third month, when the visitador — the inspector Valignano — went up to Azuchi for his audience with Nobunaga and brought along a black man he had carried with him from Africa. Nobunaga was astonished beyond measure, and could not for the life of him believe the man was black by nature and not smeared over with ink; in the end he had him stripped naked to a loincloth and ran his hands over the whole of him, and only then was satisfied.

What was more, the black man was strong, could do a turn or two, and spoke a little broken Japanese, so that Nobunaga was more delighted than ever and never tired of talking with him. He took the man for his own and made him a body-servant, kept him always at his side, and was hugely pleased whenever people gaped — so that Valignano gained no little credit by the gift.

Nobunaga had this black man with him when the blow fell at Honnō-ji.

The black man stood and faced Akechi's soldiers as they poured in, but they were unnerved by him and gave way, none daring to close with him, so that he broke calmly out through the ring and fled to the Nanban-ji.

Tamo, who from the dry bed of the Gojō had seen the flames going up over the quarter of the Nanban-ji and had come dragging her body to the spot, all but ran full into this vast, coal-black shape at the temple gate; she loosed a shriek that tore from her throat and dropped in a dead faint. For what she had seen was a phantom — the charred corpses that had lain heaped all about her when she came back to life at the Momochi fort, rising up of a sudden and bearing down on her.

The converts carried Tamo inside and tended her.

The church was crowded already: the beggars and the sick who were fed there day by day had fled in in throngs, in dread of the fighting.

In those days the common people of Japan were wretchedly poor. An infant that could not be aborted, nor strangled at the birth, was set out as a foundling; lepers and others in the grip of grave disease were driven from their homes; famine came round again and again, and many starved.

The Christians could not look on all this and pretend not to see it, and so, in one place after another, they raised free infirmaries and foundling homes and homes for the aged.

The hospitals and old-age homes the people called yōjō-ya, "health-houses." And, as the jar at the Nanban-ji gate bore witness, they carried on a wide work besides, taking in alms and dealing them out to the poor.

The year after the affair at Honnō-ji, a hundred converts came together at Nagasaki as dues-paying members and founded a misericórdia — a charitable society that did its good works out of the funds they raised. People called it the "charity-house," and in time the like was set up at Funai, at Kyoto, at Ōsaka, at Sakai, and elsewhere.

So it was that the padres and irmãos even picked up a rough practice of medicine, and with their own hands would wipe and wash the pus of a leper.

This kind of social work went far beyond anything the Ikkō sect had done, and it was, in the end, one more thing that won them their crowds of converts.

By the black man's account, when the rebel army came surging up, Nobunaga had only just woken.

Still in his nightclothes, he threw the sliding door roughly open — and from the soldiers already loose in the inner court, arrows came at him thick as hail. One drove into his shoulder; he pulled it out with his own hand, caught up his long sword, and flung himself on the attackers. But before long a bullet went through him somewhere, and he reeled and toppled backward. He had strength enough left to slide the door shut; what came after that, the black man says, he does not know.

The converts, though, kept bringing in word by the minute, from every side. The mansion of the shoshidai, Murai Sadakatsu, stood right before the gate of Honnō-ji. He had tried to get inside, but seeing it hopeless with Mitsuhide's men swarming everywhere, he raced the four kilometers to Myōkaku-ji to carry the news, and urged Nobutada that Myōkaku-ji could not be held — he must fall back into the Nijō Palace.

In the Nijō Palace was Prince Sanehito, son of the Retired Emperor Ōgimachi; they moved him across to the Imperial Palace, and shut themselves in with a bare five hundred men.

By now it was full day. To see the flames roaring up from Honnō-ji, and the host that had ringed it raising its victory cry and rolling on toward the Nijō Palace, was to know past all doubting that Nobunaga was dead.

And indeed the enemy commanders were calling out, this one and that: "The Minister of the Right" — that is, Nobunaga — "is no more!" "Let the Third-Rank Middle Captain" — meaning Nobutada — "make up his mind at once to meet his end!" "How long will you not have done? It is an unseemly sight!"

The Nijō Palace had a narrow moat and low walls — no fastness to hold off an army. When soldiers climbed to the roof of the Konoe mansion that adjoined it on the north and loosed fire-arrows, the gable of the main keep's study burst into flame. It was beaten back; but by the last third of the hour of the Dragon — nine in the morning — the fighting here too was over. Nobutada killed himself at the last and put the palace to the torch; and when the main hall of Honnō-ji, that had blazed so long, burned through and fell in, there was no more fear of the fire carrying to the streets around.

But the converts at the Nanban-ji could not be easy in their minds. Mitsuhide had no love for the Christian faith, and the faith had lived under Nobunaga's protection; surely, they thought, he would burn the Nanban-ji down.

But Mitsuhide sent a proclamation through the streets — he would on no account burn the town, and would take no one's life so long as they raised no hand against his soldiers — and at that they could breathe again.

Even so, his men were going house to house through the town, routing out the survivors of Nobunaga's party, striking off their heads and bringing them in, until the heads made a hill before Mitsuhide and the bodies lay abandoned in the streets.

The black man spoken of before had, out of curiosity perhaps, slipped from the Nanban-ji before anyone noticed and wandered as far as the Nijō Palace; Mitsuhide's men seized him on the spot and dragged him before their lord. Mitsuhide gave him one glance and spat out: "Hm — so the Minister of the Right doted on such a creature as this, and for it came himself to a charred and miserable end. A thing like this is a benighted savage that knows nothing, and no Japanese at that. Hand him over to the Nanban-ji this instant!"

And so, his arms still bound behind him, the black man was sent back to the church.

The converts lost no time in giving thanks to Deus that, through all this great upheaval, church and faithful alike had come through unharmed.

But they were not altogether at ease. For it was a certainty that Mitsuhide would fall upon the Azuchi seminary, and the seminary stood hard beside the castle.

And so their thanksgiving ran on into a petition — keep the Azuchi seminary safe, we beseech thee. Three messengers had already been sent off to carry word of the affair to the seminary.

And in fact Mitsuhide, once he had performed the victory rite before the burnt-out ruins of the Nijō Palace, put himself at the head of his troops and set off for Sakamoto, his own castle.

By noon the people of Azuchi had caught the rumor that something terrible had happened at Honnō-ji and that Nobunaga was likely dead; but no sure word could reach them. This was because the Seta Bridge — reckoned the finest bridge in Japan, thrown by Nobunaga across the Seta River where it empties into the southern end of Lake Biwa — had been cut down by one of Nobunaga's own generals to slow Mitsuhide's march on Azuchi.

So the rumors of catastrophe swelled and swelled, until all Azuchi was in an uproar such as men picture the Last Judgment to be.

Organtino and the rest, certain that Mitsuhide would burn Azuchi to the ground, took hurried counsel and resolved to put the seminary's twenty-eight pupils aboard a boat and flee with them to Oki-no-shima, an island out in the lake — for a man of that island had come and, all kindness, urged them to it.

Padre Francisco Fróis carried a crucifix with the holy image upon it, a little statue of the Virgin, silver candlesticks, a censer, sacred plate; Padre Pereira too was laden with altar-vessels. But many of the townsfolk had already turned thief: Pereira had his hat and his cloak torn off him, and the very sleeves and hem cut from the robe beneath, and the others too were robbed of this and that. Still, they got aboard at last and reached Oki-no-shima — only to find that the folk they had taken for kind souls were in truth pirates.

The pirates herded the converts into a stable and, in a cold manner utterly unlike their first, demanded half of everything they carried. After no end of wrangling it was settled, at last, by the payment of ten ryō in gold.

But Organtino did not trust them, and had one of the converts steal the costliest of the sacred vessels away in the dead of night and hide them on a hill in the middle of the island. At the same time, one of the seminary's pupils — a boy named Araki, who happened to be the nephew of one of Akechi's own generals — got a message smuggled out to his uncle; and the uncle came to Oki-no-shima with soldiers and brought the converts and their sacred vessels off to safety.

Meanwhile, the repair of the Seta Bridge was finished on the fifth, so Mitsuhide and his men came into Azuchi, broke open the castle storehouses, and in two or three days shared out among themselves the whole hoard of gold and silver that Nobunaga had been fifteen years amassing.

Mitsuhide strictly forbade the setting of fires, and after three days of feasting shouldered the plunder and departed for Kyoto. Whereupon looting and highway robbery broke out on the instant, and the seminary was stripped bare — all but a few altar-vessels and books — the window shutters and even the linings of the walls torn away, till nothing was left of it but its pillars and its roof-tiles.

Hideyoshi, the moment word of the disaster reached him, patched up a hasty peace with the Mōri — on terms that had Shimizu Muneharu, commander of the Takamatsu castle he had been flooding out, cut open his own belly — and on the seventh drove through wind and rain back to Himeji, and on the morning of the ninth set out for Kyoto. On the afternoon of the thirteenth the two armies joined battle at Yamazaki; in a little while Mitsuhide's army broke, and Mitsuhide himself, fleeing under cover of the black night, was killed near Ogurusu by peasants out to strip the armor from beaten stragglers.
