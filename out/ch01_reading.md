## Chapter 1. My Childhood

*To the tune "The Immortal by the River": High Ambitions, Admiring Mulan*

*In youth, before the family hall, I diligently asked the meaning of words, / dimly looking up in reverence to the worthies of old; / Mulan's deeds I kept engraved upon my heart. / After I took up arms in the Northern Expedition / and entered the Party, my resolve grew firmer still.*

*Guiding others, the greater was my joy to meet a Bo Le who knew my worth; / stern teachers and helpful friends spurred me on. / Following the age, I wrote a new chapter. / That Women's Day of years past— / the grand assembly, unequalled before or since.*

My home county, Liuyang in Hunan province, is not only a place of clear hills and bright waters but also one rich in men of letters and talent, and it has produced many well-known figures in China's modern history. Tan Sitong and Tang Caichang, who in the last years of the nineteenth century gave their lives, one after the other, in the cause of reform to save China, were both men of Liuyang. My father, together with Tan Sitong and Tang Caichang, was a favored disciple of the local Confucian scholar Ouyang Zhonghu. Ouyang Zhonghu was the grandfather of the famous dramatist Ouyang Yuqian; deeply learned in Confucian studies and of a progressive, forward-looking cast of mind, he had a considerable and positive influence on the formation in his disciples of a will to seek progress and to dare to change. After the great Chinese Communist Party was born in the 1920s, Liuyang became, moreover, one of the advanced districts of the Chinese revolution. In the autumn of 1927 the famous Autumn Harvest Uprising that Comrade Mao Zedong led with the sons of the three Xiang regions broke out at Wenjiashi in our Liuyang, a place that has since become a protected site of important revolutionary relics and a base for patriotic education. What the people of Liuyang take pride in is that many of them followed Mao Zedong in making revolution, braving death for decades and making great contributions to the founding and building of New China; among them a whole cohort grew into senior leading cadres of our Party, government, and army—men such as Hu Yaobang, Wang Zhen, and Wang Shoudao, and New China's first woman general, Li Zhen. No wonder that "Liuyang River," the song celebrating Liuyang's glorious revolutionary tradition, has become one of the finest of China's revolutionary folk songs, savored to this day and destined to be handed down forever.

Liuyang is not only a glorious holy land of revolution; its economy and culture were also developed comparatively early. Ordinary farm and sideline produce was abundant, and it was famous besides for its summer (hemp) cloth, its firecrackers, and its Hunan embroidery; even under the Qing it was a county with a trade surplus. The local customs were simple and honest, the people industrious and brave. Hunan's handmade Xiang embroidery is an internationally known craft, and a girl, once she reached nine or ten, had to learn to embroider. In every household the men went down to the fields to work; the women, when their housework was done, would be spinning and weaving, or dressing hemp, or embroidering. In not a few homes everyone, old and young, man and woman, used their spare time to make fireworks and firecrackers by hand. Even in the old society there were very few beggars who lived by begging rather than by productive labor. In our Hunan the people's minds were opened comparatively early too: even a rickshaw puller, or a porter, or a coolie laboring on the docks had, from childhood, to learn to read, studying primers like the *Three-Character Classic* and the *Thousand-Character Text*. Because the women all took part in labor and were not looked down on within the family, the bad habit of prizing sons over daughters was also far less common here than in other places.

My great-grandfather, Ruilin, was a physician who kept an apothecary's shop. All his life he loved to do good and to give: for poor and suffering patients he not only gave free consultations and medicine but would sometimes add a few maces of silver, charging their families and children to go home and serve their sick elders with filial care. Those poor patients not only got medicine to treat their illness but gained a little nourishment besides, and often recovered the faster for it. And so the poor families of the surrounding country were deeply grateful to my great-grandfather. From wealthy and powerful households, however, the fees he charged for a house call were very steep. Because his medical art was superb, the rich were only afraid he would not come, and dared not call him dear. When my great-grandfather died, many peasants from the various villages who had received his kindness came to our house to wail and mourn. Because all his life he had loved to give, the family's savings were spent down to nothing, and by my grandfather's time the household had declined. My grandfather's given name was Huanwu, his courtesy name Maoliang; though the family's circumstances grew more and more straitened, he still carried on my great-grandfather's tradition and did many good deeds in his life. My grandmother, née Liu, was a cultivated woman, a model virtuous wife and good mother of the old society. Her own family was quite well-to-do and her dowry comparatively rich, which was a great help in easing our family's steadily declining poverty. Because money was tight at home, she spun and wove every night far into the night to supplement the household. My father was an only son, and my grandfather and grandmother loved him dearly. He was born in 1873 (the 12th year of the Tongzhi reign of the Qing); his original name was Zuxun, and after the founding of the Republic he changed it to Huang Zheng, courtesy name Yingchu, pen name Yuyuan. Clever by nature, he was praised from childhood as a "prodigy," and at the age of twelve he composed a "Poem on the Cricket":

*Cricket, cricket, chirp on chirp— / deep in the night you should fall silent. / Mother's toil, alas, might be given rest: / by the window the cock already crows.*

The gist is that the cricket should stop its chirping, for outside the window it is nearly dawn, while Mother has spun and woven the whole night through and is very weary, and ought to be let rest in peace... This poem shows, on the one hand, my father's youthful talent, and on the other his filial feeling in praising my grandmother's tender love and diligence.

In his teens my father passed the examination for the xiucai degree. Afterward my grandfather thought of taking him out of school to be an apprentice in a shop. Fortunately my grandmother could not bear it and appealed to her own family for help. My maternal grandfather and uncles had always been fond of my father, and they gave money to let him go on with his schooling; for this my grandmother sold the jewelry from her dowry. Worn out by overwork, my grandmother fell ill and died at fifty-one.

My father's first wife, née Liu, bore no children; his second wife, née Li, likewise had none. Fearing that the Huang line would fail without an heir, once my father's period of mourning was over, my grandfather had him marry again—a third wife, who was our own mother. Her surname was Li, her given name Wan, her style name Qinxiang; she came from a middle-peasant family. My maternal grandfather was also a physician, and my mother was his youngest daughter, so my maternal grandmother doted on her greatly. My mother was only eighteen when she married into the Huang family; my father was fifteen years her senior. He treated her very well: seeing that his bride had bound feet, he at once cut off the binding cloths and threw them away, letting my mother unbind her feet and be spared the suffering of footbinding. From the day she entered, my mother ran the household. My father made his living by teaching, and his income was thin; my mother was his able helpmeet and skilled at cookery. Those my father associated with were all noted men of the day, fond of gathering in literary societies; every fine day of spring or autumn they would go out on excursions arm in arm, walking there and back. My mother would prepare the food hampers—wine and dishes, and refreshments. They would sit on the ground, drinking freely and talking grandly, chanting and answering one another in verse. Both my parents played chess well; content in the Way and at peace in poverty, they treated each other with the respect of guests, and did not let the frustrations of examinations and office weigh on their hearts.

To change the bad custom of prizing sons over daughters, my father, together with his fellow townsman and schoolmate Tan Sitong and other progressive people of Liuyang, once ran a foundling home for the express purpose of taking in abandoned baby girls. By the custom of our Liuyang, when a daughter married, her family took no bride-price from the man's family but had rather to send a dowry along with her; so a girl born was called a "money-loser." To bear only one daughter was no great difficulty. A second daughter could still be managed. But by the time a third daughter was born, the family would cry that they "could not afford it," and would often drown the baby girl at the mother's bedside, or abandon her by the roadside to live or die. My father and Tan Sitong and the others preached in their home district that everyone has a right to live and that drowning baby girls was a crime. The practice of drowning girls was gradually given up, but the abandoning of them still happened often, and so they opened a foundling home for the sole purpose of taking such infants in. In ordinary times its gate was kept shut, with only a single window opening to the outside; outside the window stood a wooden box for abandoned infants, so that poor families could leave a child there by night. Some families would write the child's birth date and the eight characters of its horoscope on a strip of red paper, so it might be taken up for adoption, though very few had already been given a name. In this way the home took in, one after another, several dozen abandoned baby girls, and had them adopted out to families without daughters of their own—a charitable work in its day. Tan Sitong admired my father's talent and engaged him as a private secretary. A private secretary was in earlier times commonly called a shiye—the equivalent of today's secretary—and the retainer's fee was more generous than a teacher's. When Tan Sitong was transferred to Nanjing to await appointment as a prefect, my father went with him, sailing down the Yangzi with the current; on the boat the two exchanged many poems and lyrics. My father gathered his own compositions into a "Mid-Route Collection," but later, having to flee from danger, he lost track of these poems; in the *Yuyuan Poems* now published only the title of the collection survives, its contents missing. In recent years I have seen that part of them are in fact preserved in Tan Sitong's collected remains. Besides handling the daily correspondence, my father also helped Tan put in order his *Study of Benevolence*; and Tan wrote a laudatory preface for my father's book, *A Simple Method for Discerning Characters by Their Sounds*. Tan further charged his beloved son Huaisheng to observe the etiquette of a disciple toward my father—which shows how deeply they knew each other.

In the spring of 1897, the year before the Hundred Days' Reform, my father, deep in filial longing, set up a spirit tablet at the first anniversary of my grandmother's death to make a distant sacrifice to her; he prostrated himself and wept, and grieved himself into a derangement of the mind. Tan Sitong had no choice but to send men to escort him home, and so he was unable to follow Tan to the capital. Fortunately my grandfather was a hereditary physician of Chinese medicine, and with careful treatment my father recovered. The next year the reform failed, and Tan and the others were cruelly put to death by Cixi. On hearing the terrible news of Tan's death, my grandfather, wary of the Qing court's literary inquisitions and fearful of being implicated, went off with my father to travel far in separate directions, to keep themselves safe. The martyr Tan had prepared himself in spirit for death. At the time Liang Qichao had taken refuge in the Japanese consulate, and the gallant Wang Wu, "Wang of the Great Sword," and others urged Tan to flee; but he said, with stirring passion: "There can be no revolution without bloodshed. If I do not sacrifice myself, who will? To awaken the people through the shedding of blood is worth it." At the time Tan's father was governor of Hubei, and Tan deliberately left on his desk the family letters in which his father and wife had admonished him not to engage in reform, so that his family might escape being implicated. Tan and five others were beheaded at Caishikou in Beijing—the famous case of the Six Gentlemen in history. Tan Sitong went to his death with composure, leaving behind the celebrated quatrain written on the eve of his execution: "Fleeing to seek shelter, I think of Zhang Jian; enduring death a while, I wait like Du Gen. I face the knife and laugh up at the sky; those who go and those who stay—two Kunlun peaks of loyal heart."

In the first year of the Xuantong reign (1909) a special grace examination was held. This was one of the by-paths to distinction in the examination system of the day: on the nomination of each province, well-read xiucai who had failed the higher examinations could go up to the capital, together with the juren, to sit the metropolitan examination. Those nominated had to be persons of good character and learning both. My father too was nominated, and he passed as an "excellent tribute student" (yougong). The yougong degree was granted once in twenty-four years, the bagong once in twelve; such a special degree was not easily won. My father won it, and in the Huang ancestral hall a flagpole and a yougong plaque were raised; the whole clan took pride in it, and a poor scholar's house was promoted to a family of the study's fragrance. At the time my father was also announced as a candidate for the post of assistant salt commissioner for Guangdong and Guangxi; but with officialdom then so corrupt, how could a man with no money, no power, and no back door pass from candidate to substantive office? Out of dissatisfaction with the political corruption of the Qing dynasty and its weak, humiliating diplomacy, he still kept up secret contact and effort with his schoolmate Tang Caichang; but the plot was not kept close, Tang too was killed, and my father was entrusted with the grave charge of the orphan Tang left behind. Such were the ties—of teacher and disciple, and of families joined across generations in revolution—between my late father and the martyrs Tan and Tang and their orphaned children.

After Tan Sitong's martyrdom, my grandfather, who had gone far from home to keep out of the way, met an eminent monk of the Way in Nanchang. This monk could not only cure the sick but was also deeply versed in astrology. From him my grandfather learned something of medicine, divination, and the reading of the stars, and returning to our home in Liuyang he gave himself all the more wholeheartedly to doing good and accumulating merit. Our Liuyang is famous for making fireworks, and at every New Year and festival every household would set off firecrackers, so that not a few children were burned by them. Following a folk remedy the monk had taught him, my grandfather would gather the herb called liujinu, wash and sun-dry it, then steep it in a jar of tea-oil; when someone burned by firecrackers came to ask for medicine, he would ladle out a few spoonfuls from the jar according to the severity of the injury, and within a few days the wound would heal. I remember that as a child I often helped my grandfather in this good work of giving out medicine to treat burns!

My mother too loved to do good. After she married into the Huang family, though the household was not rich, every summer she would brew tea with boiling water in a large crock and set it out at our front gate, offering it free to passers-by to slake their thirst—the charitable act known as "giving tea-water." From my great-grandfather, my grandfather and grandmother, down to my mother, three generations did this without fail. This was the constant tradition of charity and giving in our Huang family. Put in today's words, it was carrying forward the fine moral spirit of our Chinese nation—to delight in helping others and to be kind. As the saying goes: a house that accumulates good deeds is sure to have blessings to spare.

I was born on the ninth day of the sixth month of the 33rd year of the Guangxu reign of the Qing—that is, on 18 July 1907—in the Huang ancestral hall outside the north gate of Liuyang. When I was born, our family had not a single mu of land nor a single room of its own; we were a poor scholar's house. After me came, in turn, two younger sisters and six younger brothers, though one brother and one sister died soon after birth. By the generational ranking laid down in the clan genealogy, the names of our generation all carried the character "Zhang"; I was named Zhangding, style name Shuyi. My first younger brother was Zhangchuan, my sister Zhangrong, my second brother Zhangren, my third brother Zhangxin, my fourth brother Zhangjian, my fifth brother Zhangwei. The Huang family, which for several generations had passed through a single son, in my parents' generation had two daughters and five sons about their knees—which may count as a flourishing house.

I was the eldest daughter, and because my father was already over thirty when he had a daughter, he doted on me like a pearl in the palm. Though I was born into a poor scholar's family, neither of my parents held the idea of prizing sons over daughters; my father had even freed my mother's already-bound feet, so of course I was spared the suffering of footbinding altogether. When I recall the doting love I enjoyed from my parents in my childhood, I feel my early life was extremely happy: I did not know that there was such a thing in the world as worry, or sorrow, or anything to grieve the heart.

My eldest younger brother, Zhangchuan, was three years younger than I; two years after him came my sister Zhangrong. My mother kept the household going with hard, thrifty labor, and because she could not manage it all, she hired a nanny to cook and wash clothes and to grow some vegetables in the little garden behind the house; drinking water was carried in by another hired hand, several loads of it every day.

To this day I still clearly remember my mother taking me and my brother back to her own family to visit. My maternal grandfather's home was in the countryside of Liuyang's eastern district; in those days a middle-peasant family's house was quite good, with many rooms, a large open space in front for drying grain, and a courtyard where they kept chickens and ducks and had planted a few peach and pear trees. The country women, old and young alike, still bound their feet; by old custom the binding began at six or seven, and it was extremely painful. To force two natural soles into "three-inch golden lotuses," the bones of sole and toe had to be broken by main force—how could it not be agony? When I went to my grandmother's, I saw that the elder cousins, a few years older than I, had all bound their feet; only I not merely had unbound feet but ran about freely playing with the boys, and they all thought it a great novelty. My mother would then spread what my father said about the good of unbinding women's feet: that men and women ought to be equal, that women must first be freed from the pain of footbinding, and that this was not only good for their own bodies but good for women's taking part in productive labor, and could raise women's economic standing within the family. That I was a girl with unbound feet, who loved besides to sing and to read—these too were among the strange things by which the country cousins felt that we "city people" were different from them, which reflects, I suppose, how great the gap between town and country then was.

Because we could seldom get out to my grandmother's in the country, we were "rare guests," and my maternal grandmother was especially warm and loving toward us, giving us good dishes every day—chicken, fish, meat, more than we could eat. My mother ran her own house very sparingly; at home we ate meat only on the first and the fifteenth of the month, and killed a chicken only when a guest came—the rest of the time it was vegetarian fare. We Liuyang people treat the ox as the chief tool for plowing, and in the country the killing of oxen was forbidden, so there was no beef to eat. Only in the two seasons of spring and autumn each year, at the great ceremonies sacrificing to Confucius, was the ox used as the principal offering—called the tailao (the sheep being the shaolao)—and then an ox might be killed. Once my father had become a yougong, he was qualified to take part in the sacrifices to Confucius, and when the rites were done, those who had taken part could each be allotted a share of the beef. This beef became a great rarity, and a mark of pride for a "family of the study's fragrance" in the old society, not something an ordinary household could enjoy. Yet this most precious sacrificial beef was the one thing I could not eat. I am a bit odd: from birth I would not eat beef, and if I did I would vomit. This was a habit formed from infancy—not, as it happened, something that came only after my father later urged me to take up Buddhism. When we returned to the city, my grandmother would have us take home the country produce we liked, especially the dried bamboo shoots my father loved best, and dried vegetables made by drying fresh eggplant, winter melon, and cowpeas.

After the 1911 Revolution, the Northern Warlords headed by Yuan Shikai soon usurped the fruits of victory; only the signboard was changed—to the Republic of China—while politically things remained very dark and corrupt. At this time my father's thinking turned to a belief in "saving the country through industry." He served two years as head instructor at the Yuelu Academy, whose director was Tan Yankai, the military governor and provincial governor. My father proposed to Tan the mining of antimony and the building of railways. After the antimony venture failed, my father was squeezed out and resigned in indignation. Prizing his talent, Tan transferred him to the Zhuping Railway as head of the documents section. The Zhuping Railway Bureau was a third-class bureau, and a section head's monthly wage was 60 silver dollars, which in those days was no small sum. My father came home only once every two years, and sent 30 dollars home each month. We lived in the common quarters of the Huang ancestral hall and paid no rent. Prices then were cheap: a picul (dan) of rice, 150 catties, cost only three dollars; a nanny's wages were very low too. In her spare time my mother did fine needlework—embroidery, papercutting—and had the nanny take it to the street to sell, which brought in a little to help the household. Because my mother kept house thriftily and reckoned every penny, she could even lay by a little surplus each month.

From childhood my memory was exceptionally strong. At three or four my father taught me to recite Tang poems, and I could soon reel them off fluently; once I started school, whatever a teacher taught in a single lesson I could recite after reading it only two or three times. My mother had not studied before she married; afterward my father taught her to read the "Four Books for Women," which she too could recite, though the toil of housekeeping left her no leisure to practice writing. When I was in primary school and my father was away at work, my mother would dictate and have me write her letters to him in her stead; when I met a character I could not write, I had to draw a circle in its place. In each reply my father would correct these characters I could not write or had written wrong, one by one, and would praise me besides for being careful in my reading and writing! In Liuyang, on the fifteenth of the seventh lunar month each year, the rite of "burning packets" to sacrifice to the ancestors was held (this is the Buddhist Ullambana festival): the names of several generations of ancestors were written on paper bags, which were burned after the worship. By the age of six or seven I could already copy out the characters from a model. When I think back on the joy of asking after words before my parents in childhood, the aftertaste is sweet to me even now!

Before the third grade I attended the little primary school in front of the nearby Huoguan Temple, going in the morning, coming home at midday to eat, and going back in the afternoon. Later I entered the higher primary school inside the Yingfo Temple in the west of the city; it was some distance from home, so I took my lunch and ate it at school. My mother loved me dearly, and though we often ate only vegetarian food at home, she would often put some very fragrant fermented-bean-steamed cured pork in my lunch box, which I was especially fond of. This higher primary school of mine was a girls' school; its honorary principal was Tan Sitong's widow, and she had engaged several women teachers returned from study in Japan, of very progressive mind and resolved to remain single for life, taking pride in devoting their whole lives to women's education and to winning women's rights to equality and freedom. These teachers left a deep impression on my mind—how they gave themselves to women's education, how they cherished their students and never beat or cursed them as the old private-school masters did. Even when a student did occasionally do wrong, she was only required to admit the fault in public and then made to stand straight in class for five minutes, which was mortifying enough. Such a method of teaching led the students to form good habits of self-motivated diligence and self-respect, and made them love and respect their teachers all the more. I remember that at each class the monitor would call out: "Rise! Attention! Bow!"—and the teacher would bow in return to the students. This was a very fresh thing in those days. In primary school my marks in Chinese, gymnastics, and every other subject were very good, and I graduated first. When the school's odd-job man brought the diploma to my house, he set off a long string of firecrackers. My mother was of course delighted, gave him a red packet, and set off firecrackers at home as well. This in turn stirred the neighbors, who all said that my graduating first from higher primary was just like passing the xiucai examination in the days of the old examination system, and came one after another to congratulate us. Each neighbor who came with congratulations brought us a small string of firecrackers; and my mother laid on three tables of food and wine to entertain the congratulating neighbors and villagers. This too shows how honored—and respected by the neighbors—we first graduates of that girls' school of ours then were.

The year I was eleven I finished primary school, just when the Northern and Southern warlord factions were fighting confusedly over Hunan: the Hunan Army under Zhao Hengti drove out the Northern Army under Zhang Jingyao. Zhang Jingyao's soldiers were exceedingly bad, raping and looting and doing every kind of evil, and the common people both hated and feared them. When word came that Zhang Jingyao's routed troops would pass through Liuyang in their flight north, every family, supporting the old and leading the young, fled to the countryside to take refuge. My great-grandmother's family had a relative surnamed Zhou living at Tangjiazhou across the river from the south market, off the main road the routed troops would take, and my mother took the whole family to the Zhous' to shelter. The Zhous were a middle-peasant family, half fishing and half farming; being near the river, they usually netted some fish and shrimp, living off the water, and also grew some grain and vegetables, having their own house and more than enough to live on—a most honest and generous family, who received our whole household, come to shelter with kin, very warmly. Toward night, word suddenly came that a few scattered routed soldiers of Zhang Jingyao's might come drifting this way, and we had no choice but to slip out through the night with the Zhous and hide behind a small hill. My mother's milk was then not enough, and for fear that my third brother, hungry, would cry and draw the routed soldiers to the sound, I had to chew a little milk-cake to feed him. That night's tense flight, and the atrocities I heard people tell of—warlord soldiers preying on the people and doing every evil—gave my small heart a dim understanding of the reactionary warlord forces that oppressed and exploited the people in this society, and planted in me a defiant resolve that when I grew up I would firmly oppose and root out the evil power of the warlords.

That flight passed off safely in the end, alarming but without real danger, and after taking our leave of the Zhou relatives we soon returned home. My mother, thrifty as she always was, had with great difficulty saved up two hundred silver dollars; before fleeing she had hurriedly put the dollars in a porcelain jar and buried it under the chicken coop where we kept the hens. The first thing she did on getting home was to hurry and check the jar buried under the coop, and only when she saw the dollars still lying just as they had been did she set her heart at ease. Because of this experience, our family was very grateful to the Zhou relatives, and at every New Year and festival my grandfather would go to the Zhous' to pay his respects and take gifts. When anyone of their family came to our house, my grandfather and my parents treated them with great courtesy and warmth, and the feeling between the two families was always good. Not long after the business of hiding from the routed troops, Hunan was overwhelmed by floods, and my father, who was by then working at the Canton–Hankou Railway Bureau in Wuchang, sent someone to bring our whole family to live in the railway's apartments at Xujiapeng in Wuchang. My grandfather, old and attached to his home country, would not leave Liuyang and stayed for the time being in the old home; the rest of us thus said farewell to our beautiful, beloved native Liuyang.

The Canton–Hankou Railway Bureau was to build the line between Wuchang and Changsha, and Zhan Tianyou, the director in charge at the bureau, transferred my father to work at the Canton–Hankou Railway Bureau set up at Xujiapeng in Wuchang. Zhan Tianyou was China's earliest railway engineer; he had directed the building of the first railway China constructed on its own—the Beijing–Zhangjiakou line (today's Beijing–Baotou line, the Beijing-to-Zhangjiakou section)—and in building the grade at Badaling he had, adapting to the terrain, used a "zigzag" (the character ren) line to reduce the volume of work, and a "vertical-shaft construction method" to dig the tunnel and shorten the schedule; a bronze statue commemorating him still stands there to this day. My father had done excellent work at the Zhuping Railway Bureau, drafting its railway management regulations and its system of personnel rewards and penalties; these were submitted by the Canton–Hankou director Yan Deqing and by Zhan Tianyou to the Ministry of Communications, and once approved were in force nationwide for decades. My father also wrote a "Proposal for National Railway Construction," for which the Ministry of Communications awarded him a bonus of 500 dollars and a "Striped-Tiger Medal." Later, however, with the warlords fighting year after year, many of the plans in this proposal could not be carried out. Zhan Tianyou, admiring my father's ability, promoted him to be head of the documents section at the Canton–Hankou Railway Bureau. The Canton–Hankou Railway Bureau was a first-class great bureau, and my father's wage rose too, from 60 dollars a month to 120. He worked at the railway bureau eighteen years in all, his wage starting at 60 dollars and rising in the end to 180. In those days railway employees' wages and welfare were as good as a bank's, far more generous than in other departments; besides the high wages, there was a year-end bonus, so working there was called in society an "iron rice bowl of gold." Our family passed from poor scholars to a household of modest comfort, entirely thanks to my father's rising wage and my mother's thrift. My father's idea of "saving the country through industry" was one expression of the patriotic ardor of many Chinese intellectuals of the day. He made great contributions in his post, but the social conditions of old China could not let his talent be fully displayed, and his ideal of "saving the country through industry" naturally could not be realized. In the latter half of his life my father became a railway employee, a brush-and-ink brain-worker to the end, an enlightened and patriotic man.

We lived in Wuchang for a good ten years or more—first at No. 50 of the Canton–Hankou Railway apartments, later moving to No. 12. Apart from half a year in the first half of 1919 at the Zhounan Girls' School in Changsha, I lived in Wuchang the whole time, not leaving until 1927, so it counts as one of the places I lived longest. I was very good at picking up local dialects, especially as a child, when it came quickly and easily. In Changsha I learned the Changsha speech; in Wuchang I soon learned to speak Hubei. In the dormitory quarter at Xujiapeng of the Canton–Hankou Railway Bureau lived a number of staff who had returned from study in America, of various native places, and from these neighbors I learned Shanghai speech, Suzhou speech, and Cantonese. My mother was never good at dialects, and in dealing with the neighbors, the language being a barrier, she needed me beside her each time to interpret. Though the various dialects I spoke were not very standard, later, when I joined the revolution and went to many places, being able to understand many northern and southern dialects was a convenient and useful skill for keeping up my work contacts. Among these neighbors who were my father's colleagues was a traffic-department head surnamed Xiao, also a returned student, and a close friend of both Zhan Tianyou and Yan Deqing and of my father. He had seven daughters, all very clever and well educated. One of them, some ten years younger than I, was Xiao Shufang, who later became the wife of the famous painter Wu Zuoren. She herself was a noted painter too, but as she was still young then, neither of us left much impression on the other.

During my father's service at the Canton–Hankou Railway Bureau, though he still could not fully use his gifts, his work was very steady. He went to work each day, at first riding the hand-pushed "rocking cart" on the rails, and later buying a rickshaw of his own (a privately used one was called a "contract cart"). It happened that a cousin of mine, Pan Bingwen, had come to the Canton–Hankou Railway Bureau to look for work and had not yet found a job, so he pulled the cart taking my father to the bureau and was in charge of bringing my father his lunch; later my father got him a job in the Canton–Hankou Railway Bureau. We still kept a nanny at home then, mainly for washing, sweeping, and other household chores; the cooking my mother did herself, for her culinary skill was very fine. The several hired hands who had worked in our home were all honest people, and my parents treated them with warmth and care; though it was a relationship of employment, in daily life they were treated as close as family, with almost no distinction of master and servant.

A year after we moved to Wuchang, my cousin Pan Bingwen went back to Liuyang and brought my grandfather too, and the family was reunited in the joys of kinship. My grandfather died at seventy-eight, when I was already seventeen, so my impression of it is deep. Skilled in medicine, divination, and astrology, he often said he wanted to pass it on to me; but because I had already taken in new ideas and thought such things all superstition, I would not learn it, and he did not force me. My grandfather had long since told his own fortune before he died. He called me to his bedside and said: "You are my eldest granddaughter, and I love you best. You were unwilling to be born and will be unwilling to die"—because when my mother bore me she was three days and three nights in labor, so my grandfather said I was "unwilling to be born"; "unwilling to die" meant I was sure to be long-lived—"but your life will be very rough, though your old age will be very happy. Your mother is with child now and cannot manage my funeral, so it can only fall to you, my eldest granddaughter, to help see to my after-death affairs." I told my father what my grandfather had charged, and my father said there was nothing for it but to obey. He put into my keeping the clothes and other things my grandfather was to be changed into at the end, and had my cousins come to help as well. I sat by my grandfather without the least fear. My father did not go to sleep either; we all kept by my grandfather with tears in our eyes, and he too kept back his tears and said nothing. My father lit incense and silently recited the Guanyin Bodhisattva's *Heart Sutra* and the *Rebirth Mantra*—which, by my grandfather's account, were all better than weeping. Before he passed, my grandfather had me offer him three cups of the "rising wine," and so we saw the old man off. When I recall my grandfather's blessing of me in those years—just as he foretold, my life has passed through countless storms and hardships, and now I have lived past ninety, which may count as long life indeed.

At fifty-odd my grandfather, unable to forget my grandmother's deep affection and virtue, kept faith and did not remarry. He was kind to others and never advertised himself. His one indulgence was the cup; he styled himself the "Wine Immortal." Filial by nature, my father prepared for him jars of yellow wine of fifty catties each; needing no fine dishes to go with it, he would help himself as the mood took him, free and easy, taking his own quiet pleasure in it. He used to say that gulping and heavy drinking harm the stomach and dull one's palate for good wine; a slight tipsiness was best. Wine could lift the spirits; to drown one's cares in wine only deepens them—that is the self-torment of the literati school. My father admired the never-flagging manner handed down from Li Bai, and had a broad, contented, easygoing heart; I too dearly love to chant Li Bai's "Bring in the Wine," and in a lifetime of social drinking have never been badly drunk, never once lost my composure, and can find pleasure even in adversity, bitter as it is—all of which may come down to me from my grandfather. To this day I still cherish the memory of the kind face and voice of that old man.

In his hours off work my father loved to chant verse, exchanging poems now and then with noted scholars of the day such as Fan Fanshan, Yi Shifu, and Yuan Lianren, published in the *Fulun* monthly run by the Ministry of Communications. He also compiled, by rhyme, the famous lyrics of the ages, revising the draft several times and settling on the title *Prosodic Standard for the Forest of Lyrics*. I remember helping to copy it out as a child, but the final draft was all in my father's own hand, which shows how conscientious and painstaking he was in his scholarship. Through the years of war it was thanks to my mother and my fourth brother, who protected my father's manuscripts in every way, that they survived. After my third brother made his fortune, he paid for the work, and my fourth and fifth brothers took part in editing it; it was first published in Taiwan, and after my rehabilitation it was reissued by Zhonghua Book Company and presented to the libraries of the noted universities on the mainland that have Chinese departments, as a reference for lovers of classical prosody, poetry, and lyrics. When my fifth brother went back to Liuyang, he presented the book to our home region, placing it in the Dafudi, the former residence of the martyr Tan, both to treasure the friendship of the older generation and in the hope that later generations would carry on the tradition and grow ever more flourishing.

At my father's side, through constant exposure, my love of poetry and lyrics was nurtured. Yet my father did not teach me the rudiments of composing them; he said that even a great woman lyricist of her age, like Zhu Shuzhen or Li Qingzhao, had only used poetry and lyrics to record the desolation of a beauty's hard fate. He urged me instead to read history, and to read the *Biographies of Exemplary Women*. He held the martyr Qiu Jin in the deepest reverence, and was an enlightened father who supported my turning toward revolution. While I was at home, busy cramming my own lessons and tutoring my younger brothers and sisters, I had no leisure to take up poetry and lyrics. Only later, when I was able to study under the masters Yu Biyun (Jieqing) and Mao Guangsheng (Heting), did I learn the rudiments of the art.
