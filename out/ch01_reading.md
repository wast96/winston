### 1. A Poor Household: Leaving Home for Wuhan at Thirteen
Shangyu is a canal town in Zhejiang, a little more than seventy kilometers from the city of Hangzhou. Southwest of the town, at Xiaoxiantan in Shangpu, lies the site of an Eastern Han celadon kiln. The vessels dug from it are mostly stamped with geometric patterns or incised with rippling water lines: jars, washing basins, bowls, dishes, the porcelain fine and close-grained. Many Eastern Han celadon kiln sites have come to light in the county in recent years, and Xiaoxiantan is the most typical of them; they prove that Zhejiang was already firing celadon under the Eastern Han. Five kilometers to the northwest lies the famous White Horse Lake, once called Yupu Lake. It is more than twenty kilometers long, ringed on three sides by rank upon rank of hills, its water a clear jade. Thirty-six ravines from those hills gather into it. Guisi Hill, Yang Hill and Yue Hill stand in its waters, and the fishing hamlets, farmsteads, and fields around it compose a scene worth painting. According to the Commentary on the Water Classic, the embankment kept collapsing when the lake was first dyked, so the people sacrificed a white horse to it; and that is how it got its name, White Horse Pool. Here, on October 7, 1906, Chen Yangshan was born.
His birth-name was Cheng Yangshan, his courtesy name Yingliu; Chen Yangshan was a native of Qianjiang village in the town of Baiguan, Shangyu county, Zhejiang.
Qianjiang village lies in the foothills of the Kuaiji range, a place of hills and water, with a tributary of the Cao'e running along its edge. It is a lovely village of clear streams and green hills. There once stood here the shrine of the filial daughter Cao E, on the bank of the Cao'e River. The story goes that on the fifth day of the fifth month in the second year of the Han'an era of the Eastern Han (143 CE), a man of Shangyu named Cao Xu lost his footing and drowned in the river. His daughter Cao E, just fourteen, searched seventeen days for her father's body and, failing to find it, threw herself into the water and died. The people of that age held her up as a model of filial piety. In the first year of the Yuanjia era (151 CE), Du Shang, magistrate of Shangyu, buried Cao E on the south bank and raised a stele to her. The inscription was the work of Handan Chun, and on the back of the stone was a colophon by Cai Yong, both long since lost. The stele in the shrine today was cut afresh in the eighth year of the Yuanyou era under Emperor Zhezong of the Northern Song (1093 CE), in the hand of Cai Bian. The shrine was first built in the Song; the buildings that stand now are a Republican-era restoration. As a boy the subject of this book, Chen Yangshan, often went with his mother to burn incense there. But loyalty and filial duty, the old saying runs, can rarely both be served; and for the revolution's sake he lived wherever the work sent him, and when his old mother lay dying he could not reach her for a last farewell.
By birth Chen Yangshan bore the surname Cheng; he changed his name only after he joined the revolution and took up secret work. His people came originally from Huizhou prefecture in Anhui, five brothers of one generation, a gentry family of landlord-official stock. His great-grandfather was the eldest; the other four moved during the Xianfeng years to Shaoxing prefecture and the Baiguan district of Shangyu county in Zhejiang and settled there. Some went into trade, some practiced medicine, and one became a Daoist priest.
Chen Yangshan's grandfather held the metropolitan degree and had served in the conspicuous office of salt commissioner of Huizhou prefecture under the Qing. During the Taiping upheaval, fearing reprisals from the Taiping army, he moved from Anhui to Baiguan in Shangyu county and settled at Qianjiang, among its clear hills and waters. But as newcomers from another district the family had neither land nor business in Shangyu, and its fortunes slid steadily downward into poverty.
Chen Yangshan's father, Cheng Buqing, was a failed licentiate and a doctor of Chinese medicine. He had two sons and a daughter; Chen Yangshan was the middle child, and the eldest son, Yingju, and the daughter both died young of tuberculosis. Cheng Buqing was a generous man, tender toward the poor, and would often treat and dress their hurts for nothing, so that people thought well of him.
Chen Yangshan's mother, born Lu, was a local farm woman of Shangyu, a devout Buddhist, kind, open-handed, warm-tempered, and good with the ordinary people around her, so that in hard times the family could always count on the neighbors' help.
Late in life Chen Yangshan looked back on his family's circumstances and said: "My home life was rather hard. My father sat the examinations and made licentiate, but you could call him a poor scholar; he taught in middle schools and primary schools. The household was a heavy burden, six mouths to feed: my grandmother, my mother, my elder brother, my little sister and me. It was not an easy living. After class he practiced medicine as well, and later, when his health failed and he could no longer teach, he lived by his medicine alone. Every year he spent more than he earned and was always borrowing. He owned no land and no house, and worn down by years of overwork he died in 1926." Before the age of ten Chen Yangshan studied at the Mingqiang Primary School in Jiangcun; the course of study then ran seven years.
Chinese society in those days was a nation in peril: foreign powers carving up the country from without, factions of warlords at war within, the common people ground into the dirt with no way to live. In such bitter times Chen Yangshan felt his whole spirit stifled.
When the May Fourth Movement broke out in 1919 it stirred Chen Yangshan, and the creed of "national salvation through industry" took root in him. His family was poor, and so at thirteen he left home; through an introduction he was taken on as an apprentice at a money shop in Wuhan, hoping to grow rich in trade and save his country and keep his family. But three hard years passed before he even rose to shop clerk, and he still earned less than he spent, with nothing to send home.
Six years as a clerk changed Chen Yangshan's thinking and set him on the road toward revolution.
Even during his apprenticeship, Chen Yangshan had taken the old feudal code of ritual as his guide and dreamed of making his fortune. When the apprenticeship ended, the plain facts of life shattered that dream of wealth.
The young Chen Yangshan was deep in confusion. One of his fellow clerks, Zhu Kongyang, was a man of advanced ideas: he read the newspapers every day himself and urged Chen Yangshan to do the same, helped him think through problems, and introduced him to the new currents of thought and to the affairs of the nation. Little by little the lost young man felt his mind break free of the feudal past that had held it.
And just at this turning point in Chen Yangshan's thinking, an event took place in Wuhan that shook the whole land of China.
On February 7, 1923, the great strike on the Beijing-Hankou Railway broke out. The Chinese Communist Party had long been at work organizing the railwaymen. On February 1, 1923, delegates from the sixteen union branches along the line and guests from across the country, close to two hundred people, met at Zhengzhou to found the General Union of the Beijing-Hankou Railway. Wu Peifu, the northern warlord who ruled there, forced the meeting to stop, threw troops and police around the hall, penned the delegates in, and smashed up the union's premises, rousing the workers to boundless fury. The newborn general union met in secret, resolved to set up a strike committee, and on February 4 called a general strike across the whole Beijing-Hankou line, under the ringing slogan: "Fight for freedom, fight for human rights."
Once the strike began, the whole length of the line, more than two thousand li of it, lay paralyzed within hours. It was a striking display of the working class's discipline and its power to organize, and it threw the imperialists and the warlords into a panic.
On February 7 the warlords Cao Kun and Wu Peifu dropped all pretense of "protecting labor." They sent in troops, who fell on the workers in a frenzy at Changxindian, at Jiang'an, and at stations all along the line, shooting over forty dead, wounding some three hundred, arresting more than sixty, and dismissing upward of a thousand. This was the notorious February 7 Massacre.
One of the strike's leaders, the Communist Lin Xiangqian, was bound on the night of February 7 to a lamppost at the Jiang'an station. His captors hacked at him again and again to force him to order the men back to work. Bearing the agony, Lin Xiangqian answered without a flicker: "Without the general union's order, you may have my head, but the men will not go back." He would sooner die than yield, and they killed him. Shi Yang, legal adviser to the Wuhan Federation of Labor Unions and himself a Communist, was arrested soon after and would not bend for all their threats and inducements. On February 15, crying "Long live labor," he met his death unflinching. Here was the pioneering role the Communists played in the workers' movement, and their fearless readiness to die.
Chen Yangshan saw this heroic and terrible scene with his own eyes, and it shook him deeply. He grasped that to survive at all, men would have to join together and fight the warlords. The great February 7 strike transformed how he saw his life. From a view fixed on his own livelihood and his own fortune, he turned to a life lived for the good of society. He threw over the creed of "salvation through industry" and set out again to find the road that would save the country and its people.
Chen Yangshan said: "The great February 7 strike on the Beijing-Hankou line broke out, and the workers dared to fight the Zhili warlords head-on. The strike was crushed, yes, but it proved that the people have power, that the warlords can be resisted. I saw that a people ruled by warlords could never live well, and once I tied my own future to the rise and fall of the nation, patriotism came to me naturally, and I went looking for a plan to save the country. First I believed in 'salvation through education,' then I began to doubt it, and after that I believed in the theory of 'salvation through industry.' None of these plans would work, but they pushed my thinking along. They laid the ground for me to take in the theory of revolution."
And it was just at this time that Chen Yangshan came upon a copy of the magazine China Youth, edited by none other than Yun Daiying.
### 2. Seeking the Truth: “Yun Daiying Was the Man Who Led Me to Revolution”
Early in 1924, Chen Yangshan, not yet eighteen, was handed a copy of the magazine China Youth by his fellow clerk Zhu Kongyang. That single issue was a lamp to him in the dark.
Worn out from a long day's work, Chen Yangshan still read on tirelessly into the night. He read the articles in China Youth and judged them a new theory of national salvation, sound and down to earth: they urged the forming of reading groups, the pursuit of learning, the making of social surveys, all good ways to widen one's knowledge and study real problems. So he fell to discussing the new questions China Youth raised with a few like-minded clerks and with Zhu Kongyang, and they all agreed that society had to be remade and a revolution made. Chen Yangshan lent the magazine on to a clerk named Zhang Zhiyuan and others, and gained another kindred spirit.
What moved Chen Yangshan most of all was the writing of China Youth's editor, Yun Daiying, which he loved above the rest. Yun Daiying's opening editorial in the magazine left its mark on him:
{v} China Youth means to guide the ordinary young reader onto a practical road, to set before him some solid reference material worth studying, to help him gain a little that is close to real use, and yet not easily to be had in the schools.
Chen Yangshan, with a young man's ardor, thought: "This is exactly what the great mass of aspiring youth so badly want. It points us out of the study and out of empty dreaming, into the practice of society, into the study of that practice, so that step by step we may see our way forward and become men who remake society."
In an essay, "A Few Demands on Those Who Aspire," Yun Daiying wrote:
{v} First, we must join our strength and break the present state of things. We must overthrow every plundering class and build a free and happy society. Second, the reason we can do nothing effective today toward that end is, first, that our aims differ; second, that our talents are weak; third, that our tastes are unsettled; and fourth, that we have no organization. Third, I therefore hold that to study current affairs and the theory of social reform is a thing that helps us rise together, sharpen our talents, fix our tastes, and bring organization about. Fourth, I therefore also hold that we must all learn to sacrifice time and money for causes that do the most good to the remaking of society. This too helps us hone our talents, train ourselves in organization, test our theories, and raise our own awareness.
He went on to say that they must wage revolutionary struggle against the warlords, the officials, and the landlord class; that they must organize themselves and study the practice of society.
Chen Yangshan pored over Yun Daiying's articles like a parched man drinking after long drought; they opened his mind and left him seeing clearly.
Yun Daiying (1895–1931) traced his family to Wujin county in Jiangsu and was born on August 12, 1895, in Wuchang county, Hubei. His courtesy name was Ziyi and he also went by Yun Quxuan; he wrote under the pen name Daiying and used the alias Wang Zuolin. He threw himself into revolutionary work as a student and was one of the chief leaders of the May Fourth Movement in the Wuhan area. He joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1921 and later served as an executive member and head of propaganda of the Central Committee of the Socialist Youth League of China, editing China Youth. In the spring of 1922 he founded a branch of the Socialist Youth League in Luzhou. Early in 1923 he taught at the Chengdu Higher Normal School and at Southwest Public School, carrying on the work of spreading Marxism. In January 1926 he attended the Second National Congress of the Kuomintang at Guangzhou and was chosen a member of its Central Executive Committee; he served as chief political instructor at the Whampoa Military Academy and concurrently as secretary of the Party's special branch at the school. In January 1927 he moved with the Guangdong national government to Wuhan, where he became a standing committee member and chief political instructor of the branch military-and-political academy and ran its daily affairs, later also sitting on the Hubei provincial government. He fought hard against the treachery of Chiang Kai-shek and Wang Jingwei. That April he was elected to the Central Committee at the Party's Fifth Congress; in July, on the Center's orders, he went to Jiujiang and took part in organizing and launching the Nanchang Uprising, and in December helped lead the Guangzhou Uprising. In the autumn of 1928 he was transferred to Shanghai as secretary of the Party's central propaganda department, editing the central organ Red Flag and compiling the Daily Propaganda Points. In June 1929 he sat in on the Second Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee at Shanghai and was elected a full member of the Central Committee; at the end of that year he became secretary of the Party's central organization department. In February 1930 he went as a representative of the Party Center to Xiamen to guide the Second Congress of the Fujian Party, and traveled to the base area in western Fujian to investigate at first hand. On May 6, 1930, he was arrested by a British constable at the Ewo cotton mill. The next day he was handed over to the Shanghai Garrison Command, and in June transferred to Caohejing prison. He let his hair and beard grow, hid his name, kept up the fight, and was not found out by the Kuomintang authorities. That October, still in prison, he was named a member of the National Revolutionary Military Commission of the Soviet areas. In February 1931 he wrote in his cell a popular tract for workers explaining the Party's ten great political principles, and organized his fellow prisoners to study it. Just as the Party Center was working hard to have him freed early, the traitor Gu Shunzhang, who had defected on April 25, gave him away to Chiang Kai-shek. A senior official of the Kuomintang war ministry came with his photograph to identify him and press him to surrender. He answered with unbending dignity: "I myself am a Communist. It is my task to make revolution against the Kuomintang reactionaries. That is my solemn duty now, and I am ready at any moment to give everything for the revolution." In prison, threatened and cajoled by turns, Yun Daiying gave nothing away. At noon on April 29, 1931, on the execution ground at Yuhuatai in Nanjing, he cried out "Long live the Communist Party! Down with the Kuomintang reactionaries!" and died a martyr, thirty-six years old.

Yun Daiying was Chen Yangshan's good teacher and dear friend, the man who led him from a shop clerk to a soldier of the revolution. On Yun Daiying's advice, Chen Yangshan formed a reading group with Zhu Kongyang, Zhang Zhiyuan, and a few other like-minded colleagues. Ten hours a week, guided by Yun Daiying, they studied current affairs and society and held discussions, and gained a great deal from it, coming step by step to understand something of revolution. In February 1924 Chen Yangshan read the special number China Youth brought out on the death of Lenin, and learned that Russia was the first socialist country in the world, a state of workers and peasants built by the working class after it had overthrown the landlords and the bourgeoisie, and led by Lenin. The reading group discussed it, tying the Russian revolution to conditions in China. All agreed that China's revolution had to follow the Russian road, and that with Russia's example and Russia's help, victory would come much more easily.
In May 1924 Chen Yangshan wrote to Yun Daiying, reporting on the reading group's activities and its progress and telling him how the Hankou financial world was in league with the warlords. Half a month later Yun Daiying's reply came. He encouraged the group in its work and its gains, and urged that study be joined to the study of business, that beyond their reading they make more friends and carry on what activity they could in commercial circles. After this Chen Yangshan wrote to Yun Daiying several more times, and each time Yun Daiying wrote back promptly with guidance and praise.
One day in July 1924 Chen Yangshan received, to his surprise, a postcard from Yun Daiying asking him to come to his house. Delighted, Chen Yangshan showed the card to the others in the group, who all thought it a great honor, and together they drew up the questions their studies had left unanswered, for Chen Yangshan to put to Yun Daiying while he was there.
Yun Daiying lived at Hansangong in Wuchang. Chen Yangshan came to his house at the appointed time. It was a shabby little courtyard with three or four small, dim rooms; they talked in a guest room off the first court, its table and chairs all old. Yun Daiying wore a pair of rimless spectacles and had a shaved head and an old cloth gown, plainly a hardworking, deeply read teacher. He greeted Chen Yangshan warmly, as though they were old friends.
Earnest, of middling build, his eyes hungry for the truth, the young Chen Yangshan made a deep impression on the older man, more than a dozen years his senior.
After the first pleasantries Yun Daiying asked after the reading group and after Chen Yangshan's own affairs. Chen Yangshan reported in detail on the members of the group and on the plans for its growth. Yun Daiying took a keen interest, breaking in now and then with questions, and gave a good deal of guidance.
Decades later, in 1982, Chen Yangshan was still moved when he recalled that unforgettable day. He said:
{v} It was a very hot day. We talked from about ten in the morning straight through to four in the afternoon; the conversation ran six or seven hours.
{v} At that meeting I put dozens of questions to Comrade Yun Daiying. None of us in the clerks' group understood Marxism then; we could make little of the Communist Manifesto, and knew very little even of what imperialism, capitalism, and socialism were. How had the Soviet revolution triumphed? How was China's revolution to be made? What sort of government would be set up in the end? … None of it was clear to us. So I laid a whole heap of questions before Comrade Daiying, and he explained every one of them patiently. What I remember most is his saying: China's revolution must overthrow imperialism and warlord rule, and set up a Soviet-style government, and only then can the people be freed.
This meeting left a deep mark on Chen Yangshan, and into his old age he spoke of it again and again:
{v} That talk I shall never forget as long as I live. It gave me enormous heart. I learned what kind of man I ought to be, grasped some elementary truths of revolution, saw much that was basic made clear, and was filled with confidence about the future. It steadied my will for revolution and my resolve to struggle, and laid the ground of thought for a lifetime of revolution.
After this Chen Yangshan kept up a long association with Yun Daiying, and Yun Daiying's unshakable will and his hard, unstinting devotion to work stayed with Chen Yangshan for good. He said:
{v} My knowing Comrade Yun Daiying grew naturally out of revolutionary work. Comrade Daiying taught me with real warmth; he taught me how to study and how to work, how to be a man useful to society. He was my good teacher and dear friend, the man who led me into the ranks of the revolution, and none of it will I ever forget.
After this talk Yun Daiying arranged for Yu Shisong and Liu Bowen of the Wuchang telephone bureau to keep in touch with Chen Yangshan. In effect it placed the reading group Chen Yangshan had built under the Party's guidance.
One day in October 1924, Liu Bowen took Chen Yangshan, Zhu Kongyang, and Zhang Zhiyuan—the three of them—out to open country beyond Wuchang and held the ceremony admitting them to the Socialist Youth League of China. After that the reading group grew fast, and by the beginning of 1925 it had swelled to thirty or forty. Chen Yangshan rented two rooms in a guild hall for a reading room and enlarged the group into the "Hankou Youth Self-Improvement Society," drawing in more clerks to read the books and papers. Every evening more than twenty clerks came to read or to take part in its entertainments, and it was much loved among them. Later this Self-Improvement Society grew a great deal further, and it played an important part in drawing the Hankou clerks together during the May Thirtieth Movement.
One day in January 1925, Chen Tanqiu took Chen Yangshan aside and told him that he had passed from membership in the Socialist Youth League of China to membership in the Chinese Communist Party, and that his responsibilities from now on would be heavier still. Not long after, Chen Tanqiu called a meeting at a ruined temple in the outskirts of Hanyang, where Xiang Ying relayed the resolutions of the Party's Fourth Congress. More than thirty attended, Chen Yangshan among them.
As the clerks' League organization in Wuchang grew, the Youth League there lacked the leadership to match it. In February 1925, Lin Yunan, secretary of the Wuhan district committee of the Youth League, resolved to set up a special League branch for the Wuchang area.
Lin Yunan (1898–1931) came from Huanggang county in Hubei. He styled himself Xiangpu and also went by Lin Yulan, Lin Fu, Lin Xiangfu, and Lin Rulan; he wrote as Lin Gen, Ruoyu, and Gen, was otherwise known as Tiexuan, and used the aliases Li Jingtang and Li Shaotang. He was an older cousin of Lin Biao. In 1915 he entered the middle-school section of the Zhonghua University in Wuchang, met Yun Daiying, and swiftly took up the new culture and the new thought. In October 1917 he joined the Mutual Aid Society and became one of its most active members. Fiercely loyal and tireless, he founded the Huang Society at his school and led his fellow students boldly into the patriotic struggle against the treaties the Northern warlords were signing with Japan to sell out the country. He was a reader of New Youth and other progressive journals, searching in them for the truth that would save the country and its people. In March 1919 he helped found the fortnightly New Voice, the first new-culture publishing house in the Wuchang area. Early in 1920 he and Yun Daiying and others founded the Liqun Book Society, selling progressive works and drawing comrades together to explore the "new life." He was a chief leader of the student movement in Wuhan. In July 1921 he left his studies and returned to Wuhan, and with Yun Daiying, Li Qiushi, and others founded the Gongcun Society, an organization already Communist in character, and through practice made his own turn to Marxism. As soon as the Chinese Communist Party was formally founded he joined the Wuhan branch of the China Labor Union Secretariat and threw himself into the workers' movement. In January 1922 he attended the First Congress of the Communist Parties and Revolutionary Organizations of the Peoples of the Far East in Moscow. He returned that spring and soon joined the Chinese Communist Party. He headed the Hankou branch of the China Labor Union Secretariat and served as special commissioner and secretary of the union at the Wuchang No. 1 Cotton Mill. In July he went to Shanghai and sat in on the Second National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party.
In January 1923 Lin Yunan helped lead the preparations for the General Union of the Beijing-Hankou Railway, and with Chen Tanqiu, Xiang Ying, and others led the Wuhan labor bodies and a band to the founding meeting at Zhengzhou. After the reactionary warlords broke it up, Lin Yunan led the Jiang'an railwaymen out first on strike, and marched more than ten thousand men from Jiang'an and the various labor bodies in a demonstration. When the February 7 Massacre came, Lin Yunan helped chair meetings through the night, led the Wuhan labor bodies out in a general strike in protest against the warlords' bloody slaughter, and wired the whole country to expose their crimes in crushing the workers. He organized a fund drive and pressed the work of rescuing the arrested and relieving the martyrs' families and the jobless. Lin Yunan was a chief leader of the early workers' movement in Wuhan and Hubei.
In the spring of 1924 Lin Yunan returned to Wuhan as an officer in the youth department of the Kuomintang's Hankou executive office, and in April became the League Central's special commissioner for Hubei, taking part in building Kuomintang-Communist cooperation in the Wuhan area and guiding the youth movement in Hubei.
In mid-January 1925 Lin Yunan attended the Fourth National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party at Shanghai and helped draw up its resolutions. On January 22 he was appointed a member of the Party Center's committee on the workers' movement.
In 1926 he attended the Third National Labor Congress at Guangzhou and gave a rousing speech there. That autumn, as the National Revolutionary Army carried the Northern Expedition victoriously into Hubei, Lin Yunan was sent back to Wuhan by the Party Center. In August he became a member of the Hubei district executive committee of the Party and ran the work of the Communist Youth League's Hubei district committee. In October he became head of propaganda for the Party's Hubei district committee. In November the Party Center set up its "Two Lakes" Party school, with Lin Yunan as its principal; he also taught at the Wuhan workers' movement training institute and directed the training of Party cadres from Hunan and Hubei and other provinces. In mid-January 1927 he helped prepare and chair the First Congress of Hubei Workers, serving as its secretary-general and being elected an executive member and head of propaganda of the Hubei General Labor Union.
In the spring of 1928 Lin Yunan was recalled to the All-China Federation of Trade Unions to help lead its work. That autumn the Party Center sent him to Kaifeng as a central special commissioner to reorganize the provincial Party and League committees, and for a time he led the work of the Party's Henan provincial committee, pressing the rebuilding of the organization. That winter he was recalled again to Shanghai to work at the federation. In November 1929 he took charge of preparing and helped chair the Fifth National Labor Congress at Shanghai, serving as its secretary-general, reading out the Party Center's message of greeting, and carrying through the workers' program of struggle and the congress manifesto and resolutions that he had directed the drafting of. He was elected an executive member of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions and served as its chief secretary and head of the editorial committee, running the federation's daily affairs.
In the summer of 1930 Lin Yunan served as secretary-general of the preparatory committee and helped lead the First Congress of the Soviet Areas of China at Shanghai, which passed the Soviet government program, the labor protection law, the resolution to expand the Red Army, and other measures he had helped draft.
On January 17, 1931, Lin Yunan was arrested during a meeting at the Dongfang Hotel on Third Avenue in Shanghai. On the 19th he was handed over from the International Settlement to the public security bureau of the Kuomintang's Shanghai municipal government, and on the 23rd transferred to the lockup of the Songhu Garrison Command at Longhua. Under interrogation Lin Yunan would not break, and bore every torture. He heartened his fellow prisoners and kept up the struggle, and with Li Qiushi wrote a long letter to the Party Center reporting on their arrest and their conditions in prison and showing an iron faith in the certain victory of the Party and the people's revolutionary cause. On February 7, Lin Yunan, together with He Mengxiong, Li Qiushi, and twenty other fellow prisoners, gave his life at Longhua in Shanghai.
Between Chen Yangshan and Lin Yunan too there was a deep friendship. So when he learned in 1931 that the enemy had killed Lin Yunan, he grieved bitterly.
After Chen Tanqiu announced Chen Yangshan's admission to the Chinese Communist Party, Lin Yunan was the first to speak with him on the Party's behalf. He told Chen Yangshan of the Party's ultimate goal, why one joined it, and how to be a Communist who would shrink from no sacrifice for the liberation of all mankind.
At that time the Party's Wuhan district committee used the money shop where Chen Yangshan worked as a courier station, and so many people came to see him that the shop's owner grew suspicious. For his safety the Party decided to move him out.
In April 1925 the Wuhan League district committee had an urgent dispatch to send to the League Central in Shanghai. Lin Yunan sent Chen Yangshan to carry it in person, and asked him whether he was willing to leave the money shop. Chen Yangshan said he was. Lin Yunan had him hand over his work in the Wuchang League and introduced him to the League Central in Shanghai.
So Chen Yangshan ended his life as a clerk and began the fighting life of a professional revolutionary. He said: "I never thought I could leave a clerk's life behind and begin something wholly new, still less that I could live the life of revolutionary struggle within a revolutionary organization. So I felt not only joy in my heart, but a longing to learn still more of revolution."
### 3. The May Thirtieth Storm: Tempered in the Flames
It was a bright day in May when Chen Yangshan, entrusted by the Party, came to Shanghai, where a red storm of revolution was brewing.
On May 3, 1925, Chen Yangshan reached Shanghai. Following the contact arrangements, he found that Yun Daiying was living at Fudeli, at the corner of Avenue Edward VII and Chengdu Road, and went to see him. He reported on the situation in Hankou, and Yun Daiying, well pleased with his work there, asked whether he would be willing to stay and work at the League Central in Shanghai. Chen said he would. Yun Daiying had him take on the League Central's courier work for the time being and go to Fukangli at the corner of Chengdu Road to live with the League Central secretary, Ren Bishi. Ren Bishi had the attic room; Chen Yangshan and Zhang Baochen, who kept the accounts and papers, had the front rooms.
May 5 was the 107th anniversary of Marx's birth, and the Party held a commemoration in the hall of Shanghai University on Seymour Road, with some five or six hundred people. Yang Xingfo spoke first, then Yun Daiying. Yun Daiying was a gifted speaker, lively and full of humor, making hard things plain, so that everyone listened with rapt attention, breaking now and then into applause or laughter; one never tired of hearing him, and he sent people away heartened. Later Chen Yangshan learned that his talks were always warmly received. Ren Bishi spoke last.
It was the first meeting of the kind Chen Yangshan had ever attended, and he found it all fresh and felt lucky and glad, hoping there would be many more such meetings and more to learn from.
The year 1925 saw the workers' and peasants' movement surge and the revolutionary tide run high, with strikes flaring up everywhere. The victory that February of the strikes at the Japanese cotton mills in Qingdao and Shanghai spurred strikes on across the country. Under the force of the strikes the Japanese owners were driven on the one hand to sign agreements with the workers, while on the other they bided their time to strike back. They were forever docking wages, delaying pay, beating and cursing and dismissing the men, and stirring up trouble. On May 7 the Japanese Cotton Spinners' Association in Shanghai brazenly resolved to refuse to recognize the unions and threatened to close the mills if the workers struck. So the workers of Naigai Cotton Mills Nos. 3 and 4 struck first in protest, the other mills at once fell in behind them, and as the struggle spread day by day the Japanese owners put it down by force.
The Japanese owners had misjudged the workers' will and their strength, imagining that sacking a few would smother the tide. On May 14 the workers of Naigai No. 12 struck again. At six the next morning the West Shanghai Workers' Club at Tanziwan was packed, and Liu Hua, chairing the meeting, warned the No. 7 workers that No. 7 depended on No. 12 for its cotton yarn; the Japanese owners might use a lack of raw material as a pretext to force No. 7 to shut down, breeding division among the workers and weakening the struggle. Should that happen, he said, the No. 7 workers must hold to their posts.
And so it fell out. Soon after the No. 7 day shift came on, the Japanese overseer used the closing of No. 12 and its want of yarn as a pretext to drive the workers out of the mill and lock the gates. In the afternoon, when the night shift asked to work, the owners would not let them in; and when the workers forced the gates, the Japanese managers Motoki and Kawamura, ready and waiting, led their thugs against the workers with their fists and then opened fire.
Seven workers were gravely hurt and dozens lightly. The Communist Gu Zhenghong, in the front rank of the fight, took four bullets; his wounds could not be healed, and he died the next morning at the patrol-house hospital, a martyr.
The blood of the martyr Gu Zhenghong roused the fury of the Chinese people. On May 16 eight thousand workers of Naigai Nos. 5, 7, 8, and 12 struck in anger. That same day the unions of the Japanese cotton mills and the clerks' federation called together thirty-five bodies from labor, commerce, and the schools to a joint meeting and founded a society to wipe out the shame of the Japanese slaughter of their countrymen, in support of the strike. On May 16 and 19 the Party Center issued notices one after another calling for an anti-Japanese movement and a general assault on the Japanese imperialists.
As May drew toward its close the feeling of the Shanghai people ran ever higher. On the 24th the Party's Shanghai district committee decided on a public memorial for Gu Zhenghong at Tanziwan. A great couplet in Liu Hua's own hand read: "Though the man be dead, his spirit lives; while the murderer runs wild, where is justice?"—calling on the people to press forward along the martyr's trail of blood.
More than ten thousand workers, students, and representatives of every walk of life came to the meeting. Chen Yangshan attended with Ren Bishi and Yun Daiying. The hall was hung with the funeral couplets and banners of the various unions and bodies. Many workers went up to speak, and Yun Daiying spoke too. After the 26th the students began handing out leaflets printed with anti-imperialist slogans. The Chinese Ratepayers' Association, the General Chamber of Commerce, and other bourgeois bodies issued declaration after declaration and lodged protests against the Municipal Council's four proposals; the capitalists even hoped for a riot, to stop the proposals passing. The anti-imperialist feeling of the Shanghai people had reached the point of boiling over.
The imperialists, for their part, were making ready to strike. The Japanese overseer Yada Shichitaro bustled about demanding that the unions be stamped out altogether. The Municipal Council sent out large numbers of constables to round up the workers and students who spoke in the streets. On the 26th the council's chief inspector, McEuen, issued an order: when an officer judged the situation grave enough to require opening fire, with carbine or pistol, he was to aim at the most threatening part of the mob.
A struggle carried this far needed the right decisions. On the 28th the Party Center and the Shanghai district committee met jointly and laid it down that the workers' economic struggle must be joined to the anti-imperialist struggle then rising fast, that the workers' struggle must take on a plainly anti-imperialist character, so as to win the help of every anti-imperialist force. They resolved that on the afternoon of the 30th, when the Mixed Court would "try" the arrested students, they would organize a speaking demonstration inside the settlement.
May 30 was a day of oppressive heat in Shanghai, as if it foretold the coming of a storm. Led by the Chinese Communist Party, students and workers broke the settlement's decades-old ban and moved with fearless revolutionary spirit along the roads of the International Settlement. "Avenge Gu Zhenghong!" "Down with imperialism!"—the cries rang through the streets. Nanjing Road, thronged as ever, became a world of banners. Every ten or twenty shopfronts a speaker stood waving a small white triangular flag, and the listeners answered with roar upon roar. After midday the student speakers grew more numerous still. The constables drove the crowds this way and that and arrested the speakers, and by around two o'clock more than a hundred had been taken into the patrol house. Hearing this, the demonstrators poured toward the Louza patrol house demanding the students' release, and before long a great crowd had gathered. At 3:45 in the afternoon the British inspector suddenly gave the order to fire. The bullets tore into the unarmed throng, and Nanjing Road ran with blood in an instant. Thirteen were killed, among them He Bingyi of Shanghai University and Yin Jingyi of Tongji University, and dozens more were wounded. This was the May Thirtieth Massacre, which shook China and the world.
The anti-imperialist fury the outrage kindled spread swiftly across Shanghai and across China.
That night the Party Center met in emergency session and resolved to set up an action committee, to build an anti-imperialist united front of every class, and to bring all Shanghai out in strikes of the workers, the students, and the merchants.
On the 31st the Shanghai General Labor Union was publicly founded, with Li Lisan as its chairman, and led the city's workers in a general strike.
That same afternoon a mass meeting was held at the General Chamber of Commerce. Grieving students, workers, and merchants denounced the imperialists' outrage one after another and called for a general strike of workers, students, and shops. Fang Jiaobo, vice-president of the chamber, who arrived long after it had begun, announced his agreement to the merchants' strike. But at half past ten that night he sent word to the Municipal Council that this was a surrender to the coercion of "agitators."
On June 1 the whole city's students struck their classes. With this the "triple strike" was fully joined across Shanghai, and even most of the Chinese constables of the International Settlement walked off their posts.
That night the Shanghai General Labor Union was formally inaugurated and declared a general strike. In this "triple strike" Chen Yangshan, with Ren Bishi, helped organize the demonstration on Nanjing Road and the petition at the Shanghai General Chamber of Commerce.
From June 1, more than 200,000 Shanghai workers struck, more than 50,000 students left their classes, and the great majority of the merchants closed their shops. The strata that joined the "triple strike" were remarkably broad: even the Chinese constables of the "International Settlement," the servants of foreigners, and the street cleaners threw themselves into the movement. The people of Shanghai had found their voice.
The anti-imperialist blaze spread swiftly from Shanghai across the whole country. In Beijing, Tianjin, Jinan, Nanjing, Hangzhou, Zhengzhou, Hankou, Chongqing, and other cities, and in the out-of-the-way towns of Shanxi, Shaanxi, Inner Mongolia, Yunnan, and elsewhere, the "triple strike" broke out. The great strike of 250,000 workers in Guangzhou and Hong Kong lasted sixteen months, one of the longest strikes in the history of the world's labor movement. The peasants of Henan, Guangdong, Hunan, and other provinces joined the struggle too. Overseas Chinese and students abroad also stood behind the just struggle of their countrymen.
The May Thirtieth Movement raised a revolutionary tide across the nation, struck a heavy blow at imperialism, strengthened the revolutionary government then seated in Guangzhou, and showed the vast power of a Chinese people united against imperialism.
In the wake of the May Thirtieth Movement, a national-democratic revolutionary movement aimed at overthrowing imperialism and warlord rule swept across the land of China like a rolling flood. The Chinese Communist Party led the working class and the toiling masses, joined with the Chinese Kuomintang under Sun Yat-sen, built a revolutionary united front, and struck fiercely at imperialism and the warlord powers.
At this crucial hour the young Communist Chen Yangshan faced into the storm and threw himself, heart and soul, into the world-shaking struggle of the great revolution.
### 4. Running Explosives: Secretly to Wuhan and Back to Shanghai
In July 1925 the Wuhan district Party committee wrote to the League Central proposing that Chen Yangshan be moved to Wuhan. After looking into it the League Central took up the proposal and decided that Yun Daiying should speak with Chen Yangshan and tell him to go secretly to Wuhan as soon as he could.
One day in mid-July Yun Daiying took Chen Yangshan aside and told him: "The Wuhan district Party committee wants you to go and work in Wuhan." Chen Yangshan said he had no objection to going, but that with no experience of the work he feared he might not be equal to it, might not do it well.
Yun Daiying set him right: "Your knowledge of Wuhan is a great advantage. Where you lack experience you can listen to the masses, talk things over with your colleagues, and ask your superiors. As a Communist you should do all the more for the Party, and take on heavy tasks with courage."
At Yun Daiying's words Chen Yangshan put his misgivings aside at once, said he would obey the Party's assignment, and set off soon for Wuhan.
A few days later Chen Yangshan reached Wuhan. He first called on the comrades he had once worked alongside, then reported to the Wuhan district Party committee.
By this time Lin Yunan, secretary of the Wuhan district League committee, had already been moved to the League Central, and the Wuhan League committee had no one at its head. So Chen Yangshan was made its acting secretary and told that his work would center mainly on the students. He knew the financial world well but nothing at all of the world of education, and this daunted him. He was about to beg off when he remembered Yun Daiying's teaching before he left Shanghai; his misgivings fell away at once, and he said he would obey his assignment and take the post of acting secretary of the League district committee.
Before long the League Central sent Huang Jing to Wuhan as secretary of the League district committee. Chen Yangshan handed the work over to him and returned to the Wuhan district Party committee.
Because Chen Yangshan had once joined the Kuomintang and had lived and worked in Wuhan, and knew the place well, the Wuhan district Party committee set him to united-front work. He was to serve, as a member of the Kuomintang, as a standing committee member and head of organization of the Kuomintang's Wuhan municipal party office.
At that time the Kuomintang and the Communists were cooperating, and to build the revolutionary united front, the Party Center had decided that Communists and Youth League members should all join the Kuomintang as individuals. This both drove the national revolution forward and shielded our Party's secret work: an ingenious arrangement.
Chen Yangshan went to the Kuomintang's Wuhan municipal party office to do united-front work under the assumed name Chen Yingzhou. He was only nineteen then, a young man just out into the world. With a green recruit's fearlessness he went deep into the Kuomintang, building the revolutionary united front and giving his own share to the cause of Kuomintang-Communist cooperation.
But it cut both ways. Chen Yangshan's familiarity with Wuhan and its people was certainly an advantage, but for secret work it was also a liability. In view of this it was thought unwise for him to do secret work in Wuhan, and he was moved back to the League Central in Shanghai.
By this time Yun Daiying was setting up the Shanghai Transport Office of the Kuomintang central propaganda department. This so-called Shanghai Transport Office was a secret organ that the Kuomintang central propaganda department had planted in Shanghai to forward progressive books and papers. Newspapers, books, and magazines mailed from Guangzhou to Shanghai in those days were often held up on arrival by the reactionary Shanghai authorities, who used the method to block progressive, revolutionary news. To break this blockade, the Transport Office worked through members of the seamen's union aboard the ships that plied between Guangzhou and Shanghai and between Hong Kong and Shanghai, secretly carrying large quantities of progressive books and magazines into Shanghai, from where the office forwarded them to the Kuomintang party offices in every province.
Because so many books and magazines came in each time and had to be mailed out promptly to the provinces, the work was heavy and the staff too few, and the distribution needed someone to lead it. So Yun Daiying named Chen Yangshan head of the distribution section and told him to lose no time in setting the flagging distribution to rights. Chen Yangshan took up the task, threw himself into it, and soon had the great volume of distribution running smoothly. Yun Daiying was well pleased.
While at the Shanghai Transport Office Chen Yangshan came to know many of the ships' seamen, and also a Kuomintang member (of the left) named Bao Junfu. After 1928, under Chen Yangshan's guidance, this man did a great many useful things for our Party.
Running explosives was another fresh test for the young Communist Chen Yangshan.
The May Thirtieth Movement of 1925 pushed the country's anti-imperialist, anti-feudal struggle to a new stage. The workers' and peasants' movement surged, the unions grew stronger by the day, strikes broke out without cease, and the peasant associations spread fast.
As the workers' and peasants' movement rose across the country, the Guangdong revolutionary base was swiftly consolidated and unified. This surging revolutionary tide terrified the imperialists, the feudal warlords, and the comprador bourgeoisie, who joined forces and pressed their attack on the revolutionary forces from every side. Comrades Mao Zedong, Zhou Enlai, Chen Yannian, and others urged a counterstroke. From February 21 to 24, 1926, the Party Center held a special conference in Beijing. It resolved that our Party should prepare in every way for the Northern Expedition, and laid down that the program of the expedition should turn on the solution of the peasant question; that mass work must be pressed in Hunan, Hubei, Henan, and the other regions the expedition would pass through, organizing and rousing the peasant masses to support and join it, driving forward the revolution's victorious advance and beating back the reactionary attack.
The conference decided to strengthen military work, and set up a Central Military Commission made up of Zhou Enlai, Wang Yifei, Nie Rongzhen, and Yan Changyi.
Once the Central Military Commission was formed, under Zhou Enlai's leadership it set to work at once preparing for the Northern Expedition, strengthening its hold on the workers' pickets and the peasant self-defense corps and putting them through training.
In the spring of 1926 the printing workers of the Zhabei district of Shanghai, led by Chen Yun and others, struck for higher wages, shorter hours, and better conditions. To bring the strike to victory the Jiangsu provincial committee sent Chen Yangshan, who had been through the May Thirtieth Movement, to help lead it. It was then that Chen Yangshan met Chen Yun, and met too Wang Yifei, secretary-general of the Central Military Commission, who had come to guide the strike.
Wang Yifei joined the Socialist Youth League of China in 1920 and the Chinese Communist Party in 1922, while studying in the Soviet Union. After returning home in 1924 he served in turn as secretary of the Party's Shanghai–Jiangsu–Zhejiang district executive committee and as secretary-general of the Central Military Commission. In March 1927 he helped lead the third armed uprising of the Shanghai workers. He was elected to the Central Committee at the Party's Fifth Congress, and after the Party's August 7 Conference became secretary of the Party's Hunan provincial committee. In January 1928 he was killed by the Kuomintang at Changsha.
Wang Yifei and Chen Yangshan were fellow townsmen, both natives of Shangyu in Zhejiang. Wang Yifei had come, as secretary-general of the Central Military Commission, to guide the strike. Knowing that Chen Yangshan had once worked in Wuhan and knew it well, he entrusted him with getting three bombs from Shanghai to the Central Military Commission in Wuhan.
Chen Yangshan knew well how hard the task would be, that it might even cost him his life and was no child's play; but without a word he took it on, glad of the charge Wang Yifei had given him.
Shanghai in those days was a bewildering tangle. There were the garrisons and constables of the imperialists; there were the army, police, gendarmerie, and secret-service organs of the Kuomintang; and there were the evil powers of the feudal warlords and the gangster societies and secret sects of men like Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng. The railways, the roads, and the docks were all in enemy hands, and moving explosives out of Shanghai was a formidable business. After turning it over and over, Chen Yangshan judged that the trains were searched most strictly and a steamer was safer; that a cargo ship was safer than a passenger ship, and a foreign ship safer than a Chinese one. So he decided to send the three bombs by foreign cargo ship. With the help of a Chinese seaman he knew who worked aboard a British freighter, he got himself aboard her and carried the three bombs from Shanghai to Wuhan right under the enemy's nose, undetected, discharging the hard task Wang Yifei had set him. The affair left Zhou Enlai with a strong impression of Chen Yangshan. Chen Yangshan for his part was glad and proud to have done something for the Northern Expedition.
In the winter of 1926 Zhejiang urgently needed cadres for united-front work. Chen Yangshan was a Zhejiang man and had done such work before, so the Party moved him to the province. He was assigned to the Kuomintang's Zhejiang provincial party office in Ningbo for united-front work. Having many old friends in Ningbo, he soon made many new ones as well; the work went smoothly and he gathered a good deal of important intelligence. Chen Yangshan was very pleased with what he brought back from his united-front work in Ningbo. Through this taste of practice he came to see that secret work required a wide circle of friends, since only that way could a man protect himself and gather intelligence.
In March 1927 our Party was building its organization in Zhejiang. The Party moved Chen Yangshan to Hangzhou to take part in that work. But he had not long been there when the April 12 counter-revolutionary coup broke out in Shanghai. The white terror in Hangzhou grew fearfully severe, and finding it hard to keep a footing there, he went back to Shanghai to find the organization.
Shanghai now was pitiless: the enemy hunting Communists everywhere, the Party organization all gone underground. It cost him great trouble to make contact again. The organization was then being reshuffled, and Chen Yangshan was assigned to the South-of-the-French-Concession district committee as head of propaganda.
By this time the counter-revolutionary forces had grown far greater than the Party's, China's revolution had begun to ebb, and the Party could only go underground. Many members lost touch with the organization, and some of the less steadfast fell away from the revolution one after another. But Chen Yangshan was not cowed by the deadly conditions; if anything they hardened his resolve to see the revolution through.
### 5. Shielding He Long: A Legend of Chen Yangshan’s Fighting Days
On April 12, 1927, Chiang Kai-shek launched his counter-revolutionary coup in Shanghai, seizing and killing Communists and revolutionary people wholesale. In its wake, counter-revolutionary massacres followed one after another in Guangdong, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and other provinces. At the same time the Fengtian warlord Zhang Zuolin, acting in concert with Chiang Kai-shek, seized and killed Communists in Beijing. The whole country lay under a white terror.
On July 15 of the same year, Wang Jingwei called an emergency meeting of the Kuomintang Central Standing Committee in Wuhan and openly betrayed the policy of Kuomintang-Communist cooperation and the anti-imperialist, anti-feudal program that Sun Yat-sen had laid down, formally declaring a break with the Communist Party.
With Chiang and Wang now joined, the country's political scene sank into a rain of blood. Great numbers of Communists and revolutionary people were killed. Revolutionary figures beloved and honored by the revolutionary masses in the days of the great revolution—Li Dazhao, Zhao Shiyan, Chen Yannian, Xiao Chunü, Wang Shouhua, Xiong Xiong, and other proletarian leaders—fell one after another under the enemy's butcher knife. China's mighty revolution was cut down in mid-course; but the Chinese Communist Party was neither cowed nor conquered.
At this grim hour of life and death the Party Center was reorganized. The general secretary, Chen Duxiu, was removed from his post for pursuing a line of right-opportunist capitulation. In his place a provisional Central Standing Committee was formed of five men: Zhang Tailei, Zhou Enlai, Li Lisan, Zhang Guotao, and Li Weihan.
To save China's revolution, the Central Standing Committee resolved on an armed uprising at Nanchang in Jiangxi. On August 1 of that year, under Zhou Enlai's leadership, He Long, Zhu De, Ye Ting, Liu Bocheng, and others directed the uprising. After more than four hours of fierce fighting they took the city of Nanchang, firing the first shot of armed revolutionary resistance against the Kuomintang reactionaries.
Then, following the Party Center's set plan, the uprising forces pulled out of Nanchang on August 6 and made straight for the Chaoshan region of Guangdong by way of southern Jiangxi and western Fujian. When they reached Chaoshan in early October they were surrounded and overwhelmed by superior enemy forces and scattered. But the success of the Nanchang Uprising had shaken the Kuomintang reactionary authorities. Wang Jingwei issued a warrant offering a reward of 100,000 silver dollars for He Long's capture. In early November He Long made his way to Shanghai.
To make certain of He Long's safety, the Party Center, on Zhou Enlai's suggestion, gave Chen Yangshan the task of shielding him. In a Shanghai gripped by so fierce a white terror, keeping him safe at all was a tall order.
Charged with this, Chen Yangshan followed Li Weihan's instructions and settled He Long together with Zhou Yiqun in a secret lodging. Then he carefully studied the surroundings, mapped out routes for going about, and arranged the vehicles to be used and the signals for making contact—everything thought through in full, to He Long's great satisfaction.
Once all was settled, He Long first went to see Zhou Enlai. At this meeting Zhou Enlai suggested that He Long take the chance to go and study in the Soviet Union first. He Long said he would abide by the organization's arrangement.
A few days later Li Weihan went to see He Long and asked his views on the arrangement. Speaking of the situation in western Hunan, He Long said: "If the peasants there are roused, western Hunan holds promise." Li Weihan reported the substance of the talk to the Central Standing Committee, and Qu Qiubai held that He Long should go to work in western Hunan. So Li Weihan went again to ask He Long's view, and He Long said he was willing to go to western Hunan and unwilling to study in the Soviet Union. But by then the Center had already decided that he should leave Shanghai secretly on November 17 with Liu Bocheng and Lin Boqu to study in the Soviet Union.
As it happened, on November 16 one of our Party's secret organs was discovered by the enemy, who learned that He Long was in Shanghai and at once mounted a close search to seize him. So He Long could not show his face for a time. Liu Bocheng and Lin Boqu, meanwhile, set off from Shanghai for the Soviet Union on November 17 as originally arranged.
Now He Long told Zhou Yiqun his own wish to go to western Hunan and take up armed struggle there, and hoped to talk it over with Zhou Enlai. When Zhou Yiqun passed the idea to the Center, Zhou Enlai met He Long again. Very gravely He Long put his request to Zhou Enlai: "Let me go back to western Hunan and raise a force. Where I fell is where I will stand up again. There I know the people and the ground, and it will be easy to build an army."
On January 6, 1928, Zhou Enlai reported He Long's request to the Central Standing Committee. The committee approved it and gave him seven or eight men to form a nucleus, with Zhou Yiqun as secretary and He Long, Guo Liang, Xu Teli, and Liu Keming as members, to go to the Hunan-Hubei border, rouse the masses, build a worker-peasant revolutionary army, take up armed struggle, and found a revolutionary base.
Having completed the task of shielding He Long, Chen Yangshan was sent at the end of December 1927 to Wuhan to work with the clerks' union. But he knew too many people in Wuhan for secret work to be wise there, and so he returned to Shanghai. The white terror in Shanghai was severe now and the Party organization had all gone underground; back in the city, Chen Yangshan could not for a time make contact and had nowhere to stay, and so he went home for the while to Shangyu.
The white terror in Zhejiang was fierce too then, with Kuomintang troops and police forever hunting Communists. Chen Yangshan's mother, afraid he would come to grief, wanted him to quit the revolution. He flatly refused, and so she pressed him to take a wife. The woman was named Zhang Suzhen, daughter of a doctor of Chinese ophthalmology from Zhangjia village in the Shangyu district. Chen Yangshan's father had been a doctor of Chinese medicine as well, of the same trade as Zhang Suzhen's father; the two men knew each other, had talked of a match between their children, and had settled the betrothal. Now, hoping a wife would tie him down and turn him from the revolution, Chen Yangshan's mother arranged the wedding at once. But the old woman's scheme went awry: not only was Chen Yangshan not held fast by his wife, he led Zhang Suzhen onto the revolutionary road as well.
Zhang Suzhen came of a family of independent means. She had never been to school, but she had ambition; she was against the old feudal code and put her faith in science. After marrying Chen Yangshan she came under his influence and grew sympathetic to the revolution. Soon after the wedding, when Chen Yangshan was organizing a peasant uprising in Haimen county, he was betrayed to the authorities, and the county party office sent troops and police to the house to seize him. Zhang Suzhen bravely took on the task of shielding him, helped him slip out of danger, and got him safely back to Shanghai.
Zhang Suzhen's conduct in shielding Chen Yangshan was something he never forgot. Later, while working in the Central Special Branch, he told the story to Chen Geng. Chen Geng thought highly of Zhang Suzhen's brave and quick-witted conduct and proposed to Zhou Enlai that she be taken on for shielding work. With Zhou Enlai's approval, Zhang Suzhen came to Shanghai in the spring of 1929 and joined the Central Special Branch. From then on she worked at Chen Yangshan's side on the hidden front.
In mid-January 1928 the Party once more charged Chen Yangshan with setting up a courier line for He Long, to make certain that He Long and the others got safely out of Shanghai. Charged with this, Chen Yangshan again brought it off cleanly. In mid-January 1928, guarded by Lu Dongsheng, He Long and Zhou Yiqun left Shanghai by ship and reached Wuhan safe and sound, the whole way without mishap.
Twice Chen Yangshan took on the task of shielding He Long, and both times he brought it off, keeping He Long safe throughout his time in Shanghai. He Long did not forget it.
Fully sixty years later, in 1988, Chen Yangshan's secretaries Tang Baosen and Li Fanmin went with him to a meeting in Weihai, Shandong, and met a comrade who had worked at Chen Yangshan's side during the War of Resistance against Japan. This comrade told them that in the latter part of the war, when Chen Yangshan was director of the general investigation bureau of the Jin-Sui Border Region under He Long's command, He Long, speaking once of how important intelligence and security work was, said to some comrades who slighted such work that, after the failure of the Nanchang Uprising in 1927, when he went to Shanghai to find the Party, he had come near to being seized by the enemy but for Chen Yangshan's shielding. He Long pressed that intelligence and security work be taken seriously, and that the comrades who had given to the Party's intelligence cause and had rescued their fellows be honored. They felt this a valuable piece of history; they had never heard Chen Yangshan speak of it, and so they went to ask him, and he answered that yes, it had happened. But he charged them not to spread the story about, and not, even after he was gone, to tally up his merits for him. He Long, he said, was a remarkable man, who never forgot the help his comrades gave him. And he said that as a Communist, to work for the Party and to do things for his comrades was no more than his duty.
Five months after Chen Yangshan's death, Luo Qingchang wrote an essay in his memory, "The Hard Road of a Founding Life, Fought Through a Hundred Battles," one section of which was headed "Shielding Comrade He Long in Shanghai." He wrote:
{v} To answer the Kuomintang's betrayal of the revolution, our Party launched the "August 1" armed uprising in 1927. Under Comrade Zhou Enlai's leadership, Comrade He Long served as acting commander-in-chief of the uprising forces. When the uprising forces reached Chaoshan they were surrounded and overwhelmed by superior enemy numbers, and Comrade He Long made his way to Shanghai. Before the uprising He Long had been commander of the Kuomintang left's 20th Army, already a renowned figure, and the Kuomintang was under strict orders to hunt him down. In a Shanghai steeped in white terror, shielding He Long took real doing. The Party Center gave this hard and perilous task to Comrade Chen Yangshan, and from finding a place of concealment to arranging food and lodging and contact, he handled it all faultlessly and kept Comrade He Long entirely safe.
{v} On the question of Comrade He Long's line of work, the Party Center asked his own view, and he said he was willing to begin again where he had fallen, to go back to the northwest of western Hunan and build an army and a base. The Party Center then had Comrade Chen Yangshan set up a courier line and escort Comrade He Long smoothly to the Honghu region, and once again Chen Yangshan saw the task through. This gave Comrade He Long a first-hand sense of how vital intelligence work was; at the Party's "Seventh Congress" in 1945 he said in Yan'an that the work was remarkable and that this man Chen Yangshan was remarkable, and worth any amount of money spent on it. Comrade He Long thought highly of Chen Yangshan, and proposed to the Center, insisting on it, that Chen Yangshan be moved to the Jin-Sui Border Region as director of the Chinese Communist Party's general investigation bureau there.
