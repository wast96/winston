## The Women's Night Market

When Kondō Isami, commander of the Shinsengumi, was alone with his vice-commander, Hijikata Toshizō, in a place where no one else could hear, he would say,

{j} "Toshi,"

{j} or so the story goes. And when the two of them alone were weighing whether or not to cut a man down,

{j} "What're we gonna do about that bastard?"

{j} —and out would slip, unbidden, the country speech of Bushū Tama, where the two had been born. Kondō came from Kamiishiwara, Toshizō from Ishida village; both were hamlets along the Kōshū Highway, not three ri apart. Come the start of summer, it was farm country where every thicket, every last clump of grass, turned rank with the smell of vipers.

Now, then—about this "Toshi."

The life of Toshi—Toshizō, youngest brother of Kiroku, a farmer of Ishida village—changed course in the early summer of the fourth year of Ansei, in the viper season, just after the eighty-eighth night had passed.

It was hotter than most years.

That evening Toshizō left the village, turned straight onto the Kōshū Highway, and hurried the two and a half ri toward Musashi Fuchū.

He had the skirts of his yukata hitched up high.

He was tall. Broad in the shoulder, supple at the hips, and he walked with his weight sunk low. To anyone with an eye for it, it was the gait of a man who had put in long training with the sword.

His face was wrapped in a broad tenugui of plain dark blue, the ends let down over his cheeks and hanging, rakishly, to his chest.

He was a dandy.

Even with nothing but a length of hand-cloth he would contrive some style of his own—and, strangely, it suited him.

Speaking of dandyism, his topknot was of an odd cut. A farmer's son ought to have worn the plain *sukobin* style, but in the whole village this one man dressed his hair in a peculiar knot of his own devising—and, brazenly, one made to resemble a samurai's topknot.

About this outlandish topknot,

{j} "Know your station,"

{j} the village headman, Satō Hikogorō, had once scolded him; but Toshizō only lowered his eyes, a smile playing about his mouth.

"Ah, well—I'll be a samurai one of these days,"

{j} he said.

He never did change the topknot after that; he only took to wrapping his head in the dark blue cloth. And so in the village they jibed,

{j} "Toshi's let-off topknot,"

{j} —meaning this: the Hijikata house and the Satō house were kin, and because they were kin the headman let the oddity pass.

But more than the wrapped cloth, it was the eyes shining beneath it that marked the man. Large, double-lidded, long at the corners—eyes the women made a fuss over, calling them cool.

The men of the village, though, would say,

{j} "That Toshi—there's no telling what those eyes of his'll get up to,"

{j} so it was said.

And truly, there was no telling what the man might do.

Even now, for all that he walked the highway in nothing but a yukata, underneath it he wore, in secret, a jūjutsu practice robe.

As he came out at the edge of the post-town, an acquaintance heading home from the fields called to him,

{j} "Toshi—where you off to?"

{j} but he held his tongue.

He could hardly say he was going to force himself on a woman.

That night was the festival of the Rokusha Myōjin of Fuchū—popularly called the Darkness Festival.

Toshizō's scheme was this: to take advantage of the festival dark, catch some worshipping woman by the sleeve, pull her down, and have her. He would strip off his yukata then and spread it on the ground, so she would not be soaked by the night dew, and lay her upon it. The jūjutsu robe he wore was his provision against the chance of a scuffle with the men of her party.

The fault was not Toshizō's alone.

It was that kind of festival.

The worshippers on such a night came not only from around Fuchū, nor merely from the villages of the three Tama districts, but from as far off as Edo, staying the night; and once the lights of the whole district were put out and heaven and earth became a sacred darkness, men and women reverted to the ancients and joined with whoever came to hand.

From about the point where he passed Shimo-Yaho, the swarms of lanterns making for the Rokusha Myōjin of Fuchū began markedly to thicken.

In the direction of Edo, the moon rose.

Beneath the moon every man and woman pressed forward, a lantern in the left hand and a staff of green bamboo dragged in the right, raising an uncanny clatter as they went. It was the viper season, and so they had split the ends of the bamboo into a *sasara* brush and walked beating the road, driving the vipers off before them.

Toshizō too carried a green bamboo, but his staff alone was no ordinary one: he had knocked out the joints and run in molten lead, so that it lay heavy in the hand as an iron bar.

For frightening men, rather than vipers, this served very well.

In those parts they had a name for Toshizō, spoken behind his back—

{j} "the *baragaki* of Ishida village."

The word is written *bara-gaki*, "thorn-hedge"—the thorn that pricks you at a touch. It was cant for a rough, a hooligan; and since even now, in the country around Kōbe, they call a delinquent youth a *baraketsu*, it may be that in those days the slang was current across the provinces.

Toshizō reached Fuchū a little before the hour of the Dog.

Along the eaves of Fuchū's six hundred post-town houses hung pun-painted paper lamps and lanterns of sappanwood red; and down the two chō of the approach, the avenue of zelkovas was packed close with lanterns raised on high poles, bright as day.

***

It was, in a manner of speaking, a night market of women.

Toshizō walked about, appraising the women. Now and then he passed girls or wives from his own village, and one or another would pluck at his sleeve from the far side, but—

{j} "Stop that,"

{j} he would give her a fierce look. Toshizō had a curious streak of shame in him, and had never once lain with a woman of his own village; in one's own village the thing came out sooner or later. And so,

{j} "Toshi keeps himself to himself,"

{j} there was even such a reputation of him. Toshizō dreaded, to an extreme degree, being made the talk of the village over an affair.

There was no reason for it.

A kind of quirk, no doubt. And so,

{j} "Toshi's a cat,"

{j} some said as well. A dog, true enough, is blatant, but a cat never shows its couplings. And come to think of it, in more than that one respect Toshizō resembled this fierce, unbiddable night-prowler of a beast.

There was, in fact, a reason Toshizō had no wish to lie with the women of his village: a dirt-farmer's woman roused no desire in him whatever.

(A woman is her rank.)

{j} —so he believed. Not beauty, nor the lack of it. This was something like an article of faith with Toshizō.

Toward a woman of higher station than his own he felt an allurement that set him trembling. Few men, surely, are made to desire in just this fashion.

It was so, for one, when this man had his way with a certain unmarried girl the winter before.

The woman was the daughter of a great Shinshū temple in Hachiōji, and by the custom of that sect the daughter was addressed by its parishioners as—

{j} "the young lady."

{j} That was all Toshizō heard, and before he had so much as set eyes on the girl he wanted to bed her.

To get at her, Toshizō went to the trouble of lodging several days in Hachiōji, two ri off.

Incidentally, the people around Hachiōji knew Toshizō as "the medicine seller."

In those days the man peddled medicine as well.

True, Toshizō's house, farmers though they were, was reckoned a *daijin*—a house of wealth—in that district, and could have lived without hawking medicine; but the family had handed down a secret house remedy, called Ishida Powder, of proven virtue for broken bones and bruises.

The raw material was a weed resembling the morning glory, gathered on the bed of the Asakawa that ran near the village, its leaves said to bear thorns. The plant was picked on the Ox day of the midsummer *doyō*, dried well, then charred black and ground down in a medicine mortar into a powder, which the patient was made to swallow at a single draught in hot sake. It worked to an uncanny degree. Later, after the Ikedaya Affair, when Toshizō tried it on the wounded men of the Shinsengumi, prying open the mouth of each one to get it down him, it is said the knots left by their bruises loosened in about two days, and even the broken bones healed without the flesh mortifying.

Peddling this family remedy, the Ishida Powder, Toshizō tramped not only through Bushū but as far as Edo, Kōshū, and Sōshū. This had been his method of sword training since boyhood: he would stop in at the dōjō of one town after another, make a present of his medicine for bruises and broken bones, and in return beg a lesson or two.

Kajikawa Keiji, for one—a master of the Shintō Munen-ryū who kept a dōjō in Sakuramachi in Kōfu, where Toshizō in those days often extended his rounds and lodged—heard, later, the rumors of the Shinsengumi in Kyoto and said,

{j} "Hijikata Toshizō—that Bushū medicine seller? Yes, a man like that would do as much,"

{j} or so it is told.

It was the convenience of his medicine-selling that had let him worm his way into the Shinshū temple at Hachiōji.

The temple was called Senjubō.

The abbot took a liking to Toshizō.

"Put up in the temple shed, why don't you, and sell your wares about the neighborhood a few days,"

{j} he offered. Toshizō did not catch sight of the daughter, but through the daylight hours he studied the temple's buildings and grounds in detail, and learned that the girl's room was a small *sukiya*-style chamber the temple called the guest hall.

The next day he saw her for the first time. Meaning, perhaps, to feed the fish, she sat at the edge of the garden pond, bathing in the morning sun, when she noticed Toshizō passing and raised her face.

She knit her brows, her look wary.

It was no wonder.

Wrapped about the head in dark blue cloth, in a striped silk kimono with a *kenjō* sash—so far he had every look of a headman's eldest son; yet he had his skirts jauntily tucked up behind. And seen only from his leggings and the medicine box slung on his back, he seemed nothing but a peddler. What kept him from seeming even that, though, was the one point: the young man was also carrying a set of fencing gear.

She had never seen so mismatched a man. And yet, strangely, it all suited this fellow with the cool eyes.

(Who can he be?)

The girl stared at him.

To Toshizō's eye the girl was no great beauty, but she was small and looked meek, and that, he thought, was to his taste.

He did not so much as bow, however.

For all that he liked a woman of high station, this man had no taste for lowering his head to one and scattering charm about.

He only stepped two or three paces nearer and said,

{j} "Before long,"

{j} and that was all.

Before long—to do what?

By the time the girl lifted her eyes to ask, Toshizō had turned his back and was walking off toward the temple gate.

That night, at the hour of the Rat, Toshizō ran his water down the rain-shutter of the girl's room and slid it open without a sound. The young men of the villages of Bushū Tama use this trick when they go courting a girl by night.

Two women lay sleeping.

One was the girl's wet nurse, and when Toshizō listened at her pillow to her breathing, she was dead to the world.

Next he took in the girl's sleeping breath—low, small, and mellow; she too was lost to sleep.

Toshizō came round to the foot of the bedding. Lifting the quilt gently away, he bared the lower half of the girl's body.

He took the big toe of each foot between his fingers and lifted. To raise both legs by the toes alone is a terribly heavy business, but Toshizō knew it was the only way to keep the girl from waking.

Presently the girl's legs, parting the skirts of her robe, opened of their own accord, without a will in them. She had no more sensation than a corpse.

By the time the girl woke, the strange thing had already happened.

What surprised Toshizō, though, was that the girl did not cry out. Beyond holding her body rigid, she stifled even her breath and made no sound.

—Before long.

{j} The girl must already have understood what Toshizō had meant by it. If anything, she may have been secretly hoping that this good-looking young traveler would come stealing to her. For a young man to court a girl by night was no rare event in that country.

Seeing the girl's unexpected composure,

{j} (So this is the young lady?)

{j} it was Toshizō who felt let down. And the next day, when he saw her half-buried in the mulberry field that spread behind the temple, in field clothes, picking mulberry leaves, his disappointment only deepened.

(This is not it.)

{j} —for she was not the girl he had imagined. Girls in field clothes gone rank with the smell of mulberry there were in his own village too; he need not have come all the way out to Hachiōji. That evening the man set out from Hachiōji, and after that never once called at the Senjubō again.

It is a little unhinged; but the episode is proof of how strong his longing was for a woman of noble station.

Noble station, he might say—but the land of the three Tama districts of Bushū was all shogunal domain and temple-and-shrine land, with no warrior houses in it. The villages held only peasant girls reeking of horse dung. For want of anything better, some years back Toshizō had won over Kozakura, a bell-shaking shrine maiden of the Rokusha Myōjin at Fuchū, and now and then stole into the priests'-house tenement where she lived.

For tonight's festival Kozakura would be out dancing the maiden's dance, so he supposed he could not meet her; but at the daybreak when the rites ended, he meant to try stealing into the tenement.

In the meantime he would appraise the women. If some likely one caught his eye, then—by the vulgar custom of this festival—he would mark her out while the lamps still burned, and lie with her the moment the dark came down.

***

But there was no woman to his liking.

(A warrior's daughter, come from Edo—that would do.)

{j} —and Toshizō walked beneath the eave-lamps, and roamed about through the grove of the shrine precincts.

(None to be found?)

He had been at his appraising a good hour and more. But no daughter of an Edo *hatamoto* was likely, after all, to come to so bawdy a festival.

The worshippers, to be sure, were a bawdy lot, but the Rokusha Myōjin itself—the Ōkunitama Shrine—had from ancient times been the head shrine of Bushū, and the dignity of its festival was conspicuously high: the priests of the various shrines of Edo were pressed into service as its underlings. So lofty was its standing among shrines.

(Nothing for it, then.)

Toshizō thought he might as well go home. Several times, as he searched, a woman with the look of a smallholder's wife had whispered to him, but he would not so much as turn his head.

Then, from the wood about the main shrine, came the sharp cry of a festival officer; and no sooner had the drum of the hour of the Rat rung out, announcing the procession of the *mikoshi*, than the ten thousand lanterns went out all at once, and everything around fell into darkness.

A sacred darkness.

Only the stars were visible, and the tens of thousands of the crowd held their breath, waiting for the male god's *mikoshi* to go to the goddess. It was during this interval that the coupling of men and women took place. That too, the worshippers believed, was a sacred rite to gladden the gods of the Rokusha.

And so men and women joined as shadows only, and made not a single sound; they feared to defile the god's majesty. There were virgins taken standing upright, and wives thrown down beneath the feet of the crowd; but every one of them, though she clenched her teeth, let slip no cry.

Toshizō's luck that night was this: at the very instant the ten thousand lanterns went out, there was a woman at his side.

Why the woman had drawn so close to Toshizō, there is no telling.

The place was not the approach, packed with the crush of the crowd, but within the grove of the precincts. It had been dark to begin with, so that even while the lamps burned he had not marked the woman's shape—nor, likely, had she his. Only when he drew her to him and found that she wore a silk of extraordinary softness to the touch was Toshizō startled.

(What can she be?)

Groping over her dress, he found it four robes layered hiyoku-fashion with a changed under-hem, such as not even a headman's daughter wore in that country. And she seemed to keep a scent sachet hidden in her bosom—a fragrance the likes of which a man such as Toshizō had never smelled.

"You—who are you?"

{j} At last he broke the prohibition, and whispered it.

But the woman—believing, perhaps, that this was a sacred rite—shook her head in silence.

"Tell me."

"I cannot say it."

It was a bright voice. And it was not the broad country speech of Bushū; the ends of her words were soft.

"You—does it not trouble you?"

"It does not trouble me."

Toshizō pressed the woman down onto the grass and took her, with the dizzying rush of a man knowing a woman for the first time. That in taking this woman he had, before long, taken hold of his own new destiny along with her—of that, of course, he had no inkling now.

(I can't make sense of her.)

The woman's body had known a man already. And yet, by the manner of her dress, she was an unmarried girl.

Even as he held her, Toshizō deftly drew out from within the woman's sash a dagger sheathed in a brocade bag. With this, he thought, her station could be learned afterward.

The woman, never noticing, presently set her disordered dress to rights there on the grass and vanished into the dark.

When the rites had ended and day was beginning to break, Toshizō had stolen into Kozakura's tenement within the shrine-maidens' quarters.

"Here's the thing,"

{j} he said, and showed her the dagger.

The blade was a splendid piece, its ground raised in a *hijiki* seaweed grain, and the signature read Norishige. If it was the Etchū Norishige, there were but a handful of them in all the world.

But Kozakura would not so much as look at the blade; she took up the brocade bag, held it to the lamp, and then—

{j} "You—with this person?"

{j} —made a show of astonishment.

"You really lay with her?"

"That's right. The scent of that sachet is on my body still."

"Do you know this crest?"

{j} —and Kozakura pinched up and held out the crest of a five-leaf chrysanthemum, worked in gold thread on red brocade.

"Never seen it."

"It's the reverse crest of the Saruwatari house, the chief priests here in Fuchū. You've done a dreadful thing. This dagger-bag—I know it. It belongs to the Lady Osae, younger sister of the present Saruwatari, Lord of Sado, of Junior Fourth Rank, Lower."

"Is that so."

Toshizō took up the bag and stared, as though to bore into it, at the five-leaf chrysanthemum crest.
