## The Birth of the Central Special Section

### Without Intelligence and Security Work, You Take a Beating

From its founding in 1921, the Chinese Communist Party lived under the murderous hand of its enemies—first the Northern Warlords, then the Kuomintang reactionaries—and could work only in ways that were "wholly illegal." Simply keeping the Party's organizations alive across the vast territory the Kuomintang ruled was extremely hard, and strengthening and spreading them harder still. All of this had to be done under a savage White Terror, with the reactionary authorities hunting Communists down and killing them. It demanded not only great courage and endurance, but keen intelligence and capable leadership.

"Know the enemy and know yourself, and in a hundred battles you will win a hundred victories." This is the art every strategist lives by, the talisman of victory. In the chapter "Attack by Stratagem" in Sun Tzu's *Art of War*, it says: "Know the enemy and know yourself, and you will never be in peril in a hundred battles; know yourself but not the enemy, and for every victory you will suffer a defeat; know neither the enemy nor yourself, and you will lose every battle." What holds for armed struggle holds for political struggle too. It is a law of victory, and nothing anywhere, in any age, escapes it.

The Chinese Communist Party's intelligence and security work was created in the fire of a harsh class struggle, to forestall the enemy's sabotage and keep the Party safe. In its early days the Party did not yet grasp how important it was to wield intelligence as a weapon. Because it failed to take up that weapon, it lagged in knowing the enemy and protecting itself, and when the enemy struck it was often caught off guard and beaten.

Mao Zedong said: "The salvoes of the October Revolution brought us Marxism-Leninism." Imperialism and the feudal warlords feared nothing so much as the awakening of the Chinese people and their willingness to fight back. So the moment Marxism-Leninism reached China, these oppressor forces treated it as a monstrous flood, and the Communist Party and every progressive body as heresy. They posted detectives everywhere to keep watch, sent troops and police to round up suspects, and meant to strangle the revolutionary movement in its cradle. The lessons written in blood are there for all to see. For instance:

On July 23, 1921, the First Congress of the Chinese Communist Party opened at the home of Li Hanjun, 106 Rue Wantz in the French Concession of Shanghai (today 76 Xingye Road). On the last day of the congress a stranger suddenly pushed into the meeting room, saying he had come to the wrong place looking for someone. Maring, the Comintern's representative, was alert; he asked whether anyone knew the man, and when everyone said no, he was sure the man was a spy and told them to leave at once. The meeting broke off and the delegates got out quickly. Ten minutes later the French Concession police came to search the room. The delegates moved to a pleasure boat on South Lake at Jiaxing and finished the last day's session there. (The spy who had burst in was Cheng Ziqing, a detective-sergeant in the political section of the French Concession police. A Zhenjiang man, he entered the French police in 1905 and served there thirty-eight years, until 1943.)

In truth the enemy's watching and hunting had begun before the Chinese Communist Party was even formally founded. Chen Duxiu, dean of the College of Letters at Peking University, had joined the Shanghai Communist group—set up in early 1920 with the Comintern's help—while still living in Beijing. That year, for no more than handing out May Day leaflets in a public place, he was arrested. Freed after much effort on his behalf, he was forced to leave Beijing for Shanghai, and *New Youth*, the Marxist journal he edited, moved with him and was published there. In October 1921 Chen Duxiu was elected secretary of the Party's Central Bureau—in effect, its general secretary. Soon afterward, enemy agents raided his lodgings at No. 2 Old Yuyang Lane in Shanghai. They took him away, and with him the others who had been meeting at his home: Bao Huiseng, Yang Mingzhai, Ke Qingshi, and Chen Duxiu's wife, Gao Junman. Only after much effort, and eight days in custody, was he released on payment of a fine.

No. 2 Old Yuyang Lane was the distribution point for *New Youth* and other progressive publications, and the Party often held meetings and made contacts there; a great many people came and went. Lodging Chen Duxiu in such a place, for want of experience, was as good as handing the Central Bureau's office to the enemy. The Party was still in its infancy then, with no sense of the enemy's presence and no strength to build an intelligence and security body; when danger came, all it could do was "move the revolution elsewhere" for a time and cut its losses. So the enemy's persecution went on. In the spring of 1922, no sooner had Zhang Guotao hung out the sign of the "China Labor Union Secretariat" at his rooms on Chengdu Road in Shanghai than the International Settlement police searched and closed the place, and Zhang was forced to flee. In July 1922, soon after the Party's Second National Congress, the Central Committee founded a weekly to be sold openly, its organ *The Guide*. It ran only four issues before the concession police searched and banned it. From the sixth issue on, its editing and printing had to move from Shanghai to Beijing, and the Central Bureau moved with it. After the "February 7" strike failed in 1923, the warlord government pressed its assault on the revolution harder, the situation in Beijing turned suddenly tense, and the Central Bureau had to move back to Shanghai. Before long Shanghai worsened too, and the Party's Third National Congress, along with *The Guide* and *New Youth*, had to move to Guangzhou. Only in September 1923, when Shanghai improved, did they move back.

In 1924, after the first Kuomintang-Communist united front, some members of the Chinese Communist Party joined the Kuomintang and carried on open revolutionary work under its name. At the same time the Party drew a strict line between open and secret work: wherever activity could be public, it used that public standing to shield the illegal struggle. In this way the Party began to do intelligence and security work through the united front. In the spring of 1925 the revolution turned quickly for the better, and Party organizations led the workers' and peasants' movements across the country. That year the Party-led "May Thirtieth" anti-imperialist movement threw the whole country into strikes of workers, students, and shopkeepers. Some foreign factory owners crushed strikes, forced or tricked workers back to their machines, and even murdered union cadres and rank-and-file. To keep the strikes going and protect the workers, the pickets led by the Party's West Shanghai, Pudong, and East Shanghai district committees crushed a number of vicious enemies, steadied the strike, and struck back at imperialist provocation. Later, in the three armed uprisings of the Shanghai workers, the Shanghai Workers' Pickets were formed: 5,000 strong, with 300 guns, they resisted the enemy's repression and stood up to the bullying of scabs and running dogs. In these struggles the pickets showed formidable strength, and they gathered experience in tightening secrecy and security.

Yet it was only after taking to heart the bitter lesson of the "April 12" counter-revolutionary coup that Zhou Enlai and other leading comrades of the Central Committee came to understand intelligence and security work and to weigh it as it deserved.

### Red Intelligence Workers First Prove Themselves in Wuhan

In March 1927 Zhou Enlai led the Shanghai workers' third armed uprising to victory. He had left Guangzhou in December 1926; reaching Shanghai, he became secretary of the Central Committee's Organization Department and a member of its Military Commission. In February 1927 he was made secretary of the Military Commission of the Party's Shanghai District Committee. On February 23 the Central Committee and the Shanghai District Committee held a joint meeting and resolved to set up a Special Committee, with Zhou among its members, and a Special Military Commission with Zhou as its secretary, to prepare the workers' third armed uprising; when the uprising came, Zhou served as its commander-in-chief.

The uprising, launched on March 21, was directed as a whole by Zhou Enlai together with Luo Yinong, He Songlin (Wang Shouhua), Chen Duxiu, and Zhao Shiyan. After twenty-eight hours of fighting they took the North Railway Station, the Northern Warlords' last strongpoint in Shanghai. Victory rested wholly on the courage of the organized industrial workers, who beat several thousand reactionary troops and police. It was a shining page in the history of the Chinese working class's own armed struggle.

Just as the Shanghai workers were celebrating their victory, Chiang Kai-shek and others led the Northern Expedition army into the city and at once stabbed the newly risen working class in the back, launching the "April 12" counter-revolutionary coup. From the night of April 11 into the morning of the 12th they first disarmed the workers' pickets by trickery, and on the 13th they began a frenzy of slaughter and arrest; Communists and workers were cut down in large numbers. Bai Chongxi, who had led the Eastern Route of the Northern Expedition army into Shanghai when the workers' uprising won, set up a Kuomintang garrison command in the Longhua arsenal and made Yang Hu its commander. The Kuomintang's political sub-council member for Shanghai at the time was Chen Qun; the two of them worked hand in glove to kill Communists, and people called them "a pack of tigers and wolves." By incomplete count, in the three days after the April 12 coup more than 300 people were killed, more than 500 arrested, and more than 5,000 driven into exile or reported missing. Massacres followed elsewhere one after another. In the Guangzhou "April 15" massacre alone, more than 2,100 Communists and workers were butchered.

A White Terror settled over the whole of China, and the Chinese Communist Party's organizations were all forced underground.

On April 14 Zhou Enlai moved to a small attic in a worker's home at Xujiazhai, near Wusong, and went on working there. He drafted a telegram to the Central Committee, signed jointly with Zhao Shiyan, Luo Yinong, Chen Yannian, Li Lisan, and Yin Kuan, urging that troops be sent at once to punish Chiang Kai-shek. But the Soviet adviser Borodin and the Party's general secretary, Chen Duxiu, did not take up the proposal.

When Chiang Kai-shek launched his coup in Shanghai, the Party and the people, wholly unprepared, were struck without warning, and the revolution suffered heavy losses. At this critical hour for the Chinese revolution the Central Committee moved from Shanghai to Wuhan and, in late April, held the Party's Fifth National Congress in Hankou. Zhou Enlai did not attend, but the ability he had shown leading military work in Guangdong and the third workers' uprising in Shanghai was by now understood and acknowledged across the Party. The congress elected him to the Central Committee, and its plenum went on to elect him to the Politburo.

In late May 1927 Zhou Enlai came secretly from Shanghai to Wuhan, where the Central Committee sat. He joined its work as soon as he arrived. On May 22 he sat in on a meeting of the Standing Committee, and on the 25th on its tenth meeting, which appointed him head of the Central Military Department and ruled that the head of that department would join the Standing Committee when needed. On the 29th the Standing Committee made Zhou an acting member and brought him into the Party's core leadership. From then on, for half a century, he was always one of the Central Committee's chief leaders.

By now the bitter lessons of the revolution's defeat weighed on Zhou Enlai and on many comrades who had held central posts. Through the practice of revolution, and above all through defeat, the Party had drawn a hard lesson: to fight a strong enemy you must know him, know his military and political moves, before you can strike him accurately and to real effect, and so defeat him. Once Zhou took charge of the Military Department, he set up beneath it a Special Services Section centered on intelligence and security work. The department's load was especially heavy just then: it had to plan and deploy the whole of the Central Committee's military work and stand ready at every moment for armed struggle with the Kuomintang reactionaries.

This new intelligence and security work, under Zhou Enlai's direct leadership, stood on the front line of the military and political struggle between the enemy and ourselves, and it made a major contribution.

Li Qiang, who took part in intelligence and security work at the Central Military Department in Wuhan—later commonly called the Central Military Commission—spoke of those days in October 1981. He said: "After 'April 12' I went to Wuhan, to the Central Military Commission there. Its secretary then was Comrade Zhou Enlai. Under the commission were two chiefs: one over a staff office, whose chief of staff is still with us, Comrade Nie Rongzhen; and a secretary-general, Comrade Wang Yifei, who was killed. There were also two sections. One was the Organization Section, headed by Comrade Ouyang Qin; later a Special Services Section was formed, also called the Central Military Commission Special Section, run by Gu Shunzhang, who had been elected to the Central Committee at the Fifth Congress. I was in the Special Services Section, and it was divided into four squads. One was the guard squad, which saw to the safety of the central leaders and the central offices. Take the guarding of Comrade Zhou Enlai: the comrade who guarded him is still alive, in Shanghai, named Yang Fulin, eighty-four this year, once a deputy district head in Shanghai's Putuo District, now retired. I found him last year. There was also the task of guarding eight important leaders, and Borodin—sixty men guarded him. The head of the guard squad had come from Shanghai too, a man named Li Jianru. In those days the men of the guard squad were not counted as soldiers, nor were they police; every one of them wore a student's three-pocket tunic. Another was the intelligence squad, which gathered intelligence, collecting each day in Wuhan whatever could generally be learned. Its head was Fat Dong—Dong Xingwu, perhaps, or Dong Shengwu. Another was the special-service squad, which eliminated traitors and enemy agents and handled other special tasks the Central Committee assigned. The special-service squad did the 'Red Squad' work; when people said 'Red Squad,' they meant the 'Red Terror Squad.' As for the so-called 'Dog-Beating Squad'—before I ever arrived it had done two jobs, one that came off and one that did not; after I came, we did a few things there too. This was the special-service squad, and as its head I had only three men under me. One was called 'Old Whiteface,' one 'Little Whiteface,' one Wang Zhuqiao, and there were others brought in for a time. There was also a 'bandit-work squad'—an odd name; it did 'bandit work,' meaning it set out to win over and remake the local armed bands people called 'bandits.' The Red Spear societies and the rest of them all came to us, joined the revolution, and we took them into our army. Four squads, then: one guard, one intelligence, one special-service, one 'bandit-work.' That was the Special Services Section. How many squads the Organization Section had, I don't know. The commission's office was at Yuji Lane in Hankou; we worked at No. 12 Yuji Lane. The houses there were like the three-up-three-down, two-wing lane houses of Shanghai. Comrade Zhou Enlai worked there too, the secretariat was there, and so were the Organization Section and the Special Services Section (the chief of staff, Comrade Nie Rongzhen, worked at Zhonghe Lane in Wuchang). Of the people who worked there, only about three are left now: myself, old Nie, and Xu Yixin—the Xu Yixin who was later ambassador to Pakistan. He was on the secretariat, six years younger than me; I was twenty-two then, he was only sixteen, and he kept Premier Zhou's files. To this day I have not found a fourth. At the time of the 'Cultural Revolution' there were still five of us: Premier Zhou was alive, Ouyang Qin was still here…"

Li Qiang went on: "At that time the situation in Wuhan worsened step by step. Wang Jingwei turned from a 'Nanjing-Wuhan standoff' toward a 'Nanjing-Wuhan merger.' There were reactionaries in the army too—Xia Douyin's mutiny, Xu Kexiang's 'Horse Day Incident' at Changsha. We were preparing then to gather our forces for an uprising and to bring troops over. Our own force was the 24th Division, Ye Ting's command under Zhang Fakui. The rest—men serving as regimental or battalion commanders in other units—we won over what we could and gathered at Nanchang. That was the work we did. By this time Borodin could stay no longer and had to go. So from the sixty men of the guard squad's escort we picked thirty and sent him to Moscow. How? We got hold of a fleet of cars and took him through Henan, into Shaanxi, up to northern Shaanxi, across Mongolia, and on to Moscow.

"In those days every division in the army had two Russian advisers, each carrying a gun, and before they left they had to hand the guns in—every gun came to me. After they withdrew, our own comrades in the army, and others outside it, all made ready to move out for the Nanchang Uprising. Our work there changed by degrees. In the end I had a hand in everything, a part in the running of it all…"

The Special Services Section of the Wuhan Central Military Department did not last long, but its achievements were considerable. In those days the Kuomintang's reactionary troops raised revolt after revolt, meaning to wipe out the revolutionary forces in Wuhan. The Party's intelligence work played a large part in that struggle, exposing the enemy's military plots in time. The reactionaries' main schemes—Xia Douyin's defection and attack on Wuhan from Yichang on May 17; Xu Kexiang's revolt and slaughter of Communists at Changsha on May 21; the secret talks Wang Jingwei and others held with Feng Yuxiang at Zhengzhou on June 12; Chiang Kai-shek's conference with Feng Yuxiang at Xuzhou on June 20 over anti-Communism and a Nanjing-Wuhan alliance; the secret meeting Wang Jingwei and others convened on July 14 to propose breaking with the Communists—of all these major moves we obtained intelligence in time, by open channels and secret ones alike, so that we could well have avoided every surprise attack and beaten back the enemy's assaults with warning in hand. But because Chen Duxiu clung to a mistaken line of capitulation, we grasped the enemy's situation in time and yet failed to shape the response the struggle called for, failed to strike the enemy hard while the moment held. As Zhou Enlai put it: "Because the whole political line was wrong, we had the intelligence and were numb to it all the same. We had intelligence on every major question, and the plain result was that we watched ourselves go down to defeat; political immaturity brought us to it."

Brief as the section's life was, its founding by Zhou Enlai in the Wuhan Military Department mattered enormously and reached far. Setting up a dedicated intelligence body within the Party, running intelligence operations, building a security apparatus—this was something new in the Party's history. Because such an organization now existed, we held the initiative in the struggle with Wang Jingwei, and it did much to smash the enemy's anti-Communist plots and to head off counter-revolutionary massacre. Above all, it gathered experience and trained cadres for the covert struggle, for the protection of the underground Party.

### Building the Central Special Section to Protect the Party Organization

On "July 15," 1927, Wang Jingwei followed Chiang Kai-shek in formally declaring a break with the Communists and openly betraying the revolution. The Great Revolution, for all its fire, had failed. On the eve of Wang's betrayal, the Party's general secretary, Chen Duxiu, offered his resignation, which the Comintern approved. On July 12 the Central Committee was reorganized: a Provisional Standing Committee of five—Zhou Enlai, Zhang Guotao, Zhang Tailei, Li Lisan, and Li Weihan—was formed, and Chen Duxiu was suspended. To save the Chinese revolution, the Central Committee resolved in mid-July to mount the Nanchang Uprising, and Zhou Enlai was ordered to head the Party's Front Committee. On July 26, with Chen Geng at his side, Zhou hurried to Jiujiang; on the 27th he came in secret to Nanchang, still under Kuomintang control, and stayed at Zhu De's lodgings. On August 1, Zhou Enlai, together with He Long, Ye Ting, Zhu De, Liu Bocheng, and others, led the Nanchang Uprising and fired the first shot of armed resistance to the Kuomintang reactionaries, opening a new age in which the Chinese Communist Party led revolutionary armed struggle on its own and built an army of its own.

After the Nanchang Uprising, that same night, a staff-council meeting chaired by Zhou Enlai resolved—following the Central Committee's set plan to march south at once and take Guangdong—to send the insurgent army south straight for the East River. Zhou led it through the Kuomintang forces that blocked the way and into Guangdong by way of Fujian in September. On the 23rd and 24th its main body took Chaozhou and Shantou in Guangdong without trouble, only to be surrounded at once by Kuomintang troops three times their number; Chaozhou and Shantou both fell by the month's end. In early October, marching west, the insurgents fought a hard action against Kuomintang forces in the Baishi district of Jieyang and the Liusha district of Puning. Zhou Enlai, gravely ill by now, still stood with He Long, Ye Ting, and the others directing the men in a spirited counterattack; outnumbered in the end, they were beaten.

Zhou Enlai had malignant malaria; by the time he reached Liusha he was gravely sick and often lay unconscious. In early October he was carried on a stretcher, with Ye Ting and Nie Rongzhen, led by Yang Shihun, the local Party chief, to Taizi Harbor in Lufeng, where they found a small boat and fought the open sea for two days and a night to reach Hong Kong. After more than two weeks of treatment his health slowly mended. The Central Committee, by now moved back to Shanghai, had decided to hold an emergency conference—later called the enlarged meeting of the Provisional Politburo—and told Zhou to reach Shanghai before November 7 to attend. So in early November he took a boat from Sham Shui Po in Kowloon to Shanghai.

The conference sat for two days, November 9 and 10. It added Zhou Enlai and Luo Yinong to the Standing Committee of the Politburo. Because the Party was now underground, the meeting also changed the shape of the central apparatus: below the Standing Committee there would be no departments but a single Organization Bureau, directing the sections for organization, propaganda, military affairs, investigation, special services, courier work, records, and publishing. The bureau was made up of three men—Luo Yinong, Zhou Enlai, and Li Weihan—with Luo as its director. When Luo left late that month to guide the work in Wuhan, Zhou acted in his place, and on January 10, 1928, a meeting of the Politburo confirmed Zhou as director of the Organization Bureau. From then on he carried the responsibility for the Central Committee's day-to-day work.

By the time Zhou Enlai reached Shanghai, the Central Committee had been back from Wuhan for two or three months. There was much in Shanghai's favor as the seat of the Party's leadership. It was the country's largest city, its industry and commerce thriving, a gathering place of industrial workers. Here the working class was the largest and the most militant, with a proud fighting tradition. Here the Chinese Communist Party had been born; here stood its strongest organization in the land, bound close to the people of Shanghai, who sheltered the Party well. Yet Shanghai was also an imperialist colony, a paradise for the world's adventurers, and for nearly a hundred years the base from which imperialists ruled, squeezed, and enslaved the Chinese people. From the day foreign invaders first set foot on this soil, countless merchants under grand pretenses, priests in black cassocks, and diplomats in plumed hats and swords had swarmed in from far-off lands like flies to carrion. Behind them came the stateless desperadoes, the criminals without passports, the traffickers in human beings, the brokers of smuggled opium, the pirates who killed for plunder—each pressing in under the great banner of "extraterritoriality," all treating this city of gilded dissipation as their last refuge. Criminals of every stripe, Chinese and foreign, plied their trade in the concessions. With Chinese and foreigners jumbled together, authority split among many hands, and the enemy camp riddled with its own contradictions, there were many gaps a revolutionary could use; the city's people were a mixed multitude, household registers went unchecked, lodgings were easy to find, and it was far easier here than in any other city to set up a Party office and run secret work under cover. And so both the Central Committee's organs and the Jiangsu Provincial Committee were based here.

There were disadvantages too. The Central Committee's organs sat in the French Concession; but the French Concession, like the International Settlement, was a "state within a state" that the imperialists had planted in Shanghai on the strength of unequal treaties, its administrative power held wholly by the French consul-general and beyond the reach of any Chinese government. Under the treaties' provisions on extraterritoriality, judicial power in the concessions lay with foreign consular courts, and Chinese courts had no standing to intervene. Yet in the business of opposing the Communist Party and crushing the revolutionary people, the foreign powers stood on common ground, shared a common interest, and colluded and acted in concert. The nerve center of imperialist rule over the Chinese within the concessions—the political department of the French Concession police—was the very organ through which they, in league with the Kuomintang reactionaries and with bureaucrats, politicians, warlords, local gentry, and street toughs, spied out political, military, and economic intelligence, meddled in China's affairs, and crushed the people's revolutionary movement. Imperialist land and sea forces massed at Shanghai. Constables of Britain, France, India, Annam, and other nations stood on every street corner, ruling the concessions. A host of Chinese constables, detectives, hoodlums, secret-society men, and reactionary elements served the imperialists as their watchdogs. Beyond this, Shanghai was the Kuomintang's political, economic, diplomatic, and cultural center; heavy garrisons stood in its territory, with military police, police, and secret-service organs on every hand, visiting a cruel White Terror on the people.

From the time the Central Committee moved back to Shanghai, Kuomintang secret agents and detectives worked hand in glove with the constables and detectives of the French Concession and International Settlement police to hunt down the Party's leaders, its members and Youth League members, and worker leaders; every week someone was seized or killed. Some men turned coat and gave themselves to the enemy, and these soul-selling traitors, too, made common cause with the reactionaries, betraying revolutionaries and the Party's secrets. For a time the traitors were wildly bold. As early as the end of June, a Jiangsu Provincial Committee courier was arrested and turned, informed on his comrades, and so brought about the arrest, one after another, of the provincial secretary Chen Yannian, the organization chief Guo Bohe, and the provincial secretary-general Han Buxian. Once taken, Han Buxian turned traitor: he not only identified Chen Yannian and Guo Bohe, sending them to their deaths at the enemy's hands, but gave up the address of another provincial leader, Zhao Shiyan, and led the detectives there to seize him, so that Zhao Shiyan too was killed.

Seeing the enemy so brazen, Zhou Enlai felt keenly that if in such conditions no intelligence and security work were built, and no strict discipline of secret work kept, they would be open at any moment to attack from enemies of every kind, and the Party's very survival did not bear thinking about. Weighed down as he was just then with many heavy central duties, for the Party's safety he turned the matter over and over and concluded that only by taking the offensive could they protect themselves. So he set energetically about organizing the covert struggle. Drawing together the lessons of past work, in the autumn of 1927 he proposed to the Central Committee that a Central Special Section be formed, and he set out the thinking behind it: the underground Party must have a dedicated body for intelligence and security, and must act through the methods of intelligence and security work, to keep the Party safe and forestall the enemy's sabotage.

Even before the Central Special Section existed, cadres of the Wuhan Special Services Section, Li Qiang among them, had taken on much of the work of guarding the Central Committee as it moved back to Shanghai—the courier line escorting leading comrades from Hankou to Shanghai, the transfer of important documents and equipment, the setting up of the leading organs in Shanghai, the lodging and safety of the chief comrades—and through much effort they carried the move through. Around the time the Central Special Section was formed, the former Special Services men were mainly responsible for setting up the central offices and for other security work; most of the section's early cadres came from that quarter as well.

The Central Military Commission Special Section and the Central Special Section were both born when the Kuomintang had betrayed the revolution, the Great Revolution had failed, and the Chinese Communist Party was beginning to wage armed struggle on its own. The Military Commission section made the swift building of the Central Special Section possible. The Central Special Section took up the tradition of the Wuhan-era Military Commission special work and carried a new struggle into new conditions.

The Central Special Section and its branches were built, and gradually brought to full strength, under Zhou Enlai's direct leadership, beginning in the autumn of 1927. First came Section One, a renaming of the old "General Office" that had served the central organs alone. Section Three was built on the Red Squad (the "Dog-Beating Squad"), formed from the former Wuhan Special Services Section and part of the Shanghai armed workers' pickets, and was charged with putting down traitors and enemy agents. Then came Section Two, whose main task was gathering intelligence and keeping track of the enemy. Last to be set up was Section Four, which ran the radio station.

On November 14, 1928, a meeting of the Party's Standing Committee resolved to form a Special Committee of Xiang Zhongfa, Zhou Enlai, and Gu Shunzhang to lead the work of the Central Special Section. Zhou gave much of himself to that leadership and strengthened the Party's security work on every side.

In the spring of 1928, to train cadres for secret work to meet the demands of struggle in the new conditions, the Special Committee opened a twenty-day training class. Gu Shunzhang, Chen Geng, and nearly all the members of the Central Special Section took part. The teaching turned on how to do secret work, how to sustain the struggle in the White areas, how to protect oneself and strike the enemy, and the like; there was training too in the techniques and the discipline of secret work. Zhou Enlai, Yun Daiying, and others gave the trainees a number of political talks.

For the underground Party to survive and act under the White Terror, every member had to keep the discipline of secret work strictly, use open and legal forms to shield "illegal" activity, stay close to the masses, and guard at all times against the enemy's attacks. Zhou Enlai set great store by this. He checked constantly on how the discipline was kept and dealt sharply with comrades who broke it. On April 19, 1930, the Central Committee issued "A Letter from the Central Committee on Secret Work to All Comrades Working in the Central Departments and Committees," which said: "The Central Committee, alive to the importance of secret work, has pressed the matter on every comrade again and again; yet in daily life the ordinary comrade still neglects it, whether consciously or not, and this is no small hindrance to the safety and progress of our offices and our work, and, in the larger view, aids the enemy." Along with this the Central Committee laid down fourteen articles binding every comrade to grave responsibility for the safety of the offices. Zhou also asked the comrades in the Special Section to find open occupations wherever they could, to cover themselves—to grow into settled trades and into society, mixing with people of every kind and making friends widely.

The rules of secrecy Zhou Enlai laid down for the Central Special Section he led were extremely strict. Everyone in it was kept to a single line of contact; each held the content of his own work in strict secrecy, to be disclosed to no one, not even his own kin. Chen Yangshan entered the section soon after the Intelligence Section was set up in April 1928 and stayed until the section was disbanded in September 1935—the longest service of anyone in the Central Special Section. He once remarked that in those days, working on a single line of contact, he knew only his own piece of the work; even the name of the unit he served in, "the Central Special Section," he did not learn until he reached Xi'an in 1936. The detail is startling, and it shows just how deep the secrecy ran.
