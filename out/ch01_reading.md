## Chapter One

The sun had just sunk below the horizon. A soft wind blew against people's faces in gust after gust, oddly ticklish. The turbid water of Suzhou Creek shimmered into a gold-green and slid away westward, lightly, quietly. The evening tide of the Huangpu had somehow already risen; now the boats of every kind along both banks of the creek rode high, their decks a good half-foot above the wharf. The wind brought over the music from the park on the Bund, though of it only the brass drums, rattling like beans in a hot pan, came through clearly — and stirred the blood most of all. Dusk, wrapped in a thin haze, shrouded the towering steel frame of the Waibaidu Bridge, and whenever a tram ran across it the wires slung through the air beneath that frame threw off little sprays of malachite-green sparks. Looking east from the bridge you could see the foreign warehouses of Pudong crouching like huge monsters in the murk, glinting with hundreds upon hundreds of little lamplit eyes. Looking west, what startled you was a vast neon sign mounted high on the roof of a Western house, throwing out a fiery red glare and a jade-green flame: Light, Heat, Power!

At this hour — this heaven of a May evening — three 1930-model Citroëns came across the Waibaidu Bridge like streaks of lightning, swung west, and ran straight on along North Suzhou Road.

Past the Shanghai General Chamber of Commerce, at the mouth of North Henan Road, the stretch of road to its west — nicknamed the "Iron-Horse Road" — was where the little inland steam launches gathered. Reaching this point, the three cars slackened their speed. The driver of the first car said in a low voice to the burly giant of a man beside him, who was dressed head to foot in black glazed silk:

"Old Guan! That's Daishengchang, isn't it?"

"Of course it is! How could you forget? That little tramp must have addled your wits!"

Old Guan too spoke low, baring a mouthful of big teeth that looked as if they could bite through an iron bar. He was the bodyguard. Just then the car stopped short; Old Guan sprang down at once, felt for the Browning at his waist, shot a glance in every direction, then went and opened the car door and stood beside it, awesome and imposing. A head craned out of the compartment first — a square, purple-brown face, thick eyebrows, round eyes, the skin scattered with little pimples. Seeing the six big characters, "Daishengchang Steamship Office," over the gate of the small Western house facing him, the man too jumped down and walked straight in. Old Guan followed at his heels.

"Is the Yunfei steamer nearly in?"

The purple-faced man asked it haughtily, his voice ringing and clear. He was about forty, powerfully built and stern in manner, plainly one of those "big shots" long used to ordering people about. Before he had even finished, the steamship clerks sitting there sprang up all together, and one lanky fellow among them, his whole face wreathed in smiles, stepped forward and answered with great deference:

"Soon, soon, Third Master! Do sit a moment. — Bring tea!"

As he spoke the lanky man dragged over a chair and set it behind the Third Master. A muscle moved in the Third Master's face, something like a smile; he threw the lanky fellow a glance and turned his eyes to the doorway. By now the Third Master's own car had driven off, and the second car had taken its place; from it stepped a man and a woman, who came in too. The man was short and thickset, a little plump, with an amiable, pale face. The woman was much taller, square-faced as well, somewhat like the Third Master, but fair and smooth of complexion. Both were past forty, though the woman, being fashionably got up, looked at most about thirty. The man spoke first:

"Sunfu, are we to wait here?"

Before the purple-faced Sunfu could answer, the lanky clerk had already put in with an ingratiating smile:

"Just so, just so, son-in-law. We have already heard her whistle sound. I have posted a man to watch, and the moment the boat is alongside he will come in and report. Five minutes more at the very most — five minutes!"

"Ah, Fusheng, so you are still here? Good! In business one must have staying power. The old master always said you were willing to better yourself. It must be some years since you saw the old master?"

"Last month I went back to our village and paid my respects to him. — Do sit down, Madam."

The lanky man called Fusheng, hearing the mistress praise him, was as pleased as could be; answering quickly, he turned and dragged over two more chairs to set behind the son-in-law and his wife, and busied himself offering tea and cigarettes. He was the son of an old servant in the household of Sunfu, the Third Master; quick-witted from boyhood, he had been placed in this Daishengchang Steamship Office at the express bidding of Sunfu's father, old Mr. Wu. But Sunfu and his two companions would not sit down first; their eyes were all on the doorway. Out on the road by the entrance stood another burly giant of a man, his back to the door, glancing ceaselessly left and right — the bodyguard the son-in-law, Du Zhuzhai, took about with him.

Mrs. Du let out a soft breath of relief and sat down first; taking a small printed silk handkerchief, she dabbed at her lips a few times and turned back to Sunfu:

"Third Brother, last year when Zhuzhai and I went home to sweep the graves, we came on this Yunfei boat too. She is a fast boat. Straight through in a single run, a little over half a day and you are there; only she pitches dreadfully. It sets the bones aching. This trip will surely have worn Father out. With that hemiplegia of his, half his body simply cannot move. Zhuzhai, last year we saw that when Father had sat a long while he would say his head swam —"

Here the mistress broke off, sighed lightly, and her eyes seemed to redden a little. She was about to go on when all at once a steam whistle came flying in from outside. Then a man ran in shouting:

"The Yunfei's alongside!"

The mistress stood up at once, steadying herself with a hand on Du Zhuzhai's shoulder. By then Fusheng had already darted out ahead; walking, he twisted his neck round to call back:

"Third Master, son-in-law, Madam — no hurry; wait until I have seen to things first, then come out!"

The other clerks in the office began bustling about too, a chorus of voices calling for porters. A big rattan chair, made ready beforehand, was carried out by two sturdy porters. His eyes on the outside, Sunfu said:

"Second Sister, presently you ride with Father in No. 1889; let Fourth Sister come in my car, and Zhuzhai take Ah Xuan."

The mistress nodded, her eyes on the outside too, her lips faintly moving — she was telling her beads! Zhuzhai, a cigar between his teeth, smiled a little and gave Sunfu a look, as if to say, "Let us be going." Just then Fusheng came back in, frowning as though in great difficulty:

"Most unlucky. There's a Suzhou-line tug moored on the inner side —"

"Never mind. We'll go out to the wharf and look!"

Sunfu cut Fusheng short and walked out. Old Guan the bodyguard hurried after him. Behind came Du Zhuzhai and his wife, and Fusheng too. The bodyguard who had been standing at the door brought up the very rear.

The Yunfei was indeed berthed on the outer side of a big tug — one of the so-called "company boats." The big rattan chair had already been set on the Yunfei's bow, the two sturdy porters standing beside it. The wharf was cold and still, with no idlers about; two or three clerks from the office were bawling there at the tops of their voices, driving off the rickshaw men and pedlars who pressed in close. As Sunfu and the other two stepped onto the deck of that "company boat," old Mr. Wu had already been helped out by the Yunfei's steward and settled into the rattan chair. Fusheng hopped over quickly and, with a gesture, ordered the two porters to lift old Mr. Wu and carry him slowly across onto the "company boat." Then son, daughter, and son-in-law all came forward to greet him. Hard as the journey had been, the old master's colour was not bad; two circles of red rested at his temples. But he made no sound; he looked at son, daughter, and son-in-law, gave a single nod, and closed his eyes.

Just then the Fourth Young Lady, Huifang, and the Seventh Young Master, Ah Xuan, who had come up with the old master, squeezed aboard the "company boat" as well.

"Was Father well on the way?"

Mrs. Du — the Wu family's Second Young Lady — took hold of the Fourth Young Lady and asked in a low voice.

"Nothing to speak of. Only he kept saying his head was giddy."

"Get into the cars, quick! Fusheng, go and have the new No. 1889 driven up first."

Sunfu said it as if out of patience. Leaving the two young ladies gathered round the old master, he, Zhuzhai, and Ah Xuan walked on ahead to the wharf. No. 1889 drove up, the rattan chair was brought ashore, and old Mr. Wu was helped into the car and settled; the Second Young Lady — Mrs. Du — got in beside him. Old Mr. Wu, who had still had his eyes shut, was pricked by the scent about the Second Young Lady into opening them for a look, and said slowly in a trembling voice:

"Fufang, is that you? Bring Huifang here! Huifang! And Ah Xuan!"

Sunfu, hearing this from the car behind, knitted his brows a little but said nothing. The old master's temper was odd and stubborn, as both Sunfu and Zhuzhai knew. So the Fourth Young Lady Huifang and the Seventh Young Master Ah Xuan both got into the old master's car. The Second Young Lady Fufang, loath to leave her father, crowded in as well. The two young ladies had the old master wedged between them. The motor sounded, No. 1889 led off and had already begun to move, when all at once old Mr. Wu cried out again in a piercing voice:

"The Taishang Ganying Pian!"

It was a strange cry, like silk being torn across. In that one shout the old master's last remaining vital force seemed to blaze up again; his aged eyes flashed, the pale pink at his temples deepened to a dark crimson, though his lips were trembling violently.

The driver of No. 1889 braked at once and turned his face round in alarm. The cars of Sunfu and Zhuzhai stopped behind. Everyone was dumbfounded. But the Fourth Young Lady understood what the old master wanted. Seeing Fusheng standing nearby, she called to him:

"Fusheng, quick, go to the Yunfei's saloon and fetch that copy of the Taishang Ganying Pian! It's in the yellow silk slipcase!"

Ever since old Mr. Wu had fallen from a horse and injured his leg, and at last become a hemiplegic, he had worshipped the Taishang Ganying Pian devoutly, the same for more than twenty years; besides having it printed and given away each year, he had once copied out the whole text in his own careful hand, and it never left his side, waking or sleeping.

In a little while Fusheng came bearing the yellow-silk-cased Taishang Ganying Pian. Old Mr. Wu took it, laid it reverently on his knees, and shut his eyes, a faint smile of relief rising to his withered lips.

"Drive on!"

The Second Young Lady gave the soft command, breathed easier, tilted her face up and rested the nape of her neck against the sprung cushion, and could not help smiling too. Now the cars went faster and faster, running east along North Suzhou Road; at the Waibaidu Bridge they turned and headed south, and the three of them tore on like a gale — half a mile a minute, a new record for the 1930 models.

To ride in such a marvel of modern transport, speeding down the avenues of Shanghai, the great Eastern metropolis of three million souls, and yet to be clutching the Taishang Ganying Pian, the mind fixed wholly on the Lord Wenchang's injunction that "of all vices lust is the first, of all virtues filial piety comes first" — the contradiction was plain enough. And what sharpened it above all was that old Mr. Wu's devotion to the Taishang Ganying Pian was genuine, utterly unlike the "charity racketeers" of Shanghai who cheat people of money in the name of doing good. Yet thirty years before, old Mr. Wu had been a first-rate "reform-party" man. His grandfather and father had both been vice-ministers, and the favour of the imperial house upon the family could not be called thin; even so, the young Mr. Wu of those days had a breast full of "revolutionary" ideas. In the conflict of fathers and sons so common in that age, the youthful Mr. Wu had played a leading part. Had he not, twenty-five years ago, injured his leg practising horsemanship, and then had the misfortune to sink by degrees into paralysis of half his body, and the further misfortune, soon after, to be widowed — perhaps old Mr. Wu would not now be clutching the Taishang Ganying Pian all day long. But from the time he hurt his leg, the high spirit of his young manhood seemed to have been lost entire; for twenty-five years he had not set foot beyond his study! For twenty-five years, apart from the Taishang Ganying Pian, he had not read a single book or paper! For twenty-five years he had not once known life outside his study! And the second generation's "conflict of fathers and sons" had broken out, past all mending, between himself and Sunfu. If the vice-ministers of the elder generation might be reckoned eccentric and obstinate, old Mr. Wu was no whit weaker than his sire: the study was his stronghold, the Taishang Ganying Pian his talisman, and he had resolutely refused any compromise with his son now for a good ten years!

Though he now sat in a 1930-model motor-car, it was not that he had given in to his son. He had long since declared that rather than see with his own eyes the life of "apostasy" his son led, he would sooner be dead! He would on no account go to Shanghai. Sunfu, for his part, had never pressed him to come; this time, because the bandits were truly running wild, and the Communists' Red Army in the neighbouring province had all the makings of a spreading blaze, it was really not safe to leave the old master resting easy at home. This too was the son's filial care. Old Mr. Wu simply did not believe that any bandits, any Red Army, could harm a virtue-hoarding old father who worshipped the Lord Wenchang! But being a man who had to be helped to sit or lie and could not stir a step, what was he to do? He had no choice but to let them carry him out of his "stronghold," put him aboard the Yunfei steamer, and at last aboard this "monster the Master would not speak of" — the motor-car. Just as, twenty-five years before, it was this cursed paralysis that had kept him from making a thorough "reform-party" man of himself and forced him to yield to the "father," the old vice-minister, so now it was this same cursed paralysis that kept him from "hoarding virtue" to the end and forced him to compromise with the "son," the modern entrepreneur! In just this way he played out his tragedy from first to last!

But after all he still had the talisman of the Taishang Ganying Pian in his hands; and moreover the Fourth Young Lady Huifang and the Seventh Young Master Ah Xuan, a very pair of golden boy and jade maiden, were at his side, so that though he had entered a "den of demons," his "virtue" need not necessarily fall — and so, after resting his eyes a while, old Mr. Wu opened them again, by degrees, calm and content.

The car raced madly on. Old Mr. Wu looked ahead. Heavens! Hundreds of lamplit window-holes like hundreds of monstrous eyes, skyscrapers soaring into the blue, came surging at old Mr. Wu like a landslide, an avalanche — and were suddenly gone; bare lamp-posts rearing straight up from level ground, endless, pole after pole, came striking at old Mr. Wu's face — and were suddenly gone; a string of black monsters drawn out like a marching serpent, each with a pair of great eyes at its head blazing a light that dazzled the sight, came roaring pu — pu —, hurtling like lightning, hurtling straight at the little box old Mr. Wu sat in! Nearer! Nearer! Old Mr. Wu shut his eyes, his whole body shaking. He felt as if his head were spinning on his neck; before his eyes was red, yellow, green, black, glowing, cubes, cones — a jumbled mass, leaping and whirling there; his ears were flooded with hong, hong, hong! ya, ya, ya! pu, pu, pu! — waves of fierce, jangling sound fit to make the heart leap out of the breast.

After who knows how long, old Mr. Wu leisurely drew a breath, and a sound of talking floated at his ear:

"Fourth Sister, Shanghai isn't peaceful either! Last month it was the bus workers on strike, this month the trams! At the end of last month the Communists made trouble on Beijing Road; several hundred were seized, and one was beaten to death on the spot. The Communists have guns! Third Brother says the workers in all the factories are restless too. There could be trouble at any moment. They are forever wanting to rise up. On the walls of Third Brother's factory, on the walls round Third Brother's mansion, they have scrawled Communist slogans all over —"

"But don't the police arrest them?"

"Of course they arrest them! But there's no arresting them all. Aiya! I really can't think where so many people come from who set no store by their own lives! — But, Fourth Sister, that outfit of yours is truly a sight to make one laugh. It is the dress of ten years ago! Change into something new tomorrow, quick!"

This was the talk of the Second Young Lady Fufang and the Fourth Young Lady Huifang. Old Mr. Wu opened his eyes with a start, and saw on every side, before and behind, little boxes like the one he sat in — motor-cars. All perfectly still, not stirring. Across the way, not far ahead, as if a river had been opened, cars of every kind streamed hurriedly and in confusion from south to north and from north to south; and among the cars were men and women of every kind, all running pell-mell as if a devil were at their heels. From some high place a shaft of red light fell, and it fell just on old Mr. Wu.

This was the very crossing of Nanjing Road and Henan Road, the place called Paoqiuchang. The east-west traffic just now stood waiting there for the order of the red and green lights that directed it.

"Second Sister, I have not yet met Third Sister-in-law. This countrified get-up of mine will surely give her a fit of laughing."

Huifang said it in a low voice, stealing a glance at her father, and then at the fashionable women seated at ease in the cars on every side. Fufang gave a laugh and took out her handkerchief to dab her lips.

A wave of heavy perfume drove straight up old Mr. Wu's nose, ticklish and somehow hard to bear.

"How strange, Fourth Sister! I went down to the country last year and even there saw nothing so old-fashioned as this gown and skirt of yours."

"Just so. The country women dress very smartly too, but Father would not let me —"

Like a fine needle stabbing into old Mr. Wu's bewildered nerves, his heart gave a jump. His eyes went instinctively to the Second Young Lady Fufang. For the first time he consciously took in how the Second Young Lady was dressed; though it was still only May, the day had turned suddenly sultry, and she was already in full summer wear; a pale blue gauze wrapped her sturdy body tight, a pair of full breasts thrusting plainly out, the sleeves drawn up above the crook of the arm to bare half a snow-white forearm. An unspeakable disgust suddenly filled old Mr. Wu's breast, and he quickly turned his face away — only to have there burst into his sight, off guard, another modish young woman, half naked it seemed, wearing nothing but a sheer gauze bodice through which the very skin showed clear, perched high on a rickshaw with one bare white leg cocked up, for all the world as if she wore no trousers at all. "Of all vices lust is the first!" — the words struck old Mr. Wu like a drum-stick and set his whole body shaking. And that was not all. Old Mr. Wu's eyes rolled, and he caught sight of his darling Ah Xuan, mouth agape, greedily and raptly ogling that half-naked, seductive young woman! The old master's heart gave one wild throb and then, as if it had burst, would move no more; his throat was scorching, as though a great fistful of chillies had been stuffed into it.

Just then the traffic light changed to green, and old Mr. Wu's car went forward again. Cleaving the sea of cars of every kind, cleaving the sea of red and green, flesh-gleaming men and women, forward! The din of machines, the foul breath of the cars, and the scent off the women's bodies, the red glare of the neon — all the nightmare goblins of the city bore down without mercy on old Mr. Wu's decayed and feeble spirit, until there was nothing left in him but swimming eyes, ringing ears, a reeling head! until his over-goaded nerves ached as though they would burst, until his wildly, ceaselessly pounding heart could pound no more!

A gurgling sound came from old Mr. Wu's throat, but the din of the city was too loud, and the Second Young Lady, the Fourth Young Lady, and Ah Xuan none of them heard it. The old master's colour had changed too, but in the ceaseless play of red and green light no one could have told that anyone's face looked in any way strange.

The car sped on like a whirlwind. It had already crossed Xizang Road and was going flat out along the level surface of Jing'an Temple Road. Small Western houses, hidden in green shade and giving off a point of lamplight, came rushing up in rows and were gone in the blink of an eye. The cool wind of the May night blew against the car windows with a rustling roar. The Fourth Young Lady Huifang breathed easier, as if some weight had been lifted, and said to Ah Xuan:

"Seventh Brother, so now we are to live in Shanghai for good. Whatever there is to enjoy about Shanghai, all I feel is a headache from the hubbub."

"You'll be all right once you're used to it. Lately there are too many bandits in the country, and everyone is moving into Shanghai. Fourth Sister, look at the new houses all along this road — they have all gone up within these last two years. However many new houses you put up, there are always that many people to live in them."

The Second Young Lady put in, opening her red leather handbag, taking out a powder-puff, and beginning to make up her face at the little mirror set into the bag.

"The country is really still peaceful enough. There aren't so many rumours as in Shanghai. Isn't that so, Seventh Brother?"

"Peaceful? I doubt it! Two weeks ago a company of soldiers came, and no sooner had they billeted themselves in the Guandi Temple than they demanded fifty young women of the chamber of commerce — to do their mending and washing; when the chamber said there were none, those soldier-lords came out and hauled them off themselves. Wasn't the Chen wife next door, who keeps the fruit shop, dragged off by them? And our own Amah Lu didn't dare go out the front gate for days on end —"

"How wicked! We in Shanghai knew nothing of it at all. All we hear is that the Communists mean to carry women off and hold them in common."

"I never saw a single Red soldier in the town. It was just that one company of troops that made everyone's life a misery!"

"Pah, Seventh Brother, you really are a simpleton! Wait till you see them too — then where will you be! Zhuzhai says the Communists nowadays are truly formidable; among every trade and calling, they are everywhere. There's no guarding against them. Not until they strike right before your eyes, like a thunderbolt, do you feel it."

So saying, the Second Young Lady sighed softly. The Fourth Young Lady felt her flesh creep too. Only the none-too-sensible Ah Xuan went on laughing foolishly, mouth agape. Hearing the Second Young Lady make the Communists out to be so uncanny and elusive, he found it hugely entertaining: "Would they really strike before your eyes like a thunderbolt? Perhaps they have magic arts!" he asked and answered in his own belly. This Seventh Young Master, though he was nineteen this year and exceedingly handsome, was rather a simpleton, having always played the "golden boy" to old Mr. Wu.

Just then the horn on the car suddenly gave two hoots, and the car turned left into a quiet cross-street thickly shaded on both sides, where the lamplight sifted down through the dense layers of leaves and fell in dapples on the Second Young Lady and the rest. The car slowed too. The Second Young Lady quickly put away her vanity bag, turned her face to the old master, and said softly:

"Father, we're almost there."

"Father's asleep!"

"Seventh Brother, you shout so loud! Second Sister, when Father has shut his eyes to rest, no one dares disturb him!"

But the car's horn hooted again, three times running, the last with a long-drawn tail. This was the signal. The two great oil-black iron gates of a big Western house ahead swung suddenly open, and the car glided lightly in. Ah Xuan sprang up from his seat and saw the cars of Sunfu and Zhuzhai follow in close behind, and saw, on either side of the iron gates, four or five servants standing, among them armed police. Then, bang — the iron gates shut. Now the car ran over the asphalt drive of the garden with a faint hissing sound. Trees stood black and dense on either side of the drive, and here and there in twos and threes electric lamps glimmered among the shade. Abruptly the car turned again, and all at once a dazzling brightness sprang up before them, so bright it made the eyes swim: a great Western house of three storeys, five bays wide, stood ahead, radio music wheeling in the air from within, and with a gu — the car stopped.

A crisp voice called beside the car:

"Madam! The old master and the gentlemen have all arrived!"

Old Mr. Wu, just coming to himself out of the assault of giddiness, opened his eyes with a start. But the very first instant that his waking consciousness seized on was nothing else than the devilish, greedy look in the eyes of the Seventh Young Master Ah Xuan as he had stared at that half-naked, seductive young woman when they stopped at "Paoqiuchang," and the sound of that one sentence of the Fourth Young Lady Huifang's: "The country women dress very smartly too, but Father would not let me —"

No sooner had they reached this "den of demons" of Shanghai than old Mr. Wu's "golden boy and jade maiden" had changed!

The radio music stopped, and a burst of women's laughter came out of that five-bay house, followed by the clatter of high-heeled shoes; two or three figures came dancing over, and among them a young woman in pink, tall and gracefully poised, swaying her slender waist, darted to the side of old Mr. Wu's car, pulled the door open with one hand, and said with a coquettish laugh:

"Father, you must be worn out! Second Sister, are these Fourth Sister and Seventh Brother?"

At the same moment a stiflingly rich sweet fragrance came down over old Mr. Wu's head. And in that scented mist old Mr. Wu saw a mass of fluffy, loose hair falling in disorder over a round face of a bluish white, a pair of shining, darting black eyes, and below them two frighteningly red, parted lips. Suddenly this loose-haired head gave a toss, and out came again a voice like a silver bell:

"Sunfu! You go on in first. Second Sister and I will help Father! Fourth Sister, you get down first!"

Old Mr. Wu gathered the last vital force of his whole body to shake his head. But no one paid him any heed. The Fourth Young Lady brushed past that loose-haired head and got down; the Second Young Lady took the old master's left arm, Ah Xuan lent a hand from the side, and the old master, past all willing of his own, was brought to the side of the loose-haired head, where a smooth, slippery arm clasped him round the waist, with another peal of alluring laughter and another gust of fragrance full in his face. Old Mr. Wu's heart only shook the harder, and he clutched the Taishang Ganying Pian tight against his breast. Through his all-but-bursting brain there passed this thought: "This is a very yaksha, a demon!"

A hatred and fury above all else suddenly gave old Mr. Wu a strength he had not known in a long while. Half-supported, half-carried by the Second Young Lady and Wu's young mistress, he mounted the five stone steps quite easily and went into that brilliantly lit great drawing-room. A room full of people! Coming to meet him were Sunfu and Zhuzhai. Then two young women came flying up, both with long hair loose over their heads, and surrounded old Mr. Wu, crying out their greetings. Chattering and laughing, they thronged about the old master and settled him into a high-backed sofa-chair.

Old Mr. Wu only stared, eyes goggling. Hatred, fury, and overwhelming shock had burned his face to a purplish blue. He saw the whole drawing-room full of many-coloured electric lights whirling, whirling, and whirling faster and faster. Close beside him was a strange thing, a perfect disc of golden light, humming and turning slowly this way and that, blowing out a fierce wind that choked the breath, like some gold-faced goblin wagging its head and working a spell. And this golden light too swelled larger and larger as it turned, until it filled the whole room and flooded all the space! All the red and green lights, all the oblong, oval, many-angled furniture, all the men and all the women, leaped and whirled in that golden light. The pink Wu young mistress, an apple-green girl, another girl in pale yellow, all danced there like mad things, danced! The filmy silk on their bodies could not hide the outline of the muscles beneath, the high-thrusting breasts, the tender-red nipples, the fine hairs under their arms! Numberless high-thrusting breasts, quivering, quivering breasts, went flying and dancing all through the room! And wedged in the midst of that dance of breasts were Sunfu's pimpled square face and the wholly devilish look in Ah Xuan's eyes. Suddenly old Mr. Wu saw all these quivering, flying breasts shoot at his own breast like a flight of arrows, piling up, piling up, bearing down, bearing down, pressing on his chest, pressing on the Taishang Ganying Pian that lay on his knees; and then he heard again the wild, wanton laughter, and the house rocked as if it would fall.

"Demons!" old Mr. Wu seemed to shout, and gold stars burst from his eyes. He felt ten million catties press on his chest, felt something in his skull burst and break in two; abruptly two figures shot up out of the ground, the pink Wu young mistress and the apple-green girl, both baring blood-red lips as if to bite. There was a crack inside old Mr. Wu's skull, his eyes rolled up, and he knew nothing more.

"Uncle! Don't you know me? Susu — I'm Zhang Susu!"

The girl standing before old Mr. Wu in the apple-green Grafton gauze went on saying it with a smile, but the Wu young mistress beside her, holding a cup of tea, suddenly cried out, and the teacup fell to the floor. Everyone in the room gave a jump! An instant of dead silence! Then a thunder of footsteps, all crowding to old Mr. Wu's side. A dozen mouths asked and cried out at once. Old Mr. Wu's face was white as paper, his lips all flecked with foam, his head lolling askew. The yellow-silk-cased Taishang Ganying Pian fell to the floor with a slap.

"Father, Father! What is it? Wake up, wake up!"

The Second Young Lady cradled old Mr. Wu's head, calling in a shaking voice; Zhuzhai craned his neck, pressing in below the Second Young Lady's shoulder, his whole face aghast. But Sunfu, gripping the old master's left hand, wore a look of fury, and railed harshly at the servants and maids crowding in:

"Get back! Why don't you fetch an ice-bag, quick? Quick, quick!"

An ice-bag! An ice-bag! The old master's taken a heat-stroke! — the cry was passed along. The servants ran helter-skelter about the room. The girl in pale yellow, standing a little farther off, took hold of Zhang Susu and asked in a low voice:

"Su! Did you see how the old master came over so faint the moment he arrived?"

Zhang Susu stared wide-eyed, unable to speak, her full bosom heaving like waves. Over there the Wu young mistress was saying, breathlessly and in broken snatches:

"I brought the tea in — and saw, saw, Father — his head lolled to one side, his eyes shut, foam came out of his mouth — foam! And his colour changed completely. Heat-stroke, heat-stroke... is it phlegm-fire? Has Father always had this trouble?"

The Second Young Lady, pinching the old master's philtrum with one hand, pressed her questions urgently on the Fourth Young Lady, who stood there dazed and dripping tears:

"Fourth Sister, Fourth Sister! Has Father had this kind of attack before? He has, hasn't he? Speak, speak, do!"

"If it's a rising of phlegm-fire, once he gets his breath back it will be all right. Only let him get one breath, one breath!"

Zhuzhai said it, looking at Sunfu, and flurriedly held out the snuff-bottle he carried about with him. Sunfu took the snuff-bottle with one hand and, without answering Zhuzhai, only cast furious eyes about him, front and back, barking: "Crammed in so tight! The sheer press of people is enough to smother the old master! — Why isn't the ice-bag here yet! Peiyao, we don't need your help here for the moment; go and telephone for Dr. Ding yourself! — Amah Wang! Go and hurry up that ice-bag!" Then he waved a hand at the Second Young Lady: "Second Sister, don't fluster yourself! Father's chest is still warm! This sofa-chair is no good; let us first carry Father over to that long couch." So saying, and without waiting for the Second Young Lady's answer, Sunfu lifted the old master up, and everyone came to lend a hand.

They had just laid the old master on a long, broad couch cushioned in blue velvet when the Wu young mistress, who had gone to telephone for the doctor, came back. Dr. Ding, she said, could be there within ten minutes; and until he came the patient must on no account be disturbed, but should lie in a quiet room. Just then Amah Wang brought the ice-bag. Sunfu took it in one hand and pressed it to the old master's forehead, and looking at the servant Gaosheng, who stood at the drawing-room door, said:

"Go and call a few men to carry the old master into the small drawing-room! And Dr. Ding is coming any moment — tell the gatehouse to keep a look-out!"

Suddenly the old master's hand moved a little, there was a sound in his throat, and foam like a clot of phlegm bubbled out of his mouth. "That's better!" — several mouths cried at once, as if a weight had lifted from their hearts. The Wu young mistress snatched a white silk handkerchief from Zhang Susu's bosom and wiped the stuff from the old master's mouth, meanwhile making eyes at Sunfu. Sunfu frowned. Zhuzhai and the Second Young Lady wore bitter faces too. The veins that stood out at the old master's temples were as thick as earthworms, the sound in his throat grew louder and more urgent, and the foam kept bubbling up. Presently his hand moved again, his eyelids twitched a little, and at last his eyes came half open.

"Why hasn't Dr. Ding come? Carry him into the small drawing-room first!"

Sunfu said it to himself, rubbing his hands, and turned to wave at the four servants standing there awaiting orders. The four servants came forward and lifted the long couch, carrying it into the small drawing-room to the left of the great one; Zhuzhai, Sunfu, the Wu young mistress, the Second Young Lady, and the Fourth Young Lady all followed in. Ah Xuan, who had been standing lost in a daze from the first, now seemed to wake, looked about him in a fluster on every side, and ran into the small drawing-room too. Bang — the door of the small drawing-room shut.

Those left in the great drawing-room waited quietly; no one opened his mouth. Zhang Susu leaned against a fine, huge radio set, her head bowed, looking at the Taishang Ganying Pian on the floor, and seeming to be turning something over hard in her mind. Two men guests in Western dress each took a sofa-chair, propped their heads in their hands, and smoked slowly, now and then casting an anxious glance at the door of the small drawing-room.

The lamplight still shone softly on everything. The round gold face of the little fan still hummed and turned slowly, sending cool air over everyone and stirring their clothes. Yet these people, who were always so gay, now had a nameless unease weighing on their hearts.

By the piano sat the girl in pale yellow, idly turning the leaves of a book of music. Her face was very like the Wu young mistress's — she was the young mistress's own younger sister, the second Miss Lin.

Zhang Susu, staring vacantly, suddenly seemed to think of something; she jerked her head up, looked about her as if wanting someone to talk to, and catching sight of the girl in pale yellow looking at her too, ran to the front of the piano, clapped her hands together, and said in a low but earnest voice:

"Peishan! I think the old master is surely done for! I have seen —"

The two men guests over there started up, their eyes wide with question, and came to Zhang Susu's side.

"How do you know he is surely done for?"

Lin Peishan asked it hesitantly, rising to her feet.

"How do I know? Ai — because I have seen how people die!"

Several menservants and maids had gathered round these two young couples by now, and hearing Zhang Susu say such a thing, could not help laughing out loud. But Zhang Susu kept a straight face and did not laugh. Very mysteriously she dropped her voice and declared further:

"Do you think what the old master brought up was phlegm? It was not! A hundred times no! It was foam! When a man dies in hot weather he brings up foam like this — I have seen it. Would you say it wasn't hot today? Eighty degrees! Truly strange, and it's only the seventeenth of May — Yuting, am I right? Say so!"

Zhang Susu turned her face to fix one of the men guests, as if she would force him to give a nod. This was Li Yuting: middling in height, sharp-chinned, wearing very thick near-sighted spectacles. He said neither "yes" nor "no," but only smiled faintly. This displeased Zhang Susu greatly; she shot Li Yuting a white-eyed look, pouted her small scarlet lips, and muttered:

"Good! I'll remember you for this! People who teach are always so grey, and university professors worst of all. Student A says this, student B says that, and our professor, not daring to take the left side or lean to the right, can only put on a hangdog look and grin like a fool. — But, Professor Li Yuting! You are not giving a lecture here; this is the reception-room of the Wu mansion!"

Li Yuting really did stop smiling then, with the air of a man who had been struck. The man guest standing behind Lin Peishan leaned to her ear and murmured something or other, and Lin Peishan burst out with a titter, sweeping her lovely eyes across Zhang Susu's face. At once a red cloud flew up over Zhang Susu's tender face; she wheeled sharply and flung herself on Lin Peishan, saying resentfully:

"What mischief are you cousins up to! Talking me down behind my back? I won't let you off till you beg for mercy!"

Lin Peishan, giggling and guarding the most ticklish parts of herself, backed away step by step, and called out through her laughter:

"Bowen, you're the one who made the trouble, and here you stand looking on with your hands in your sleeves!"

Just then a car-horn suddenly sounded, and in a twinkling was before the great drawing-room; a tall, middle-aged man in Western dress came running in at a great pace, followed by two nurses in white uniform bearing a very large bag. Zhang Susu let go of Lin Peishan at once and hailed the newcomer:

"Splendid, Dr. Ding! The patient's in the small drawing-room!"

So saying, she sprang to the door of the small drawing-room, twisted it open, let Dr. Ding and the nurses in, slipped in herself, and drew the door to behind her.

Smoothing her hair, Lin Peishan said to her cousin Fan Bowen:

"Look how Dr. Ding's car came charging up to the drawing-room like a fire-engine."

"But Dr. Ding's mission is to light the fire of life in old Mr. Wu's body, not to put that fire out."

"Are you making poetry again? Hee —"

Lin Peishan threw her cousin a mock-angry glance and started toward the small drawing-room. But before she reached it the door opened, and Zhang Susu stole softly out, a nurse behind her, who held up the white porcelain tray in her hand to a servant attending the room and said one word: "Water!" Then the nurse drew back in, and the door of the small drawing-room shut again.

Questioning looks shot out from every side and gathered on Zhang Susu's face. Zhang Susu shook her head, said nothing, and walked gloomily round a round table of rosewood. Then she stood before the three of them — Lin Peishan and the other two — and said in a low voice:

"Dr. Ding says it's a cerebral haemorrhage, brought on by a sudden violent shock. Whether there is any saving him is still not certain. A violent shock? What a strange business!"

Those who heard it looked at one another and said nothing. After a while Li Yuting, as if to make up for Zhang Susu's earlier flash of temper, put in like an echo:

"A strange business indeed!"

"And yet my eyes are going to find in this strange business nothing strange at all. That old Mr. Wu suffered too strong a shock is certain. Only think: how still it was for the old master in the country; those twenty-odd years of a life that never peeped out of doors were nothing more nor less than a life in the grave! That study of his was, as I see it, a very tomb! Suddenly coming to Shanghai today — of all he saw, heard, smelled, which had not a strong, too-strong, power to shock? With a body like his, and old besides, if he did not have a cerebral haemorrhage, that would be the strange business!"

Fan Bowen said it in his slow, womanish tones, his face shining as if very well pleased with himself. Having finished, he let his glance slide over in search of Lin Peishan's eyes. Lin Peishan gave him a quick look back and smiled with pursed lips. All this fell within Zhang Susu's keen observation; she put on a stern face on purpose and gave a snort down her nose:

"Poet Fan! Making poetry again? A man dies, and even that is a theme for your verses!"

"Even if my timing for poetry is poor, there's no call for Miss Zhang to rail at me so!"

"Good! It's your Cousin Lin you want railing at you, is it?"

This time it was Lin Peishan's face that flushed red. She spat a "Pah!" at Zhang Susu and walked off in embarrassment. Fan Bowen followed her without the least concealment. But Zhang Susu seemed to feel it the more sadly; knitting her brows, she walked round the rosewood table again. Li Yuting stood there stroking his chin. The room was very quiet, with only the little fan's monotonous hum. Now and then the horn of a car on the road outside came flying in, but that too was as spiritless as if it were dropping off to sleep. Several menservants stood stiff as sticks. Amah Wang and another maid put their heads together in close talk, but only their lips could be seen to move; no sound could be heard.

The door of the small drawing-room opened, a tall figure flashed — it was Dr. Ding. He went to the oval brass table where the cigarettes were set out, took a cigar from the silver box and lit it, breathed out a puff, and sat down in a sofa-chair.

"Well?"

Zhang Susu went up to Dr. Ding and asked in a low voice.

"Nine chances in ten there's no hope. I've just given him another injection."

"He won't last the night?"

"It's a matter of tonight, no more!"

Dr. Ding set down his cigar and went back into the small drawing-room. Zhang Susu ran softly over, drew the door of the small drawing-room to, then whirled about, flung herself before Lin Peishan, clasped her round the slender waist, cheek pressed to cheek, and, capering wildly, cried out in anguish:

"Peishan! Peishan! My heart is wretched, wretched! To think a person can die, and die all of a sudden — I am wretched indeed! I won't die! I certainly must not die!"

"But we must all die one day."

"No! I certainly must not die! Peishan, Peishan!"

"Perhaps you are different from the rest of us and will slough your shell when you are old — but, Su, don't maul me so; you have made a fine mess of my hair! Oh, oh, oh! Let go!"

"Never mind, we'll go to the Beauty Parlour again tomorrow — oh, Peishan, Peishan! If one must die, I for one would rather die of too strong a shock!"

Lin Peishan gave a cry of surprise and looked into Zhang Susu's eyes, which now shone with a strange, excited light, quite unlike their everyday look.

"Yes, too strong a shock! I think dying of too strong a shock is perhaps the most delicious of all; only I want nothing of the kind of shock the old master had today. What I want is another kind — a raging storm, an erupting volcano, a great earthquake, a shock as vast as the chaos of the first universe, a mighty upheaval! Ah, ah, how magnificent, how sublime!"

Crying out so, Zhang Susu let go of Lin Peishan, stepped back, dropped into a rocking-chair, and covered her face with her hands.

Li Yuting and Fan Bowen, standing there listening to them, both laughed, as if they had not looked for so unexpected a turn and close from Zhang Susu. Seeing Lin Peishan still standing there in a daze, Fan Bowen went and gave her hand a tug. Lin Peishan started, saw clearly that it was Fan Bowen, and gave him a coquettish little show of pique. Fan Bowen cocked the thumb of his right hand and pointed it vaguely toward Zhang Susu, saying in a low voice:

"Do you understand? The kind of shock she wants is not one a 'grey professor' can give her! And yet, just now, she really had something of the poet's spirit about her."

Lin Peishan smiled at first, but hearing the last sentence she suddenly threw Fan Bowen a cold glance, gave a light snort down her nose, and sauntered off. Fan Bowen understood at once that his words had been somewhat misread, and quickly stepped ahead to catch Peishan by the shoulder. But Lin Peishan, as if very angry, shook Fan Bowen's hand off and ran through a door at the right rear of the room, shutting it with a bang. Fan Bowen hesitated a moment, then hurried after her too, flung the door open, and called "Sister Shan."

The sound of Lin Peishan shutting the door startled Zhang Susu out of her musing. She raised her eyes to look, then dropped them again; the yellow-silk-cased Taishang Ganying Pian, lying on a low, oblong music table, was the first thing to meet her eye. She picked up the volume and opened it. It was regular script, upright and correct, on tribute paper ruled in vermilion columns. At the end of the scroll was a postscript old Mr. Wu had written "in mid-spring of the jiazi year":

Having already printed one hundred thousand copies of the Lord Wenchang's Taishang Ganying Pian, to spread good affinities far and wide, and having further copied out the whole text by hand...

Zhang Susu could not help a laugh, and was about to read on when a low voice spoke behind her head:

"Old Mr. Wu may truly be said to have had faith, to have had a cause, and to have held to it his whole life long, never wavering."

It was Li Yuting, leaning just behind Zhang Susu's chair, a cigarette between his fingers. Zhang Susu tilted her head and looked up at him, then bent again to leaf through the Taishang Ganying Pian. After a while she pressed the Taishang Ganying Pian to her knees and asked abruptly:

"Yuting, what kind of society do you think this society of ours really is?"

Taken off guard by such a question, Li Yuting seemed for a moment at a loss; but being after all a professor of economics, he had his answer ready in an instant:

"That's hard to fix. But you have only to look at this little drawing-room here and you have your answer. In it there is a magnate of the financial world and a titan of industry; this little drawing-room is China's society in miniature."

"But there is also an old master who worships the Taishang Ganying Pian!"

"True, and yet this old master will soon — breathe his last."

"The interior is full of countless old Mr. Wus besides."

"That there surely are. Only they too, once they come to Shanghai, must breathe their last. Shanghai is —"

Li Yuting's sentence was never finished, for the door of the small drawing-room opened and out came the Wu young mistress. Save for a slight knitting of her brows, this young and beautiful mistress was as lively as ever. Seeing only Li Yuting and Zhang Susu here, the Wu young mistress rolled her eyes as if much surprised; but she smiled at once, by way of greeting the two of them, and called Gaosheng and Amah Wang to give her orders:

"The old master looks as though he won't get through tonight. Gaosheng, telephone Mr. Mo at the factory and tell him to come at once. We must first draw up a list of the relatives and friends to be sent word of the death. In the garden, and everywhere, have men set to putting things in order. The furniture stowed under the fourth-floor roof must be brought out too. If there aren't hands enough, send to the son-in-law's, the Du mansion, for more. Amah Wang, take a few people and set the guest-rooms on the third floor to rights; the window-gauze, table-cloths, and sofa-covers in every room must all be changed."

"And what is the old master to be dressed in when he goes? And what sort of coffin —"

"You needn't see to that. Nothing is settled yet; perhaps it will be put out to the International Funeral Parlour. Telephone the factory at once and call the accountant, Mr. Mo. If the factory can spare the hands, let several more come."

"The old master's luggage has just come, sent over by 'Daishengchang' — twenty-eight pieces in all."

"Then, Amah Wang, go first and see to it; whatever luggage isn't wanted, put it all up under the fourth-floor roof."

Just then "Peiyao" was being called in the small drawing-room, and the Wu young mistress turned and ran back; but before drawing the door to she put half her head out and asked:

"How is it Peishan and Bowen are nowhere to be seen? Sister Su, please go and find them."

Zhang Susu nodded, but sat on without stirring. She was calling back her discussion with Li Yuting just now, trying to pick up the broken thread. Li Yuting too said nothing, smoking his cigarette and pacing in measured steps. By now it was nine o'clock, and outside in the garden people were coming and going, all at once astir; among the trees, on the rockery, in the several pavilions, the electric lamps had all been lit together. Amah Wang brought a few rough-work maids into the drawing-room and set about changing the crimson gauze at the windows. A great bundle of sofa-covers lay on the floor. The carpet of the drawing-room was carried out to be beaten too.

Suddenly, after a stir in the small drawing-room, came a confusion of weeping, with cries of "Father" among it. The faces of Zhang Susu and Li Yuting both grew tense. Zhang Susu stood up, paced a few anxious steps, then ran to the door of the small drawing-room and pushed it open. The moment the door opened, the weeping flooded the great drawing-room. Dr. Ding, rubbing his hands, came out into the great drawing-room and said, looking at Li Yuting:

"He's gone!"

Then Sunfu came running out too, his face dark and heavy; he told the servants to telephone for Lawyer Qiu, and turning to Li Yuting, said:

"Tonight I must trouble you to help see to things here. It is now past nine, and the newspaper offices may already refuse to take pre-editorial advertisements, but this notice of the death of ours simply must appear in tomorrow's papers. I must trouble you to go and make the arrangements. Zhuzhai is drafting the copy over there. Have it run in all five of the big papers at once! — Gaosheng, how is it Mr. Mo still isn't here?"

Gaosheng, standing on the stone steps outside the great drawing-room door, was about to answer when the Second Young Lady came running out and caught hold of Sunfu:

"I have just talked it over with Peiyao, and we feel it would be better to put the hour of the old master's encoffining off to the morning of the day after tomorrow — first, it won't be so rushed, and second, Uncle Zeng Canghai will be able to get here in time. Uncle is the greatest one for finding fault!"

Sunfu pondered a while, then answered resolutely at last:

"We'll send an urgent wire tonight with word of the death; whether he makes it in time or not, we shall just have to leave to chance. Whatever Uncle may have to say, I'll take it all on myself. The encoffining is at two o'clock tomorrow afternoon, and it absolutely cannot be changed!"

The Second Young Lady still wanted to argue, but Sunfu had already run back into the small drawing-room. The Second Young Lady chased in after him.

Just then Lin Peishan and Fan Bowen, hand in hand, were coming out through the door of the great dining-room at the right of the drawing-room, and catching sight at a glance of that scene of confusion, both stood stock-still. Peishan looked at Bowen and said in a low voice:

"Can it be that the old master has already passed away?"

"It doesn't surprise me in the least. In the country the old master was already an 'ancient mummy,' but the country is in truth no better than a dim 'tomb,' and a mummy will not 'weather away' in its tomb. Now that he has come to Shanghai, a modern metropolis, of course he must 'weather away' at once. Go, then! You mummy of an ancient society! Go! I have seen how old China too, a mummy of five thousand years, is weathering away, fast, so fast, in the storm of the new age!"

Lin Peishan pursed her lips in a smile and flung Fan Bowen a charming show of mock-anger.
