## Chapter One. The Hour of the Snake, Second Half (10 a.m.)

{v} Countless black horsemen wheeled and galloped in the distance. Far off, above a long river, hung a round, blood-red setting sun; within the walls of a lone city, beacon smoke rose straight up, stabbing into the murky yellow sky.

{d} The third year of Tianbao, the fourteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Snake, second half. Chang’an; Chang’an County; the West Market.

The spring cold still bit, but the sun was brilliant. Not a cloud hung over Chang’an; it promised to be a fine day.

With a long creak, the two heavy leaves of the West Market’s gate were pushed slowly open, a Kaiming-beast banner hanging high at the center of the lintel. Out in the avenue a dozen or more camel trains had long since gathered, and the moment the banner went up they broke into a clamor. Hands cracked little oxhide whips to rouse the camels lying on the ground, counted the crates, called to their fellows; the shouting in outland accents rose and fell on every side.

These were the last of the foreign merchant caravans to reach Chang’an before the Lantern Festival. They had set out from far-off Fulin, from Persia and the like, traveling day and night to be in time for the capital’s most important festival. From this very evening, after all, the lantern fair would run three full nights, and the great and noble of the Tang did not spare their purses.

The clerks of the West Market office stood on either side of the western entrance, ledger in one hand, brush in the other, checking transit papers and goods one party at a time with blank faces. Today was a special day: the market had opened half a double-hour earlier than usual. Every clerk wanted the work done and to be home for the festival, and their inspections went a shade faster than usual.

One old clerk finished registering a party of Persian merchants at speed, then beckoned to the next in line. A foreign trader in a chestnut-brown short robe with double lapels stepped forward and presented his travel pass with both hands.

The old clerk took it, glanced at it, and froze.

The pass itself was flawless. The applicant was named Cao Poyan, a Sogdian from Kang. He had come to Chang’an with fifteen companions, fifteen camels, and one stallion, carrying thirty woolen felt rugs and assorted furs; every checkpoint along the way bore an officer’s inspection and seal.

The trouble was not the pass. It was the goods.

The old clerk had been at this trade for twenty years and had seen too many caravans and too many wares; his eyes had long since grown as keen as a falcon’s. Sixteen men, and only this little freight—divide the cost among them and how high did it run? And Chang’an was already into spring, with felt rugs fetching low prices. Even sold to the last piece, these goods would scarcely cover the round trip. On a road ten thousand li long, where was there a merchant this foolish?

The old clerk knit his brows despite himself and looked the foreigner over closely. Cao Poyan was about thirty, with a high nose and deep-set eyes; around his lean jaw grew a stiff ring of black beard, like a brush of coarse bristles. Counting the white peaked felt cap on his head, the man stood over seven feet.

The old clerk asked a few simple questions, and Cao Poyan answered each one. His Tang speech was stiff, the same few words over and over, and his face stayed cold and unsmiling, nothing like a merchant’s. The clerk noticed that as he answered, the man’s right hand kept drifting unconsciously to his waist. It was the habit of someone used to gripping a weapon—though just now his belt held only an empty little copper hook.

For safety’s sake, every merchant’s blades were confiscated by the gate wardens on entering the city, to be returned only when they left.

Without a flicker, the old clerk set down his brush and ledger and walked once around Cao Poyan’s train. The goods were nothing out of the ordinary, common stock. All fifteen companions were foreigners in tight trousers and pointed shoes, none far from Cao Poyan’s own age. Each led a camel in silence, every man’s shoulders drawn faintly tight.

“These fellows are nervous,” the old clerk judged to himself, and lifted his brush to mark the pass with the word “pending”—meaning the caravan’s identity was in doubt and the deputy of the market office must look into it further. But before the brush came down, a large hand stopped it.

The old clerk looked up to find a thick-browed, broad-faced man smiling at him.

“Cui Liulang?”

The man was a well-known broker in the West Market, with wide connections; whether it was moving goods and pawnbroking, renting rooms and finding people, or greasing a lawsuit, he was the middleman to go to. So though he held no office, on the ground of the West Market he did very well for himself.

Cui Liulang beamed. “You’ve not had your morning bite yet, I’d wager? I brought you a cake, old sir.” He held out a steaming sesame flatcake studded on top with glossy, plump sesame seeds, fragrant enough to make the mouth water. The old clerk gave it a squeeze and found a small ingot of plain silver pressed deep into its underside. He hefted it covertly—two taels at least, he thought; not spendable as coin, but enough to have a fine hairpin made for his daughter.

“These friends of mine are in Chang’an for the first time and don’t know half the rules; I’ll trouble you to be accommodating, old sir,” Cui Liulang said, lowering his voice.

The old clerk hesitated a moment, then took the cake after all and marked the pass with “allowed,” granting entry to the market. Cui Liulang folded his hands in thanks, turned, and rattled off a fluent stream of Sogdian. Cao Poyan only gave a slight nod, with neither pleasure nor excitement.

Led by Cui Liulang, the little camel train filed up the ramp and into the West Market.

Past the ramp lay a broad crossroads, its four wide lanes—east, south, west, north—lined on both sides with shops and stalls. Silk and cloth shops, ironmongers, porcelain shops, saddleries, cloth-and-grain shops, jewelers and inlay-work shops, instrument sellers—everything was there. The roofs of these shops were unlike the buildings of Chang’an elsewhere, flat as terraces—not out of any foreigner’s homesickness, but because here an inch of ground cost an inch of gold, and a broad flat roof could stack more goods.

The shops had not yet formally opened, but every one had already hung its signs out high, pennant joined to pennant until they all but curtained the sky above the lane. The peachwood charms hung at the lintels on New Year’s Eve had not yet been taken down, and beside them now stood bamboo frames for lanterns of every shape—all made ready for tonight’s lantern procession. The lanterns were not yet up, but the smell of festivity already soared to the heavens.

“Now, our Chang’an has a hundred and eight wards in all, fourteen streets running north–south and eleven east–west. Every ward is ringed by a wall. Whether you’re eating, taking your pleasure, talking business, or lodging, it all happens inside a ward. On an ordinary night you can’t come out—you’d break the curfew. But today there’s no need to worry: tonight there’s the Lantern Festival fair, and the curfew’s lifted for it. The festival proper is tomorrow, mind, but the fair starts this very evening...”

As he walked, Cui Liulang warmly acquainted his guests with the lore of Chang’an. Cao Poyan’s eyes swept left and right, ever wary, like a fierce hawk not yet broken to the hand. All around, horses and mules brayed and cart wheels rumbled, and the travelers passing by were all hurrying on their way; no one paid the little caravan any mind.

The two came to the very center of the crossroads. Cui Liulang halted. “Where to next? Shall we find an inn, or has your lordship a house already engaged?” Cao Poyan drew a folded slip of paper from his robe and handed it over. Cui Liulang started, then smiled. “Ah, you’ve booked it all already. Come, this way.” He stretched out his arm and pointed to the right with a touch of flourish, then strode ahead, the others close behind.

What Cao Poyan did not know was that this small exchange between him and Cui Liulang had been taken in, every bit of it, by the constable in the watchtower not far off.

The watchtower was a tall pavilion of black-lacquered timber, more than eight zhang high, standing at the dead center of the West Market, from which one could look down on all the market’s comings and goings. The constables who manned it were carefully chosen for their sharp eyes; nothing that stirred in the market escaped them.

From the moment Cui Liulang and Cao Poyan entered the market, the watchtower had kept them under close watch. Seeing Cui Liulang’s gesture, a constable straightened, took up a solid black flag, and waved it three times toward the east, repeating the signal three times over.

Two snaps of the fingers later, another watchtower three hundred paces to the east waved the same black flag; then a tower farther east swiftly answered in turn. So it passed from tower to tower, and in the space of a few dozen finger-snaps the black flag’s message had crossed a whole avenue, carried from the West Market to Guangde Ward, a ward’s breadth to the east.

In the northeast corner of Guangde Ward stood the offices of the Jingzhao Prefecture, and beside them the Temple of Mercy. Wedged between the two was an unremarkable side courtyard, once the residence of Sun Simiao; but every trace of the Medicine King was gone now, replaced by a grim, martial air, and in the yard rose a tall black watchtower far larger than the rest.

The constable atop it saw the black flag waving in the distance, noted the flag’s color and the number of waves on a wooden slip, and flung it swiftly to the ground below.

A tall, sturdy runner below caught the slip and ran with it into a broad, high hall thirty paces off. High over the hall hung a black wooden board lacquered in gold, bearing three characters in regular script—“Jing’an Bureau”—the brushwork full and rounded, unmistakably from the hand of Yan Zhenqing.

The first thing one saw on entering was an enormous sand-table of the city of Chang’an. The outer walls were molded of red clay, the ward and market walls of yellow beeswax; the hundred and eight wards and twenty-five great streets were laid out as trim as a chessboard, and even the crooked lanes within the wards and the transport canals were rendered down to the last hair—only the palace city, of course, was left a blank. In one corner of the hall stood a four-tiered bronze water-clock with coiled dragons, set to the same time as the clepsydra before the Shuntian Gate.

To look down on this table, with the water-clock to aid you, was to gaze on Chang’an from the clouds, every shift of hour and circumstance laid plain.

Beside the sand-table two officials stood studying it intently. The elder was white of hair and beard, in a wide-sleeved purple robe with a round collar, a gold fish-pouch at his waist. The younger man had a small, round face, the greenness of youth not yet gone from it, yet three faint lines already showed between his brows—plainly a man who thought too much. He wore a narrow-sleeved green robe, a silver fish-pouch hanging at his waist, and yet in his hand he held a Daoist’s fly-whisk.

The runner ran up before the two officials and, slip in hand, cried out in a booming voice that rang through the hall: “The wolf has entered the West Market—past the crossroads already!”

The officials did not stir. A beautiful maidservant beside them took a step forward, took up a crescent-headed polo mallet, and pushed a black clay figurine on the sand-table from the avenue outside the West Market into the market itself, to just the spot where Cui Liulang and Cao Poyan now stood.

The hall fell quiet for a moment, and the younger man was the first to speak, probing: “Director He?” He asked several times over before the elder opened his eyes at last. “Changyuan, how have you laid it out?”

The younger man gave a slight smile and pointed his fly-whisk at the sand-table. “Cui Qi leads the party himself; fifty Lüben Guards are already positioned inside the West Market. The moment Liulang has drawn out what we need, Cui Qi breaks the door and takes them. On the outer ring, more than a hundred buliang men of Chang’an County hold the lanes; and the two gates of the West Market can be sealed by the guards at any moment. Three iron rings, one within another—this brute has no way to slip out.”

As the fly-whisk pointed, the maidservant swiftly set down one vermilion clay figurine after another. On the sand-table, in an instant the red figures ringed the black one round, airtight.

“These wolf-whelps think that dressed as Sogdian traders, with a bought-off insider, they can pull the wool over heaven’s eyes—little knowing that from first to last it is we who have been angling for them. The wary man reckoning against the unwary: how could we fail to win?” The young man drew back his fly-whisk, his chin lifting a little, the very picture of a man with everything in hand. The elder gave a soft grunt, closed his eyes again, and neither agreed nor disagreed.

Every short quarter, the big-voiced runner would come pounding in from outside to report the latest movements of Cui Liulang and Cao Poyan.

“The wolf has passed Fan’s saddlery, heading northwest of the crossroads!”

“The wolf has passed the Ruyi New Silk emporium, turned right into the Second Bend lane!”

“The wolf has passed the Third Bridge over the Guangtong Canal and turned into the side path of the lane left of the Lone Willow.”

Crescent mallet in hand, the maidservant kept moving the black figure to match. Cao Poyan’s path took visible shape before the two men in charge: the caravan was drawing ever farther from the busy quarter, edging toward the Lone Willow in the southwest of the market.

The Lone Willow was the West Market’s appointed place for beheading criminals; merchants shunned it as unlucky and kept well clear, so that the people around it grew fewer and fewer.

The younger man tilted his head slightly. “Recorder Xu, what buildings are there thereabouts?”

Behind the two officials, a dozen or more desks were ringed about, piled with scrolls, where several dozen junior clerks bent over their work. A slightly plump, middle-aged clerk heard the summons, hastily set down the scroll in his hand, and ran to the sand-table. His eyesight was poor, and he had to lean laboriously out over the edge to make out where the black figure stood.

Recorder Xu thought for a moment, then answered at once as if reciting from a book: “The northeast lane—low-lying, damp ground; only sixteen warehouses there, abutting the Guangtong Canal. In the fifteenth year of Kaiyuan a rainstorm sent the canal into flood, and the stored goods of three foreign merchants were all destroyed, worth five thousand strings of cash...” His memory was astonishing; he rattled it off without the least hesitation.

The younger man cut off his flow. “These sixteen warehouses—is there any way out nearby?”

“Ah, ah—no, there isn’t; but...”

Just then the runner burst into the hall again, cutting him off. “The wolf has entered Warehouse Bing-6—not come out!”

The message set the whole hall astir at once, every gaze turning to the sand-table.

“This is the place!” The younger man’s eyes flared bright. “Send word to Cui Qi to make ready to move; the buliang men are to clear the ground around the warehouse at once and let no one in or out. The two market gates stand by.” Order after terse, forceful order came from his mouth, an excitement he could not hide in his voice.

The runner noted the commands and left the hall at speed. The younger man braced both arms on the edge of the sand-table, leaned forward, and murmured to himself as he stared at the black clay figure:

“I should very much like to see what these Türk wolf-whelps have come to Chang’an to do.”

The command passed from the hall of the Jing’an Bureau up to the watchtower, then through a series of flag signals crossed the avenue at speed and came back to the watchtower on the north side of the West Market. The constable copied the flag signals onto a wooden slip, tossed it down, and shouted, “Commander Cui, take the order!”

Before the slip reached the ground, a large hand caught it fast.

The one who caught it was a tall, curly-bearded giant with arms as thick as roof-beams. He took the slip, scanned the order on it, and with a surge of spirit turned and roared, “All hands, form up!”

From the storehouse beside him, fifty Lüben Guardsmen filed swiftly out. Each wore ink-black infantry armor and carried a short arm-drawn crossbow, a ringless saber hung at the waist; ten of them also bore long bows slung across their backs. Through the whole forming-up not a man spoke; there was only the dull tramp of feet and the sound of breathing.

Cui Qi swept the ranks with a grim face. “The target is in Warehouse Bing-6. Surround first, strike after; take them alive so far as you can. Keep your wits about you, and let no man shame the Lüben Guards!” With that he waved his hand and set off at a run. The soldiers followed their commander close, five to a rank, first at a trot, then at a hard sprint.

They flashed across the crossroads by the routes they knew well and slipped into the winding lanes, making for the southern part of the West Market. Merchants along the way, seeing the dust suddenly fly and so many soldiers pounding past, showed their alarm. Before they could put their heads together, a great many buliang men came up and asked each shop to close its doors for the moment; the people in the streets, too, were ushered into nearby shops to wait, and no one was allowed to leave.

At the West Market’s east and west entrances, the gate soldiers heaved the stone ward-bars up out of their pits, ready to shut the gates at a moment’s notice.

The spider’s web was spun swiftly, layer upon layer; a sharp arrow was already flying toward its mark.

Once inside the ground of the Bing warehouses, Cui Qi made a few hand signs, and the Lüben Guards, long since of one mind with him, split into three directions and closed silently on Warehouse Bing-6; the buliang men had already quietly sealed every road nearby. The only things in this quarter were a few caravans’ horses and beasts tethered here, watched by two or three hands. Buliang men went over, exchanged a few words, and led all the animals well away.

With that, Warehouse Bing-6 was cut off entirely from the West Market.

Half-crouched at the corner of an earthen wall near Warehouse Bing-6, Cui Qi took the heart-guard mirror from his chest, hung it on the tip of his saber, and eased it carefully out into the open. By the mirror’s reflection he could see what lay ahead without ever putting his head out.

Warehouse Bing-6 was a timber building with low, overhanging eaves, sixty paces long and forty-five wide, very nearly square, with only one entrance and vent windows on all four sides—but the windows were especially small, too narrow for a grown man to pass. Because the quarter lay close to the canal and flooded easily in summer, the building’s floor hung clear of the ground, borne up on sixteen wooden pillars, somewhat in the style of the buildings of Lingnan.

A big-nosed foreigner guarded the door—one of Cao Poyan’s fifteen companions. He leaned against the wooden door and now and then bent his head to toy with a string of wooden beads at his wrist, looking distracted. Cui Qi gauged the crossbow’s range: if it truly came to it, he was confident he could break the door within ten finger-snaps.

Cui Qi fixed his eyes on the entrance and held his breath. Everything was ready; all that remained was to wait on what stirred inside.

Inside the warehouse, a single wooden wall between him and the world outside, Cao Poyan stood facing the entrance, arms folded, his back to a corner. He had taken off the white peaked felt cap, baring a thick head of black braids. The others had scattered among the shelves, murmuring in twos and threes—but not in Sogdian; in Turkic. Cui Liulang, standing by the window, wore, of course, the look of a man who understood not a word.

Cui Liulang rubbed his hands and smiled. “Master Cao, who found you this place? It’s dreadfully damp, and there’s not an eating house or a shop nearby. Why not let me arrange another for you?”

Cao Poyan answered coldly, as though he had not heard the question at all. “To business.”

Cui Liulang was not put out. “Good, good. Just what is it you wanted me for—can you tell me now?”

Cao Poyan snapped his fingers, and two companions came over and spread a roll of cloth on the ground; unrolled, it made a broad square. Then they brought out a small wolf-hair brush, a stick of ink, and an inkstone. Cui Liulang was startled and could not tell what it meant—were they about to sit an examination and compose verse?

Then he looked again at the stiff yellow cloth and drew a sharp, cold breath. Countless squares were drawn on it, thick and close, the ink lines crossing lengthwise and across—a map of Chang’an’s hundred and eight wards. But the map was far too rough, no more than the outlines of the wards and markets and their names.

“This thing is kept only in the secret archives of the imperial city. For any commoner’s house to hold one in private is a crime punishable by death!”

Cao Poyan narrowed his eyes. “...You dare not take it on?”

Cui Liulang laughed aloud and stepped back to sit cross-legged on the ground. “If I didn’t dare, I’d never have brought you into the West Market. Fortune is sought amid danger, and in my line of work, how many of us give a fig for the statutes of the Tang? Come—brush and ink at the ready. What is it you want marked?”

“I want you to mark on this ward-map of Chang’an every hidden door, secret culvert, wall-cavity passage, and other vital point,” Cao Poyan said, one word at a time.

Cui Liulang assented while his mind raced. The lay of the land within Chang’an was a tangled thing, not so simple as its twenty-five crossing streets. Between the wards ran ways by water and by land; between the city walls there were cavity-walls; under the bridges there were ditches, and along the slopes there were drops. How they all linked into a net, and where each led—most inhabitants of Chang’an never worked it out in a lifetime.

With such a complete map in hand, half of Chang’an’s strengths and weaknesses would be in one’s grasp, and one could come and go at will. It seemed these Türks were after no small thing...

One man drew out a leather flask, poured a little clear water on the inkstone, and in a moment had ground out a shallow pool of ink. Cui Liulang wetted the tip of the wolf-hair brush with his tongue, dipped it, and drew a few strokes—then suddenly stopped. “Master Cao, you’re no man of the Central Plains; you don’t know cloth. This won’t do. This is called stiff yellow cloth—fine for making clothes, but the ink drags on it. Better I go and buy some first-grade Xuan paper...”

“You are not to leave,” Cao Poyan flatly refused.

Cui Liulang shook his head and set to sketching. He had just filled in one corner of the city when he looked up again. “Chang’an is too vast; if I put in every last detail, three days and three nights wouldn’t finish it. What exactly do you mean to use this map for, Master Cao? If I know that, my brush will know what to spell out and what to leave.”

Cao Poyan said, “That is no concern of yours.”

Cui Liulang spread his hands. “You want me to fill in the whole map of Chang’an within two double-hours, yet you won’t even say what it’s for—sorry, I can’t draw it.”

At this string of talk Cao Poyan flew into a rage, strode a step up to Cui Liulang, and reached out to throttle his throat.

Cui Liulang hesitated but did not dodge. He knew the Jing’an Bureau’s men were just outside; one shout and not a single Türk would get away. But then all his earlier pains would be wasted. He was gambling that Cao Poyan was only bluffing for now, and would not truly kill him before he had the map.

One more bluff of his own, and he would have their true purpose.

The hand at Cui Liulang’s throat abruptly stopped, and inwardly Cui Liulang relaxed, knowing he had bet right. Holding the pose, Cao Poyan suddenly cocked his head toward the window, as if listening. Cui Liulang tensed—could some careless Lüben Guardsman have made a noise? He asked quickly, “Master Cao, what is it?”

“Do you hear anything?” Cao Poyan pointed out the window.

Cui Liulang listened. Outside there was silence. He shook his head, a little at a loss. “There’s nothing at all.”

“Just so—nothing at all.” Cao Poyan bared the savage grin that only a wolf of the steppe can wear, and his fingers suddenly bore down. “When we came in just now there were plenty of beasts tethered nearby, a lively din of them. And now there’s not so much as a single horse’s cry.”

At these words Cui Liulang’s face changed all at once—first from terror, then from want of air.

Waiting outside, Cui Qi grew more and more uneasy. Nothing stirred over at the warehouse, yet something felt wrong to him. As an old soldier, his instincts of this kind were usually sound.

Again he eased the heart-guard mirror out on his saber, this time trained on the window of Warehouse Bing-6. The window was small, and the mirror showed no more than a shadow moving. Suddenly a figure vanished from before the window, and at the same instant came a “thud,” as of something heavy falling to the floor.

No good! Cui Qi’s heart skipped a beat; he snatched the saber back and roared urgently to those around him, “Break the door! Now!”

The Lüben Guards had long been ready at their posts, and at the command eight crossbow bolts shot at once from three directions, pinning the Türk at the door into a hedgehog of bolts. In the same moment two soldiers leapt up the wooden steps before the door, brushed past the enemy just crumpling to the ground, and drove their thick shoulders hard against it.

The bamboo door-pivot could not hold against the pressure and split in an instant. With a crash the soldiers’ bodies fell inward together with the door-plank. Behind them, two more soldiers stepped without hesitation over their comrades’ bodies and charged into the room. They loosed one volley from their crossbows into the interior, then swiftly dropped low. By now the two who had gone down with the door had rolled up and lifted the plank to form a makeshift wooden shield, guarding their fellows and buying them the time to span their bows again.

The whole sequence flowed like drifting cloud and running water, matchlessly smooth, as if rehearsed a thousand times over.

The few Türks nearest them rushed forward roaring, then all at once pitched headlong to the ground with cries of pain. Three long bows had loosed from across the warehouse, and iron arrows two chi long passed cleanly through the narrow vent windows to skewer the men through the thigh.

That volley bought time enough. More soldiers charged into the warehouse, crossbows leveled, shouting as they advanced: “Down! Get down and we won’t kill you!”

But the Türks seemed not to understand; wave after wave they hurled themselves out from the corners behind the shelves. Crying out the name of the khagan, they charged bare-handed. To the Lüben Guards these men were simply living targets, and for a while the warehouse was full of the dull sound of metal driving into flesh and the screams of men.

The soldiers were in no hurry to press on; in groups of three, covering one another, they edged slowly forward. The moment a Türk so much as showed himself, several crossbows found him at once.

The soldiers’ orders were to take prisoners where they could, so they aimed as much as possible at nonvital parts. But these desperate wolves of the steppe were fierce and unafraid of death, and would find a way to strike back with their last breath. Several soldiers, unable to bring themselves to the killing blow, hesitated a moment and were ambushed in turn, wounded or slain. And even when a Türk had no strength left to fight, he would kill himself on the spot, without the least hesitation.

Soon the room fell quiet again, with only corpses sprawled every which way among the passages and the wooden racks. At the cost of three soldiers dead, the Lüben Guards had taken the whole warehouse at last.

The soldiers did not slacken their guard, but searched cautiously, shelf by shelf. Suddenly a Türk who had been lying on the ground sprang up and threw himself at the nearest soldier. Caught off guard, the man was seized about the waist, and the two grappled together. The Türk opened his great mouth to bite off the soldier’s nose—then his movement froze, and he pitched to the ground, a glistening crossbow bolt jutting starkly from the back of his skull.

At the end of the passage, the soldier’s comrade stood with a spent crossbow, his arm slowly sinking, panic in his eyes. He should have kept the Türk alive, but what had befallen his brother-in-arms had made him forget the order.

“Fool! Is that what I taught you?”

Cui Qi snatched the crossbow from the soldier’s hand and cuffed him across the face. His swarthy features seemed coated with a film of leaden gray, dull and lightless.

Breaking the door had taken only ten finger-snaps, and the enemy was wiped out within twenty-six—a fine showing among all the capital’s guards. But the Türks had been too ferocious: not a single one had been left alive, and that was not the result those above wanted.

Cui Qi paced the passage with anger in him, his gaze sweeping the corpses, his fingers uneasily gripping and releasing his hilt. Suddenly he stopped short, then took two quick steps forward—ahead of him lay the body of Cui Liulang.

The eyes were wide and staring, and there were plain finger-marks on the throat; no coroner was needed to know he had been strangled.

“Elder brother!”

Cui Qi gave a tiger’s roar of grief and rage, knelt on one knee on the floor, and made to bend and gather the dead man in his arms. The two had the same brows and eyes—they were brothers of one womb—only now one of them would never open his eyes again.

“If I had given the order three finger-snaps sooner... if I had broken the door myself...” Regret gnawed at Cui Qi’s heart like ants, and his fingers shook so violently he could scarcely hold his brother’s hand.

A Lüben Guardsman ran up, and seeing his commander in such a state, did not quite dare come near. Cui Qi turned his head and asked with a look what the matter was. The soldier came smartly to attention. “We’ve finished counting the bodies—fifteen in all.”

Cui Liulang aside, sixteen Türks had gone into the warehouse. Which meant one was still uncaught—and once the dead were identified, the missing man was their leader, Cao Poyan. Cui Qi drew a sharp breath and rose to his feet again, flame leaping in his eyes.

“Search!” he barked, his face grim.

The warehouse was no dwelling but one great open room without partitions, only a few wooden shelves down the middle. Cui Qi paced through it several times and found nothing amiss. In so plain and open a space, seen through at a glance, where could the man hide? Did the fellow know some sorcery of the Western Regions and walk through walls?

Suddenly Cui Qi felt a chill draft on the crown of his head. He halted, jerked his gaze up, and his pupils shrank in an instant. Directly above him was a wooden cover the size of a well-mouth, set slightly askew, letting through a thread of deep blue sky.

There was a vent up here after all!

Warehouse Bing-6 had low, overhanging eaves, so no one had imagined the roof might hold a vent—normally only flat-roofed buildings were built that way.

It had likely been cut in secret by some earlier tenant and never reported to the market office. Cui Qi cursed bitterly, ordered a ladder brought, and loaded his crossbow with a bolt from which the head had been removed. Fury had not robbed him of his reason: this was the last man, and he had to be taken alive, or the whole plan was finished.

The warehouse was ringed now with Lüben Guards; even if Cao Poyan had gone to the roof, he had nowhere to run—it was as good as catching a turtle in a jar.

For fear of any further slip, Cui Qi mounted the ladder himself and climbed. At the top, just as he was about to push the cover aside, he felt a sudden killing intent. He pulled his head back in haste, and a hard wooden bar studded with iron nails whipped past, grazing his scalp. Without a word he raised the crossbow and loosed. There was a soft thud, as if it had struck something. Heartened, Cui Qi scrambled on up—only to be caught full in the left eye by a lashing belt.

The belt was of cured oxhide, exceedingly hard, and the blow sent a wave of blinding pain through Cui Qi. A small copper hook at its end, drawn back, opened a long bloody gash down his cheek. The attack roused Cui Qi’s fighting fury; rather than retreat he pressed in, whipped his hand back to catch and coil the belt, and with a hard heave forced his way up onto the roof.

Before he could steady himself, the belt suddenly went slack—the other man had let go. Cui Qi lost his balance at once, flailed his arms, and only just recovered his footing. In that very moment he heard a clatter of footsteps running over the tiles, then a rushing leap, and from far off a dull cracking sound, and then a splash of water.

The sound was strange—not like a fall onto ground. Cui Qi was seized with alarm; his left eye was swollen and blind, but his mind was still clear. He realized he had made an enormous mistake.

Beside Warehouse Bing-6 ran the Guangtong Canal, hard against the ward wall. A year before it had been widened for transport, to carry timber from the Qinling Mountains, so it was deep and full, broad enough for boats. It was still the first month, and the canal had not yet thawed; over it lay a thin sheet of ice, level as the Vermilion Bird Avenue—and the water-gate had no guard posted at all. In his earlier arrangements Cui Qi had thought only of the land routes and simply overlooked this.

What he had heard was precisely the sound of Cao Poyan breaking through the ice and dropping into the water.

After flowing out of the West Market, the Guangtong Canal joined the Yong’an Canal and the Qingming Canal, and farther off connected to the Longshou Canal and the palace canals, passing through more than thirty wards and crossing better than half the city. In other words, once Cao Poyan had swum underwater past the West Market’s water-gate, he could slip the cordon with ease and come ashore anywhere in the whole city.

Cui Qi could have slapped his own face; the blunder was too stupid for words.

In his desperation he too leapt for the canal—but he had forgotten the heavy gleaming armor he wore, and the instant his feet touched the ice it cracked apart and dragged the commander straight to the bottom.

Just before he went under, his right eye barely made out a spray of water speeding toward the water-gate.

A high embankment wall stood between the canal and the warehouses. The Lüben Guards could only go around from the far end, which took no little time; then they stripped off their armor and went into the water, and by many hands hauled their commander up onto the bank. In that delay Cao Poyan had long since vanished beyond the water-gate.

Hauled up onto the canal bank, Cui Qi lay on his front retching up mouthful after mouthful of icy water, his face iron-gray. In his hand he still gripped an oxhide belt with a copper hook on it.

It was the whole operation’s only prize.

***

The air in the hall of the Jing’an Bureau hung heavy as quicksilver, every man there treading softly and holding his tongue, for fear of provoking the two displeased officials.

No one had dreamed that a capture as sure as nine in ten could let the cooked duck fly off the plate. The assault just now had been flawless—and utterly pointless, for it had left not one man alive.

Cui Qi knelt on one knee before the dais, soaked through and with no time to dry himself, the water spreading in a ragged stain across the floor. After Cao Poyan’s escape he had been summoned back to the Bureau in haste. Those above were anxious to learn just where the thing had gone wrong, and since the watchtower flags could not carry a message so involved, he had had to come in person.

Facing the Director and the Deputy Director of the Bureau, Cui Qi dared conceal nothing; kneeling, he laid out the whole affair point by point, then bowed his head to await judgment. The elder brushed his sleeve and let out a long sigh. “We meant to invite the guest into the jar—and instead we have led a wolf into the house...”

Everyone knew how grave those words were. This Cao Poyan had just shown himself ferocious, cunning, and terribly quick to turn a moment to his advantage. With such a malignant Türk loose in Chang’an on the eve of the Lantern Festival, no one could imagine what might come next.

Worse still, this wolf could almost be said to have been escorted in the whole way by the Jing’an Bureau itself; and if blame for that were pressed home, no one could bear it.

“Your servant has already sent men to search along the canals,” Cui Qi added carefully, hoping to soften a little the shame of his failure.

The younger man’s face was iron-dark, and he flicked his fly-whisk. “What good is that handful of men? Do you know how long the Guangtong, the Yong’an, the Qingming, the Longshou canals are? Call out the constables from every street post and the ward wardens, seal the wards, and search them for me one by one!”

“Changyuan, a fly-whisk is not for striking people with.” The old man raised a palm and checked him, gently but firmly. “Sealing the West Market for half a double-hour just now already smacks of overstepping. A citywide sweep would throw all of Chang’an into unrest—and today is the Lantern Festival, with lanterns being raised and set out on every street. Make too great a disturbance and even the Sage will call for an accounting.”

The younger man would argue on. “Director He does not carry this charge, so he cannot know! These sixteen with Cao Poyan are only the last party to enter the city; they have many more confederates long hidden within it. If we do not learn the Türks’ intent soon, I fear a great calamity is about to fall on Chang’an!”

His tone had come near to insolence. But the elder did not take offense; he put out one finger and pointed to the northeast, where the palace city lay. “I did not say leave it be. But an open manhunt will not do—we cannot make trouble for that person.”

At the elder’s mention of “that person,” the younger man’s eyes dimmed a moment. He mused a while, then flared up more fiercely than before. “If the Director holds that nothing can be moved in the open, then what if I dispatch only a small body of picked men and take the villain in secret?”

At this the elder stroked his beard, seeming to waver.

Hearing this, Cui Qi suddenly lifted his head and cried out, “Cui Qi knows he has committed a grave error; he asks no pardon, only the chance to cut down the enemy with his own hand and avenge his brother!” In today’s defeat he had blundered again and again; unless he raised the banner of blood-vengeance to redeem himself, his own fate might well be grim.

But the younger man and the elder shook their heads together.

Chang’an held close to a million souls—Han and foreigner, the hundred offices and the nine ranks of trades, every faction crossing and knotting with the rest: a tangled whirlpool of the seen and the unseen. Cui Qi had taken up his post in the capital only half a year before; to take the field and kill an enemy he could do, but to weave through the city hunting a man was hardly to be expected of him.

The Jing’an Bureau had gathered talent from every quarter—able old clerks versed in market trade and grain and cash, recorders and scribes who forgot nothing they read, fierce and valiant fighting men, even a body of foreign staff who knew the ways of the outlanders intimately—and it lacked now only one thing: an old hound with a keen nose that could roam the dark places of Chang’an.

They had had the most fitting man for it—Cui Qi’s own brother, Cui Liulang—only he had died in the line of duty. Cui Qi knew what his superiors were lamenting; his eyes reddened, and he drove a fist into the floor so hard that the brick cracked with a fine seam.

After a silence the old man took the futou headcloth from the side table, set it on straight, and tied his reckoning-pouch and kerchief at his waist. The younger man started and hurriedly asked where the Director was going. The old man sighed. “The palace takes the Türk Wolf Guards very seriously; today’s affair cannot be hidden for long. I will go into the palace and try to buy a few double-hours’ delay. In the meantime, Changyuan, you had best think of an answer and make good the earlier error, or else...” The old man’s white brows drooped, and he left it unsaid.

The younger man’s shoulders dropped a little; secretly he let out a breath, and at the same time felt a rise of contempt. The slippery old fellow—the moment a thing went wrong, he found a reason to leave and would take no share in deciding a course. Well, let him go; it spared him having his own hands tied.

Now a single quarter-hour was worth a thousand in gold, and he had little to waste sparring with his own side.

The younger man saw the old man off to the spirit-screen, then turned back into the hall visibly more at ease. He cast a stern glance at Cui Qi, still kneeling below the steps, and flicked his sleeve. “These are extraordinary times; your punishment is deferred for now. From here on you are not to slacken by so much as a hair!”

Cui Qi composed his face, cupped his hands, and withdrew. He knew that the old man surnamed He held the post in name only; the one who truly ran the Jing’an Bureau—and held Cui Qi’s own life—was this young man before him named Li Bi. Young as his superior was, his methods were genuinely sharp, decisive in killing and sparing, and the whole Bureau had been drilled into perfect obedience under him.

Done with Cui Qi, Li Bi rapped the corner of the desk hard and called all the section recorders over. “Now think hard, all of you—who is a fit man to take Cui Liulang’s place? Remember, I want the best.”

The recorders in the hall all fell to pondering, and not one made a sound. There were only four double-hours until the lantern fair, and to find Cao Poyan before then was a task all but impossible. Do the work well and there might be no reward; do it badly and one might well become the scapegoat—and even the man who made the recommendation would suffer.

Seeing his subordinates shrink and cringe, Li Bi was about to rebuke them when his gaze suddenly fixed: the weak-sighted Recorder Xu was hesitantly raising a hand. Li Bi knew the man was called Xu Bin, once a clerk-scribe in the Ministry of Revenue with a marvelous memory, forgetting nothing he read, which was why he had been transferred to the Bureau as a recorder—only he stammered a little. Li Bi lifted his chin, signaling him to speak.

Recorder Xu hesitated, then said, “Ah, ah... your servant does have a man in mind, though I don’t know whether he’ll suit you.”

“Say it!”

“He is a friend of mine, called—ah, ah—called Zhang Xiaojing. He was once a squad leader in the army of the Anxi Protectorate, then transferred back to Chang’an on his merits, and has served as buliang chief in Wannian County for nine years now. I thought perhaps he might suit the Deputy Director...”

“Oh?” Li Bi narrowed his eyes.

The record sounded plain, but pondered closely it was no ordinary thing. A buliang chief was deputy to the constable-magistrate who ran down thieves—the topmost clerical post among the sub-officials, in charge of catching robbers and keeping the peace. For a mere squad leader from a protectorate to rise to buliang chief of a whole county was already most uncommon—and this was no ordinary county, but Wannian.

Chang’an was split into two counties, east and west: Chang’an County to the west, Wannian County to the east. Wannian lay under the Son of Heaven’s very feet, home to many princes and nobles, its connections gnarled and interlaced like tree roots—and that this man had held the place steadily for nine years suddenly stirred a little interest in Li Bi.

“Where is he now?”

“Ah, ah... he ran afoul of the law last year and is now in the prison of Chang’an County, already a man awaiting execution.” Xu Bin weighed his words. Those around him whispered: had Recorder Xu lost his wits, recommending a prisoner? And a condemned one at that? Was he not courting his superior’s ill temper?

But Li Bi’s face gave nothing away. “I do not want a saint; I want an able man. Is this the best there is?”

Xu Bin hurriedly raised his voice. “Within Chang’an there is none to surpass him at running down thieves.”

A silver fish-pouch arced through the air; Xu Bin scrambled to catch it and nearly missed. Li Bi said, “Take my horse and fetch him. Within two quarters I want that man here before me.”

It took Xu Bin a moment to grasp his superior’s meaning. First he tied the silver fish-pouch at his waist, then thought that improper, hastily undid it and cradled it in his hands, and hurried out of the hall.

Li Bi looked around to find everyone else craning their necks after him, and flew into a temper. “What are you idling there gawking at? Go and investigate for me at once! The travel passes and market records of the two markets, the gate wardens’ inspection rolls, the reports from every street post—go through the lot of them again, quickly!”

The Bureau’s officials hastened back to their places and bent to their work, and the hall was busy again. Li Bi took from the maidservant beside him a patterned brocade cloth scalded in boiling water, scrubbed it hard over his face, then remembered something and said, “Yao Runeng, go to the Jingzhao Prefecture and bring me Zhang Xiaojing’s service record.”

A young clerk rose at once and dashed out.

Li Bi pulled open the breast of his robe, braced both arms at the edge of the sand-table, and leaned forward to gaze down again on the model of Chang’an. His keen eyes swept over every last building, as if he would gouge that wolf bodily out with his stare.

In the corner of the hall the bronze water-clock let its drops fall, still unhurried. However pressing the affairs of the world, it had never once changed.

***

Desert, ruins, and the thick reek of blood.

Countless black horsemen wheeled and galloped in the distance. Far away, above a long river, hung a round, blood-red setting sun; within the walls of the lone city, beacon smoke rose straight up, stabbing into the murky yellow sky.

He struggled to raise himself upright and shouted a warning in fury. But heaped in layer upon layer around the city walls were mountains of corpses, and not one man rose to answer his cry. Only a battered, ragged dragon banner drooped over the battlements, its pole leaning crookedly, all but snapped through at the middle.

Boom, boom, boom—the enemy’s war drums sounded the assault, and bone-tipped arrows swarmed thick as flying locusts. This time, he alone stood to face them...

Zhang Xiaojing woke with a start, only then realizing he was not in the Western Regions at all, but in the death cell of Chang’an County. A cangue locked his neck and both hands fast, so tight that even jolting awake from the dream he could not move.

The boom-boom of the war drums in his dream turned out to be someone rapping the wooden bars with the butt of a whip. He lifted his eyelids and saw two men standing before the cell door: one was the death cell’s warden; the other had a narrow face and short brows, five wisps of unkempt long whiskers on his chin, and a look of concern in his eyes.

“Xu Bin, Xu Youde?” Zhang Xiaojing was briefly taken aback, then laughed. “I’d never have guessed the last man to see me off would be you.” In his words there was not the faintest trace of a soul unmoored on the eve of execution.

Xu Bin knew he had misunderstood, but it was awkward to explain, so he cupped his hands toward the warden and said, “Trouble you to open the cell and strike off the cangue.” The warden bulged his two slightly protruding eyes, like a toad that refused to back down. But when his gaze swept over the silver fish-pouch clutched in Xu Bin’s right hand, he shrank back, and could only fish out the key, unlocking the cell with a clatter and sending two turnkeys in to remove the cangue.

The two turnkeys trembled, seemingly in great awe of Zhang Xiaojing, so nervous that they could not get the cangue apart no matter what. Zhang Xiaojing gave a cold snort. “Idiots. It’s a triple-twist snake lock. Pry it from below with your thumb, and bear down in the middle.” The turnkeys followed his instruction, and with a crack the cangue finally split into two pieces. Each held one half and backed away in alarm. Zhang Xiaojing shot a sidelong glance at the warden, who gave a shudder and hurriedly avoided his eyes.

Zhang Xiaojing was not tall, but solid as a boulder of Mount Tai, his forehead slightly domed, and beneath it two short, black, arresting silkworm brows. Shaking his aching wrists, he looked around and said loudly, “Where’s the food and drink? The county lays on a last-meal wine, five hundred cash by custom. Mind you don’t skim it.”

The men around him shied away as though from a plague and none would answer. Xu Bin stooped into the cell, took him by the arm, and said in a low voice, “Someone wants to see you...”

“Hm?”

Zhang Xiaojing’s face filled with astonishment. So Xu Bin had not come to see him off, but to haul him out? Yet where would a mild, good-natured fellow like him get the power to rescue a man from the death cell?

Xu Bin did not explain overmuch, only pressed the warden to see to the formalities at once. Soon a clerk brought down a document for Xu Bin to sign. One look at its side-seal and Zhang Xiaojing knew this was no pardon but a prisoner-transfer order—the kind used when the Court of Judicial Review or the Ministry of Justice removed a prisoner from a county jail. And neither of those, in fetching a man out, would strike off his cangue first.

Doubts crowded thick in Zhang Xiaojing’s mind, but this was not the moment to ask; he held his silence.

Xu Bin signed his name with a flourish like flying dragons, and then the whole party left the dim death cell and came back up to ground level. Sunlight streamed in through the entrance, throwing the last few steps into sharp contrast of light and dark. As Zhang Xiaojing set foot on the last step, he suddenly halted, a stir of feeling in his face.

This one step was the dividing line between the worlds of the dead and the living. He had set his heart on death—and never thought that, having turned once at the very gate of the underworld, he would somehow, inexplicably, come back.

Whether what came next was fortune or ruin he did not yet know—but to have looked once more on the sunlight was, at the least, worth it.

Zhang Xiaojing walked toward a well as if no one else were there. It was somewhat against the rules, but the guards around him all stood well back, and none rebuked him. His hands, like iron pincers, hauled at the well rope hand over hand, and soon drew up a bucket of well water flecked with ice. He raised the bucket high and doused himself from head down; the icy water poured over his head and made him shiver with pleasure, washing away all the filth and lethargy of the dungeon.

Zhang Xiaojing set down the bucket and tilted his head far back, the cold water dripping down his hanks of hair, a fierce, keen aura faintly radiating from him. The sun was at its height now, and golden light spilled down and fell into his left eye socket. There was no longer any eyeball there, only a very deep old knife scar, all the more savage in the sunlight.

“Bright heaven above, it’s been a while.”

He raised his fist and swung it hard at the sky. In that instant the light and shadow wavered, and his profile, as if hewn by blade and chisel, was ferocious as a guardian god.

The transfer settled, Xu Bin hurried Zhang Xiaojing out of the Chang’an County offices. Xu Bin was in a fever of haste, with no time even to let him change out of his convict’s clothes. At the hitching-stones before the offices stood two Liangzhou coursers, each with a striking tortoiseshell browband across its forehead—a mark that the two mounts might gallop down any avenue at all, the imperial roadway of the Vermilion Bird Avenue included, free of the restrictions of the Ceremonial Statutes.

Each mounted a horse, and Zhang Xiaojing asked, “Where to?” Xu Bin answered, “Ah, ah—back to the Jing’an Bureau in Guangde Ward.” He glanced at the sundial before the gate. “We must get there fast—mm, fast—it’s a ride of a quarter and a half.”

“I’ll have us there within one quarter.” Zhang Xiaojing flicked the horse’s ear with his ring finger, and the beast’s quick response pleased him.

Chang’an’s outer city was split by the Vermilion Bird Avenue, the east under Wannian County, the west under Chang’an County; and so the Chang’an County jail lay in Yongda Ward in the western city. To reach Guangde Ward one had first to cross three avenues heading west, then go north four street-crossings—better than ten li in all. To make it within a quarter, one had to ride flat out, without the least delay.

The two whipped their horses onto the avenue and tore away. As the two tall mounts surged along, everyone on the street—travelers on foot and sedan chairs alike—scrambled aside for fear of a collision. Xu Bin’s horsemanship was plainly no match for Zhang Xiaojing’s; he lay all but flat along his horse’s back, both hands clenched on the reins, cutting a sorry figure.

Zhang Xiaojing eased his pace a little to draw level with Xu Bin, and squinted his one eye sidelong. “Brother Youde, what is this really about?”

Xu Bin barely mastered his seat, caught his breath, and only then spoke. “The one that hauled you out is the Jing’an Bureau.”

“The Jing’an Bureau?” Zhang Xiaojing was mildly surprised; he knew the offices of Chang’an intimately, yet had never heard the name.

Xu Bin explained, “‘To quell revolt and pacify is jing; when the four quarters are without trouble, that is an.’ The Jing’an Bureau is an office newly set up by the court, with charge over all threat-prevention for the western capital—this was all after you went inside—and just now they are recruiting men of talent, so I put your name forward.”

Zhang Xiaojing arched a silkworm brow. The keeping of order in Chang’an fell to the street commissioners of the Jinwu Guard, to the patrol commissioners of the Censorate, to the constable-magistrates of Chang’an and Wannian counties. What manner of “threat” could this be, to force the court to found a whole new office to meet it?

Xu Bin went on, “The man in charge of the Bureau is called Li Bi, courtesy name Changyuan. He holds the deputy directorship of the Bureau while serving as a Hanlin academician-in-waiting. It is Deputy Director Li who wants to see you.”

Zhang Xiaojing gave a hiss between his teeth, his doubts only mounting; this was stranger yet. The Bureau’s charge was “threat-prevention,” its business bound to be heavy and hard. To set a Hanlin academician—an idle, honorable sort of civil post—over the catching of thieves? Was this not nonsense?

Zhang Xiaojing searched the name in his memory, and it came to him. “Could it be... the prodigy who versified on the chessboard?”

Xu Bin nodded, with a meaning behind it.

In the thirteenth year of Kaiyuan, a seven-year-old prodigy named Li Bi came to court for an audience. The Son of Heaven was playing chess with Zhang Yue, the Secretariat Director. The emperor set the two, Zhang Yue and Li Bi, to versify on the chess with “the square and the round, motion and stillness” for their theme. Zhang Yue wrote: “Square like the board; round like the stone. Moving as the stone comes to life; still as the stone lies dead.” But Li Bi spoke out: “Square like the doing of right; round like the use of wit. Moving like the display of talent; still like the fulfilling of one’s will.” It won the emperor’s high praise, and he sent the boy into the Eastern Palace to be a study-companion to the heir apparent.

Reckoning it up, Li Bi would be twenty-six now, just at the age when a man burns with ambition and begins to make his mark. The deputy directorship of the Bureau was low in rank but heavy in power, a place to build up a record in practical affairs—a perfect rung for climbing. At the thought, Zhang Xiaojing scraped at his left eye socket with his little finger and gave a low laugh. “If Deputy Director Li thirsts so for talent, it seems the Bureau has got itself into some great trouble?” There was always a faint taste of mockery in the way he talked.

Xu Bin turned his eyes away, a little embarrassed. His friend’s eye was too venomously sharp, and his tongue too blunt, and the two together were more than a man could bear.

“Sorry, I can’t say that yet. Ah, ah... Deputy Director Li will tell you presently.”

Zhang Xiaojing laughed aloud. “Fine, I won’t ask. Whatever it is, it’s all the same to me—could anything be more wretched than losing one’s head?”

Xu Bin looked ahead, his face grave. “That... ah, ah... is truly hard to say.”

***

Even as the two galloped toward the Bureau, Cao Poyan had just clambered up the steep bank of the transport canal. On the bank, as it happened, stood a bluestone road-marker more than two zhang high, bearing four characters: “Yong’an North Canal.” He scrambled to it on hands and feet and sat down with his back against its face, deathly pale and gasping for breath.

His left elbow stayed bent; from the joint jutted the dark tail of a steel crossbow bolt, and blood showed faintly on the sleeve. He was lucky—had the bolt been fitted with its head, the whole arm would likely have been ruined.

Suddenly Cao Poyan’s ear twitched; he swiftly crouched low, hiding himself behind the marker. On the road not far off, a Jinwu Guard street patrol came rumbling past. That road was thick with people and carts and forever jamming up, and the patrol had to shout loudly to open a way through—and amid such a press, almost no one would notice a stir beside the canal.

When the patrol had gone off into the distance, Cao Poyan clamped his right hand over his left elbow and rose slowly. He looked about him and was on the point of stepping out when his gaze sharpened. Some way off a man had left the main road, stepped over the drainage ditch, and was weaving toward the marker.

It was a drunkard past forty, in a white side-slit robe with a wet stain spread down the breast, reeling three ways to every step—he had plainly had a good deal to drink. Cao Poyan could only lower himself again and hush his breathing as much as he could.

The drunkard came up to the marker, first let out a resounding wine-belch, then with one hand hitched up the hem of his robe through the slit and with the other fumbled his belt loose, and fell to pissing against the stone. It was a very long piss, and the drunkard, taking a lively interest, took his member in hand to sluice the loose dust off the marker. Done, he cinched his belt back with a careless tug and was turning to go when he suddenly lowered his head and let out a sound: “Eh?”

He had seen, along the bank between the canal and the marker, a straggling line of wet footprints. Curious, the drunkard stepped forward a few paces, rounded the stone, and came eye to eye with Cao Poyan behind it.

The drunkard blinked, then burst out laughing. “Zimei—so you’re back after all! Come, come, let’s have a drink, the two of us.” Cao Poyan reached out and put an arm around his neck; the drunkard was still muttering, don’t fool about, don’t fool about. In the next instant, from behind the marker came the sound of a neck-bone being wrenched apart, and the muttering stopped short.

Before long Cao Poyan, now in the side-slit robe, walked toward the avenue with an easy air. A foreigner in Chinese dress was as common as could be in Chang’an. So he walked into the crowd, like a single grain of sand falling into the desert.

***

Zhang Xiaojing and Xu Bin reached Guangde Ward in exactly one quarter—at the cost of Xu Bin’s headcloth, jolted loose along the way. After a strict search, the two were brought to see Li Bi in a secluded courtyard behind the Bureau’s great hall.

It was a retiring room—plain walls, gray tiles, flat mats and a bare desk; beneath the window honeysuckle, redbud, and a few clumps of half-withered yellow bamboo grew untended. The master had plainly spent no thought on ornament. The one unusual thing was a small bronze-sparrow sundial slanting up at the sky—a sign that the master cared greatly for time. Around the sundial a little channel had been cut, and clear water murmured and wound away to the back of the yard.

Xu Bin returned the silver fish-pouch, bowed, and withdrew, leaving Zhang Xiaojing and Li Bi to face each other alone.

Zhang Xiaojing made a deep bow with both hands, and used the moment to size the man up swiftly with his single eye. The fine-featured prodigy of the chessboard wore a dark green lan robe, proper to the sixth rank of a Hanlin academician. But the fish-pouch was permitted only to officials of the fifth rank and above; that he had been granted a silver one meant a bestowal from the Son of Heaven above his grade—and from that one small detail the rich scent of imperial favor could be caught.

Yet Li Bi just now was hardly riding high. Hard as he strove to keep calm, the muscles at his brow and lip stayed taut, and at a glance Zhang Xiaojing could see that the young man was under enormous strain.

Most curious of all, Li Bi held a fly-whisk in his hand—one could not think why an administrative officer of the Bureau should be carrying such a Daoist implement.

Li Bi gave the fly-whisk a shake and, without any pleasantries, came straight to the point. “What I am about to tell you is a secret of the highest order to the court. You have only two choices: work for me, or go back and wait for death.”

Zhang Xiaojing held his silence; he knew the other did not need an answer but was only fixing who led the conversation.

Li Bi went to the desk, gave a hard pull, and tore down the broad sheet of thin white silk on the wall, uncovering a general map of the Tang domains, and pointed his fly-whisk at a place in the north.

“In the eighth month of the first year of Tianbao, the Türks fell into civil strife; the newly raised Ozmish Khagan would not submit to our rule and rose in revolt. Wang Zhongsi, Military Commissioner of Shuofang, joined with the Basmyl, the Uyghurs, the Karluks, and other tribes to march against him, and fought a full year and a half; now the Türk khagan is at the end of his road.”

His voice was clear, cool, and wholly orderly, as if he had rehearsed it many times over.

As he spoke, Li Bi took from the bookshelf beside him a records-scroll tagged with red silk and tossed it to Zhang Xiaojing. It was a long roll, with slips of paper pasted across it one after another. The hand on the slips was all hasty scrawl—a hundred characters at most, a single line at least—arranged in order of time. Taken singly, each was vague; but as the scroll slowly unrolled, the more Zhang Xiaojing read, the deeper his misgivings grew.

“Early in the ninth month of the second year, a secret memorial came from the Shuofang rear-office, saying the Türk khagan had sent several parties of his close-attending Wolf Guards to slip into Chang’an, meaning harm to the Son of Heaven, to turn the war at the front. Those Türk Wolf Guards are the steppe’s most fearsome elite—cruel, cunning, and utterly loyal to the khagan. It was to guard against precisely this menace that the court established the Jing’an Bureau.” Li Bi paused a little, then went on. “But what the Türks’ plan actually is, we do not know. The rear-office and the Bureau strained every nerve, and could barely so much as track the movements of one of their parties.”

Here Li Bi tapped the pinewood desk lightly with his knuckle. “The Bureau laid a plan to invite the guest into the jar—to use this one party of Wolf Guards as bait to draw out the others in hiding. But my men are mediocre, and the thing failed on the very brink of success: half a double-hour ago they let the key man escape!”

Li Bi had all the documents from that operation brought for Zhang Xiaojing to look over, with a faint air of testing him. Zhang Xiaojing leafed through them once and pointed at one record. “The Türks come from the steppe and are keenest of all to the crying of horses. You gave the order to clear the beasts from around the warehouse too soon, Deputy Director; sound turning to silence would naturally rouse their guard.”

At this Li Bi was struck motionless where he stood. The Bureau had argued the matter before—how Cao Poyan had seen through the trap—and had reached no agreement. Li Bi had all along held that it was Cui Liulang’s incompetence that showed the flaw, and never dreamed the cause lay in himself. He had meant to test this man, to see whether he had real ability; instead the man had picked out his own mistake.

At the thought Li Bi felt first a touch of shame, and then a slow smile—was this not the very man the Bureau was looking for?

Zhang Xiaojing’s face, for his part, was as usual. Nine years a buliang chief in Chang’an, he had seen every strange and outlandish case there was, and this small piece of reasoning was nothing at all.

Li Bi sighed. “Since the jar-trap failed, every thread has snapped. The one thing we are sure of is that the Wolf Guards will make their move tonight, at the Lantern Festival fair!” And with that he looked to the sundial outside the window, his gaze severe.

Zhang Xiaojing started at the words. The lantern fair had always lit its candles at the hour of the Rooster; it was past the hour of the Snake now, and by the fullest count only four double-hours were left.

In four double-hours the Bureau must root out every Türk Wolf Guard from a Chang’an of a million souls—a task all but impossible.

Only now did Zhang Xiaojing understand why Li Bi had been so urgent to pull him from the death cell. The matter was too vital, too hard, too pressing; ordinary means could not do it, and the young official had had to take a desperate gamble and stoop to parley with a condemned man.

Li Bi’s tall frame leaned slightly forward. “Within four double-hours—can you do it?”

Zhang Xiaojing asked in return, “Why me?”

“I have looked into your service record. You dealt with the Türks before, in the Western Regions, so you should have experience in handling them; and you were nine years a buliang chief in Chang’an, so no one, I daresay, knows this city better than you.” He paused deliberately, then raised a hand. “Bring this task off, and I’ll secure you an imperially sanctioned special pardon.”

For a condemned man, nothing could tempt like a pardon.

But Zhang Xiaojing showed no flush of joy. His one eye narrowed slightly, as though weighing something, and then he cupped his hands respectfully. “I thank the Deputy Director for his kindness, but I would rather go back to my cell and wait for death.”

Li Bi’s brow twitched. The man had actually refused his one chance to live? Why?

“Chang’an has a hundred and eight wards; to find a handful of Türks within four double-hours—not even an immortal could do it. It’s death either way, and if I go back to my cell now, at least I’m spared the bother.” Zhang Xiaojing spread his hands, then turned and started for the door.

“I’ll appoint you Commandant of Proclaimed Merit, and add a substantive post as vice-commander of a superior garrison. Is that enough?”

“This isn’t a question of pay.”

Li Bi’s face darkened. “I have no time to waste—name your terms!” He could not believe a man would throw away this chance, unless he had no wish to live.

Zhang Xiaojing walked on. “I’ve told you—it has nothing to do with the size of the reward. Can’t be done is can’t be done.”

“Do you hate the Türks?” Li Bi suddenly asked, a question that seemed beside the point.

Zhang Xiaojing’s steps halted.

“I do.” The voice held neither joy nor anger.

Li Bi’s pitch rose sharply. “You hate the Türks so bitterly—do you mean to stand by and watch these beasts run wild in Chang’an?”

Zhang Xiaojing kept his back to him still. “Chang’an has the Son of Heaven and his hundred officials above, and a hundred thousand strong troops below. How is it that catching Türks becomes the duty of one condemned man?” There was that faint taste of mockery in his tone.

Li Bi said sternly, “Because the one man who can save Chang’an now is you!” It was said almost like a scoundrel’s plea; Zhang Xiaojing was about to shake his head and leave when, to his surprise, Li Bi strode forward, seized his sleeve heedless of his own station, spun around to bar his way, and drew his two sword-straight brows almost together:

“Zhang Xiaojing, I know you bear a grievance against the court. But this day’s business has nothing to do with the Son of Heaven’s face, nor with Li Bi’s career—it is for the safety of a whole city of common people! Do you understand? For the people. If you simply walk away, how will your heart be at peace? I don’t care what you think, but you must see this done! It’s hundreds of thousands of lives! Human lives!”

By the end his voice had come to tremble a little, plainly from the surge of feeling. That was a rare thing to see.

Zhang Xiaojing had not expected the young official to lose his composure so. When he heard the words “human lives,” a ripple stirred at last within him. For some reason the scene from his dream rose again—mountains of corpses, a sea of blood, the savage wolf banner woven through with the sound of weeping. He was silent a long while, and then let out a long sigh. “All right, Deputy Director Li. You’ve persuaded me.”

Li Bi released his sleeve, stepped back, and resumed his reserved manner. “My other promises still stand.”

Zhang Xiaojing mused a moment, then spoke. “But I have one condition. Officialdom is too full of misgivings, its hands tied at every turn. If you want me to take this villain within four double-hours, it must be by my rules.”

“And your rules... are what?”

“That there are no rules.” A dangerous, unbroken glint flashed in Zhang Xiaojing’s right eye.

Li Bi was a clever man and grasped his meaning at once. The waters of Chang’an ran too deep, its factions checking and crossing one another, every undertaking hedged with obstacles. Without a sharp blade to cut through the tangle, never mind four double-hours—even four months might yield nothing. For Zhang Xiaojing to take the Türks within four double-hours, he must have an absolute authority that crushed all before it—to do whatever he pleased, with everyone cooperating and no one able to stand in his way.

Li Bi hesitated. This fellow had been nine years a buliang chief in Chang’an and had every ruthless trick there was; if he truly acted without scruple, there was no imagining how great the consequences might be.

Seeing him say nothing, Zhang Xiaojing gave a cold laugh and turned again to go.

“Wait!”

Li Bi made up his mind at last. He raised his right hand and showed a glinting yellow bronze waist-tally, four characters incised on it: “Jing’an: the securing of the peace.”

“From this moment you are a commandant of the Jing’an Bureau. By this tally, the watchtowers and street-post constables of Chang’an, the ward wardens and gate keepers, the mounted patrols, the gate guards, and the buliang men of the two counties under the Jingzhao Prefecture are all yours to command. To see the tally is to see me.”

Without ceremony Zhang Xiaojing took the tally, tied it to his belt, and made it fast with a nine-river knot. From now on he was the most powerful condemned man in all Chang’an.

Li Bi suddenly asked, “I give you such great authority—what if you flee without a word?”

“There’s no guarantee,” Zhang Xiaojing answered without hesitation. “You chose the man; I chose the road. We each answer for our own choice.”

So the conversation ended. Li Bi shook the bell on his desk and summoned two maidservants. They took Zhang Xiaojing to a nearby side room, had him strip off the gray convict’s clothes, and changed him into a short jacket and brown cotton trousers easy to move in. When he was ready, Li Bi himself brought Zhang Xiaojing to the great hall of the Bureau.

This was the nerve-center of the whole Bureau, where the pick of every section gathered, where intelligence from every quarter was collated and gamed out; a side chamber held a vast storeroom, piled with files on every aspect of Chang’an from the Six Ministries to the two markets, ready to be drawn at any time. It was for his expertise in just this that Xu Bin had been seconded here.

What impressed Zhang Xiaojing most was the Bureau’s watchtowers.

Throughout Chang’an, every ward had two or three watchtowers, used in ordinary times to watch for thieves and fires. Under Li Bi’s arrangement they had taken on a new function: each had a dedicated flag-constable posted, who could communicate by agreed flag-signals—flags by day, the light and dark of lanterns by night.

In this way, whatever any watchtower in Chang’an saw could be relayed swiftly to the Bureau’s center; and likewise, the center could send an order swiftly to any point.

The system was plainly learned from the frontier beacon-fires, but handier. The watchtowers stood no more than half a li apart, and word could cross the whole of Chang’an in an instant. At a glance Zhang Xiaojing saw its practical worth: it meant that wherever in the city he was, he could stay in touch with the Bureau through the towers—one more great unseen eye looking down on Chang’an.

But the tower system cost a very great deal to run, and only a freakish creature like the Jing’an Bureau could afford it.

Cui Qi was in the hall too by now, talking in low voices with the maidservant who worked the sand-table. Li Bi called his name, and Cui Qi hurried over and dropped to one knee; he had not forgotten he was a man under blame.

Li Bi said evenly, “Commander Cui, Liulang’s death sprang from a careless slip in clearing the ground. The order came from me; I bear a share of the responsibility.” Cui Qi jerked his head up, scarcely believing his ears. First, he had never guessed his brother’s death owed to so small an oversight; second, that this superior would own his own error—could this be some trick to buy men’s hearts?

At this Li Bi curled his lip; he had no time now to play at power-games—he was simply too proud to deign to shift blame onto another. He pointed to Zhang Xiaojing. “It was Commandant Zhang here who solved the puzzle. He will take your brother’s place from here on and hunt the Wolf Guards.”

Cui Qi looked Zhang Xiaojing over, gratitude and doubt both in his eyes.

He knew Zhang Xiaojing was a condemned man and could not see why Li Bi would stake everything on him. But a soldier’s first duty is obedience; he made a military salute and said in a ringing voice, “I have three hundred Lüben Guards under my hand, foot and horse alike, and within two quarters I can reach any point in Chang’an—I hope Master Zhang will give me the chance to cut down the enemy with my own hand and avenge my brother!”

Zhang Xiaojing noted that the man said “Master Zhang,” not “Commandant Zhang”; this sharp blade Li Bi had handed him would not, it seemed, be so easy to wield.

Time was too short. What followed was tight and dense: Zhang Xiaojing memorized the watchtower signals and the necessary means of contact, then went up to the great sand-table to hear a brief account of the Türks.

The one who explained was the graceful maidservant with the crescent mallet. Facing the table, she moved the pieces and spoke by turns, her voice bright and clear with a faint trace of a foreign accent. Zhang Xiaojing gave her a slightly impertinent second glance: this girl called Tanqi, with her high nose-bridge and coiled black hair, must be of mixed Han and foreign blood.

“The heart of it is—how do the Türk Wolf Guards mean to strike?” asked Zhang Xiaojing.

Tanqi said, “That we don’t yet know. The one piece of intelligence comes from the Shuofang rear-office. A Türk chieftain of one tribe once declared that the whole of Chang’an was about to become Que-le Huo-duo—you know what that means, don’t you?”

Zhang Xiaojing nodded. Que-le was a Türk word, close to the nine-layered blood-hell of the underworld, and huo-duo meant to be turned to dust. The whole phrase was at once a curse and a legendary beast of destruction. Even one who knew no Turkic could feel the towering murder in those words, “Que-le Huo-duo.”

That Chang’an was about to become Que-le Huo-duo might be an extravagant figure of speech, or a metaphor for something—no one knew.

Tanqi knew time was pressing and spoke quickly. “...This is a scrap of cloth we recovered in Warehouse Bing-6, with half of Chang’an’s outer city sketched on it. Most likely what Cao Poyan wants is a detailed ward-map of the whole of Chang’an.”

At the words “ward-map of Chang’an,” Zhang Xiaojing’s two silkworm brows knotted together. Li Bi, noticing his look turn grave, asked, “In your view, what do the Türks want the map for—hm, let me put it another way: with the map in hand, what could they do?”

“Poison the canals along their course, fire one ward after another, kill good folk under cover of night, spread baleful prophecies, force their way into the imperial city... At the lantern fair, they would need only to scatter a few copper coins where the crowds throng thickest—Chongren Ward, Yanshou Ward, the Xingqing Palace, the Qujiang Pool—to touch off a great riot. With the map to guide them, they could come and go at will through Chang’an, and the things they might do, I fear, are too many to count.”

Zhang Xiaojing ticked them off on his fingers and held forth at ease, and with every sentence the faces around him grew a degree colder.

Li Bi’s face was grave. He had reckoned the situation dire enough already, yet had not imagined such unthinkable, vicious devices. The Bureau’s men were officials, after all, and in these matters their knowledge fell far short of this former buliang chief, so used to the tricks of the underhand and the malevolent.

“In your view, if we cannot search openly, how should we set about it next?” Li Bi asked.

Zhang Xiaojing answered, “To hold an imperial ward-map in private is a crime punishable by death; apart from the government, no ordinary household would have one. Since Cao Poyan could not get it from Cui Liulang, he must either steal it from the imperial city, or else...” His gaze moved to the sand-table, and he shifted toward Tanqi until they were almost shoulder to shoulder. “Where did the watchtowers last see Cao Poyan?”

Tanqi was a little startled at his boldness and hesitated before answering. “Cao Poyan went over the water-gate too fast for the towers to track. But by our reckoning, he likely came ashore around Yanshou Ward or Buzheng Ward. Both are crowded with people, good for hiding. We’ve already sent men to search.”

Zhang Xiaojing said, “My guess is he won’t have gone far—in the end he’ll have to come back here.” And with that he pointed at the sand-table.

“The West Market?” Cui Qi was surprised. But Li Bi gave a slight nod, and said in one voice with Zhang Xiaojing: “The foreign merchants!”

The foreign merchants gathered mostly in the West Market, and among them were great magnates worth immense fortunes. A ward-map of Chang’an was a boon to business, and it would be no wonder if some kept one hidden away. Zhang Xiaojing knew their nature all too well: these men were born chasers of profit, with nerve bigger than a camel’s.

After Cui Liulang was exposed, Cao Poyan would not dare approach a Tang man again. To get the map in the shortest time, he had no choice but to turn his mind to the foreigners.

“But do you know which merchant to look for?” Li Bi asked with a frown. The foreign traders of the West Market were far too many to check one by one.

Zhang Xiaojing clenched his fist and answered lightly, “Extraordinary times have their own extraordinary methods.” Li Bi looked faintly uneasy, but in the end swallowed the words on his lips.

The “extraordinary methods” this fellow spoke of were likely some means not in keeping with humanity. But there was no time now for the luxury of debating law and conscience. In the corner of the hall the bronze water-clock still struck its drops one by one against the time-cylinder. Each drop might mean the loss of hundreds of lives.

“Commandant Zhang, the fortunes of the dynasty and the safety of a whole city’s people are entrusted to you.” Li Bi flicked his broad sleeve, gravely cupped his hands, and bowed with a solemn face. Seeing it, the officials behind him all rose as one and cupped their hands together.

Zhang Xiaojing did not return the bow, only brushed the dust from his left eye socket with a hand and said coolly, “I do this for the people of Chang’an; the rest I don’t care about. Let none of you mistake my meaning.”

In an instant every face changed. What talk was this? For all that they nursed grievances against the court in private, how could a man say such a thing out in the open?

Zhang Xiaojing grinned, then turned and walked out of the hall. The Bureau’s staff, hearts in their mouths, all looked to Li Bi. His face was as usual, the fly-whisk laid over his arm, as though it were nothing to him at all.

The fellow was hinting to him that he would not be controlled by anyone.

At the door, Cui Qi had already made ready a whole kit: a finely wrought screen-saber, close-fitting soft armor, smoke pellets, oxsinew binding-cords, and the rest, along with an arm-drawn hand crossbow. Zhang Xiaojing put it all on with practiced ease, then crouched to bind his trouser-cuffs tight with two lengths of hemp cord. Fully equipped, he gave off a hard, capable air of menace.

Zhang Xiaojing took up the hand crossbow, worked the empty string back and forth several times, listened to it with his ear, and said to Cui Qi, “Take off the sighting-post, and tighten the trigger-catch two notches more.” Cui Qi was taken aback: the sighting-post was an aid to aim, rather cumbersome, and a man with a true eye disliked fitting one; the trigger-catch governed the bolt’s speed, and the faster it flew the greater its force, but the harder its aim was to control. This one, it seemed, was a master of the crossbow.

He hurried off with the crossbow to have a craftsman adjust it, and Zhang Xiaojing seized the chance to draw Xu Bin aside and say in a low voice:

“Youde, I’ll trouble you to send someone to the southwest corner of Dunyi Ward. There’s a Wen’s incense shop there; get a word to the proprietor: leave Chang’an at once, don’t lose a single quarter. And best you urge your own family out of the city as soon as you can, and on no account go to the lantern fair.”

Xu Bin’s eyes went wide; he could not fathom what Zhang Xiaojing meant.

Zhang Xiaojing’s tone was of the utmost severity. “I’ve spent so many years in Chang’an that I know better than anyone how fragile this city is. If what Deputy Director Li says is true, then by my reckoning—” Here, rare for him, he hesitated, then weighted his words:

“This time Chang’an will not escape its doom.”

Cao Poyan just then was standing at the main gate of a certain ward. He had added a broad bamboo hat now, and unless it was lifted, his face could not be seen at all.

The ward gate stood wide open, countless peddlers hawking their wares along the foot of the ward wall, their cries rising on every side. A dozen or so idlers gripped the two ends of a thick rope on an open patch of ground, playing at tug-of-war, with ten times as many crowding round to cheer them on. Beside the gate stood a great bamboo lantern-wheel more than five zhang high, a tassel of colored silk hanging from each of its corners, waiting only for the candles to be raised at dusk.

Cao Poyan pulled the hat low and walked past the ward guards into the ward. The Bureau had already sent round a notice bidding the ward guards watch for a full-bearded foreigner—but the thing had come up in such haste that no likeness was attached. The guards were busy cheering the tug-of-war, and seeing that Cao Poyan’s dress was no foreigner’s robe, they did not even trouble to look him over, and let him pass.

Cao Poyan went to a quiet corner near the crossroads, drew a small paper roll from his robe, glanced at it, then stopped a child running past to ask where Li’s bamboo-ware shop was. Seeing his fierce looks, the child hurriedly said it was in the house at the end of the broad back-lane.

Cao Poyan followed the directions, and sure enough there was a bamboo workshop, its passage and doorway heaped with lantern frames not yet papered and with bamboo strips—simurghs and phoenixes, cloud-dragons, and immortals and lucky emblems of every kind. Business seemed good; even on the very eve of the Lantern Festival they were still hard at work.

He knocked—three long, one short, then two long again. Inside there was silence for a moment; then a gaunt bamboo-worker with a high nose and deep-set eyes put out his head, a pointed bamboo-splitting knife held at his chest.

“Where in the royal camp is the white-felt golden tent pitched?” he asked suddenly, in Turkic.

“The eagle of the steppe fears no gale.” Cao Poyan lifted his bamboo hat and answered, also in Turkic.

The other opened the door a crack and let him slip inside.
