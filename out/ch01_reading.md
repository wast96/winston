## Chapter 1. New Waves

The final week of the third month of Tenshō 10, 1582. Evening.

That spring, people said, the cherry blossoms had bloomed everywhere in Kyoto and beyond with a wild, almost frantic abandon. Nobunaga held the whole realm under his eye from his great castle at Azuchi, and in that long calm the townsfolk had come into a rare stretch of untroubled days. So they poured out to see the blossoms: to Ōhara, to Daigo, to Arashiyama. One sigh of admiration piled onto the next until word went round that this year the flowers had opened more beautifully than ever.

But now, those same blossoms lay fallen and browning along the roads; and beneath the Gojō Bridge, out of a beggars' hut, a single beggar came crawling into the last of the light.

Her hair hung in matted ropes, dried and gray as river-weed, thick with the dust of the bank. Through it a face showed dimly. The left side had been burned away to something raw and terrible; the right was merely crusted with grime, the face of a young woman showing underneath.

Her eyes did not match. The good right one and the puckered, half-shut left one had each gone dull and clouded. Anyone who held her gaze for long enough could see why. They had been given over so wholly to a grief too deep to sound that nothing else could find room in them. Whatever the world held up before that gaze, it reached her and found nothing to catch upon.

From under her rags, which hung down in the same weedy strands, came two legs like a pair of charred bones stood on end. The left one was hurt. She dragged it behind her, leaning on a stick, hauling herself along at a crawl.

She followed the bank of the Kamo River to the foot of the Shijō Bridge, turned west off Kawaramachi, and went on down Tako-Yakushi Street.

Just then a strange bell came threading through the thick evening air. Strange, but only in a manner of speaking: for five years now it had been a familiar sound in this quarter of Kyoto. What made it strange was that it was nothing like the temple bells of the city. Not low and solemn, but light and high and quick, a sound that seemed to rejoice, to play, to laugh.

Two young women were coming the other way. On the point of passing the beggar, they heard it and stopped dead.

"The bell for the Angelus," said one.

They pressed their palms together, lifted their faces to the sky, and began, in one voice, to recite their orasho:

"O saving Victim, who openest the gate of Heaven: the armies of the enemy press hard upon us; lend us thy power, come to our aid, amen. To the one Lord in whom the Three Persons dwell, praise without end; and grant us, we beseech thee, life everlasting in Paradise, amen."

Then they walked on past. The beggar had halted too, and put her hands together as they did. But her lips never moved.

To the people going back and forth, a figure at prayer was nothing to remark on. They gave her no more than a glance.

Her slow feet came to their journey's end.

Before her stood a gate in the Chinese manner, a karamon, its black tiled roof sweeping up at the eaves. A row of low shops ran alongside it, their plank roofs weighted down with stones. They sold bright Nanban hats, and crosses, and the rosary-beads called contas. At the foot of one gatepost stood a jar, its mouth a foot across, and a signboard beside it in a fine hand: "A vessel for the rice given in alms to the poor, for the garça, the grace, of Deus."

Through the gate she came into a forecourt, walled in pale-green plaster. Ahead stood a second gate, a size larger than the first.

Just as she reached it, the bell stopped. In its place rose a music stranger and finer still. It welled up out of a tangle of instruments: organ, viola, harp, lute, gravicembalo the forerunner of the piano, rebeca the forerunner of the violin. The sounds braided together and seemed to drift, and float, and weep, and rejoice. They raked at the breast and sank in, with a strange feeling that had no name.

She crouched in the shadow of the gatepost and covered her ruined face with both hands. Look closely, and you saw the left hand. It was festered dark-red to the elbow, and every finger was barely half its length.

Now voices joined the music, a chorus so clear and pure it seemed to come from another world.

It was the boys' choir, innocent of every stain, singing the Ave Maria.

She knew no Latin, of course. But she had heard the song many times, in halting Japanese, from Urugan-Bateren, Padre Organtino. So she knew it for a hymn in praise of Lord Jesus Christ, who saves the poor and the suffering, and of his mother Maria, gentlest and most merciful. As she listened, the tears ran and would not stop, until the rags over her knees were soaked through.

The music drew out one last long chord and died into the air. She rose to her feet and came up to the three-storied tower that soared in front of her.

I keep reaching for the word strange, and there is none better: this too was a strange building. White walls. Pillars blue as deep water. Long eaves that thrust out and curled up at the ends. The ground floor was a great chapel, room enough for several hundred worshippers.

The second floor was ringed on all four sides by a railed veranda; inside were six rooms, the priests' quarters.

Higher still it climbed. From the top you could see out over the whole capital, and past it to the far fields.

And at the very peak, like the last stroke of the work, a golden cross rose from the roof-ridge and pointed at heaven.

This was Kyoto's first Christian church, finished five years before. It had gone up on a vermilion-seal warrant from Nobunaga, out of gifts pooled from every quarter: money, timber, labor. Not only the Christian lords of the nearby provinces gave, but Murai Sadakatsu himself, the shoshidai of Kyoto, and believers past counting, high and low.

Francisco Xavier had been the first European priest ever to sail to Japan. He landed at Kagoshima thirty-four years before, on the fifteenth of August, the Feast of the Assumption of the Virgin; and so the church took the name Nossa Senhora da Assunção, Our Lady of the Assumption. But the people of Japan called it, flatly, the Nanban-ji: the temple of the southern barbarians.

After Xavier they came in a steady stream, from Spain, from Portugal, from Italy, and the faith spread fast across the country. Like the Ikkō sect before it, it sank first and deepest into the peasants, who suffered most. For at bottom it was a creed of human liberation: before God, it taught, all people stand equal, with no rank of high or low, no line drawn between man and woman.

There was more to it than that. The Ikkō sect asked only that you chant the nenbutsu, and nothing else. The Christian faith came furnished with a whole picture of the cosmos, one the science of the day could find no way to refute. How all things were made, the earth and mankind among them. The world that waits beyond death. And, at the last, a prophecy of the end of the universe itself.

It carried a stern moral code as well. Strict monogamy. The keeping of the Sabbath. The ban on worshipping other gods, the ban on usury. Rules like these ran head-on into the ideas and habits of the Japanese of that day, and slowed the teaching's spread.

Hideyoshi is a case in point. He heard the whole of it set out by Padre Gaspar Coelho, and then said: "This teaching is surely finer than the teaching of the bonzes. Everything you have told me of it satisfies me completely, and I would find nothing at all hard in becoming a Christian, save the one commandment that will not let a man keep many wives. Relax me that single point, and I will turn Christian." Nobunaga's eldest son, Nobutada, told the irmão much the same: "While that commandment stands, I cannot become a believer." And beyond doubt the rule kept many a townsman from the faith.

Yet for a great many others, those same rules were the very thing that drew them.

As he saw off the worshippers from the evening service, a foreign priest appeared in the doorway. He stood tall enough that you looked up at him. An old book, the Raichō Jikki, describes his kind: "his head small, his color ruddy, his eyes round and yellow, his nose high, his ears reaching to his shoulders, his mouth wide, his teeth white as snow like a horse's, his nails long like a bear's, his hair and beard the color of a mouse; he seemed about fifty years of age."

The priest caught sight of the beggar woman and opened that red mouth. "Oh. Tamo, so you've come. Why didn't you go on inside?"

Padre Organtino crouched down to speak with her more easily, so that his head, the crown shaved round in a tonsure, came below the level of her eyes.

Into the eyes of the woman he had called Tamo came a shy, childlike smile. It was a frail thing. A single sharp word would have wiped it out. But for this one moment it had the strength to push back the grief that filled her.

Besides this three-storied main hall, the Nanban-ji had five other buildings: a refectory, a school, a guesthouse for honored visitors, an infirmary for the sick.

It was into the school that Organtino led Tamo. School, here, mostly meant the music for the choir. The real school was at Azuchi.

Nobunaga had taken to the rare and wonderful treasures of Europe the missionaries brought along with their doctrine, and he meant to use this new faith to sap the strength of Buddhism. So in Tenshō 7, at Organtino's petition, he had a broad stretch of paddy filled in, north of the Minami-shindō below Azuchi Castle, and a three-storied wooden building raised on it, a chapel on the ground floor and thirty-four rooms above. This he made a seminário, a training house, and into it he enrolled a crowd of high-born sons, among them retainers of Takayama Ukon. Organtino himself became its rector. The Kyoto Nanban-ji he had left, ever since Eiroku 7, nineteen years back, in the hands of Luís Fróis, who was in Japan; but he shuttled between the two without cease.

This Azuchi seminário turns up as an engraving in a book at the archives in Modena, in Italy: the Compendium of the Great Deeds of Pope Gregory XIII. There it appears as a wholly Western building, three stories of stone, complete with a square medieval bell-tower. But the artist had never laid eyes on the thing built at Azuchi and drew it from imagination. In truth it was a Japanese temple-style building, with only the faintest Western flavor added.

The Christian schooling climbed by stages. First the casa de probação, the preparatory house; then the noviciado, the novitiate; then the seminário, the training house; and last the colégio, the college, whose graduates were placed in formal teaching posts. Even before Azuchi opened, the first seminário and colégio in Japan had been founded at Arima, then a colégio at Funai in Bungo, and a noviciado at Usuki. Great numbers of students bent over their books: retórica, rhetoric; filosofia, philosophy; lógica, logic; matemática, astronomy and mathematics; cânones, canon law; teologia, theology; and Portuguese, and Latin, and instrumental and vocal music, and painting, and copperplate engraving.

In Tenshō 9 the Vice-Provincial, Coelho, sent home a report on how the Arima seminário was faring. "Among the pupils," he wrote, "some twelve or thirteen, of seventeen or eighteen years, are of very rare talent. Being of superior wit, these boys have made great progress, and several have shown, within a few months, the power to begin preaching; we are no little pleased that this foundation has been laid. In the teaching of the Catholic faith from its very roots, what a boy learns in three years, these will master in three or four months. Most of them write our script well. They divide their hours, part of the day for reading and writing the Japanese script, part for Latin; and those with energy to spare, hard as Latin is for them, become good Latin scholars through memory and application, in the same time as our own boys, or even less. They have learned, too, to sing to the organ and to play the cravo, and there is already a choir of some size that can sing a formal Mass with ease."

When the Azuchi seminário was finished, Nobunaga came one day to see it. He watched the young men at their eager studies and praised the new-style school warmly. And when he heard Jerome Itō, son of Itō Yoshimasu, the daimyo of Hyūga, play the clavichord, he showed, they say, great satisfaction.

The rooms of this Nanban-ji, floor upon floor, had been laid with tatami given by a certain widow.

It was at the close of the previous year that Organtino had first come upon this beggar woman.

Ask her what he might, she gave not a word, and only tried to bolt. He coaxed and cajoled her to the temple, had her washed, treated the terrible burn-scars, and gave her rice and clothes. Where she had come by the burns, he could not learn.

But the clothes were snatched from her by someone, and she went on in the same old rags.

Only about two months ago had she at last begun, a very little, to talk. From what she said, she had been caught in a battle at a fortress high on a mountain, and had come through something dreadful. The place was put to the torch and wiped out. As she went down in the flames and smoke, a man caught her up and held her. He covered her small body with his own, and, burning to death himself, kept the fire from her. That was how her left hand, her left leg, and half her face came to be burned. She lost consciousness. A cloudburst the next day, it seems, brought her back to her senses. The man who had shielded her with his body she buried somewhere; who he was, she sealed her lips and would not say. Then she turned beggar and drifted her way into Kyoto.

Everywhere castles were falling, and with each one a countless multitude, young and old, men and women, died or were maimed or starved or simply vanished.

Over long years Organtino had preached to numberless souls, in Europe, in the southern seas, in the East, and he knew the fine workings of the human heart. He never once pressed her. He only comforted her gently, and tried, each time, to soak a little deeper into that shut heart.

Out of a true and living faith, the padres had crossed ten thousand leagues of storm-driven sea and come the whole way to Japan, careless of their lives, to carry the grace of God to peoples they called unenlightened. They wound rope about themselves for a sash, lived in rough plank huts, and preached.

But they soon learned that nothing came of it until the rulers were won. So they made believers of rulers by the score. They bore themselves with ceremony, made gifts of rare treasures, dealt out the profits of trade. For the peasants, meanwhile, they did other work. Vilela's report calls those peasants "slaves who toil for the samurai and the bonzes and keep for themselves only the very worst." The padres healed their sick, gave them rice and money, took in the aged and the widowed and the orphaned, comforted body and soul alike. They turned the close-knit bonds of the villages to account, too, and built confraternities: the misericórdia, the brotherhood of charity; the brotherhood of Santa Maria; the sexta, the Friday societies.

Could so many of them really never have grasped that this work of good will was running the errands of their home governments' wars of conquest? They had been shown the proof, over and over, as they pushed on through India, Java, Borneo, Indochina, the Philippines: one sickening case after another of a ruler turning a country into a colony. And still, it seems, they never grasped it.

Or rather, they did not read it as conquest for plunder at all. They believed, no doubt, that they were gathering the peoples of heathen darkness into the ranks of the civilized, Christian nations.

Yet the plain fact is that these missionaries were serving a design of the men who ruled them, and serving it at the cost of fearful hardship freely borne, and of their own lives. The design was simple. First seize the soul, and then the country.

When Tamo had her rice and her medicine from Organtino, she went trudging off from the Nanban-ji. Barely a chō away, the tall roof of a great temple reared up and glared across at the Nanban-ji's three-storied tower.

A flock of crows swept cawing over the two roofs and made for their roosts on Mount Hiei.

This was Honnō-ji itself, the head temple of the Hokke sect. That within three short months history would swing on its hinge here, with this very place for a stage, Tamo of course had no way of guessing.

But before the great thing at Honnō-ji, history had already set another stage, for another event, off to the east in Kōshū.

The house of Takeda had been lying low. At the battle of Nagashino it had lost the greater part of its veteran commanders and elder retainers, and an army of more than twenty thousand men, and even the Hōjō and the Uesugi had cast it off. But that year, in the first month, its vassal Kiso Yoshimasa of Shinano went secretly over to Nobunaga, and Katsuyori, Shingen's son and heir, moved to bring him to heel. Yoshimasa appealed to Nobunaga for aid. Here is my chance, Nobunaga thought, and poured troops into Shinano with his son Nobutada in the van, while Ieyasu answered the call and pushed into Suruga.

The castles on Katsuyori's side fell one after another. When even his kinsman Anayama Baisetsu surrendered, he fell back on Kōfu with a bare forty men. Now every trusted retainer he leaned on turned traitor. At the last he tried to hole up on Mount Tenmoku, east of Kōfu, but there was no holding out, and on the eleventh of the third month he took his own life, together with his wife and his kin.

So the house of Takeda came to its end, and its lands passed into Nobunaga's hands.

Sasaki Jōtei and other beaten Takeda commanders fled to Erin-ji, a temple Shingen had held in deep devotion, and the abbot, the priest Kaisen, sheltered them a while. This enraged Nobunaga. He drove Kaisen and every last one of the temple's hundred and eighty souls, young and old, up into the mountain gate, banked grass and straw and firewood beneath it, set it alight, and burned them all to death. It was then that Kaisen spoke the famous verse: "Quench the mind, and the very fire is cool."

On the twenty-first of the fourth month, no sooner had Nobunaga made his triumphal return to Azuchi than he ordered Nobutaka and Akechi Mitsuhide to put down Shikoku, and Mitsuhide went back to Sakamoto to make ready for the field.

On the night of the fourteenth of the fifth month, a great comet threw the people of Kyoto into alarm. It dragged a long, long tail behind it, and when it had not faded by the next night either, they decided heaven must have hung the star there to tell them something. What it meant to tell, they could not make out. "What is it?" "What's going to happen?" they whispered to one another.

Down on the dry bed of the Gojō, Tamo saw the strange star for the first time in her life, and it carried her clean away. Ah, the beautiful thing. She had nothing left in the world to lose, and so, perhaps, nothing left to fear. Only, she thought, with a piercing ache, if I could show it to Kashii too.

Kashii was the young man who had made a shield of his own body and kept Tamo from the fire.

In the Iga basin, here and there, the two of them had often watched the stars. Sometimes the whole sky was a lavish, endless spill of them. Sometimes there were only a lonely star or two, coming out red in the early dark.

To Tamo the stars were beautiful things, shining and wheeling of their own accord in some far place that had no bearing on the world of men. To Kashii they were an important instrument of the shinobi's art.

The arts of evasion are the very heart of ninjutsu, and taken together they go by the name of the Five Escapes.

The outer five are the escapes by wood, fire, earth, metal, and water. The inner five are the escapes by man, bird, beast, insect, and fish.

Divided again under the three heads of heaven, earth, and man, they run as follows.

Heaven's are ten: the escapes by sun, moon, star, cloud, mist, thunder, lightning, wind, rain, and snow.

Earth's, to which the outer five belong, break down into ten: by tree, grass, fire, smoke, earth, house, metal, stone, water, and hot water.

Man's, which are the inner five, break down into ten: by man, woman, old, young, noble, base, bird, beast, insect, and fish.

Kashii had learned these arts from his uncle Kasumi. The first thing they made him recite was this: "Now, the art of evasion is the art of turning the false into the true and the true into the false." Make the false look true, make the true be taken for false, and by that slip away. The means you use in the doing are these several categories.

Wood-escape, for instance. You topple a stacked pile of timber all at once to drag every eye toward it while you get away; or you swarm up a great tree with a hand-hook and hide in the high branches. That sort of thing. Fire-escape means raking dead leaves together, setting them alight, and bolting the other way.

Metal-escape turns the glint or the ring of something metal to account. Man-escape means fleeing in the guise of another.

The old tales of Nikki Danjō turning into a rat, or Jiraiya working the sorcery of the toad, are inflated fictions, spun out of the beast-escape branch of man-escape: the trick of loosing rats or toads to snag the eye and slip off.

Among the ten methods of heaven-escape is the art of star-escape. From ancient times the stars had been studied with a scientist's eye by the Egyptians and the Arabs, and until the compass was invented they were mankind's one guide across the seas. But they were also taken for something occult, and bred a tangled thicket of superstition: China's yin-yang and five-elements lore, the Shinto star-festivals, the divinations of the onmyōji. Some of it had seeped into the way of the mountain ascetics, itself one of the roots of ninjutsu. But what served no practical end fell away of its own accord, and in the Iga school star-escape never came to much.

Kashii was a master of the beast arts, but at star-escape he was a poor hand. All he had taught her was the courses of the Big Dipper, of Venus, Saturn, and Jupiter, and how to fix a direction by them.

So to Kashii the stars were a thing there was still study to be done of, and yet no real way to know. A vexing business. And so, even lying side by side to watch them, the two were forever thinking at cross-purposes, and their talk never quite met.

Around the middle of the fifth month a rumor went round: Ieyasu, at Nobunaga's invitation, was coming up to the capital to take in the sights of Kyoto, Osaka, Nara, and Sakai, and the Nanban-ji was to be given over for his Kyoto lodging.

At the time the church held the padre Cariam and the irmãos Lourenço and Bertolameu, while Organtino was away at Azuchi.

On the twenty-first came word that Ieyasu had started for the capital, and at the Nanban-ji they were at their wits' end how to receive him. But he never lodged with them at all. He put up elsewhere for a couple of days, then went off to see Sakai, and one and all breathed again.

On the twenty-ninth, Nobunaga set out from Azuchi and took quarters at Honnō-ji, a stone's throw from the Nanban-ji, while his son Nobutada lodged at Myōkaku-ji, over by the Kurama gate.

Just then urgent calls for reinforcement were coming in from Hideyoshi, who had pushed into the Chūgoku country and was flooding out Takamatsu Castle. So Nobunaga sent off Takayama Ukon and a great many men, and kept only a bare handful of guards about himself.

The night of the first of the sixth month, the twentieth of June by the solar calendar, the town of Kyoto could not sleep. The muggy heat had hung on since daylight and would not slacken. The city tossed and turned, and dropped off only as dawn came on.

At the Nanban-ji, Padre Cariam had risen while it was still pitch dark to say the dawn Mass of the second. He was changing into his vestments.

Then, all at once, someone was hammering at the gate. The gatekeeper opened it, and five or six of the faithful came tumbling in, the color gone from their faces. "It seems something dreadful's broken out at Honnō-ji," they said. "It looks to be a grave matter, so you'd best hold off a while on the Mass."

They strained to listen, and, sure enough, a tumult of noise and voices carried to them. Before long came the crack, crack, crack of bursting gunfire, and at last, with a great whoosh, flames leapt up.

The converts who came running up behind were crying, "It's Koretō!" "It's Lord Akechi!"

Mitsuhide, who by Nobunaga's own order should long since have marched to reinforce the Chūgoku front, had turned traitor without warning and thrown a ring around Honnō-ji.

That anyone would rise against a man whose very name set people trembling was a thing not one soul had dreamed of.

And so all Kyoto stood thunderstruck. The cry that wanted out froze in the throat.

The padres went white.

Nobunaga was a cruel man, true. But he was also the protector of the Christian faith. Only because he gave his leave did the Nanban-ji stand at all, and the Azuchi seminary with it. And he was the easiest of men to win over. He was weak, hopelessly weak, for anything rare and strange.

Take the visit the year before, in the third month. The visitador, the inspector Valignano, went up to Azuchi for his audience and brought along a black man he had carried with him from Africa. Nobunaga was astonished past all measure. He could not for the life of him believe the man was black by nature and not smeared over with ink, and in the end he had him stripped to a loincloth and ran his hands over the whole of him, and only then was satisfied.

There was more to please him. The black man was strong, could do a turn or two, and spoke a little broken Japanese, so that Nobunaga grew more delighted still and never tired of talking with him. He took the man for his own, made him a body-servant, kept him always at his side, and was hugely pleased whenever people gaped. Valignano gained no little credit by the gift.

Nobunaga had this black man with him when the blow fell at Honnō-ji.

The black man stood and faced Akechi's soldiers as they poured in. But they were unnerved by him and gave way, none daring to close with him, and he broke calmly out through the ring and fled to the Nanban-ji.

Tamo had seen the flames going up over the quarter of the Nanban-ji from the dry bed of the Gojō, and had come dragging her body to the spot. At the temple gate she all but ran full into this vast, coal-black shape. She loosed a shriek that tore from her throat and dropped in a dead faint. For what she saw was a phantom: the charred corpses that had lain heaped all about her when she came back to life at the Momochi fort, risen of a sudden and bearing down on her.

The converts carried Tamo inside and tended her.

The church was crowded already. The beggars and the sick who were fed there day by day had fled in in throngs, in dread of the fighting.

The common people of Japan were wretchedly poor in those days. An infant that could not be aborted, nor strangled at the birth, was set out as a foundling. Lepers and others in the grip of grave disease were driven from their homes. Famine came round again and again, and many starved.

The Christians could not look on all this and pretend not to see it. So they raised free infirmaries, and foundling homes, and homes for the aged, in one place after another.

The hospitals and old-age homes the people called yōjō-ya, health-houses. And, as the jar at the Nanban-ji gate bore witness, they carried on a wide work besides, taking in alms and dealing them out to the poor.

The year after the affair at Honnō-ji, a hundred converts came together at Nagasaki as dues-paying members and founded a misericórdia, a charitable society that did its good works out of the funds they raised. People called it the charity-house. In time the like was set up at Funai, at Kyoto, at Osaka, at Sakai, and elsewhere.

So it was that the padres and irmãos even picked up a rough practice of medicine, and with their own hands would wipe and wash the pus of a leper.

This kind of social work went far beyond anything the Ikkō sect had done, and it was, in the end, one more thing that won them their crowds of converts.

By the black man's account, when the rebel army came surging up, Nobunaga had only just woken.

Still in his nightclothes, he threw the sliding door open. From the soldiers already loose in the inner court, arrows came at him thick as hail. One drove into his shoulder. He pulled it out with his own hand, caught up his long sword, and flung himself on the attackers. But before long a bullet went through him somewhere, and he reeled and toppled back. He had strength enough left to slide the door shut. What came after that, the black man says, he does not know.

The converts kept bringing in word by the minute, from every side. The mansion of the shoshidai, Murai Sadakatsu, stood right before the gate of Honnō-ji. He had tried to get inside, but with Mitsuhide's men swarming everywhere it was hopeless, and he raced the four kilometers to Myōkaku-ji to carry the news. Myōkaku-ji could not be held, he told Nobutada; the young lord must fall back into the Nijō Palace.

In the Nijō Palace was Prince Sanehito, son of the Retired Emperor Ōgimachi. They moved him across to the Imperial Palace, and shut themselves in with a bare five hundred men.

By now it was full day. The flames roared up from Honnō-ji, and the host that had ringed it raised its victory cry and rolled on toward the Nijō Palace. There could be no more doubting that Nobunaga was dead.

And indeed the enemy commanders were calling out, this one and that. "The Minister of the Right is no more!" they cried, meaning Nobunaga. "Let the Third-Rank Middle Captain make up his mind at once to meet his end!" meaning Nobutada. "How long will you not have done? It is an unseemly sight!"

The Nijō Palace had a narrow moat and low walls, no fastness to hold off an army. Soldiers climbed to the roof of the Konoe mansion that adjoined it on the north and loosed fire-arrows, and the gable of the main keep's study burst into flame. It was beaten back. But by the last third of the hour of the Dragon, nine in the morning, the fighting here too was over. Nobutada killed himself at the last and put the palace to the torch. And when the main hall of Honnō-ji, that had blazed so long, burned through and fell in, there was no more fear of the fire carrying to the streets around.

But the converts at the Nanban-ji could not be easy. Mitsuhide had no love for the Christian faith, and the faith had lived under Nobunaga's protection. Surely, they thought, he would burn the Nanban-ji down.

But Mitsuhide sent a proclamation through the streets. He would on no account burn the town, and would take no one's life so long as no hand was raised against his soldiers. At that they could breathe again.

Even so, his men were going house to house, routing out the survivors of Nobunaga's party, striking off their heads and bringing them in, until the heads made a hill before Mitsuhide and the bodies lay abandoned in the streets.

The black man spoken of before had slipped from the Nanban-ji before anyone noticed, out of curiosity perhaps, and wandered as far as the Nijō Palace. Mitsuhide's men seized him on the spot and dragged him before their lord. Mitsuhide gave him one glance and spat out: "Hm. So the Minister of the Right doted on such a creature as this, and came for it to his own charred and miserable end. A thing like this is a benighted savage that knows nothing, and no Japanese at that. Hand him over to the Nanban-ji this instant."

And so, his arms still bound behind him, the black man was sent back to the church.

The converts lost no time in giving thanks to Deus that through all this upheaval church and faithful alike had come through unharmed.

But they were not altogether at ease. Mitsuhide would fall upon the Azuchi seminary for a certainty, and the seminary stood hard beside the castle.

So their thanksgiving ran on into a petition: keep the Azuchi seminary safe, we beseech thee. Three messengers had already been sent off to carry word to it.

And in fact Mitsuhide, once he had performed the victory rite before the burnt-out ruins of the Nijō Palace, put himself at the head of his troops and set off for Sakamoto, his own castle.

By noon the people of Azuchi had caught the rumor that something terrible had happened at Honnō-ji, and that Nobunaga was likely dead. But no sure word could reach them. For the Seta Bridge, reckoned the finest bridge in Japan, thrown by Nobunaga across the Seta River where it empties into the south end of Lake Biwa, had been cut down by one of his own generals to slow Mitsuhide's march on Azuchi.

So the rumors of catastrophe swelled and swelled, until all Azuchi was in an uproar such as men picture the Last Judgment to be.

Organtino and the rest were sure Mitsuhide would burn Azuchi to the ground. They took hurried counsel and resolved to put the seminary's twenty-eight pupils aboard a boat and flee with them to Oki-no-shima, an island out in the lake. A man of that island had come and, all kindness, urged them to it.

Padre Francisco Fróis carried a crucifix with the holy image on it, a little statue of the Virgin, silver candlesticks, a censer, sacred plate. Padre Pereira too was laden with altar-vessels. But many of the townsfolk had already turned thief. They tore the hat and cloak off Pereira, and cut the very sleeves and hem from the robe beneath, and the others too were robbed of this and that. Still, they got aboard at last and reached Oki-no-shima. Only there they found that the folk they had taken for kind souls were pirates.

The pirates herded the converts into a stable and, in a manner gone cold and hard, demanded half of everything they carried. After no end of wrangling it was settled, at last, by the payment of ten ryō in gold.

But Organtino did not trust them. He had one of the converts steal the costliest of the sacred vessels away in the dead of night and hide them on a hill in the middle of the island. At the same time, one of the seminary pupils, a boy named Araki, happened to be the nephew of one of Akechi's own generals. He got a message smuggled out to his uncle, and the uncle came to Oki-no-shima with soldiers and brought the converts and their sacred vessels off to safety.

Meanwhile the Seta Bridge was mended by the fifth, and Mitsuhide and his men came into Azuchi. They broke open the castle storehouses, and in two or three days shared out among themselves the whole hoard of gold and silver that Nobunaga had been fifteen years amassing.

Mitsuhide strictly forbade the setting of fires, and after three days of feasting he shouldered the plunder and left for Kyoto. On the instant, looting and highway robbery broke out. The seminary was stripped bare, all but a few altar-vessels and books. They tore away the window shutters, and even the linings of the walls, until nothing was left of it but pillars and roof-tiles.

Hideyoshi, the moment word of the disaster reached him, patched up a hasty peace with the Mōri. The terms had Shimizu Muneharu, commander of the Takamatsu castle he had been flooding out, cut open his own belly. On the seventh he drove through wind and rain back to Himeji, and on the morning of the ninth set out for Kyoto. On the afternoon of the thirteenth the two armies joined battle at Yamazaki. In a little while Mitsuhide's army broke, and Mitsuhide himself, fleeing under cover of the black night, was killed near Ogurusu by peasants out to strip the armor from beaten stragglers.

The next day, the fourteenth, was a day of much head-taking. An order had gone out that the heads were to be brought to Honnō-ji, to be offered up for the repose of Nobunaga's own head, and before long more than two thousand heads were brought in.

Past the front of the Nanban-ji people went by in an unbroken file, heads swinging from their hands. There were parties of three lugging thirty heads and more, roped together, some slung over a shoulder, some dragged along the ground.

The heat that day was savage, and the stench lay thick over the whole quarter. Whenever the wind came from the direction of Honnō-ji, the Nanban-ji, heat or no heat, had to shut every window tight.

Akechi Mitsuharu, Mitsuhide's nephew, heard of the rout at Yamazaki, threw up Azuchi in haste, and fell back on Sakamoto Castle. That very day Sakamoto fell, and Mitsuhide's wife and children, and all the Akechi kin with them, perished in the flames. In the confusion fires were set, and Azuchi Castle in all its boasted splendor, and the seminary with it, were in no time reduced to ash.

The dead of those eight days came to more than ten thousand. Padre Joseph, who went up from Sakai to Kyoto on the seventeenth, came back to the Nanban-ji and reported that in the course of that one afternoon he had met more than five hundred corpses drifting down the Yodo River.

To this same Nanban-ji the whole company of the Azuchi seminary, burned out of house and home, had come to settle, so that the place grew crowded and full of life.

Hideyoshi had Mitsuhide's head and trunk skewered together on bamboo spits, raised on a cross, and set up for show at a place called Hi-no-oka, east of Higashiyama and north of Yamashina.

Hearing the rumor of it, Tamo went dragging her feet up the slope that runs south from before the Nanzen-ji, and came at last to the cross. The head had lain a while buried in the earth at Ogurusu, and had fallen horribly to rot: a thing of dread to look upon.

Nobunaga had massacred Kashii and all the rest of the Iga folk in a wall of fire. This man had burned that same Nobunaga to death in the flames of Honnō-ji. And here he was, a bare fifteen days after killing Nobunaga, hung up in this hideous shape.

And Tamo herself. Ten years before, at Ichijōdani in Echizen, the left side of her face had been burned, and she had died, as it were, once. Last year her left hand and left foot had been burned, and she had died a second time. Her body had never been defiled. But the ugliness of men, and the terror of them, she had been taught past all bearing, in her wandering from Echizen to Iga.

Facing the crucified corpse, a lone forlorn shape between heaven and earth, she was gripped by a loneliness that gnawed at the bone. If this was the loneliness she must taste, she wanted no more of life. She wanted to die and be rid of it.

But just as she began to totter off, meaning to hang herself from some tree or throw herself in the water, the Angelus bell began to ring, far in the distance. No one yet knew who would be the new master of the land, nor how that master would deal with the Christian faith; and so, for caution's sake, they rang it only softly, a thin and feeble sound.

Yet that faint sound, and the memory of the people there, the only people who ever gave her a kind word, held Tamo back to life.

Even now she did not truly believe in the Santa Maria or the Jesus Christ that Organtino and the others told her of. It was only that some vague thing hovering about the Nanban-ji had become a slender thread, and drew her in.

We have spent all this while on the great convulsions of history, and for a good stretch there has been no room for the ninja to come on. Here, at last, their turn comes round.

It begins with the famous Tarao Crossing of Tokugawa Ieyasu, and out of it was born the bond between the house of Tokugawa and the ninja that would hold all through the Edo shogunate.

Fifteen chō north of Iga-Ueno, on the far bank of the Hattori River, lies a village called Hattori.

The Hattori, like the Hata, were of the Kurehatori, the Kure weavers, descended from immigrants naturalized out of the mainland. By their own genealogy they sprang, in the beginning, from the spirit of smoke. An ancestor had served as a retainer of Taira no Tomomori, come back alive from the battle of Dan-no-ura, gone to ground in the Yono estate two ri south of Ueno, and set himself up as a local magnate under the surname Chigachi. His descendants turned to the ninja's art. They took the lower shinobi of the district under them, and rose to be shinobi of the middle rank.

In the generation of Hanzō's father the family left Iga to serve Ieyasu, and was taken into his close guard. They kept up a coming and going with Iga, so that although Hanzō himself was not there when Nobunaga trampled the province, he had many friends among its people.

He was a quick-witted, tireless man, and that was why he had been taken into the personal guard. He was not so grimly hard-bitten a figure as Ishikawa Goemon. But he was every inch the descendant of mainland stock: fair-skinned, long of eye, standing better than five shaku eight sun, his irises brown and the whites of his eyes touched with blue. He was now forty-two years old.

Thirteen days before the affair at Honnō-ji, on the twenty-first of the fifth month, Ieyasu had set out for the capital at Nobunaga's invitation. Nobunaga feasted him at Azuchi and treated him to kōwaka dances and sarugaku and all the rest.

It was a sightseeing journey, Kyoto and Osaka and Sakai, so his following was very small, no more than a few dozen: Honda Tadakatsu, Sakai Tadatsugu, Ishikawa Kazumasa, and the others. Hattori Hanzō was among them.

After Kyoto, Ieyasu viewed the old site of Ishiyama Castle at Osaka, went by way of Sumiyoshi toward Sakai, and put up at Myōkoku-ji. There, planted when the temple was built, stood a sago palm bought off a foreign ship. In twenty years it had grown to a wonderful size, and the sight of it took Ieyasu aback. Nobunaga had lent him his attendant Hasegawa Takemaru for a steward, and the great Kyoto merchant Chaya Shirōjirō for a guide.

After four or five days, thinking to go back up to Kyoto before long and see Nobunaga once more, Ieyasu sent Honda Tadakatsu on ahead, on the early morning of the second of the sixth month, to give word he would return that day. He set out from Sakai himself a little after.

Tadakatsu had galloped as far as Katano, just midway to Kyoto, when out of the Kyoto direction came Chaya Shirōjirō on a packsaddled horse, his face gone deathly white. "It's a catastrophe. The world is finished," he said, the tears rolling down. In the small hours, he told them, Nobunaga had been cut down at Honnō-ji, and Nobutada too had died at the Nijō Palace.

Tadakatsu was thunderstruck. The two of them wheeled about, rode stirrup to stirrup, and met Ieyasu coming up at the foot of Mount Iimori. Ieyasu was aghast at the news. With so small a band, to dither and lose his head here was out of the question. There could be no thought yet of carrying a war of revenge to Mitsuhide. The one certainty was this: get clear of the place, or Mitsuhide's men would hunt him down and kill him.

He had to reckon Mitsuhide's soldiers were ranging at will over Yamashiro, Ōmi, and Yamato. And once fighting was in the wind, the peasants everywhere would turn robber in a moment and fall on stragglers and small parties.

Honda Tadakatsu pressed them. "There's nothing for it but to pick the roughest mountain tracks and run flat out for Ise by the shortest way there is. I don't know the country, and there's not a man in the escort who does; but let's get out to Nara, cross from Shigaraki into Iga, and break through to Ise. Every moment we stand debating, the time slips by. Quickly, quickly!" And so the rest came round to it.

They caught a passing peasant and threatened him. "Set us on the wrong road and I'll wrench your head clean off." So they made him guide them, crossed Mount Shigi, passed west of Nara, forded the Kizu River, and came out at Shigaraki in the province of Ōmi.

Already, back at the crossing of Mount Shigi, Hattori Hanzō had run on ahead alone. He reached the village of Tarao on the southern rim of Kōga, and there talked round the lay-monk Tarao Dōka, who as one of the fifty-three houses of Kōga held sway in those parts.

This house of Tarao had done the very same thing once before, as far back as the first year of Ōnin, 1467, a hundred and sixteen years earlier. When Ashikaga Yoshimi, caught up in the Ōnin War, sought to flee the fighting into Ise, and in the eighth month slipped out of Kyoto, crossed Lake Biwa, and came into Shigaraki, the Tarao escorted him, brought him through Iga, and saw him safe to Ise. And in the sixth month of the very next year, when the shogun Yoshimasa wished to call Yoshimi back and laid the errand on Shōgoin Dōyo, Dōyo too went back and forth to Ise under a guard of Tarao men, and carried out his charge.

The records speak of "some three thousand mountain-warriors," so the Tarao already had a force of that size in hand. From their fortress, looking southeast, you could take in the whole of Iga at a glance. The house itself was nothing like the grim windowless strongholds of a Fujibayashi Nagato-no-kami or a Momochi Sandayū. It was no more than an ordinary residence, built a little stouter than most.

Here, with everyone else sent from the room, Hanzō sat down face to face with a little man whose face was like a boiled-down, wrinkled black bean. Hanzō was a big-framed figure in his prime, forty-one, a shade odd of feature and yet a fine-looking man. The little man was Dōka the lay-monk, then fifty-eight.

"The lord of Hamamatsu is the master my house has served since my father's time," said Hanzō.

"That I know." The black-bean monk picked the rheum from the corners of his small eyes. "But a man doesn't throw in with another so hard unless he sees something rare in him. Hmm. The lord of Hamamatsu, is it? And what do you see in him, to back him the way you do?"

He began now to pick at his teeth. "Well. Whatever the case, I've taken a shine to him myself. With Lord Nobunaga gone, mark my words, there's a general who'll rise in the world. A little trouble now, and a man could make himself the savior of the great lord's very life. This is a stone worth setting down on the board. And better yet, on the money side that great Kyoto merchant Chaya Shirōjirō stands behind it, so there's no fear there. Good. I'm decided. I'll get word to the men along the road at once. Hey, Kawarake! Out and meet him, as far as the Nono-tono pass."

From Sakai to Nara is thirteen ri by the Shigi crossing, and from there another eight ri of mountain road to Tarao. By now the night of the second had come down pitch black. The lower-ninja called Kawarake shot off into the dark.

Astride Mount Sasagatake, on the Iga–Kōga border, lie the village of Marubashira in Iga and the village of Shigaraki in Kōga. Far back in the Tenpyō era, immigrants naturalized out of the mainland settled here, and for the great shrines and the court they made plain unglazed earthenware: sake flasks, clay pots, kawarake cups and the like. In time it grew into a local trade, supplying the peasants' tableware, and it comes down to this day as Marubashira ware and Shigaraki ware. And so, in the Tarao house, the lower ninja who had no proper names of their own were named for the pottery: Kawarake, cup; Sarahei, plate-Hei; Heishi, flask; Tsubosuke, jar; Yukihira, a clay pot; Kanteki, a little stove; and the rest.

Word of the affair at Honnō-ji had reached Dōka from Azuchi by the afternoon of that same day. For Dōka's second son, a boy named Shinbei, was enrolled at the Azuchi seminary. His mother had been a beauty who turned heads in that country, and Shinbei had grown into a clever, handsome youth of seventeen. Dōka, who took a particular interest in the Christians, had placed him there, and it was from Shinbei that the news came. The young man who hid the sacred vessels on the hill at Oki-no-shima was this same Shinbei: a shrewd, tough article for all his fair looks, and that he came by honestly, from his father.

As he sent out the welcoming party, the lay-monk also sent runners to the Iga ninja of Ise, of Hattori, and of Tsuge, asking them to put on their proper black garb and come to meet the party at the Otogi Pass, on the Iga–Kōga line. Survivors overlooked in last year's Tenshō Iga War, and men who had been out of the province at the time and so escaped it, gathered on the instant, three hundred of them. For them it was a chance to get a first grip, a new field to work in, in a realm where their hated enemy Nobunaga was dead at last.

Before long Ieyasu and his men reached the Tarao house. Kōga and Iga were known the length of the land as the lairs of crooked ninja, and the party came through only because there was no other short road, and because Hattori Hanzō, himself of ninja stock, had given his word that with him along they were safe. For Ieyasu, and for his retainers too, it was the first time any of them had lodged under a ninja's roof. Once they were fairly across the threshold it was eerie after all, and even seasoned men of the battlefield traded glances and shifted where they sat.

Honda saw it and spoke up. "If Tarao Dōka means us any treachery, then thrash about as we may, there's no getting clear. We've had next to nothing to eat since we left Sakai at first light. Let's take the monk's hospitality, at the least, and put something in our bellies. Living or dying can come after that." At that they steadied their minds and eased. And Dōka, reading their mood as you would expect of him, had Hanzaemon's wife bring out his eldest son's grandchildren, a boy of six and a girl of four, and set them, of a purpose, beside Tadakatsu.

The two little ones, roused from sleep, at first only stared round-eyed. But Honda, thinking here was a fine hostage, played and played with them, until in no time they had taken to him and sat on his knee, beaming.

So for an hour or so they took their ease. With their bellies full at last, they retied their straw sandals and set off.

This time they set Ieyasu in a mountain palanquin, with Hanzō guarding its side. As they came, a ri or so on, to the Otogi Pass, jet-black figures closed in tight before and behind and fell into step with them. When, and out of where, no one could tell. A low voice would come. "The scouts from Azuchi are back." And Hanzō would answer, "Good. Report." Without a footfall they moved along. "Gamō Katahide, the keeper of Azuchi, has quit the castle," ran one, "taking Lord Nobunaga's wives and children, and made for his own seat at Hino." Reporters like this appeared without end, out of nowhere, and melted back into the dark. In the palanquin, lifting the blind now and then to watch, Ieyasu seemed struck to the heart by it.

By the time the party ran down the Otogi Pass and came to the bank of the Tsuge River, the night had broken clean into day.

There, at a short halt, Ieyasu saw a curious sight. Among the men in black were two whose build set them sharply apart from the rest. One was scooping up river-water in his cupped hand and giving the other to drink.

"Those two, who are they?" Ieyasu asked Hanzō, at the palanquin's side.

It was Dōka who answered, in Hanzō's place. "That, my lord, is Etegi of Ōyamada, with a boy called Karasumaru in his keeping."

"A boy? Where do you see a boy?"

"The one being given the water. He looks otherwise, but he is only thirteen."

"Oh? That, thirteen."

"Thirteen, and twenty-two kan in weight. Etegi there is twenty-four, and forty-one kan."

"Brothers?"

"No, my lord. Karasumaru is the orphan of one Kashiwagi Shirōbei, the commander when the Ikkō league held Toyama Castle. He was passed from hand to hand, and at the siege of Ishiyama the two of them fell in together. Ever since, being two of a kind, I daresay, they've stuck to each other and won't be parted."

"Hm. They say a ninja must shed the common flesh, that to make the body light he pares away every last ounce of it. Yet a man so fat is no hindrance?"

"No, my lord. With a frame like that there's no hope, in the end, of his making a good ninja. But the two of them are thoroughly good souls, and they set to the training so hard that one hasn't the heart to tell them to leave off. And besides, there's a use even in being fat. Last year, when Lord Nobunaga fell on us and burned all this country round without mercy, at the Momochi fort over there" — he pointed to a hill that rose away to the southeast — "the two of them all but burned to death. They missed their footing and went off a cliff. Two great fat men locked in each other's arms, sixty-three kan between them, went rolling and tumbling down and pitched clean into a muddy paddy far below, with nothing but their heads above the mud. And that, as luck would have it, was the very thing that saved their lives."

At this, headlong and desperate as the flight was, Ieyasu could not master the laughter that rose in him, and shook, doubling up with it.

"Splendid. Splendid! Make me a present of those two."

"Eh?"

"For a ninja's work they'd be no use at all —"

"Never mind that. Just let me have them. Agreed?"

"Why, of course, my lord."

"Then I'll take them along as they are. Hanzō. Put them under you."

"My lord."

"As you command."

Leaving Iga at Tsuge, they climbed into the Suzuka mountains and came to the Kabuto Pass, where in those days mountain-bandits nested in numbers. But the bandits heard that three hundred ninja were on the road and never showed themselves. By way of Kameyama, Kō, Yokota, and Noda, the party reached at last, as night came down, the beach of Shiroko. From Sakai to here was thirty-nine ri, most of it sheer mountain track. Had it not been ninja who forced the way through, no man could have got over it in two days.

Having come this far, they paid a handsome fee in thanks, took their leave of Dōka and his ninja, and put up at the house of a local merchant, Kadoya Kyūemon.

The wind stood fair, and they meant to sail at first light. As Hanzō went about the house ordering the boat and the bath made ready, his eye fell on a girl of sixteen or seventeen at work in the inner rooms. Her skin was a shade dark, but her eyes were wide and bright, and there was a life in the way she moved that seemed to wash the mind clean to watch. "Who is that?" he asked a maid. "The one daughter of the house," was the answer. "Her name is Hatsuko."

The road had been all strain since he left his own province, Kyoto and Osaka and Sakai and the crossing of Iga; and now, come this far at last, Hanzō wanted the girl, and wanted her badly. But he checked himself and thought: as I want her, so, surely, does the lord. Only, in the matter of women, Ieyasu was the sort who lusts in silence and will never say a word of it. So Hanzō went and had it out with the master, Kyūemon.

"When my lord is safe home in Mikawa, you'll come in for a handsome reward," he said. "But here's the difficulty. Among his retainers there is one who half suspects you of nursing some treachery. Send that girl to his bedchamber tonight. If she takes his fancy, then, over and above the reward, it will do you a further good turn." A humble merchant of the Ise-bay shore could hardly hold out against such words. Hatsuko was put in the bath, told to yield to whatever might be done and do as she was bidden, and sent in to Ieyasu's chamber.

For a girl of that day, all dealings with a man began in just this way, even after a lawful marriage. She was sent one night, without warning, to the room of a man she had never met, and there she must let herself be despoiled of the thing she had guarded and kept hidden till then as her most precious possession. Whatever her own nature, whatever thoughts of her own she held, none of it counted. There was no road open to her but to yield in silence.

Ieyasu was then forty-one. He was squat and barrel-built, thick-fleshed and well-larded, but hard-knit for all that, and never soft or flabby. No marvel of virility, perhaps, but of the enduring, long-lasting make: much as a wrinkled hide, stretched out, proves surprisingly long. He had thirty-five more years to live, so he had not yet spent two-thirds of his span.

Hatsuko was taken that night at this man's pleasure. Yet her nature was not for that changed, nor was she broken by it. Only, the man had happened to be, not some other, but Ieyasu, blown down to her from the Kabuto Pass; and that would draw her, from this time on, into all manner of things.

"The wind's fair! Aboard, quick, aboard with you!" At the shout Ieyasu was hauled awake while it was still pitch dark. He sprang up and made to say a word to Hatsuko, who had burrowed clean under the bedclothes, head and all. But he let it go, dressed in haste, and stepped out to the passage. And so Hanzō, who had wanted to read off the girl's face what his little contrivance had worked in her, went unsatisfied as well.

The wind was as fair as could be, out of the west. Standing straight east, they crossed Ise Bay, rounded the point of the Chita peninsula, and bore north. They ran the fifteen sea-ri without mishap, made land that same day at Ōhama in Mikawa, and on the seventh came into Okazaki.

The next day, the eighth, he gave out the marching-orders against Mitsuhide, chivvied his generals on, and on the fourteenth set out from Okazaki, pressing the six-ri road as far as Narumi. Etegi and Karasumaru were fitted out with belly-wrap and sword and spear, and though they had not the least idea how any of it was done, their faces shone with delight.

But on the sixteenth one of Tarao's lower ninja came hurrying up with the news of Yamazaki: Mitsuhide was beaten and dead, and there was no more call to march on the capital. So Ieyasu drew in his army and went back to Okazaki. And on the nineteenth a messenger came from Hideyoshi to tell him the affair was over, and there was no need to go up to Kyoto after all.

With that behind him, Ieyasu remembered Hatsuko of Shiroko, and, wishing to send for her, broached it to Hanzō. "The Shiroko merchant, Kadoya, was it?"

"Yes, my lord. Kadoya Kyūemon, he gave his name as."

So answered Hanzō, who had already caught the drift of his lord's feeling, and put on, of a purpose, a most solemn face.

"A shrewd old fellow, was he not."

"Yes, my lord, just so."

"Did you see him paid his thank-money?"

"Yes. In full."

And there it stopped. Ieyasu could carry the matter no further; the other's face was so very grave that it made the thing awkward.

Hanzō lost no time. He sent his genin Kabuto, a man from near the Kabuto Pass, to steal Hatsuko away. That done, he sent another retainer to wait on Kyūemon, hand over a fair sum in thanks, and tell him: "It is Lord Ieyasu's own wish, so have no fear." Hatsuko was brought to Hanzō's house in the castle town of Okazaki. The house was small, and the two great bulks of Etegi and Karasumaru were quartered in it; there was nowhere to put a Hatsuko he did not want seen. So, for want of anything better, he had a word with the abbot of Jiun-ji, an acquaintance, and got her lodged a while in the temple kitchen.

And that very night Hanzō stole into Hatsuko's bed. Startled, she fought him. But when she made out that it was Hanzō, the man who had brought her here, she gave over at once. "Ah. So that's the way of it. I thought I was to be the great lord's, that night; but I had it wrong, then, had I?" And once she was resigned to it, the sturdy, good-looking Hanzō was the better of the two. For Hanzō, besides, there was the added relish of neatly filching what belonged to his lord, and he lost himself in her.

Ieyasu had known little of the ninja till now. He had heard only the tale of how, at the Magari no Jin, the Kōga ninja had done great things. Hanzō, all innocence, came before him and proposed he take the ninja of Iga and Kōga into his pay. They would be sure to prove their worth.

The Magari no Jin was in the first year of Chōkyō, 1487. Rokkaku Takayori, the military governor of Ōmi, had been seizing at will the manors scattered across the province, those of the Enryaku-ji on Mount Hiei above all, and the estates of shrines and temples and court nobles, until some of the nobles were near starving and lawsuits came thick and fast. So the ninth Ashikaga shogun, Yoshihisa, resolved to lead a campaign against Ōmi in his own person, and in the ninth month took the field with three hundred commanders and ten thousand men. Takayori fled into Kōga, and with the backing of the Kōga warrior-league fought back. Advancing from Sakamoto, the shogun raised a new residence and camp at Magari, in the district of Kurita.

Within a plot a hundred and twenty ken north to south and a hundred and ten east to west, they dug a moat, marked out an inner bailey and a second and a third, and settled in for a long stay. But Yoshihisa sank into elegant idleness: poetry gatherings, the Kanze master fetched in to perform Noh, dog-hunts got up for sport, never a quiet day. The records of the time say that "as a rule he took no food at all, but only water and wine and wenching," or "he would touch nothing but water and wine, a wonder." And now weird fires broke out in the camp, again and again, and then night-raids, over and over, until the spirit of the army sank. In the third year of the encampment, at the end of the third month of Entoku 1, the shogun died in the field, twenty-five years old. Then a man in secret league with the Kōga fired the camp and fled; and the expedition ended, in the end, in failure.

In these strange fires, these night-raids, these treacheries from within, the middle-ninja of the so-called fifty-three houses of Kōga had a conspicuous hand, and at a single stroke made the name of Kōga ninjutsu ring through the realm. As the years went by, the work at the Magari no Jin grew in the telling. How one night a ball of fire came raining down out of the sky. How the several dozen men of the night-watch were every one seized by an irresistible drowsiness, and woke to find all but one of them had lost their heads. How the death of the shogun Yoshihisa was owing not to debauchery and hard living at all, but to a ninja's incantation. Every kind of such tale went round. With the fame of the Magari no Jin, the demand for the ninja of Kōga and Iga rose sharply. But there came tragedies with it: men hired in the hope of miraculous arts who, when the arts were not forthcoming, were cut down in a rage, or had their ears and noses sliced away and were driven off.

But war is a realistic business. Let the least breath of fancy creep in among the Sengoku warriors, and they came just so much the nearer to defeat. And so the worth of ninjutsu, against all the marvels dreamed up about it, was fast being cut down to its true size.

A thorough-going realist like Ieyasu had, if anything, rated the reality of it too low, and had taken small interest in ninjutsu; but the crossing of Iga made him look again. So, at Hanzō's urging, he was won over, and to begin with took a hundred or so into his service. And of this Hattori Hanzō it is worth knowing a little more. He gave his name to the Hanzō Gate we all know. He was the master of the Tokugawa spy service, the very service that became the model for the Special Higher Police, and for the whole apparatus and usage of the lock-up and the prison that we have so often had cause to rue.

***

Hanzō's given name was Masanari. His was a house of ninja for generations back, but in his father's day, for reasons of their own, they left their home country and took service with the Matsudaira; and it was the Matsudaira who in time became the Tokugawa.

Hanzō was such a hand with the spear that men nicknamed him Hanzō of the Spear. In Yotsuya he built a temple, the Saienji, and dedicated his favorite spear there. The spear stayed in the temple until the war-damage of recent years, they say, and many people saw it: a great spear whose blade alone ran to some five shaku, a thing there could be no thought of thrusting with, made rather to beat an enemy down. On the Sengoku battlefield such a weapon was for smashing straight down through armor and helmet together and hammering a man into the earth. That Hanzō swung a spear with a blade five shaku long shows he must have been a strapping, thick-thewed giant of a man.

That Hatsuko bowed so soon to her fate was, no doubt, because the man was this same Hanzō.

Now, Ieyasu had a legitimate heir, Nobuyasu, born to him by his lawful wife the Lady Tsukiyama; and out of this mother and son was brewed one of those grim tragedies so peculiar to the age of war. Nobuyasu was a brave and quick-witted young man. And yet, at a bare twenty-one, he was forced to take his own life by his own father, Ieyasu.

It was by Nobunaga's order. Nobunaga's eldest son was Nobutada, twenty-three that year. The one who might, in time, once Nobunaga himself was gone, grow into Nobutada's rival was Ieyasu's son Nobuyasu. Shibata Katsuie had no grown son of his own; neither had Hashiba Hideyoshi; and Akechi Mitsuhide's boy was still barely ten. Set Nobuyasu beside Nobutada, Nobunaga reckoned, and Nobuyasu was plainly the abler. Unless he were put out of the way, the Oda mastery of the realm might one day stand in danger.

Nobuyasu's wife, Toku, was a daughter of Nobunaga's by his lawful wife of the Ikoma house. The two were of an age, had been married at nine, and had two little girls already. Which is to say that Nobunaga had set his heart on killing his own daughter's husband.

As was the way of Sengoku warlords, Nobuyasu too kept a concubine, and from that the marital quarrels began. Toku wrote to her father at Gifu a bill of twelve articles blackening Nobuyasu. That he had shot a town dancing-girl dead with an arrow for dancing badly. That his mother, the Lady Tsukiyama, was of loose life. That there was talk of treason: she had sent for a Chinese from Takeda Katsuyori's country in Kōshū and was hatching a rebellion. And so on down the list.

Nobunaga at once sent to Hamamatsu, summoned Ieyasu's senior retainer Sakai Tadatsugu, and put him to the question. Tadatsugu was on bad terms with Nobuyasu and said not a word in his defense. And so Nobunaga demanded that Nobuyasu be made to cut his belly.

This was three and a half years after Nagashino. Ieyasu's power had grown a good deal, but his domain was still only the two provinces of Mikawa and Tōtōmi, while directly to the north lay Takeda Katsuyori's, spanning Kai, Suruga, and Shinano, and half of Kōzuke besides. With so strong an enemy at his shoulder, Ieyasu had no choice but to do as Nobunaga bade. And so, on the fifteenth of the ninth month of Tenshō 7, Nobuyasu cut his belly and made an end at Futamata Castle.

The inspecting officer that day was Amagata Yamashiro, and the second was Hattori Hanzō. Nobuyasu commended the future of his two daughters to the two men, gave keepsakes to the household of Ōkubo Tadayo, the lord of Futamata, and to others, chanted the nenbutsu, and drove the blade into his belly. "Now," he said. "The stroke." But Hanzō could not, for the life of him, bring himself to take his master's head. He burst out weeping and pitched forward on the ground. "Quick! Quick!" Nobuyasu urged him. And Yamashiro, unable to bear the sight, struck the head off.

The Saienji in Yotsuya, spoken of a moment ago, is the temple Hanzō raised to pray for the repose of this Nobuyasu.

The next year Nobunaga took Toku back, settled three thousand koku on her, and never after gave her to any man in marriage. Toku died at Kyoto, seventy-eight years old.

The Lady Tsukiyama had been named in Toku's indictment as an accomplice in the plot to rebel against Nobunaga in league with the Takeda; and ahead of Nobuyasu's death Ieyasu summoned her to Hamamatsu. She went by the Honzaka pass from Okazaki, crossed Lake Hamana by boat, passed through Irino into Lake Sanaru, and on the twenty-ninth of the eighth month left the boat at Koyabu. As she started up onto a rise some five hundred meters from the lakeshore, on the road to Hamamatsu Castle, she was cut down in her palanquin by Okamoto Heiuemon and Hattori Hanzō, on Ieyasu's order.

Hanzō, who had killed the mother, could not seventeen days later bring himself to cut down the son, and threw his sword away.

And what would have come of it, had Hanzō carried out both killings? Amagata Yamashiro did the beheading, and only because he could not bear to draw out Nobuyasu's agony with the blade in his belly; yet afterward he was seized with a dread of being known as the man who took his lord's son's head, and fled to live a masterless rōnin. Just so, had Hanzō seen the office of second through to the end, he must surely have fallen into the same plight, and had to flee.

As it was, the Lady Tsukiyama, lawful wife though she was, had long been estranged from Ieyasu and living apart, and with concubines by the dozen she was, to him, a needless thing. But Nobuyasu was a beloved son, on whom he had set his hopes. That Hanzō balked at cutting the boy down melted Ieyasu's heart. Had Hanzō not been a man risen out of the ninja, he could hardly have softened his lord so far. Either way, by this deed he became one who kept ever after close at Ieyasu's side, reading his mind. And it told, too, that he had had a hand in what became the great weak point of Ieyasu's whole life.

Now, as for that Hatsuko. Before even a month was out, she was fetched from Jiun-ji into Hanzō's house, and set the neighbors' ears pricking. When Hanzō was from home, gales of merry, squealing laughter would start up. That, no doubt, was why he had dared carry her off in the first place: he had foreseen well enough that even should it come out, no great blame would fall on him. For in spite of her nightly bouts with Hanzō, Hatsuko, brimful of young energy, would sing out, "Come on, a round of sumo!" and go charging into Etegi's thick, padded chest. She would bounce off that springy chest, sprawl head over heels, and shriek with glee. Women in those days wore no drawers; only at her monthly time did a woman put on a narrow loincloth. So this romping of Hatsuko's was, for Etegi, and for Karasumaru looking on from the side, wildly exciting, a state like dreaming inside a dream.

"There, come at me again!" And Hatsuko, hurling herself in, had to be owned a fairly formidable girl. Through all this stretch Ieyasu was desperately busy. In the seventh month he fought Hōjō Ujinao, who had seized on the turmoil to push into Kai, and only in the tenth month did he patch up a peace. It was the season for thin clothes, and the senses of Etegi and Karasumaru were thrown into a hopeless, ceaseless disorder.

Etegi had told Karasumaru about his own father: that he was one Kashiwagi Shirōbei, a samurai once in the Mōri's service, who turned rōnin and joined the Ikkō sect, and was set up as a commander of the league army holding out in Toyama Castle, and one night had his head taken by some unknown ninja; a very great man, whom all had loved like a father.

Karasumaru had one memory he could never lose: a man called Koichi, of Higashi-Yubune. In truth Koichi was a small-built man, but to the five-year-old Karasumaru he loomed huge, and that great warm back is the very first thing there is. Carried on that back, racing over hill and field for days on end: the clear feel of it lives still in Karasumaru's chest, and in his hands and feet. The six years that followed, until he was eleven, were a truly happy time. Then came the great castle of Ishiyama.

Koichi lived with them too. He had borne Karasumaru on his back from Etchū to Nagashima in Ise, and on again from there to Ishiyama Castle. But he was a very busy man, forever off somewhere and away.

That Koichi was always saying: "Young master. You grow up a great man, like your father. Your father, now, the things other men shrank from, he would take on himself and gladly, and however they used him he never once lost his temper. Other men's troubles he fretted over as if they were his own. His own affairs he'd set aside, and put himself out first for everyone else. Even to an unlettered lump like me he'd explain a thing gently, the way you feed a child a mouthful at a time. And that gentle man, when it came to a fight for the Law, would stand right out in the front, face any host however great, and battle like an Ashura. He was born a samurai, mind you, so fighting was his own trade. By that one man's strength alone, Toyama Castle held. And while Toyama Castle stood there firm, the peasants of Etchū and Kaga governed their own country themselves, and let no samurai lay a finger on them, and got their year's tribute brought right down besides. Then one night he was killed, by a wicked Iga ninja named Kashii, and the castle, its linchpin gone, came clattering down. A great man, he was. You grow up a great man like your father, young master."

Those words, little as he understood them, had stuck fast in the back of his ears. At Ishiyama Castle, Etegi drilled him patiently in the art of the night-road, the art of sensing a presence, the art of the false sound, the art of ordering the breath, and the rest.

Etegi himself, at better than forty kan, and a good soul beyond compare into the bargain, was fated never to make a master ninja. But the deadly earnest with which he set to the arts would freeze the grin on the face of anyone who had started out laughing at him.

Karasumaru now, at thirteen, weighed twenty-two kan, a big body even for a grown man. But look at his face. The wheat-brown skin under its soft down, the crisp double eyelids, the lips with their vivid color showing through, the full dimpled chin, the guileless light brimming in the kite-brown eyes: all of it belonged to a fresh young child, not yet roughened by the winds of the floating world. The backs of both hands were plumply swollen, with five little dimples sunk at the roots of the fingers. What heart, what gifts might be folded away in that breast and head, there was no telling; but he was the kind of boy the very sight of whom makes you wish him a happy life.

See a girl of that age with a face of rare charm, and toward her, as toward one promised a luck above the common, you can feel at least a mingling of reassurance and envy. But should her heart or her gifts give the lie to that face, you cannot help feeling instead a certain unease, a misgiving. And with a boy it is the same. In the eyes of those who looked on Karasumaru, too, there was something of this.

The author of this tale, that is, I myself, have two boys about me, a grandson of six who weighs thirty-four kilos and a nephew of eighteen who weighs a hundred and fifteen. And of course, by the time these children are grown, Japan too will surely have become a socialist country. But what fate they must pass through in the revolutionary years until then is a thing that nags at me and will not let me be. To picture this young giant, shackled day and night to a vast body that takes energy merely to shift about, turned inward and self-effacing from the habit of shame before other people's eyes, floundering through an upheaval that demands quickness and nimble movement: it is enough to make the heart ache.

But Karasumaru had been in the thick of upheaval from the day he was born. Three separate times he had known the misery of a castle's fall. He had watched, with his own eyes, tens of thousands of people killed. From the first dawning of sense he had had neither father nor mother, and had drifted, without cease, from place to place. And on top of that he had kept up the training of the ninja arts, in mind and body alike. Even if the gravity-defying leaping-arts were beyond him, in other things he had honed a taut, unsleeping art of the mind and of the body. He was in no case, then, to be merely floundering.

Who Hatsuko was, was a five-league fog to Karasumaru. What she might be to his master Hanzō he had not the least idea. But taking her, at all events, for one of the master's household, he did not cross her. "Karasumaru dear, let's play. Come on," Hatsuko was forever calling to him. To Hatsuko, this Karasumaru, huge of body yet in truth a child, was a strong attraction she could enjoy with an easy mind, and never a danger to fear.

Her body, moving on from sumo, took up the study of ninjutsu. She had strong, supple springs in her, and in the leaping-arts she was soon a match for Karasumaru himself. Tucking the skirts of her kimono up between her legs, she would spring nimbly into the air, and turn somersaults too.

Some readers may remember Tamo, now a beggar-woman past all knowing, as she had been at twelve or thirteen. In those days, working the cat-art among the "four-legged practices," Tamo would run up a zelkova in three bounds, flit along a branch near the crown, spin through the air, and come down without a sound, and leave Kashii, watching her, struck dumb.

Hatsuko was a bigger, better-fleshed girl than Tamo, and had by so much the more strength.

I have lately come to know Mr. Hatsumi Masaaki, the thirty-fourth master of the Togakure school, who lives at Noda, in Chiba. His teacher was one Takahashi Yokuō, the thirty-third master, of Kashiwara in Nara, who received the transmission in Meiji 40 from the thirty-second, Toda Shinryūken Masamitsu. This Shinryūken, in the Ansei years, when his father Toda Daisaburō Chikashige was living as a wandering monk, once watched his father go at a round pillar three shaku across in the main hall of a mountain temple. From some four ken off the father ran at it, ran up it in one rush, and near the ceiling, with a single cry of "Ei!," kicked off the ceiling with both feet, curled up like a cat as he fell, and landed on his feet with hands and feet flung wide. And Master Takahashi told how Shinryūken himself, saying "I cannot do what my father did, but—," ran at a great round pillar in a certain temple, went a ken and a half up it, and with one shout seated himself square on a ceiling beam better than two ken overhead, and sat there laughing. Takahashi saw it with his own eyes. He kicked the pillar with both feet; and no sooner did he seem to have caught the beam with both hands than he had whipped his body over, and with another shout turned a somersault down and landed on the floor in a low crouch. So the story goes; and this Takahashi told to Mr. Hatsumi.

Lately I too saw, on a television program called "Circuses of the World," a performer do something near to that art. By reason alone the thing is perfectly possible, and what Tamo and Hatsuko did was nothing else. Only, they did it in a flapping-skirted Japanese kimono.

Hatsuko made outstanding strides with the shuriken as well. Flinging twenty in a blur too quick for the eye, she could plant as many as eighteen of them in the black spot at the center of the target. And she showed a fine hand at the blow-dart, the art of holding a little tube between those red lips, blowing hard, and loosing the needle set inside it to pierce a man through.

One day Etegi brought himself to speak out. "Lady Hatsuko. Strive how you will, in the end a woman cannot become a ninja."

"Who decided that?" Hatsuko cocked an eyebrow.

"It has been so from of old."

"Why?"

"By what I had from my uncle Kasumi, a woman, train as she may, can never match a man. And a woman's feelings swing too wildly, and she cannot master the swinging. Above all when her heart is stolen away by a man, it comes to no earthly use."

"Be quiet. Hmph. And where is this uncle Kasumi of yours?"

"Last year, when Lord Nobunaga fell on us, he died at the Momochi fort in Iga."

"Mmm, a pity! Were he alive, I'd have made him see, once and for all, that not every woman is that woman."

"He can hardly have known the whole of Japan."

"But in Iga and in Kōga alike, from ancient times, there has never once been such a thing as a woman ninja."

"There, you see. And when you say there are none, you mean the ninja who take up the art as a trade, a way to feed themselves, and hire out to whoever will pay. I've no mind to sell my ninjutsu. I'll not sell the art. I'll use it as I please, for my own ends."

"That may be so, but—"

"I am a woman ninja!" So Hatsuko declared.

The reader must be told this. Down to the years before the recent war, a woman was held to be "a modest thing, in bondage to man." My own mother was so retiring a woman that even over the telephone her voice came out too small for the person at the other end to catch. Widowed at thirty-five, she raised the two of us, my younger brother and me. And my late father's stepmother, a foolish woman, kept up a ceaseless bullying of her daughter-in-law. However vicious the persecution she met, she never resisted it, and with that mild face of hers served the old woman faithfully to the last. When I was in my fifth year of primary school, I could bear it no longer, and once, meaning to kill that grandmother, chased her about the house brandishing a kitchen knife.

But the women of Japan down to the Sengoku age were not like that. The peasant women above all, who gave the same labor as the men, carried themselves just as the men did. It was the upper warrior class that first made a chattel of woman. Under Tokugawa feudalism this evil custom worked its way down to townsman and peasant, and the Meiji age took it over whole. So there is nothing to marvel at in Hatsuko, daughter of the Kadoya, a marine-goods wholesaler of the Ise-bay shore, having a spirit that would yield to no man.

Now, while Hatsuko is hard at her ninja training, let us turn our eyes back to the capital.

The Azuchi seminary had burned, so the whole company had removed to the Nanban-ji in Kyoto. But that stood in the crowded heart of the city, on cramped ground, so they moved again, to Takatsuki, about halfway between Kyoto and Osaka, and opened a seminary there. The lord of that castle was Takayama Ukon, Justo by his Christian name, the famous Christian daimyo, who had upward of thirty thousand vassals. The chapel grounds were ample and the buildings splendid. Those already there and those come over from Azuchi made thirty-two pupils boarding together. One of them, needless to say, was Shinbei, second son of the Kōga middle-ninja Tarao Dōka.

Two more had joined him: Ukon, third son of Taki Shirōbei of Aburahi in Kōga, nineteen; and Moriichirō, heir of Torii Kihachirō of Ōhara, nineteen.

In the annual report Fróis sent that year to the General of the Society of Jesus, this is set down: "Among the pupils of the seminary, some twelve or thirteen, of seventeen or eighteen years, are of very rare talent. Being of superior wit, these boys have made great progress, and several have shown, within a few months, the power to begin preaching; we are no little pleased that this foundation has been laid. In the teaching of the Catholic faith from its very roots, what a boy learns in three years, these will master in three or four months. Most of them write our script well." And so it runs on.

The sons of the Kōga ninja houses named above are important players. Before long they will act out a struggle to the death with our Ishikawa Goemon; so I must ask you, readers, to fix them in your memory.

Tarao Shinbei, as the exploit of saving the sacred vessels on Oki-no-shima shows, was a quick, handsome youth: the youngest of the three, and yet rather the one who led. Taki Ukon, set against Shinbei's flashing quickness, was reserved and by no means quick-witted. But he was the sort to store up all manner of data and build a conclusion by piling one fact on another, so that his conclusions often ran wide of other men's slapdash, hunch-bred ones, and yet, in the end, it was his that hit the mark. The third, Torii Moriichirō, though the eldest, was rather flighty, and quick to take fire. His complexion was a sallow yellow, his eyes dull and clouded, his nose cocked at the ceiling, his flesh thick all over. And for all that, his nature was the most sinister of the three, with a bent toward cruelty.

The whites of his sunken eyes showed a queasy yellow. His was the longest face of the three, and his cheekbones sharp. The forefinger of his right hand was gone from the root, snapped off, they said, when he was small, in a trap set for foxes.

The three had three faces of their own, and yet a common stamp lay somewhere among them, very unlike the stamp of the lower ninja of Iga. The Iga men, barring such exceptions as Ishikawa Goemon, had small triangular skulls, faces the color of tanned paper, and altogether a plain and paltry set of features. These Kōga men ran, in the main, to large features deeply cut, and big, well-fleshed bodies.

And where does the difference come from?

Along the borders of Kōga, Iga, and Ise the Suzuka range humps up, running east to west: Nasugahara, Aburahi-dake, Iwao-yama, Sasagatake. But even at its loftiest, Mount Gozaisho, it is no more than twelve hundred meters above the sea, and south of the Suzuka pass, about Tarao at the far southern end, it stands around six hundred. From these mountains bunched in the southeast the land tilts sharply off to the northwest, and slopes gently down through the Ōmi plain to Lake Biwa. So, set beside Iga, all mountains and not one great river, the soil here was rich, and Ōmi rice had early won the highest name in the land. And thus, in the Sengoku age, when the manorial system had begun to crumble, a great many local magnates and land-samurai sprang up; and as the power of the Ashikaga shogunate waned, theirs grew, until even a campaign led by the shogun in person could do nothing at all.

Of these local magnates and land-samurai, those who lived in the mountain country near the Iga border took up ninjutsu, and so became the ninja houses.

By the research of Mr. Okuse Heishichirō, Kōga was once part of Iga, and split off only by degrees, through administrative changes, between the close of the Kamakura period and the Sengoku age. Indeed, the lord of Iga at the end of the Kamakura period was one Kōga Saburō Kaneie, who held the two lands as one. His grave lies in the precincts of the Aikuni Shrine at Iga-Ueno.

So the ninjutsu of this region was, to begin with, a single thing, and divided in two only at the last, along with the changes of administration.

While ninjutsu was still in use in real war, and still growing, its secrets were its very lifeblood, and it passed entirely by word of mouth. Only later, in Enpō 4, 1676, did Fujibayashi Samuji, a descendant of Fujibayashi Nagato-no-kami, first gather up the traditions he had heard handed down and compile the twenty-two volumes of the Bansenshūkai. At nearly the same time, in Kishū, one Masatake, styled Fujiissui, drew the oral lore of the Kishū school together into the Shōninki. That such manuals of secret lore came to be written down at all proves just one thing: that by then, the time of the fourth Tokugawa shogun, Ietsuna, ninjutsu had already drawn far from any practical use.

Of this Bansenshūkai there are, by Mr. Yamaguchi Masayuki's count, seven manuscript copies in the whole country. The one held in the Cabinet Library was presented to the Tokugawa shogunate in Kansei 1, 1789, by three Kōga ninja houses: Ōhara Kazuma of Tadono, Ueno Hachizaemon of Aburahi, and Ichirō Oki-no-kami of Kitawaki. The records of the Ōhara house make this clear.

Copies are held besides, they say, by the Ōhara house of Kōga, the Taki house of Iga-Ueno and the Okimori house there, the Fujita house of Tokyo, the Ōsawa house of Sakai, and the Sawamura house of Mie. The one I borrowed from the Iga-Ueno city library was, I suspect, copied from the Okimori family's, for it matched theirs exactly. Now, in the Cabinet Library copy and the other Kōga copies, Fujibayashi Samuji bears the title "Recluse of Kōga," while the Iga copies give him no title at all. Yet that Fujibayashi Samuji was an Iga man is plain from his grave and the rest, and the Fujibayashi name, common in Iga, is all but unknown in Kōga. Whether you call it the Iga school or the Kōga school, in truth it was one and the same art. All eleven masters of the craft named in the book are Iga men, moreover, and the book declares that "the shinobi take Iga for their root." There can be no doubt the Bansenshūkai is an Iga-school manual, written by an Iga hand; and the "Recluse of Kōga" set before the author's name was, one may guess, a contrivance of Ōhara Kazuma and the rest, when they presented the book to the shogunate.

And again, Kōga was pierced by the Tōkaidō, which crosses the Suzuka and runs on by Minakuchi and Kusatsu to Kyoto, so the traffic of people was brisk, and it was not, like Iga, a lean-soiled, shut-away country. For all these reasons the ninja of Kōga were mostly of the middle rank, unlike the Iga pattern of a few upper shinobi over a host of lower ones. Already in the days of the Magari no Jin, history shows plainly, the fifty-three houses of Kōga, local magnates and ninja houses both, had seized the manors of the shogunal house, the court, the nobles, and the great monasteries around Kyoto, and, each throwing up its own fort, lorded it over the country.

These were the houses: Uchiki, who live now at Ishibe, and Hattori, likewise of Ishibe; Miyajima of Mikumo, and Mikumo, and Hari; Natsumi of Natsumi; Ukai of Iwane, and Aoki, and Iwane; Ban of Bantani, and Ueyama, and Nakayama, and Hatta; Yamanaka of Kashiwagi, and Uda; Minobe of Minakuchi, and Shinjō; Ayukawa of Ayukawa, and Kurokawa; Ōno of Ōno, and Akutagawa, and Tongū; Tsuchiyama of Tsuchiyama, and Hirako, and Ōkawara, and Ueda, and Torii, and Ōkubo; Ōhara of Aburahi, and Noda, and Yamanaka; Takano of Ōhara, and Wada, and Takamine, and Ueno, and Taki; Mochizuki of Miya, and Ōhara; Katsuragi of Terashō; Saga of Sayama, and Oki, and Kanda; Saji of Saji; Iwamuro of Sayama; Kuraji of Minamisoma, and Sugitani; Takayama of Kitasoma, and Yamakami; Makimura of Kumoi; Nagano of Nagano; Ogawa of Ohara, and Sugiyama; Tarao of Tarao; and Sasayama of Kōka. The Iga forts were mountain strongholds. These, by contrast, were built out in the middle of the flat land, ringed round with forest and banked with earthworks.

Of them all, I have seen the Mochizuki house that stands to this day at Ryūhōshi, in Kōnan-chō. The Mochizuki, among the fifty-three, were of the rank called the Ten Shinobi of Kōga. To the eye it is an ordinary big farmhouse. But within, it runs to three stories, with hidden rooms, spy-windows, concealed ladders, trap-doors, escape-hatches, the traces of an underground passage, alarm-rattles: a very marvel of intricate cunning. The descendants keep, today, the Ōmi Pharmaceutical Company, and make and sell a stomach-medicine called Ninjutsu-gan, and others besides.

But whether one thinks of the Nijō house in Kyoto or of this Mochizuki house, was there really any need to lavish such strange ingenuity on a private dwelling?

Once the enemy has broken right into your house, surely the fight is already lost. And so a suspicion takes shape: that these devices were not for ninjutsu at all, but had to do with the Kirishitan. Take this Mochizuki house. Its whole floor area is fifty-two tsubo, yet the hidden third story of the main building is a great room of better than thirty. There is a window there, made not to be seen from outside, a bare shaku and five sun high and some five shaku wide, but fitted with heavy iron bars and stretched over with wire mesh. Part of the floor is a trap-door, and the mouth of the hidden ladder, kept hoisted up out of the way, is strictly concealed on the first floor and the second alike.

I cannot help thinking that the whole purpose of the building was to hold secret meetings, and, at need, to give a safe way out in a moment of danger. The more I turn it over, the stronger the suspicion grows. The house as it stands is, of course, not of the Sengoku age, but likely of the mid-Tokugawa period or after. And since it is plain the ninja drew close to the Kirishitan precisely to lay hands on gunpowder and the other secret Nanban arts and instruments, it may well be a case of the mummy-hunter turned mummy himself.

To win the rulers' favor and be granted the freedom to preach, the Christian missionaries sent home for all manner of rare treasures and presented them; and among these were muskets and cannon, powder and shot. The Kōga ninja were wild to get their hands on such things before anyone else.

The three who had entered the Takatsuki seminary on just such a secret errand could not, whether they liked it or not, do without the help of one ugly woman. Tamo had become a kitchen-hand there, and had come along from Kyoto. The towering Padre Organtino, who had shown her such kindness at the Nanban-ji, did not come to this place; and the one man she would open her mouth to at all, shut fast as she was as a clam-shell, was a single one.

He had a ruined left eye, and a right so dim he was all but blind, a little fellow under five shaku, forever in a filthy, baggy black cotton cassock, mud-spattered to the very seat of it, whether from a bad gait or a bad road. When he went abroad he drew a kite-brown hood over a head shaved bare at the crown. He knew Japan through and through, every cove and corner of it, and whenever he had an idle hour he would tell Tamo of it. Ugly little man though he was, everyone called him Saint Lourenço, or Irmão Lourenço, and held him in reverence. Tamo too called him "your holiness."

"Tamo. Have you ever seen a thing called a biwa?"

"A biwa?"

"Never seen one? Good. I'll show you." He rummaged in the storeroom cupboard, drew out an old instrument like a great wooden rice-paddle, laid it slantwise across his knee, and plucked it with a plectrum shaped like a ginkgo leaf. Beeen, beeen. A sound that seemed to drag the soul down into the depths of the earth came flowing out into the dim, stagnant air. Then all at once he worked the plectrum fiercely up and down in small quick strokes, jararan, jararan, and set the notes tangling and leaping and dancing.

His figure as he played was a strange one indeed.

"I, now, Tamo, I used to stand at people's doorways and play this biwa, and beg my bread. Born in Hizen, I was, so I begged my way from one end of Kyūshū to the other. Blind in the left eye from my birth, and the right one as though a mist lay always over it. My father and my mother went off somewhere, the two of them, and were gone. From a child I was a beggar. I followed a biwa hōshi about as his boy, and learned the biwa; and when that hōshi died, he left me this biwa for a keepsake, and gave me the name Ryōsai."

"When that hōshi died, you say? Aye, I grieved. To be alone in all this wide world, just one soul, all by itself, there is no sorrow like it. You are alone too, Tamo, are you not?"

"Since the ninth month of last year, yes. Truly, to be all alone is a sorrowful thing."

"In my twenty-fifth year, I ceased to be alone."

"You took a wife?"

"No, no. By then I had crossed the sea and gone to Yamaguchi in Suō, and there I met his holiness Xavier. And ever since I have been two, I and almighty Deus. Two months on I received bautismo, baptism, and was given the Christian name Lourenço. You too, Tamo. Learn well, and be alone no more."

Tamo cast down her eyes, sorrowful, and went away. She was lonely.

Tamo was all alone.

And yet, within her, the memory of Kashii grew and grew, until it had settled into the very center of her breast and left no crack for "almighty Deus" to enter. In the flesh she was alone. In her mind she was not.

When she went to the kitchen, one of the Kōga three, Torii Moriichirō, was drawing water from the jar with a ladle and drinking. He caught sight of Tamo, threw her a sidelong look out of those sunken, yellow-whited eyes, and clicked his tongue. Tamo was afraid of him.

The pupils of this seminary were all young, yet full of a grave restraint, and lively and yet graceful. Only this Torii was otherwise.

"Tamo must have been a great beauty once. Nay, a beauty even now, seen from the right side. The right side is a thing of paraíso, of paradise; the left, a thing of inferno, of hell. When the whole of her was paraíso, how the men must have swarmed to court her. I should have liked to meet the Tamo of those days myself." He said it quietly, with a straight, serious face. But Tamo felt a freezing mockery in it. And, as she always did, she bent low, made her little bob of a bow, and slipped past. Torii saw her retreating figure off with cold eyes, clicked his tongue once more, and betook himself to the chapel, where he sat down at the very back of the confession-gathering then in progress, a congregation of some fifty.

The padre, helped along by Lourenço at his side, was preaching in his uncertain Japanese.

"Even as a physician, to heal a sick man, cures the root of the sickness and teaches him the way of health, that it come upon him no more, even so, when the anima, the soul, of any sinner is stained with the filth of his faults, there is no cleansing that filth of evil but by confession. Deus has given to his Son, Lord Jesus Christ, the office of sifting all the good and evil of mankind, and of dealing out reward and punishment both; and Christ bestowed this power upon his disciples. Therefore, whosoever shall make confession, and repent his sins from the depths, and firmly resolve to commit no fault whatsoever from this day on, for him Deus forgives, wholly, every fault that he has done, and grants him graça, his grace, besides. I am the very deputy of Deus; so that if, for any sin, out of shame or out of fear, you keep it hidden, you do but deceive Lord Jesus Christ, and heap fault upon fault, and all your confessions thereafter are made void. Let him who can confess before all the people confess; and he who cannot, let him confess to the padre alone."

On the instant, a middle-aged man flung up both hands, half-rose to his feet, and cried out: "I will make my confession! I am a man of Sakai, a maker of gunpowder for muskets. I have dealt with the Dutch pirates. I rowed out to meet their ships off Saiga in Kishū, sold them the sulfur that makes the powder, and bought of them muskets, and shot, and firearms. I knew well that, being pirates, they would use these to sink ships and kill men and plunder; and yet for four years together I kept up such dealings. And more. The muskets and shot and firearms I got in exchange, I sold at high prices to the daimyo of every quarter. Only a little while since, I sold ten muskets to Lord Tokugawa Ieyasu himself, when he came to Sakai."

And crying it out, he broke into great, manly sobs.

The padre's face went red with feeling. He stretched both hands high toward heaven and offered up a loud prayer, and the old hands of the congregation joined their voices to his: "This is my fault. This is my grievous fault. Wherefore, calling upon the Virgin Santa Maria and all the saints, I humbly beg them to plead my cause before Deus. And from this day on, holding fast to the intent of this confession, I will strive only to do good, and firmly purpose never to fall into sin again. Amen." So it seemed this was the prayer that must always be said after a confession.

All were deeply moved. Yet no one else, after him, made to rise before the crowd. And so, leaving seven who would confess to the padre in private, the gathering broke up.

Torii set off at once after the Sakai gunpowder-seller.

Now, however, we must leave that pursuit where it stands, and here turn time back, and come round once more to the place where the novel The Stealthy Ones came to its close. For the moment has arrived to set down how our hero, Ishikawa Goemon, has lived through these new waves of history in the barely a year since then.
