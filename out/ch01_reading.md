## Chapter 1. "The Gun" and "The Knife": Terror and Repression Breed a Special Organization

In the modern world, no organ is more dangerous or more shrouded in mystery than the intelligence and security service. America's CIA, the Soviet Union's KGB, Britain's MI5, Israel's Mossad: these are the perennial objects of the world's curiosity.

And China's intelligence and security services?

When Chinese people bring up this trade, their first reaction is a startled cry: "<i>Tewu</i>!"

This is because the Nationalists' Juntong agents had so fearsome a name. Out of power, the Communist Party denounced the Nationalists for running a "rule by secret police"; in power, it made "catching secret agents" a central task of consolidating its hold. And so this word, <i>tewu</i>, came to seem a term of abuse.

But definitions are always a matter of form; it is the real thing that has substance. To talk about secret agents, one should first find China's earliest "secret-service" organization.

### 1. Modern China's First "Secret Service" Organization

Search for the "secret service," search high and low, and you find it not among the Nationalists but among the Communists. On the record, in China's intelligence and security world, the first appearance of the word <i>tewu</i>, and the first organization actually called <i>tewu</i>, both trace back to the Chinese Communist Party Central Military Commission's "Special Work Section" of May 1927.

Let us look at Zhou Enlai's calendar for 1927.

On March 21, the CCP launched the third armed uprising of Shanghai's workers, with Zhou Enlai as commander-in-chief.

On April 12, Chiang Kai-shek engineered a coup and struck at the workers' pickets; Zhou Enlai, going in person to negotiate, was seized by Nationalist troops and got free only through the efforts of comrades.

In the second half of May, Zhou slipped away to Wuhan, attended a meeting of the Standing Committee of the CCP Central Political Bureau, was appointed head of the Central Military Department, and in the same period set up the Military Commission's "Special Work Section."

On July 26, Zhou hurried to Jiujiang in Jiangxi to launch the Nanchang Uprising of August 1.

In the first and middle ten days of October, Zhou led the uprising's forces in a fighting march into Guangdong, met with reverses, lost contact with the main body, fell gravely ill, and reached Hong Kong by small boat.

In the first ten days of November, Zhou reached Shanghai and attended an enlarged meeting of the provisional Central Political Bureau. At year's end, he founded with his own hands the intelligence and security organization known as the Central Special Branch.

The year 1927 was, for the young Chinese Communist Party, a blow struck full in the face. The Nationalists' massacres taught the Communists a lesson: in China, with no arms of your own, you have no right to live. At the August 7 Conference, Mao Zedong spoke a line that shook the world: "Political power grows out of the barrel of a gun!"

The CCP's "gun" was Zhou Enlai's charge, as head of the Central Military Department. Even as he threw himself into military work, Zhou was secretly building an intelligence and security system as well. In the CCP's own language, the army was the "gun" and the security apparatus the "knife": Zhou Enlai raised a gun in one hand and gripped a knife in the other!

Open military work and secret intelligence-and-security work, the one in the light and the other in the dark, were both matters of the first importance to survival.

So it turns out that modern China's first intelligence and security organ was hacked into being by the party in power!

The newly formed Central Military Commission was a lean body: Zhou Enlai its secretary, Wang Yifei its secretary-general, Nie Rongzhen its chief of staff. Its offices stood at No. 12 Yujili in Hankou, a <i>shikumen</i> house of three rooms above and three below with two wings: the organization section in the lower west wing, the secretariat in the upper west wing, Zhou Enlai working in the upstairs parlor; the special-service section was right next door in the east wing, under Gu Shunzhang.

The Special Work Section was divided into four subsections.

The intelligence subsection was to gather military and political intelligence and follow the enemy's movements. Its chief was Dong Xingwu, nicknamed Fatty Dong, at the time the commander of the Nationalist government's detective brigade for the three towns of Wuhan. Intelligence work was easy in those days: Wang Jingwei's Wuhan government was still cooperating with the Communists, and even the Wuhan police chief, Wu Defeng, was a Party member.

The security subsection was to protect the central organs and the Soviet advisory group; its chief was Li Jianru. When Borodin, head of the Soviet advisers, was forced to return home, the security subsection detailed thirty men to see him off, and once the job was done they stayed on in the Soviet Union to study security work.

The special-operations subsection was to punish traitors and spies and to handle special tasks assigned by the Center; its chief was Li Qiang, with only a handful of members, Cai Fei, Chen Liansheng, Wang Zhuqiao, and a few others. This action unit had once put to death a British spy who tried to get close to the Soviet advisory group, and had wounded a mole planted inside the group, Eugene Pick.

The bandit-liaison subsection had the task of bringing armed bandit bands over to the Party; its chief was a man they called Huzi, "Whiskers."

In its earliest days the Special Work Section was intensely busy, and it turned up no small amount of important intelligence.

A young fellow named Yang Gongsu, sent out to scout, confirmed that Xia Douyin's troops were massing at Yichang. And so, before Xia Douyin raised his Yichang mutiny and marched on Wuhan on May 17, the Communists had already made ready. When Xu Kexiang mutinied at Changsha on May 21, and when Wang Jingwei broke with the Communists at Wuhan on July 14, the Special Work Section got word of it in good time. During the Nanchang Uprising the section also worked closely with the troops to tear up the Guangzhou-Hankou Railway, and it secretly delivered the rifles of the departing Soviet advisers to the 24th Division, commanded by Ye Ting. Through merchants it even ran a little financial speculation, trading in treasury bonds, banknotes, and silver dollars, to earn secret operating funds for the Center.

This was modern China's earliest "secret-service" organization!

Why did the Communists set up a secret-service organization before the Nationalists did? Because the CCP had long been outlawed, working underground, and had to guard its own safety with the greatest care.

From the very day of its founding the CCP took security work seriously; one might say that "where there is a Party, there is intelligence and security work." The First National Congress of July 1921 met under strict secrecy, was interrupted mid-session by a patrolman's inspection, and had to shift from the streets of Shanghai to a pleasure boat on South Lake at Jiaxing, in Zhejiang. The Party Program adopted at that First Congress laid it down: "While the Party is in a state of secrecy, its important positions and the identities of its members must be kept secret." In the First United Front of 1924, many members' identities became public: Yang Yin, of the Guangdong District Committee, served as an adviser to the Guangzhou municipal police, investigated the assassination of Liao Zhongkai, and in 1926 took charge of security for the Nationalists' Second National Congress, choosing men from the Guangzhou workers' pickets and the Nanhai peasant army to form a security battalion. The great strikes the CCP led — on the Beijing-Hankou Railway, at the Anyuan mines, at the five Kailuan collieries, the Canton-Hong Kong strike — all set up their pickets, inspection squads, reconnaissance squads, investigation squads, and other security bodies; and the peasant movements in Hubei, Hunan, and elsewhere set up peasant self-defense corps far and wide to keep order in the countryside.

Still, these early security measures and security bodies had not yet grown into a tight working system. In its underground period the CCP had called this, among themselves, "secret work." Later, once there were rural base areas — the "Soviet areas," meaning the Soviet-held regions — security work set up open organs, and the work in the areas outside came to be called "White-area work" or "urban work." In 1925 the CCP Center picked three men, Gu Shunzhang, Chen Geng, and Lu Liu, and sent them to the Soviet Union to study intelligence and security work in earnest, in preparation for founding an intelligence and security system of the Party's own. Not until the Central Military Commission's "Special Work Section" was formed in 1927 was there a dedicated organ.

The word <i>tewu</i> is plainly a modern coinage. In the <i>Ci Yuan</i>, the dictionary of classical words, the term <i>tewu</i> is simply not to be found. The nearest word in meaning is <i>jiandie</i>, "spy," glossed as "to scout the enemy's affairs in secret." For an example, the <i>Records of the Grand Historian</i> says of the general Li Mu's campaigns that he "trained in riding and archery, kept careful watch by the beacon-fires, made much use of spies, and treated his soldiers generously." There is another word too, <i>jianren</i>, meaning "scout, spy"; and Sun Tzu's <i>Art of War</i> has its chapter "On the Use of Spies."

The 1979 edition of the <i>Ci Hai</i> defines <i>tewu</i> this way: "A person who joins a special-service organization, or accepts a task from a special-service organ, to carry out counter-revolutionary activities such as spying out intelligence, covert injury, sabotage, and the spreading of rumor and incitement." This definition hands the <i>tewu</i> over wholly to the enemy. In truth the name <i>tewu</i> carried no shameful sense at first. In January 1937 the Northwest Political Security Bureau issued its "Points for Special-Service Personnel," which called a leader's bodyguard a "special-service man" — a thoroughly honorable post!

The 1990 <i>Grand Dictionary of Language</i> gives four senses of <i>tewu</i>, including the English meanings: "1. (special task or duties) In an army, one charged with special duties such as guard or transport, as in special-duty man, special-duty company, special-duty battalion. 2. (special agent) Personnel engaged in espionage. 3. (spy) A spy: personnel that one hostile side sends into the other to gather intelligence and carry out sabotage. 4. (stooge) A subversive agent who serves one government against another." This definition makes <i>tewu</i> a neutral word, covering our side and the enemy's alike, and every kind of agent. It does leave out one reconnaissance role in the military category, though: an army's special-duty company always contains a reconnaissance platoon.

A 1996 book, <i>China's Secret Agents</i>, held that all these definitions were unscientific, and redefined the term: "Personnel who, for the benefit of a state, a class, or a political group, and on the appointment of an organization or a leader, quietly carry on intelligence work, or operational work such as arrest, assassination, and sabotage, and other secret work to safeguard the interests of their own country, class, or political group." The business of a secret agent, it said, comprises intelligence work, operational work, turning-and-subversion, and psychological warfare.

It seems this <i>tewu</i> really is "special" enough: however you define it, no definition comes out quite rigorous or quite complete.

So why did both sides in China, Nationalist and Communist, come to call their own intelligence and security organs "special service"? In the Soviet Union, an "All-Russia Extraordinary Commission" was set up in 1917 and renamed the "State Political Security Bureau" in 1922, whose organ was known for short as the GPU. In America, the Justice Department set up a Bureau of Investigation in 1907, enlarged in 1924 into the "Federal Bureau of Investigation." Neither in the Soviet Union nor in America did the government's intelligence and security organs use the name "special service."

Military intelligence organs, by contrast, have always been called "the Second." The U.S. Army's intelligence bureau is abbreviated Army G-2, the Air Force's A-2. In 1906 the Qing government set up a "Military Consultative Office" with powers like those of a general staff, and under it seven bureaus, the "Second Bureau" charged specially with foreign intelligence and espionage. The Nationalists had their "Second Bureau of the General Staff Headquarters," the CCP Central Military Commission likewise had a "Second Bureau," and the general staff too had a "Second Department." The senior man is always the operations department; for the intelligence department to rank second is distinction enough. The "special-duty companies" and "special-duty battalions" that take on special tasks in an army include reconnaissance platoons, and those belong to this "Second Department."

Seen this way, the name "special service" that the Chinese Communists first gave their intelligence and security organ in fact came out of military organization. It is worth noting that the Japanese army kept a dedicated "Special Service Department" whose very duty was intelligence and security work. The Chinese, learning modern science and technology, often took their translations of Western terms secondhand from the Japanese; and the name <i>tewu</i> may well have reached them by that route.

China's earliest secret-service organization in fact lasted only three months. After Wang Jingwei broke with the Communists at Wuhan, the CCP Center moved to Shanghai, and the Military Commission's "Special Work Section" wound up its work in August 1927. Yet the closing of this "section" did not mean the end of "special work." Before long the CCP set up another secret-service organization, at a higher level still.
### 2. The Central Special Branch

Within the CCP's intelligence and security system, the name "Central Special Branch" rings loud.

The Central Special Branch was founded in Shanghai, the birthplace of the Chinese Communist Party. Shanghai set no end of Chinese records: the largest city, the widest foreign concessions, the greatest press of citizens — and the most tangled, most ferocious spy war of all! The National Government's Songhu Garrison Command and the Shanghai police; the patrol houses and paid informers of the British and French concessions; the Green Gang and Red Gang of the lane-ways and the wharves; the Comintern's intelligence stations and the CCP Special Branch's dog-beating squad — Shanghai was, by common consent the world over, an "adventurers' paradise."

When the First United Front broke apart and the Communists went underground once more, the central organs moved back from Guangdong to Shanghai. In November 1927 Zhou Enlai returned to Shanghai from the East River front and was elected to the Standing Committee of the provisional Central Political Bureau. At his proposal, the Central Special Branch was set up in Shanghai that same month. In October 1928 the CCP Center also formed a three-man Central Special Committee headed by Zhou Enlai — the other two members being Xiang Zhongfa, the general secretary, and Gu Shunzhang, the Special Branch's working head — to direct the Branch's work at first hand.

If the earlier Military Commission "Special Work Section" had been a short-lived, makeshift body, this "Central Special Branch" was now a professional CCP intelligence and security organ.

The Nationalists set up a secret-service organization of their own only a step behind, a matter of a little time between them.

In February 1928, three months after the founding of the Central Special Branch, the Nationalists' Central Organization Department set up an Investigation Section under Chen Lifu, dedicated to hunting down and killing Communists. In the summer of 1930 the Investigation Section added a "special-service group" within it, aimed squarely at CCP activity.

In March 1932, Whampoa cadets close to Chiang Kai-shek, modeling themselves on Italy's Fascists and Germany's Brown Shirts, organized a secret society in China, the "Chinese Nation Revival Society"; its members dressed in blue, and it was also called the "Blue Shirts Society." Chiang, who took pains to cultivate his intimates and chose the closest of the close, set up within the Revival Society a special "Special Services Department." That department was made up of ten Whampoa men, with Dai Li as its head. Its tasks were intelligence, turning-and-subversion, and operations — precisely the standard functions of a secret service. April 1, the day it was founded, later became the "April 1 anniversary" of the Nationalist secret-service organization Juntong; every year on that day Chiang Kai-shek would attend the commemoration in person.

And so the Nationalists too had an organization called a "special service." As it happened, both of China's parties, Nationalist and Communist, gave their intelligence and security organs the very same name: "special service."

Small and half-formed as these early secret-service bodies were, they were the forerunners of the vast organizations that would later shape China's politics. The "Investigation Section" grew into the notorious "Zhongtong" — the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Nationalist Central Organization Department. The Revival Society's "Special Services Department" expanded into the notorious "Juntong" — the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Nationalist Central Military Affairs Commission. Among the Nationalists' huge apparatus of army, police, gendarmes, and agents, this "Zhongtong" of the Party wing and "Juntong" of the military wing were always the core secret-service organizations.

The CCP "Special Branch" grew by stages, setting up four sections in turn. The First Section, general affairs, under Hong Yangsheng — earlier called "headquarters" — saw to the guarding of the central organs and to other business. The Second Section, intelligence, under Chen Geng, was to penetrate the enemy's detective organs and secure intelligence. The Third Section, the "Red Squad" (the dog-beating squad), had as its main task the armed protection of the organs and the punishing of traitors and moles, under the squad leaders Cai Fei and Tan Zhongyu. The Fourth Section, radio, was to build secret wireless communications, under Li Qiang and Chen Shouchang.

An important task of the general-affairs section was to safeguard central meetings. The CCP Center of those days ran high with revolutionary zeal; outlawed though it was, it still had to convene national congresses often. The 1930 preparatory conference for the National Congress of Soviets, and the Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee in 1931, each drew several dozen. The general-affairs section would first rent a building inside a foreign concession and move its own people in as cover, then lodge the delegates, dozens of them, separately in hotels. During a session, plainclothes Red Squad men cycled patrol outside as lookouts, a "wife" played cards downstairs as a lookout, and general-affairs "servants" waited on the rooms above. The delegates met on the upper floor; at the first sign of trouble the outer ring would raise the alarm and hold off the intruders while the delegates slipped away by a hidden passage. The general-affairs section was also charged with rescuing arrested comrades. With the CCP's covert backing, a public body was formed under the chairmanship of Song Qingling — the "China Mutual Aid Society" — to rescue arrested comrades in the open, through legal channels. Ren Bishi, a member of the Central Political Bureau, was arrested twice, and both times the Special Branch bought off the concession police and got him out. In August 1929, when the Central Military Commission members Peng Pai and Yang Yin were arrested, Zhou Enlai planned in person to storm the execution ground. The enemy took Peng Pai and Yang Yin off to Longhua for execution, and the Red Squad, disguised as a film crew, moved to intercept them on the road. But the route was heavily guarded, the weapons came late, and at the last critical moment the empty-handed Red Squad could only watch the prison van roll past, with no way to strike. After Luo Yinong went to his death, the Special Branch braved the danger to gather up and bury his body, and even raised a gravestone under the assumed name "Tomb of the Gentleman Luo Siwei."

The Second Section, in charge of intelligence, was pure "special-service" work. Its men — Chen Geng, Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, Hu Di, Pan Hannian, Chen Yangshan, Ouyang Xin, Liu Ding, Li Yuchao, and the rest — were prodigies of CCP intelligence. The section chief, Chen Geng, was one of the "Three Heroes of Whampoa," had saved Chiang Kai-shek's life during the Eastern Expedition, and had studied security work in the Soviet Union. The Second Section cultivated one important intelligence connection, Bao Junfu. When the Nationalists first set up their Investigation Section in Nanjing and looked to build a secret-service organization in Shanghai, the special agent they chose to post there, Bao Junfu, happened to be a close friend of the CCP Special Branch's Chen Yangshan! And so the very Nationalist agent system meant to ferret out the CCP central organs in Shanghai was in fact in Communist hands. When the Special Branch rescued Ren Bishi, it was through Bao Junfu that the money reached the concession police. When Guan Xiangying was arrested in the French Concession and his top-secret handwritten papers fell into enemy hands, the French, who could not read Chinese, asked Bao Junfu to find an "expert examiner" — whereupon the Special Branch's own Liu Ding was put in charge of examining Guan Xiangying's documents and books. Liu Ding quietly recovered the secret papers, Bao Junfu handed the French an assessment certifying that the man's books had nothing to do with politics, and Guan Xiangying walked safely out of jail. The Special Branch's secret connections reached into every quarter. Yang Du — one of the "Six Gentlemen of the Chou'an Society" who had backed Yuan Shikai's bid for the throne — also joined the Communist Party in secret, with Zhou Enlai's approval; he worked hard to save Li Dazhao, and supplied intelligence more than once.

The Third Section, the Red Squad, was the action arm: a good twenty-odd stout fellows who braved life and death on the Shanghai streets. When Luo Yinong was betrayed by the turncoat couple He Jiaxing and He Zhihua and went bravely to his death, the Red Squad swore to avenge him and hunted the pair across the city; the two had only just gone to ground on Bushi Road when the Red Squad came to their door the next day and dealt out its reckoning. Bai Xin, the turncoat who had sold out Peng Pai, had once served as secretary of the Central Military Commission and knew a great deal of the inner workings, and the Center ordered the Red Squad to eliminate him. But Bai Xin knew the patterns of CCP secret work and dodged the Red Squad's assassins time and again. The Special Branch laid careful plans, Chen Geng and Bao Junfu scouted the ground themselves, and just as Bai Xin was setting out to leave Shanghai, the Red Squad shot him dead at the very door of the police inspector Fan Zhengbo's house!

The Fourth Section's radio work was set up and built under Zhou Enlai's own hand. In the past the CCP had passed intelligence mainly by post, through mails the Nationalists controlled and censored — most unreliable. Sending letters by dedicated secret couriers still meant running the gauntlet of police searches, and the risk stayed high. So the most secret letters had to be memorized by the courier and recited out again on arrival. Crossing mountains and rivers, slipping through the enemy's blockade, this primitive method often took months to complete a single exchange — far too slow. By the lessons of secret work abroad, the surest and quickest means of contact was still the wireless. Yet a radio set, that piece of modern equipment, was hard for the CCP to lay hands on. In October 1928 the CCP Center resolved to build its own wireless. Zhou Enlai assigned Li Qiang to assemble a set himself, sent Zhang Shenchuan to sit the entrance exam for the Nationalists' radio school, and picked Tu Zuochao and three others to study radio technology at the Frunze Military Communications School in the Soviet Union. In the winter of 1929 Li Qiang assembled the set in the British Concession in Shanghai; in early 1930 Tu Zuochao came home to help, and Li Qiang carried the set into Kowloon and reached Shanghai from Hong Kong — the CCP's first long-range wireless link, and the quiet birth of its first radio station. In September 1930 Zhou Enlai personally arranged a training class in Shanghai to train radio cadres for the Soviet areas and the Red Army units. A group of people from around the country gathered to study in secret at the Fuli Electric Company's works in Shanghai. The concession police, finding this factory rather odd, raided it and arrested five instructors and fifteen trainees. Those who escaped arrest — Li Qiang, Mao Qihua, Wu Yunfu, Zeng San, Tu Zuochao — scattered at once and carried on training radio operators.

This "Central Special Branch," founded in November 1927, marked the birth of the CCP's central intelligence and security system.

In summing up the history of the CCP's founding, one usually says that Mao Zedong opened up the rural base areas and created the Red Army. Then one ought to add that Zhou Enlai opened up the urban secret work and created intelligence and security work.

In the spring of 1928 Zhou Enlai ran a training class himself, drilling Special Branch personnel for more than twenty days. He also laid down the Special Branch's rule of "three tasks and one prohibition": to do intelligence, to punish traitors, and to carry out every kind of special task including fund-raising — and never to spy on one another within the Party. Zhou also compiled the CCP's first cipher himself, the "Hao code," with Deng Yingchao serving as the cipher clerk.

Secret though the Special Branch was, it still needed firewalls. Zhou Enlai set up other, parallel secret bodies whose people never crossed with the Special Branch's. The Central Liaison Bureau handled the secret conveyance of documents, intelligence, supplies, and personnel; the Central Military Commission's own intelligence organ scouted chiefly for military intelligence.

The CCP had run internal courier work from its founding. In 1927, when the Special Branch was created, it also created an internal courier section and an external courier section, and built three secret courier lines out to the Party organizations around the country. At the end of 1928 it brought in Wu Defeng, a member since 1924, to build in full the secret courier links between the central organs in Shanghai and the base areas. Wu Defeng, a master of secret work, put together a courier network in two months; in 1929 it carried out 5,523 central documents and brought in 4,687 reports from around the country. In October 1930 it was raised into a Liaison Bureau directly under the Center. Its director, Wu Defeng, threw everything into opening the routes to the Central Soviet Area in Jiangxi, laying three lines — east, west, and south. The Liaison Bureau not only carried secret mail but was also charged with escorting cadres and moving supplies and funds.

The whole Party was outlawed, and it was not only the intelligence and security system that needed secrecy. Zhou Enlai, working with Li Weihan, Ren Bishi, and Deng Xiaoping, drafted "Central Circular No. 47: On the Consolidation, Development, and Secret Work of Party Organizations under the White Terror," which fixed the guiding line and the methods of secret work. Every CCP organization in the White areas took to underground, clandestine methods. Even in the Red base areas the Party organizations were not made public, and the Party membership of men in the army was kept secret too.

Given the founding and the record of the Central Special Branch and the other secret bodies, one may say that the CCP's intelligence and security work began from a high starting point.

A Nationalist publication, "The Theory and Practice of Special-Service Work," assessed them this way: "Though they had no experience to speak of, yet because the right men were in charge, and in a spirit of study and invention, they laid out a whole plan and, by measured stages, pressed forward with their feet on solid ground. In less than three years they achieved astonishing growth and singular results. Standing on objective ground, even we cannot but admire their fighting spirit!"

For all its systematic organization and outstanding record, the Central Special Branch, this newly founded intelligence and security body, was still relatively green. One had only to look at the man it chose as its first working head, Gu Shunzhang, to see a fatal flaw. Then again, when had Xu Enzeng, the man in charge of the agents on the Nationalist side, ever been free of fatal flaws of his own?

Soon enough, the intelligence and security organs of the two sides would get their chance to cross swords head-on...
### 3. Showdown at the Summit

Both sides in China's life-and-death struggle, Nationalist and Communist, took up intelligence and security work early enough. Perhaps it came of the Chinese gift for "using spies"; perhaps it was borrowed from the international experience of America and the Soviet Union; but more important than either was the plain need of the struggle itself.

In the early 1930s the focus of the two sides' intelligence and security struggle was the CCP central organs. The CCP Center had operated all along right under the enemy's nose, with no legal right to exist, protected entirely by the Special Branch. And the Nationalists, holding the power of the state, threw in both their open military police and their secret agents to crack the CCP Center open.

The chance to rewrite history appeared out of nowhere.

In January 1931 the CCP Center held the Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee in secret in Shanghai; Wang Ming, back from Moscow, entered the central leadership with the backing of the Comintern representative Mif. Divisions within the Party sharpened by the day, secrecy grew slack, and Lin Yunan, Li Qiushi, and others were arrested. In April, Gu Shunzhang, escorting Zhang Guotao to the E-Yu-Wan Soviet, gave himself away by performing magic in public in Wuhan, was arrested on the 24th, and defected that same night.

Gu Shunzhang had been bold and fierce from boyhood, a student of the martial arts; in the May Thirtieth Movement he had charged in without flinching, and after joining the Party he trained the workers' pickets. Back from studying security work in the Soviet Union, he became one of the CCP's earliest security specialists, serving as bodyguard to the Soviet adviser Borodin and as commander of the workers' pickets in Shanghai's third armed uprising. Gu Shunzhang could kill a man silently with his bare hands, was a master of disguise, of shadowing and of shaking off a shadow, and stood second to none among China's agents as an operations specialist. In 1927, when right deviation was under criticism and the Comintern pressed the CCP to give more weight to worker stock in its reorganization, Gu Shunzhang began to move into the central core: member of the CCP Central Political Bureau, member of the Central Special Work Committee, and working head of the Central Special Branch. Gu was quick and capable indeed — on stage he could perform magic, off stage he could work hypnosis — but he was also dissolute and unbridled, forever calling friends together, gambling and whoring, and breaking Party discipline on the pretext that an agent's work demanded it. In May 1930 Zhou Enlai sent Nie Rongzhen into the Special Branch expressly to keep a political check on Gu Shunzhang.

And just as Zhou Enlai was weighing whether to replace the Special Branch's leadership, Gu Shunzhang came to grief in Wuhan!

Gu Shunzhang had long run the Special Branch's day-to-day work and knew every one of its people; his own relatives all lived in the central "live-in stations" and held many secret addresses; if Gu once opened his mouth, the CCP central organs would be swept up in a single net! But Gu Shunzhang, scheming for his own future, meant to hold out for a price: he would surrender all his intelligence only if he could meet Chiang Kai-shek face to face. And the Wuhan Field Headquarters detective office that had caught him, itching to claim the credit, was in a hurry to report straight to the Investigation Section. So secret telegrams went out, one after another, to Xu Enzeng at the Central Party Headquarters in Nanjing.

Right at the elbow of Xu Enzeng, head of the Nationalists' highest secret-service organ, three intelligence officers of the Communist Party's Central Special Branch lay concealed. In 1928 Chen Lifu had told his cousin Xu Enzeng to open a radio training class and enlarge the Investigation Section's agent system. Getting wind of it, the CCP Special Branch sent the Party members Qian Zhuangfei, Li Kenong, and Hu Di to sit its entrance exam and form a secret Party cell. Zhou Enlai arranged for the three to conceal their identities and bore deep into the vital points. All three, brilliant students, took important posts in turn: Li Kenong held the headquarters of the Radio Administration, Hu Di was moved to Tianjin to hold the northern organ, and Qian Zhuangfei actually became the confidential secretary of Xu Enzeng, the head of the Nationalists' agents!

Li Kenong was born in 1899 in Chao County, Anhui. His father held a post in the Wuhu customs house, and the family was comfortably off. Chen Duxiu and Hu Shi, the leading men of the New Culture Movement, were both Anhui men, and Li Kenong, while at school in Wuhu, threw himself into the progressive student movement, and was arrested in 1919 in a struggle against taxes. Together with a band of May Fourth stalwarts from Anqing, and at the CCP's commission, Li Kenong founded the Minsheng Middle School, which became a base of the revolutionary movement in Anhui. In 1926 Li Kenong joined the Chinese Communist Party. When the Northern Expedition reached Anhui, the Communist Li Kenong served as propaganda chief of the Nationalist county party in Wuhu. In Shanghai, Chiang Kai-shek joined with the Green Gang to crush the Communist-led labor unions, and at the same time put the Wuhu Green Gang boss in as police chief to fight the left for power; the CCP's Wuhu Special Branch assigned Li Kenong to penetrate the Green Gang. When Chiang launched his coup in Shanghai on April 12, 1927, and wired the Wuhu Green Gang boss on the 14th, Li Kenong secretly reported it to the CCP Wuhu Special Branch that very night. By the time the counter-revolutionary coup broke out in Wuhu on the 18th, most of the revolutionary forces had already been moved to safety. A wanted man, Li Kenong perfected in Wuhu the art of transformation — now a headmaster, now a vagrant, now a teacher, now an army officer — fencing cleverly with the enemy, and so began his career in secret work.

In early 1928 Li Kenong moved to Shanghai, and in the winter of the following year he, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di sat the exam for Xu Enzeng's radio school and penetrated the Nationalist secret-service organs. This three-man cell obtained the Nationalist agents' core secrets and supplied vital intelligence for protecting the underground organizations and the Red Army in the base areas.

Now the Communist Special Branch's own head had surrendered to the Nationalists, and Communists lay hidden at the very elbow of the head of the Nationalist agents... the first summit duel in the history of modern Chinese spy warfare!

In a single night, Wuhan sent six urgent telegrams one after another to Nanjing, and every one fell into Qian Zhuangfei's hands. Xu Enzeng, off at a brothel, had let his authority slip away — he had even given Qian Zhuangfei the codebook to keep. Qian Zhuangfei at once sent his own son-in-law, Liu Qifu, to Shanghai to warn Li Kenong, and Li Kenong scoured the city for his handler, Chen Geng...

The turncoat Gu Shunzhang, arriving in Nanjing by boat from Wuhan, set the Nationalist agents rushing upon the CCP central organs in Shanghai — only to find empty rooms. Zhou Enlai, half a step ahead, had had the entire central apparatus moved!

After they had won power across the country, the CCP's central leaders, looking back on the perils of half a lifetime, all said the same thing: that was the Center's most dangerous hour!

Small players had rewritten great history.

The three who worked this marvel — Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, Hu Di — were called by the CCP intelligence world the "Three Heroes of Longtan"! Li Kenong held that it was the fruit of the Special Branch's turn from security to intelligence, and on to counter-reconnaissance.

This heart-stopping bout of secret struggle laid bare the state of both sides' secret-service organizations at that moment. The CCP Special Branch, thanks to the higher caliber of its team, escaped catastrophe, yet it too took a heavy blow and could not shake off the defensive. Gu Shunzhang took his revenge like a madman: he fingered Yun Daiying in a Shanghai jail, went to Guangzhou to seize Cai Hesen, and the CCP general secretary Xiang Zhongfa was arrested and defected.

Zhou Enlai moved decisively to reorganize the Special Branch: of the intelligence connections planted inside the enemy, some were arrested and some withdrawn, and every Special Branch cadre Gu Shunzhang knew was moved. Here the firewalls set up in advance proved their worth: the Liaison Bureau saw to moving the cadres, and cadres from the Military Commission and the Jiangsu Provincial Committee were brought in to take the Special Branch over.

Chen Yun, a member of the Central Political Bureau, took over as head of the Special Branch, and Zhao Rong — Kang Sheng — was moved in as second-in-charge. Chen Yun also held the First Section's general affairs, Pan Hannian ran the Second Section's intelligence, and Kang Sheng also oversaw the Third Section's Red Squad. In early 1932 Chen Yun was transferred to be Party-group secretary of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions, and Kang Sheng took the Special Branch over; before long Kang Sheng moved to the provisional Central Organization Department, and the Special Branch fell to Pan Hannian once more. From then on three nicknames went around the CCP intelligence world: "the Teacher," "the Boss," and "the Young Master."

Battered as it was, the Central Special Branch carried on in hardship and reordered its work.

The Second Section's old intelligence connection, Bao Junfu, could no longer be used, so it contrived to penetrate the Songhu Garrison Command. When the CCP general secretary Xiang Zhongfa was arrested on June 22, 1931, the Special Branch's Ouyang Xin used his connections to steal the whole confession out of the Garrison Command, and Huang Mulan cross-checked it through connections of her own: Xiang Zhongfa had indeed defected.

In February 1932 a "Notice of Wu Hao and Others Leaving the Communist Party" appeared in all the major Shanghai papers. At that moment Zhou Enlai, whose code name was "Wu Hao," was on the road to the Central Soviet Area in Jiangxi; the notice was plainly the Nationalist agents' work, a stratagem to sow division. Through its connections the First Section placed, in the Shen Bao of March 4, an "Urgent Notice from the Lawyer Bahe on Behalf of Zhou Shaoshan," refuting the rumor on Zhou Enlai's behalf.

Xu Qiang, Li Yun, and others newly moved into the Special Branch went to Henan to build up military work, and obtained the Nationalists' operational plan for the fourth "encirclement and suppression" of the Central Soviet Area, aiding the Soviet's counter-campaign.

Though the Special Branch did all it could to recover its strength, the situation in Shanghai grew ever graver, and the CCP central organs and the Comintern's Far Eastern Bureau were forced to suspend operations. In January 1933 Bo Gu and the rest of the provisional Central Political Bureau leadership left Shanghai for the Central Soviet Area in Jiangxi under Mao Zedong, and the Special Branch's head, Pan Hannian, went with them. The Special Branch left behind in Shanghai came under the Shanghai Central Bureau, in Li Zhusheng's charge, and worked mainly on military intelligence. In February the Nationalist secret service set up a special "Shanghai Action Zone," and the Special Branch's Red Squad promptly shot its head, Ma Shaowu, dead. The Special Branch's leadership changed hands often: in May Wu Hujing, from the old Military Commission intelligence system, was brought in, and in October Liu Zihua after him. The new leaders knew military-intelligence work; Wang Shiying sent Xie Fusheng expressly to work the intelligence connection at the Wuhan Field Headquarters, obtaining the Nationalists' intelligence on the "encirclement" of the E-Yu-Wan base and helping the 25th Red Army break out to safety.

Because of the Center's "Left" leadership, and with experienced intelligence cadres withdrawing one after another, the Special Branch's work too began to fail. On June 26, 1934, the secretary of the Shanghai Central Bureau, Li Zhusheng, was arrested and defected, and the Shanghai Central Bureau and the Jiangsu Provincial Committee were broken up six times over. The Special Branch's Red Squad fought back to the death and put down the traitors in the open, but exposed the organization in doing so, and most of its members were arrested. When the Red Squad's Wang Deming, Kuang Hui'an, Ou Zhiguang, and others were taken to the Longhua execution ground and the authorities brought in a clergyman to pray over them and talk them round, the brave men lifted their heads and sang "The Internationale," and went to their deaths unbowed. In September 1935 the surviving Special Branch members mostly moved to Tianjin, where Wang Shiying led them in the work of the North China Liaison Bureau under the Northern Bureau. On November 19, the head of the Special Branch's Shanghai office, Qiu Jifu, was abducted, and the Shanghai Special Branch lost contact with the Center.

This was a wretched season of defeat in CCP history. It was not the Central Special Branch alone that took heavy blows: the leading organs of the Party, the Youth League, and the unions in Shanghai were smashed on a large scale, over and over, until the Party organization in Shanghai was all but wrecked.
### 4. The State Political Security Bureau

And just as the Shanghai Special Branch was passing through its ordeal, over in Jiangxi all was flourishing.

The Autumn Harvest Uprising forces led by Mao Zedong and the Nanchang Uprising forces led by Zhu De joined up at Jinggangshan and opened a large base area in the borderlands of Hunan and Jiangxi. The Jinggangshan base straddled 28 counties across the three provinces of Jiangxi, Fujian, and Zhejiang, held 15 county towns, covered more than fifty thousand square kilometers, counted a population of more than two and a half million, and fielded a Red Army of more than forty thousand men and guns. The CCP Center resolved to make this, the largest of the Soviet areas, the Central Soviet Area. On November 7, 1931, the anniversary of the October Revolution, the First National Congress of the Chinese Soviets opened at Yeping in Ruijin, Jiangxi; the congress resolved to found the Chinese Soviet Republic and elected Mao Zedong its chairman. On ground of its own the Chinese Communist Party had set up a government of its own, to stand as the Nationalists' equal!

This Chinese Soviet Republic adopted the political system of a modern state: a procuratorate, courts, an interior ministry, a judicial department — a fairly complete legal order.

One department was out of the ordinary: the State Political Security Bureau.

The Outline Constitution of the Chinese Soviet Republic provided: "The State Political Security Bureau is a part of the Soviet organization, a special organ of the Soviet's special organization. Under the leadership of the government, this organ carries on open and secret struggle against all military, political, and economic counter-revolution, and is an organ for the defense of Soviet power." The Organizational Outline of the State Political Security Bureau of the Chinese Soviet Republic provided that the Bureau, "under the jurisdiction of the Council of People's Commissars of the provisional central government, shall carry out the tasks of reconnaissance, suppression, and elimination of all counter-revolutionary organizational activity, and of detection and banditry." "The State Political Security Bureau, its branch bureaus, and its special commissioners represent state power in investigating, taking up, and disposing of all counter-revolutionary cases." Which is to say: ordinary criminal and civil cases fell to the other judicial departments, while counter-revolutionary cases came under the Political Security Bureau alone.

The CCP's intelligence and security work had always been secret work before; this "State Political Security Bureau" was an open organ. In the past, when people spoke of "legal struggle" and "illegal struggle," the law in question was always, when all was said and done, the Nationalists' law. From now on the Communists' own republic had promulgated laws of its own, and would govern in the open and by law.

The chief struggle the new regime faced was the struggle of revolution against counter-revolution, and so this State Political Security Bureau, a "special organ of a special organization," held very great power indeed. The Bureau's reconnaissance branch had full-time investigators who inspected the people and goods passing in and out of the Soviet areas, and it sent intelligence officers deep into enemy country besides. There was a special "work net" too, by which the Bureau planted eyes and ears in every Party, government, army, and mass organization. The Bureau's executive branch handled arrest, detention, and preliminary interrogation. The Bureau also had an armed force directly under it, the "Political Security Guard," charged with the safety of the Party, government, and army organs and their chiefs; the bodyguards of the Party Center's, the government's, and the army's leaders were all "special-service men" assigned by the Bureau, and the men, horses, sidearms, and sabers issued to leading cadres at every level were all fixed by the Bureau. The Bureau's organizing principle was to be "the embodiment of complete centralization": "The relations between the higher and lower levels of the State Political Security Bureau, barring special obstacles, form a consistently vertical system, in which the lower absolutely obeys the orders of the higher." The Bureau kept branch organs or special commissioners in the local governments and the Red Army units, and no local government or Red Army command had the power to alter or halt its orders.

The Bureau's members ranked high too: even a leader's bodyguard, a private's post, was filled by a cadre specially transferred in. The Guard had the best weapons as well, both rifles and sidearms. The Red Army's officers and men wore red collar tabs; the Bureau wore green tabs with a red border. On his chest a Bureau man also wore a silver badge bearing three foreign letters, "GBW." In the plain ranks of the Red Army, the security men, trim in bearing and singular in dress, stood out like a crane among chickens! A saying went around inside the Bureau: "The Party is iron discipline; the Bureau is steel discipline." So great was the Bureau's power that, in special circumstances, it could arrest military and government leaders of its own rank. Chen Fusheng, special commissioner of the Fifth Red Division, once arrested and shot a division chief of staff who tried to flee. Xiao Chi, chief special commissioner of the Red Army's medical school, stopped an escape plot got up by the school's principal, Chen Yihou.

In scale and in power alike, this State Political Security Bureau plainly far outstripped the "Special Branch" that Zhou Enlai had founded. Study the Bureau's system and its powers and you see it copied the Soviet Union almost whole. Not only were its principal cadres trained in the Soviet Union — even those foreign letters on the badge were the Russian letters for "GPU"!

This State Political Security Bureau had a prehistory of its own too.

As an ancient country of long history, China had always had organs that performed similar functions; the Yuan dynasty's "Bingma Si" was the equivalent of a modern police bureau. But the CCP of those days had no intention of inheriting the political institutions of the feudal age. The founding of the CCP's security organs plainly took its cue from the experience of the Soviet revolution. On August 1, 1927, the CCP and the Nationalist left jointly formed a Revolutionary Committee and launched the Nanchang Uprising. Under the Revolutionary Committee and the uprising army's command a Political Security Office was set up, with Li Lisan, a member of the CCP Front Committee, as its head. On December 11 the CCP launched the Guangzhou Uprising, and the Guangzhou Soviet government set up a Committee for the Elimination of Counter-revolutionaries. On March 10, 1928, the CCP Center issued a circular requiring that Soviet governments, wherever established, set up such a committee beneath them. The CCP's Sixth Congress, held in Moscow on July 10, adopted the "Resolution on the Organization of Soviet Power," proposing that town and country alike in the revolutionary base areas set up suppression and security organs. On April 5, 1929, the Fourth Red Army under Mao Zedong set up a security section within its political department. On April 15, southern Jiangxi's first county government, the Xingguo Revolutionary Committee, set up a suppression committee and a judicial department beneath it. In January 1931 a Central Political Security Office was formed, with Wang Jiaxiang, director of the Red Army's General Political Department, doubling as its head, and only five members. Before long Deng Fa took Wang Jiaxiang's place as head.

This Central Political Security Office evolved, in November of that year, into the "State Political Security Bureau." In November 1931 the First All-Soviet Congress established the state organs of the Chinese Soviet Republic, and under the Central Executive Committee it placed the "State Political Security Bureau."

Deng Fa, who had once gone to the Soviet Union to study security work, became the Bureau's first director. A Guangdong man of worker stock, Deng Fa had served as an orderly in a police bureau, had joined the anti-imperialist marches of the "May Fourth Movement," and had led workers' pickets in the 1922 Canton-Hong Kong strike. He took part in the Northern Expedition in 1926, and in the 1927 Guangzhou Uprising served as deputy commander of the Fifth District's Red Guards. After the uprising failed he went to Hong Kong to build a Special Branch organization and served as secretary of the CCP Hong Kong Municipal Committee. In 1931 he entered the Jiangxi Soviet, and served for a time as secretary of the Fujian-Guangdong-Jiangxi Special Committee.

Under the State Political Security Bureau were a reconnaissance department, an executive department, a Red Army work department, a White-area work department, and the Political Security Guard. Those who served in turn as department heads included Ouyang Yi, Zhang Ranhe, Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, Li Yimang, Wang Jinxiang, and Tan Zhengwen; those who served in turn as commanders and commissars of the Political Security Guard included Li Yutang, Wu Lie, Hai Jingzhou, Ma Zhulin, Zhuo Xiong, and Tan Zhenlin.

The State Security Bureau's reach covered every system, Party, government, and army. During the fourth counter-encirclement campaign, a bodyguard of Zhu De, the Red Army's commander-in-chief, suddenly died of poisoning. The State Security Bureau at once sent an investigator in as a groom to get close to the people around Zhu De, and quickly cracked the case. The Red Army had captured an army doctor from a Nationalist unit, and this rare and needed talent was at once assigned to serve at headquarters. But the man was a Nationalist agent, and he secretly poisoned the boiled water Zhu De drank. As it happened, Zhu De's bodyguard, being thirsty, drank the commander-in-chief's water first. Zhu De's life was charmed, and he escaped assassination. But the Bureau grew warier for it, and tightened its guard work.

In the past, CCP work had fallen into two great systems, the Soviet areas and the White areas. Security and intelligence work in the White areas was run by the Special Branch, and in the Soviet areas by the suppression committees. After the State Political Security Bureau was founded, it took charge of intelligence and security work in both, and the Soviet areas across the country reorganized their suppression committees into political security bureaus, branch bureaus, and offices, all subordinate to the State Political Security Bureau.

The old "Central Special Branch" had still been a secret organization working underground; this "State Political Security Bureau" marked the turn of the CCP's intelligence and security work to being carried on in the form of state power.
### 5. Purging Counter-revolutionaries in the Soviet Areas

"Suppression" and "security": different words for the same task. The newly founded State Political Security Bureau brought the security bureaus and the suppression committees at every level under one organization, and gave one leadership to security work and suppression work alike.

The suppression campaigns in the Soviet areas are a thoroughly tangled matter as well. Three great "Left" deviations have appeared in the history of China's public security: the "suppression" campaigns in the Soviet areas during the Second Revolutionary Civil War; the "Rescue Campaign" at Yan'an during the War of Resistance against Japan; and the political campaigns after the founding of the People's Republic, the "Cultural Revolution" above all!

Just as no great historical event is ever an isolated thing, these three great errors in the history of China's public security are bound up with one another. Was the "Cultural Revolution" foreshadowed by the "Rescue Campaign"? Was the "Rescue Campaign" sourced in the "Soviet-area suppression"?

The Jiangxi Soviet, first to begin its suppression, began with a purge of the "AB Corps," and rose to its height with the "Futian Incident."

In June 1930 the CCP's Southwest Jiangxi Special Committee, acting on an informer's tip, arrested a committee cadre named Zhu Jiahao, and Zhu Jiahao in turn gave up a batch of members of a reactionary organization, the "AB Corps." As more were arrested and interrogated, the "AB Corps" grew with the catching, and by September more than a thousand had been purged out of the thirty-odd thousand Communists in southwest Jiangxi! On October 4 the Red Army took Ji'an, and among the enemy documents seized they found an "AB Corps" badge, and a receipt written to a landlord by the father of Li Wenlin, secretary of the Jiangxi Action Committee. The General Front Committee was aghast, and on the 14th its secretary, Mao Zedong, wrote to the Party Center to report that a great many "AB Corps" elements had been found in southwest Jiangxi.

Just then the Nationalists launched their first "encirclement" of the Jiangxi Soviet; Mao Zedong and Zhu De bent all their attention to directing the fighting, and the power to conduct the suppression was devolved entirely. Li Shaojiu, secretary-general of the First Front Army's General Front Committee and head of its suppression committee, drove the purge hard, weeding more than 4,400 "AB Corps" out of a Red Army of over forty thousand! In December, on the General Front Committee's order, Li Shaojiu led a security detachment to Futian, seat of the Jiangxi Action Committee, and that very day arrested the provincial action committee secretary Li Wenlin, the 20th Red Army commander Zeng Gulin, and six other main leaders, eight in all. Li Shaojiu extracted confessions under savage torture, seizing more than 120 people and executing more than 40 in five days. Then Li Shaojiu led his detachment on to the 20th Red Army's station at Donggu to carry the purge there. Liu Di, political commissar of the 174th Regiment stationed at Donggu, made common cause with the army political department director Xie Hanchang, raised the army headquarters' own independent battalion in revolt, arrested the 20th Red Army commander Liu Tiechao and Li Shaojiu, and freed the prisoners. This was the "Futian Incident" that shook the Soviet areas!

The leaders of the Futian Incident marched their men out and raised the slogan: "Down with Mao Zedong, up with Zhu, Peng, and Huang!" They also sent letters, separately, to Zhu De, Peng Dehuai, and Huang Gonglüe, claiming that Mao Zedong had arranged for others to forge evidence against the three of them, meaning to do them harm. Peng Dehuai recognized these as forgeries done in imitation of Mao Zedong's hand, and issued a declaration in support of Mao Zedong as secretary of the General Front Committee. The Red Army's General Front Committee issued a proclamation of its own.

The suppression had provoked the Futian Incident, and the Futian Incident set off a fiercer suppression still. The handling of the Futian Incident swung back and forth, cooling and heating, until at last every cadre of the 20th Army from the commander down to the deputy platoon leaders was arrested, its soldiers were parceled out among other units, and its designation was struck off; a great many cadres were executed. The suppression spread swiftly everywhere, and cadres in the Soviet areas went in fear for their lives, every one. Chen Yi, commander and commissar of the 22nd Red Army, was marked out by Li Shaojiu too; leaving home for a meeting, he told his wife, Xiao Juying: "I am going to a meeting. If I am not back by six this afternoon, go home to the village and hide." Ambushed by bandits on the road back, he was delayed, and Chen Yi did not reach home until eight — to find that his wife had already thrown herself down a well and killed herself!

The unit that carried out the suppression was the Political Security Bureau. Under the relevant rules the Bureau had, in principle, no power to kill. "It may arrest counter-revolutionary suspects directly"; "the power to sentence and to carry out sentence on counter-revolutionary criminals and suspects in general belongs to the judicial organs, while the Political Security Bureau stands in the position of the prosecution." But once the frenzy of a campaign was on, and in wartime above all, the Bureau's power swelled without limit: from investigation to interrogation to sentence, a single body performed all the functions of police, procuratorate, and court together. Answerable to no one but its own superiors, and checked by no other organ, the Bureau reached the point of killing even without the approval of the military and government chiefs of its own rank.

Power without check led on to action beyond all check. The pioneering "experience" of southwest Jiangxi ran: "Unless the most cruel beating is used, they will never confess; one must use methods hard and soft together, keep up the harsh interrogation without cease, weigh the source of what they say, find the thread, chase down the trail with more questions, and above all make them give up the AB Corps organization, so that it may be rooted out at the source." This method, later summed up in the phrase "coerce, confess, believe," worked all too well: the chain reaction it set off made the "counter-revolutionaries" more and more numerous!

The "Futian Incident" broke out in the Jiangxi Soviet at the end of 1930, and the West Fujian Soviet began its suppression next; after it, the Hunan-Jiangxi, the West Hunan-Hubei, and the E-Yu-Wan Soviets all carried out one wave of suppression after another. The targets of the purge widened at furious speed: great numbers of Party members, League members, Red Army cadres, and soldiers were put to death, and some base-area leaders were killed as well — West Hunan-Hubei killed Duan Dechang, Zhou Yiqun, and Liu Zhixun; E-Yu-Wan killed Kuang Jixun and Xu Jishen... It was not until the Red Army was forced to abandon its base areas and set out on the Long March that the purges everywhere gradually subsided. Even so, Zhang Guotao killed the senior cadre Zeng Zhongsheng on the Long March road. By October 1935, when the suppression had stopped in all the other Soviet areas, it was only then getting under way in northern Shaanxi.

Though there is as yet no way to verify the full toll of those killed in the suppression, this much is certain: the killing of so many cadres, and of senior cadres above the county and regimental level in particular, was more than the enemy could ever have accomplished. The over-widening of the suppression also split the ranks within and sowed doubt among the people. Out of fear of the suppression, the people in some places no longer supported the revolution, and some even "turned their coats" and ran after the enemy. One may say that the consequences of the suppression shook the very existence of the revolutionary base areas.

The suppression that ran through the whole Party was plainly bound up with the "Left" leadership of the CCP Center at the time. Through the suppression years the leadership of the CCP Center was, in turn, Li Lisan and then Wang Ming, and the whole line grew more and more Left; that the suppression should lean "Left" too is no wonder.

There was an international background as well. After China's Great Revolution failed, Trotsky joined with Zinoviev, Kamenev, and others in criticizing Stalin's guidance as mistaken, and Stalin, with the support of the majority, struck back at Trotsky. In the parade marking the anniversary of the October Revolution, Trotsky's supporters shouted slogans against Stalin, and among them were some Chinese students at Sun Yat-sen University. The Soviet Party at once began its struggle against the "Trotskyists," sending the Trotskyists among the Chinese students home. Back in China, these Trotskyist students formed a few small Trotskyist groups, and gradually gathered around Chen Duxiu, who had been stripped of his Party posts; in 1929 they set up a "Left Opposition of the Chinese Communist Party" and elected Chen Duxiu its secretary. In the Soviet Union at this time a new view took shape: that an opposition within the Party could shake the convictions inside the revolutionary ranks, and was therefore more dangerous than the enemy. The struggle against the Trotskyists escalated swiftly — expulsion from the Party, deportation, physical elimination — fiercer even than the struggle against the enemy. The Chinese Party, as a branch of the Comintern, had a current phrase of its own: "Against wavering thought within the revolutionary ranks, there must be cruel struggle and merciless blows!" Speaking of struggle within the Party, Mao Zedong once put forward "Five Fearlessnesses": not to fear criticism, not to fear punishment, not to fear expulsion from the Party, not to fear one's wife divorcing one, not to fear the loss of one's head. Do not suppose this was said only to startle: every one of these "Five Fearlessnesses" was aimed at things that had actually happened in the suppression!

In looking back on a historical error, one ought to examine both the subjective and the objective conditions. There truly was, in that time, a fierce and cruel struggle of life and death, and the newborn Communist Party truly faced a powerful and ruthless enemy. Without these extremely unfavorable objective conditions, no one would ever have thought of resorting to so violent a measure as the suppression. In the Red Army's early days, from a division commander like Yu Shadu down to the men of the battalions, companies, platoons, and squads, defection and desertion never ceased. Once a security system was built, the mutiny of whole units was put to an end. And yet, even after the suppression, there were still defections, from an army commander like Gong Chu down to battalion and regimental cadres. The facts prove that the errors of the suppression cannot be laid entirely to objective conditions: a lesson must be sought in those who carried the suppression out, as well.

The Soviet-area suppression that took place in China was directly bound up with the "Left" leadership of the CCP Center, and of the Comintern above it.

In August 1929 the CCP Center issued its Circular No. 44, requiring the whole Party "to follow, in organization, the resolutions of the Comintern and the highest principles of the proletariat, and resolutely to eliminate any activity of the opposition within the Party." On November 13 the Center further instructed the Jiangxi Provincial Committee to "actively widen the struggle against the AB Corps Grand Alliance." On January 11, 1930, the Central Political Bureau passed a resolution, "On the Nationalist Reorganization Clique and the Tasks of the Chinese Communist Party," requiring the whole Party to make the struggle against the Reorganization Clique one of its central tasks. On April 10 the Center sent an instructional letter to the Fujian Provincial Committee, making the struggle against reformism, against the Reorganization Clique, against liquidationism, and against the Right an urgent task. In June the West Fujian government promulgated regulations for the punishment of counter-revolutionaries. In August Li Shaojiu became head of the First Front Army's suppression committee. Thereafter both Hunan and Jiangxi built suppression committees from top to bottom. The CCP cadres and the security system that led the suppression in the Soviet areas were still at a green stage, still without experience in dealing with moles within. On October 14 the Red Army's General Front Committee reported to the Center that a great many AB Corps elements had been found in southwest Jiangxi; on December 12 the Futian Incident broke out...

Zhou Enlai entered the Central Soviet Area at the end of 1931 and quickly saw that the suppression had been carried too far. Zhou Enlai, who had led the CCP's intelligence and security work all along, was relatively familiar with the enemy's situation and with the international scene, and he decisively reined in the suppression in the Central Soviet Area. Mao Zedong, who had taken part in the Jiangxi Soviet's suppression, had opposed the Center's "Left" leadership all along, and saw that the suppression here in the Jiangxi Soviet had its "Left" problems too. But so long as the central leadership went on turning "Left," and so long as the Comintern went on demanding a cleansing of the Party, the CCP could not wholly halt the errors of the suppression, still less draw up any overall reckoning of it.
