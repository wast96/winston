## Chapter 1. Grass and Trees Shed Their Leaves, and Dew Turns to Frost

The third year of the Xuantong reign, 1911.

The house of Rong—Bannermen, and the richest family in Shanghai’s medicine trade—lay smothered under a red that covered heaven and earth.

“When is the bride’s sedan chair due to arrive?” Old Madam Rong tilted her face up to study the weather beyond the window. Just now the sky was a leaden grey, the clouds cold and listless, without any life in them.

“It won’t be long now. The bride comes of a scholarly house, so of course there is a good deal of ceremony,” said First Madam with a gentle smile.

Third Madam gave a twist of her lips.

“Nothing’s going to go wrong, is it?” Old Madam Rong was a little uneasy. “A match struck up halfway along the road like this, with no roots and no history to it—it never sits quite right.”

First Madam took Old Madam Rong by the arm and led her across the parlor. “Set your mind at ease, Mother. I looked into it long ago. The three of them—a whole family—came from Shandong to Shanghai to make their way, and as luck would have it the man fell suddenly ill on the journey and died. That left only the mother and daughter, alone and with no one to lean on. The woman had spent every last coin she had, and wanted to marry the girl off, so she could scrape together the fare and escort the coffin home for burial.”

Third Madam could hold back no longer. She let out a “Hmph” and said, “This is no marrying off of a daughter—this is plainly a sale—” Before she could finish, First Madam turned and shot her a pair of hard looks, and Third Madam knew better than to go on and shut her mouth. First Madam went on talking to Old Madam Rong as mild as a spring breeze. “The bride—I’ve seen her too. She’s well read and well mannered, dignified and generous both, and they say she even attended a foreign-style school…”

“It looks to me as though you’re even more pleased with her than Yu’er is,” said Old Madam Rong.

“Well, I chose her myself. But it isn’t truly good unless you’ve seen her, Mother, and pronounced her good.” While mother-in-law and daughter-in-law were talking, firecrackers went off all at once in a crackling rattle, and smiles broke over both their faces.

“The bridal chair’s here—the bridal chair’s here—” the maidservants came calling in a tumble of voices. Eight-year-old young Master Rong, the eldest son, stood dressed in his finest along the corridor, taking in the excitement; his little sister Ronghua pressed quietly to his side, while his elder sister Rongrong went scampering and skipping about at the heels of their father, the day’s bridegroom. Evening was coming on, and the sky had turned bright with a soft moonlight.

In the wedding chamber the candles blazed like fire, casting a tender light over a pair of mandarin ducks embroidered in gold thread, bright and lovely in their color. The bride was slender and beautiful, her waist willowy and swaying—a pity, though, that hers were a pair of natural feet.

And as ill luck would have it, the bride’s name was Jinlian—Golden Lotus.

But Master Rong counted, after all, as a gentleman of the new school, and took a critical view of the barbarous custom of foot-binding. His whole face wreathed in smiles, Master Rong was full of hope for this, his fourth marriage.

Master Rong’s First Madam was a young lady of a distinguished house. In the very first year after she married into the Rong family she gave them their first son, who was named Rong Sheng. But this eldest young master was sickly from birth, frail in body and rather solitary by temperament; and First Madam, having borne her son, was left so depleted in blood and vital breath that nothing more ever stirred in her. In those days old Grandfather Rong was still living, his whole heart set on seeing the Rong house prosper and flourish and fill up with sons and grandsons, and so, as a matter of course, a Second Madam was brought in.

Second Madam was the daughter of a rice-shop owner: shrewd and capable, and not without a tender, attentive side. From the moment Master Rong took her, he was like a fish that has found water, a flower that has met the sun; even his person grew brisk and vigorous, young again and glowing. Second Madam, presuming on his favor, grew willful, kept him wholly to herself, paid First Madam no regard at all, and went so far as to set up house with Master Rong as if the two of them were a married couple alone. But as luck would have it, Second Madam’s belly did her no credit: two years in the house, and she had not so much as broken wind, which gave Old Madam Rong a great deal to complain of. Before long old Grandfather Rong took a lung ailment, and one illness carried him off. Master Rong, a filial son, was of course bound to keep the customary three years of mourning. In those days a son who did his mourning properly was not to live too comfortably—the plainer his lodging, the truer his filial heart was shown to be. So First Madam had the old woodshed cleaned out and had Master Rong move in there on his own; during the mourning he was required to abstain, and neither wife might pass the night with him in the shed. To First Madam it made no difference—she was used to the cold palace of neglect in any case, and was glad enough to watch Second Madam squirm—so it was Second Madam alone who suffered. It was as if Second Madam had been flipped over from the bottom of a steaming lovers’ hotpot and, before she knew it, turned into a fish in a cold, dead pan. Unable to bear the loneliness, Second Madam was forever using the pretext of bringing Master Rong his tea or an extra layer of clothing to meet her husband on the sly, while First Madam kept one eye open and one eye shut and pretended to see nothing. Strange to tell, all the while Master Rong and Second Madam had lived together openly and aboveboard, nothing had ever “flowered and borne fruit”; yet from these two or three months of stolen pleasure Second Madam conceived a child—and this broke a great ancestral taboo, for a filial son keeping his mourning in solitude had somehow mourned a child into being. To conceive amid mourning, to bear a child within the term of mourning dress: had such a thing happened under the former Qing, Second Madam would have been put to the extreme penalty, and the family property confiscated entire and handed over to the clan. The laws of the late Qing had loosened somewhat, it was true; but there was no telling who might make an issue of it—who, seeing the Rong fortune, would not covet it? Who could guarantee that some clansman would not go and lay an accusation? And besides, paper can never wrap up a fire. So Master Rong and Second Madam went to Old Madam Rong to confess themselves, and Old Madam Rong was so overcome with fury that she fainted dead away. In the end it fell to First Madam to take charge of the household: she sent for a physician to treat the old lady, and at the same time had a small sedan of blue satin hired, and Second Madam politely shown out of the mansion—the story being that Second Madam’s birth sign clashed with Old Madam Rong’s, and that she was to be sent for the time being to the countryside to keep watch over the ancestral graves. Second Madam swallowed her bitterness like a mute eating gall, unable to speak her suffering, and could only go weeping away.

After Second Madam was moved out to the countryside, she kept watch over the graves the whole day long, in the bleak wind and bitter rain, until she fell into a melancholia. When she had given birth to her son she gave him the character Gui—“Return”—hoping that Master Rong would come soon to fetch her and the child. But word was sent from the house that this child, born in a place of mourning, was a “star of mourning and calamity” through and through, and could not be brought back; he was to be raised out there among the graves and left to live or die on his own. When Second Madam heard this she lost her wits. One night she hanged herself from a withered tree on the grave hill. The country folk all said that a wild ghost had come and taken Second Madam as its stand-in; others said that First Madam, jealous of Second Madam’s favor, had seized the chance to be rid of her. However it was, Second Madam was gone. When Master Rong learned of it he wept for three whole days and nights—wept, in truth, more bitterly than at his own father’s death. Once the mourning period was over, the first thing Master Rong did was hurry out to the countryside, where he wept his heart out at Second Madam’s grave, and then entrusted Rong Gui to a reliable household, leaving him to make his home and his living out in the country.

After Second Madam’s death, Master Rong returned to that same lifeless married existence; and then, at his mother’s urging, he took a third wife.

Third Madam was a tailor’s daughter, with little learning and little breeding, but a certain prettiness and a knack for coquetry. Two years later she gave Master Rong a pair of daughters, named Rongrong and Ronghua. Master Rong had a fine gift for making money and ran his affairs in perfect order, expanding not only the traditional-medicine house but also branching into furs and a Western-pharmacy shop, cutting a great figure in the marketplace—and yet he could only sigh over how thin his line of heirs was. His eldest son, Rong Sheng, was frail and sickly; his second son, Rong Gui, could not be shown in the light of day; and Rongrong and Ronghua were girls, who must be married off in the end. This vast fortune he had toiled a lifetime to earn had to have someone to carry it on. And so Master Rong’s mind had never for years let go of the thought of taking a concubine. All along he had hoped that his “Second Madam” might come back—or that he might wait for someone of a temperament like hers, if only she bore even the faintest resemblance.

With a bang the door of the wedding chamber was flung open, and Rongrong and Ronghua came tumbling in, pulling Master Rong’s thoughts back to the present.

“Aiya, careful there, Eldest Miss.” A-Gui, the nurse who looked after the two young misses, laughingly set the two little ones back on their feet.

“I want fruit.” No sooner had Rongrong found her feet than she grabbed at the fruit dish and knocked it over. The fruit went scattering across the floor, and Rongrong at once got down on hands and knees to pick it up.

This was the daughter Master Rong loved best—plump and round, unreasonable, a little willful—and in Master Rong’s eyes these were all so many virtues.

Master Rong bent down to help Rongrong gather up the fruit, filling both her little pockets, then lifted her into his arms. Rongrong, a piece of fruit in her mouth, pressed her small face against him, and the juice running from the corner of her lips spattered onto Master Rong’s new clothes.

“Let me take her,” said A-Gui. “Look, she’s gone and ruined the master’s new clothes.”

“Never mind, never mind,” said Master Rong with a laugh.

Ronghua stood where she was and did not move.

“Why won’t you have some fruit?” Master Rong asked.

Ronghua pursed her little mouth. “It’s dirty.”

“Wipe it clean and it won’t be dirty.” The bride had come over at some point unnoticed. She drew out a handkerchief, wiped a piece of fruit clean, and handed it to Ronghua—her movements light, her face kind and pleasant, as if she were not a newcomer just arrived but the true mistress of the place.

A light of hope came into Master Rong’s eyes.

“I won’t eat it!” And with that Ronghua ran off.

“Second Miss!” A-Gui hastily scooped up Rongrong and went chasing after Ronghua.

“Second Miss won’t eat the fruit I offered her. I wonder—will the master have some?” the bride asked, as composed and unhurried as ever. She slipped off the great red wedding gown that wrapped her youth and beauty, her bearing graceful and correct, without the least trace of hesitation. Standing in the candlelight, she walked of her own accord to meet Master Rong’s dazzled gaze, and Master Rong’s blood froze in his veins, and all at once he swept the bride up into his arms.

Deep in the night, the candle wick bloomed into a double flower.

A year later, a new life was born.

Fourth Madam said the newborn infant was like the sun at its rising, a sign of the Rong family’s rise and flourishing; and so she proposed that Master Rong name the child Chu—“Beginning.”

Master Rong accepted Fourth Madam’s suggestion with delight.

The winter of 1914.

A thin snow lay over the cement road of Shanghai. A rickshaw drew up at the door of the Liu Jewelry House, and a small boy decked out in gold and jade slid all at once from his mother’s arms and went darting off.

“Slow down. Slow down.” The Rong family’s Fourth Madam climbed hurriedly down from the rickshaw, and the little maidservant Dong’er came to steady her.

“Mind the road, it’s slippery, Fourth Madam.”

“Keep an eye on young Master Chu, don’t let him fall.”

Dong’er ran over meaning to gather young Master Chu up, but he struggled and would not let her have her way.

Fourth Madam came over, fished a little rubber ball, bright with color, from her pocket, and bent down to strike a bargain with him. With the ball in his hands young Master Chu made no more fuss, and threw himself into his mother’s arms.

“There’s a good boy—let Mother hold you once more.”

Fourth Madam kissed her son’s ruddy little cheeks, feeling once more in her very body the sweetness of being a mother. “Come along.” Taking her darling son’s small hand, Fourth Madam stepped across the threshold of the Liu Jewelry House.

At that moment a woman draped in black gauze came hobbling up from a distance, while the rickshaw stayed where it stood, as if waiting for its next fare to arrive.

Time began to pass, minute by minute, second by second—

The rickshaw man waited patiently.

Step by step the woman in black gauze drew near the door of the Liu Jewelry House.

A brightly colored little ball came rolling out over the threshold of the Liu Jewelry House and stopped right at the black-gauzed woman’s feet. Out from inside ran the little young master, a gold lock hung about his neck, chuckling with laughter, and behind him came the pattering footsteps of the maid in pursuit.

Quick as a thunderclap that leaves no time to cover the ears, the black-gauzed woman shot out a hand, snatched young Master Chu up under her arm, sprang into the rickshaw, and was gone in a trail of dust.

“Young Master Chu!” Reaching the doorway, Dong’er was struck dumb and staring at the appalling scene before her eyes.

“Young Master Chu! Young Master Chu! Fourth… Madam, Fourth Madam—” Dong’er went stumbling back inside. “Fourth Madam—”

“What is it?” asked Fourth Madam, who had been listening with all her attention to the shopkeeper expounding on his jewels.

“Young Master Chu—”

“Young Master Chu? Where is he?”

“Young Master Chu—just, just now, only just now, young Master Chu was—he was carried off by someone.” Before the words were even out, Fourth Madam withered like a yellow leaf in the west wind.

Fourth Madam fainted clean away.

The whole of it had come far too suddenly.

The whole of it had been done cleanly, without a trace.

And the whole of it seemed, too, as if long since foreordained.

When Fourth Madam was carried back home, she seemed to have no more than a single breath left in her; the only thing that told anyone she was still alive was that pair of dead-fish eyes, staring blankly straight ahead.

Dong’er, long since frightened out of her wits, was fit for nothing now but to weep and wail aloud.

“Why did you not watch the little young master properly?” First Madam’s face was iron-grey, glinting with a cold light such as no one had seen in it before.

“Why? Why choose the third of the month to go to the Liu house? The third is the Liu house’s day of rest, when only two or three young shop-hands mind the place and there’s not even a doorkeeper. It isn’t as if you didn’t know.”

First Madam paced back and forth across the room like a gravely wounded leopardess.

“And another thing—we have a driver at home, a car could have been sent out. Why go and hire some rickshaw of unknown origin?!”

“Why choose the very time the master was away to go out buying a gold lock? Are there not gold locks enough already in this house?”

“How am I to face the old mistress and tell her, the lot of you?”

“Why do you not answer me!”

“This must be looked into—looked into to the very bottom, until the water sinks and the stones show through!”

“Telephone the police station at once and report it! And ring the household of Vice-Minister Wu—tell them to crack the case within a set time!”

First Madam’s fury swelled higher and higher, and Dong’er’s sobs sank lower and lower, until in the end Dong’er too had to be dragged out; according to the doctor, the fright had shattered her nerve.

The days went by one after another, and with each day Fourth Madam grew more haggard, Third Madam more comfortably content, and First Madam more disheartened.

Old Madam Rong, who had been praying in the family chapel for her little grandson, at last collapsed from exhaustion.

Master Rong came home.

The first piece of bad news to greet him was this: Fourth Madam had vanished.

The bright lamps were just being lit.

Master Rong, worn to the bone, sat in the courtyard with First Madam and began to weigh how they might find the missing mother and child.

“It is too strange,” said Master Rong.

“Not only strange—it makes no sense.” First Madam let out a sigh. “I had thought the Rong house would grow full of sons from now on; who could have known that in half a month so much would happen?”

“Who is it that bears a grudge against our house of Rong?”

“That is exactly where the knot of the matter lies. By rights, if someone has kidnapped the little young master, there must be some purpose in it. What purpose? Nothing, I should think, but money. And yet—why has the kidnapper not telephoned to demand a ransom?”

“Could it be that he has already—” Master Rong forced the words “killed the hostage” back down his throat.

But First Madam had already taken his meaning. “Surely it hasn’t come to that. That, too, makes no sense.”

“Fourth Madam is young,” Master Rong blurted out, a remark of no clear import.

First Madam heard the note beneath the words and flared up a little. “What vile nonsense have you been hearing from Number Three! The poor woman is already in this state, and you would throw stones on her down the well.”

“But—but where could the woman have gone?”

Just as they sat there fretful and uneasy, the maid Cui’er came trotting in through the moon gate and, out of breath, gasped, “First Madam… First Madam… Fourth Madam, Fourth Madam has come back.” To Master Rong and First Madam the words were nothing less than a treasure dropped from heaven, and four eyes turned as one toward the moon gate. A crisp, sweet patter of footsteps came drifting across the flagstones, and there was Fourth Madam, her whole face alight, leading a little boy of five or six by the hand and coming toward First Madam and Master Rong with mincing little steps, laughing as she said, “This is A-Chu… A-Chu… my young Master Chu.” Tears glistened in Fourth Madam’s eyes, and First Madam, deeply moved, felt her own nose sting again and again.

Watching Fourth Madam, radiant with joy, lead the little boy away, Master Rong was utterly at a loss.

“How can it have come to this?”

“The shock must have unhinged her—she can’t face what has happened, and she chanced upon this child.” First Madam wiped away her tears as she spoke.

“What do we do?”

“Raise him, what else.”

“Raise him how?”

“Let him keep company with Sheng’er.”

“Yes—let him keep company with Sheng’er.”

And so it was that a small boy bearing the same name as young Master A-Chu formally entered the house of Rong, becoming study-companion to the eldest young master and, as a matter of course, the adopted son of Fourth Madam. Because the day A-Chu was picked up was the sixteenth of March, that day became A-Chu’s birthday.
