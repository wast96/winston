## Otogi Pass

The sky over Iga rests on two ranges. To the west, along the Yamashiro border, it is propped by the peaks of Kasagi; to the north, along the border with Ōmi, by Otogi Pass. Whenever the sun sank behind Kasagi, cloud gathered without fail above the pass.

Tenshō nineteen. Only a few days of the third month were left. On an evening when the low, unwilling sun had begun to stain the Ōmi sky a deep madder, an old man was climbing the pass road beneath the burning cloud, so bent he seemed to crawl.

He wore a farmer's coarse clothes and leaned on a staff of black wood, some seven shaku long. If anything, it was the staff that seemed to carry him, for he was astonishingly small.

A long shadow trailed behind him. He set the staff down and hauled himself up the thin slope stroke by stroke, as though rowing a boat. At the top the road would run straight on into the mountains of Kōga.

The old man stopped. Someone was near. A moment later a woodcutter came up the path, treading on the old man's shadow, and made to pass him—

A small fear woke in the woodcutter's face as he turned. The old man's features were too dreadful. The right eye and the nose were crushed, an old burn crept across the whole of the face, and from lips small and slit-looking a few broken teeth showed. The teeth grinned like an old ape's.

"There's tōyaku growing."

"Eh?"

"..."

"There, at your feet. Dry it—won't you take it for your stomach?"

"...won't you."

The man hurriedly pulled two or three stalks of the green roadside weed; then, all at once at ease once he saw the old man meant kindly—

"And you, sir—where are you from?"

"Me? The east."

"The east..."

"Shimotsuge."

"Ah—Lord Jirōzaemon?"

"You know me? Well, there's no help for that."

The old man bared his teeth in a wry smile. Back in the ninth year of Tenshō—of which more later—Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon had harried Nobunaga's armies through the whole of the Iga Rebellion, and there was not a man in the province who did not know the name.

"Now then—"

The old man sank down at the foot of his staff.

"There should be a hermitage near here."

"Lord Tsuzura's?"

"Ho—sharp again, aren't you. The very place. Jūzō's."

A flicker of wariness crossed the old man's eyes. It caught at once in the woodcutter's, and his earlier fear seemed to rise in him again.

"Well—if it's Lord Jūzō's hermitage you want, there's a great hundred-year pine up above. East of the pine there's no path. Part the grass and go on a chō or so."

"I see. And Jūzō—how does he spend his days?"

"I wouldn't know."

And with that the woodcutter bolted down the slope as though in flight.

There was a low cliff.

On top of it the hundred-year pine the woodcutter had named had struck its roots.

The old man gazed up at the cliff a while; then, shifting his grip on the staff, he braced its point against the roadside and floated lightly up into the air. Drifting across the sky, he came down on the cliff-top and ran over the undergrowth like a beast.

Oak grew there, nara and kashi. Soon the wood ended and a small clearing opened out.

A hermitage stood in it.

The eaves sagged, but the building was not so small for all that. It was like a wayside chapel.

All at once the air filled with pale red light. In the western sky the setting sun must have been coming to its full. Light lodged in the beads of the drifting cloud, and the red cloud sank until it seemed to wrap the hermitage round.

The old man stood at the edge of the clearing and stared at the hermitage. He was not, of course, admiring the view.

Presently a hand-bell sounded from within the red cloud, and the low voice of the sunset gatha began to leak out. The man the old man had come to find—could this be Tsuzura Jūzō?

As the chanting began, the old man himself started up a strange dance in the grass, swaying only the upper half of his body, this way and that.

As the voice climbed toward its high notes his movements grew fierce; as it fell to the low ones they slackened. He must have been trying to trace the other man's breathing exactly onto the rhythm of his own body. And all the while his feet never stopped; they carried him closer to the hermitage.

Softly, like a rag laid down on the wet veranda, the old man's body slid onto the boards.

The sunset gatha went on.

The old man quietly slid the paper screen open.

A room came into view.

A shrine-cabinet faced west. Before it stood a hand-bell and an incense burner.

But there was no one there.

The sunset gatha went on.

The voice alone seemed to rise, unaccompanied, from the empty round mat at the center of the room.

The old man burst out laughing.

"Jūzō. That's enough now."

"..."

"Well—what a careful fellow."

"..."

"Come out. Here, in front of me."

"Master?"

"No need to make sure. You've known since the moment I came."

"What have you come for?"

"Why do you hide?"

"I may mean to have done with the world, but let me catch a presence and the body will not stay enlightened. Before I knew it, my hands and feet had fastened themselves to the beam."

"Ha! All the better for me, then. A rappa turned half-saint isn't worth so much as a dirty sketch. Today it's you I've come counting on—you, who could never quite turn holy—with a favor there's no taking anywhere else. Come, draw closer."

"Jūzō. You haven't changed."

"..."

Without a sound, like a single spider paying out its thread, a man dropped from the beam of the roof-frame.

Thirty-four, thirty-five. Thick through the shoulders. For a man they called a rappa, unusually tall.

"Nor have you, Master."

He lived like a novice monk, and yet he had not shaved his head; his wild hair was bound carelessly behind. As Jūzō settled, a smile on his swarthy face, the old man—too impatient to wait—tapped him on the knee and said:

"Kazama Gohei is dead."

"What!"

"Or he may have turned traitor."

"..."

"Either way, the last seed of a man fit for the shinobi art has died out in Iga."

"Kazama Gohei—wasn't he the young lady's—"

"Mm. Her betrothed."

"Then you must be grieved."

"Me?"

"And the young lady, surely."

"Kisaru? Her own father can't read what's in her heart. She tells me she'll go and learn what became of Kazama. If his death is certain, well and good; but if Kazama Gohei has betrayed Iga, she'll run him through with her own hand—so she says. Ah, if only she'd been born a boy."

"..."

"She would have made a fine man of Iga."

"But in a woman, a temper like that is a seed of grief."

"Only think of it. The Iga Rebellion scattered my retainers to the winds. And now, of my two disciples—the only two I had—one has come to this. —Jūzō. Where are you going?"

"To bring a light."

"No need. For my eyes there is no night or day. Or have yours gone dull?"

"Ha. Ten years of fussing with the Buddha will do that. You may not need it, Master. I do."

Jūzō rose into the darkness. The moment the old man had fixed on his retreating back, he slid swiftly across the boards to the shrine-cabinet, opened its door, and put in his hand. At that, Jūzō's laughter came booming out of the dark:

"It isn't there."

"..."

"My guardian Buddha rests here, in my breast. Were you thinking of making off with it?"

"Of course. A ninja has no use for playing at the Buddha."

"I felt it. From the moment you stood at the edge of this garden, it has weighed on me. Master—I—"

"I won't hear it. Go back to the shinobi."

"Is there work as great as all that?"

"There is. Take up Kazama's work. There is no ninja left in Iga fit for a thing this size but you."

All at once a candle's halo drew near, sweeping the dark aside. As Jūzō came back to his seat—

"And Kazama?"

"He was in the capital. Two years he did the work I set him. Then, half a year ago, his reports stopped. I sent courier after courier; every one came back empty."

The old man broke off short and twisted aside. For a while he groped in his breast, then drew out a small hemp sack and bent to some stubborn task, as though he had forgotten the man seated across from him. One by one he picked stones from the sack and set them out on his palm. The stones had a dank, dark sheen. They were wet in an unpleasant way—and one of them, all at once, moved.

"Terribly stiff, I am."

He muttered it and thrust his neck out long in front of him, eyes shut. He might have been some worn old bird of prey gripping a crag against the wind of heaven. One by one he pinched up the leeches and laid them, with great care, along the stretched cords of his neck.

The hour was surely past the last quarter of the Cock by now—past seven in the evening. The mountain seemed to be working up to rain; beyond the paper screen the trees kept up a restless stir.

On the old man's neck the leeches slowly fattened. Presently they swelled into beads of blood and fell, dripping like rain from the eaves; and Tsuzura Jūzō watched them, dull-eyed, in the shadow of the lamp.

It struck him as strange that there should be blood enough left in this old man's veins to feed another living thing.

So wasted was the old man. And by just so much had Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon lived out a cruel half-life, giving up anything and anyone without a backward look for the profit of a self bound wholly to the shinobi—though that, to be sure, was no more than the way of the Iga jizamurai.

In the fourth year of Juei, among the Heike captains put to flight at Dan-no-ura, there was one Iga Heizaemon-no-jō Ienaga. When the age of the Genji came, his line hid away in the far corners of the Iga basin, tilled the soil with their own hands, and sank into petty gōshi who barely made a living.

Of these, the men who settled on the Hattori estate and took its name were the Hattori band; those who settled at Tsuge and took that name, the Tsuge band. Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon was one of that line. Several other small clans of the Taira name held their own hills and valleys besides.

Iga has been called, from of old, the hidden country.

A little basin of no more than four hundred and sixty square kilometers, ringed by the mountains of four provinces—Yamashiro, Yamato, Ise, Ōmi—with seven passes over the hills for its only thin threads to the world outside.

The mouth toward Kōga in Ōmi was Otogi Pass; toward Yamashiro, the Kasagi Pass; toward Ise, the Kabutogoe and the Nagano Pass. Every one of them opened onto the highroads of Japan, and stop those mouths and Iga vanished from the sight of power.

And power—the road that ran out from the capital along the eastern shore of Lake Biwa and on to Gifu, Suruga, Odawara, Kamakura, Edo—was itself the great thoroughfare down which the history of Japan fought out its wars for mastery.

Iga lay back to back with that trunk-road of power, and the capital was a bare eighty kilometers off. The sound of a power crumbling, the sound of a power rising—both reached them across no more than a single paper door, and the gōshi of this country could go on enjoying their own quiet days, alone.

It was no wonder the shinobi arts came to flower among them. Each time a power broke apart in the capital, defeated warriors fled by the score into this country of back-roads. In the Juei years the Kiso men came tumbling in; the Shimazu force, routed at Sekigahara, came out to Sakai through this land as well.

In the sixth month of Tenshō ten, Ieyasu—word of the Honnō-ji Incident freshly reached him—was at Sakai with a bare handful of men. To make for his home province of Mikawa by the Ōsaka road, the Kyoto road, or the Ōmi road would have been to fling himself willingly into the jaws of the rebel army. So he went in secret by the back-roads: into Iga over Otogi Pass, down the Tsuge River and across the Kabutogoe, and away by sea from Shiroko in Ise.

Yoshitsune, for his part, turned the hidden country to bold strategic use. The Kiso army in the capital lost the enemy's trail at about the point where his force, marched out from Kamakura, began to flicker in and out of Ise. They never knew that Yoshitsune had pointedly spurned the lakeshore road and led his men into Iga to wipe out their tracks.

When at last he burst onto the Yamashiro plain and destroyed the Kiso army, the part this hidden country had played in the history of Japan was something between the wonderful and the uncanny.

Naturally, the gōshi who lived in the hidden country began to take on a strange cast. They dwelt in the home provinces, and yet it never once entered their heads to fasten onto a rising power and climb by it—because they had watched, year on year and with their own eyes, how brief a thing ruin is.

Most of them carried a strange nihilism in them. They would hire out to the lords of other provinces, but they never let themselves be kept on a stipend, and they did not even choose their masters. They took the side of any man who paid, and when the work was done they would go over to his enemy. For they knew, by a hereditary instinct, that the power of today runs straight on into ruin.

They held power in contempt, and held in deeper contempt the loyalty of the samurai, who laid down his whole life and fate for it. The warriors of the provinces despised the Iga gōshi as men without principle; the men of Iga, in their turn, laughed at the shallowness of the samurai's soul.

For the Iga gōshi, to live by one's own craft was the axis of life and of every virtue. They knew to the marrow how much fiercer a will, how much cleaner a spirit, it takes to live by one's craft alone than any loyalty could ever ask.

The craft, needless to say, was the art of the shinobi—the thief's art, and the art of concealment. The Iga gōshi had their servants trained in it and hired themselves out for the work of the spy and the schemer. In an old book called the Iranki it stands written thus:

"From dawn to the Hour of the Horse they ply the trades of warrior, farmer, artisan, and merchant; from the Hour of the Horse until dusk they hone the ways of arms and archery, and above all they drill in the art of concealment. Since the ancient age, as the bequeathed custom of Iga, the arts of intrigue have been handed down; and Tateoka-no-Dōjun, Iga Tarōbei, Takayama Jirōtarō, Kogushi, Ōgushi, Kido, and other masters of the craft so filled the province that their power in the shinobi way has come down even to the present, and there is no stronghold, however strong, into which they cannot steal. In other provinces too they were prized, and called the Iga shinobi."

From the fall of the Kamakura shogunate down to the last years of the Warring States, Iga had no lord.

The Iga gōshi described above drew up a covenant of alliance they called the Iga League Bond. "Should any band break in from another province or district, all shall make common cause, without treachery, to beat them back. Should a man of this district bring in outside men and covet another's lands, then—parent, child, or brother be damned—the whole district shall with one heart pass sentence upon him." By such articles they held the entire province under a justice of their own keeping.

It was Oda Nobunaga who dealt this order its ruinous blow.

At the beginning of Tenshō five, Nobunaga led an army in person and subdued the Saika of Kishū. He went on to set Shibata Katsuie over the taking of Kaga, Hashiba Hideyoshi over Harima, and the following year Akechi Mitsuhide over the overrunning of Tanba.

It was about then, too, that a mixed Oda–Kitabatake force under Nobukatsu—Nobunaga's son, who had succeeded to the Kitabatake house of Ise—set out to sweep the neighboring province of Iga. That first campaign ended in dismal defeat.

Even as Oda Nobukatsu was raising Maruyama Castle, the base from which Iga was to be taken, in the valleys of Kanbe, the Iga jizamurai had already read his intent; they passed the circular from hand to hand and threw up a firm league of their own.

In it Tsuzura Jūzō—a gōshi of Nabari who came with the Momochi band of Tatsunokuchi—was twenty; and Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon, of Shimotsuge, who came with the Tsuge band, was forty-two; both, one supposes, hard men in the full of their strength.

Naturally the whole army was a force of night-raiders.

A man named Tsuge Kiyohiro was made their head, and the Iga army, gōshi and common levies together, came to seven hundred.

Most of the genin had no surname worth the name. They were divided into some twenty squads, each under a gōshi as its leader, with the genin set beneath him.

They went by nicknames—Ueno-no-Hidari, Kanbe-no-Konan, Otowa-no-Kido, Tsuge-no-Komimi, and the like. They were not of samurai rank; yet it was from among them that the masters of ninjutsu who made so many later legends came.

A hundred of them, the ablest in fire-craft and stratagem, were picked out and, to mark them off from the field troops, named the shinobi corps.

Jirōzaemon, Jūzō, and Kazama Gohei among them had the command of these genin.

And the man who commanded the hundred ninja was Tateoka-no-Dōjun.

In all likelihood this was the first time in history, and the last, that ninja fought on such a scale not for hire but for their own defense.

Maruyama Castle stood with a mountain at its back.

On either flank the Iga River, main stream and tributary, had cut the ground into a broken tangle as it ran.

That night, a little past the fourth watch—two in the morning—the Iga force, gathered in from every hamlet of the basin, massed in the eastern hills and closed around the castle, quiet as wind.

The shadows of the grass, the treetops, the hollows of the rocks—every feature of the ground beyond the walls began to breathe like a god of death.

The castle's commander was Takigawa Katsuo, and not a man under his banner knew that the ring was already drawing shut.

There can never, surely, have been so silent a siege in the whole history of war.

It was high summer; but once the Hour of the Tiger had passed, the mist off the shallows of the Iga River took on, as such mist will, a desolate chill.

About that time a thin thread of a moon sank behind the western hills.

It was the shinobi corps.

As the moon went down, from all four quarters of the besieging army something like a black miasma rose over the grass.

The hundred shinobi, in their twenty squads, from the rear gate, from the wicket-gates, from the shadow of the towers, were drawn as though sucked into the castle.

"Kuroami."

Tsuzura Jūzō, who had climbed the bank from the southern stream and come out at the castle's rear gate, put his mouth to the genin's ear.

The man called Kuroami twisted his body, lowered a grappling ladder from his back, and went forward with his face to the roots of the grass. Where he touched the grass the insects fell silent; and in their place, from the teeth of the other genin around him, came the counterfeit cry of insects.

Within the gate a watch-fire burned red. Kuroami climbed the wall beside it and flattened himself there, still, as though he had turned to a roof-tile. Below him he could make out the heads of several sentries.

Kuroami raised a short bow.

By then the other genin, with Jūzō, had crawled crabwise along the wall and, far from the rear gate now, were drawn into the shadow of the southwest tower.

Several climbed to its eaves and packed the joints of the timberwork with sulphur and powder bound up in straw, letting long paper twists of tinder hang down.

At the foot of the wall a man lay flat against the earth, the tinder in his fist, waiting for the signal to strike a light.

Quietly the hour flowed on through the dark.

All at once the roof of the inner keep spat fire. In the same instant flame ran to the tinder in the fists of Jūzō's men, and one after another pillars of fire went up from several of the towers. The wind that had risen licked at the blaze, throwing the shapes of the ninja who capered in the flames into monstrous relief.

"Ah—over there!"

The sentries at the rear gate caught sight of the fire, snatched up their spears to run, and fell one after another without a cry. In the shadow of the wall Kuroami, never pausing for breath, nocked arrow on arrow and set the string singing sharp. Before long the sentries' corpses were being trampled without mercy under the Iga straw sandals of the assault troops, who had burst the gate and come pouring through.

By the time the heavy-armed assault troops came flooding in, the light-clad shinobi corps had already drawn far off beyond the walls, and, hidden here and there about the basin, watched the fire's work in silence.

The castle fell in the flames. Its commander, Takigawa Katsuo, set a handful of men to cut a way out, fled into the hills, and carried his burned body back to the capital.

The fire, too, died down with the coming of day.

By the time the sun began to climb from the hills of Ise, there was not a single Iga soldier to be seen in all the basin, far as the eye could reach—though only a few hours before, so great an assault had been fought on that very plain.

There was reason in it. They feared the daylight and the open field. Once the sun was up, a great relief force was certain to come from the main castle in Ise.

Warriors they were, but pitched battle was not their trade. Daylight cancels their powers. To break up the force at once, without waiting for dawn, was the natural thing for shinobi-warriors, though it flouted every rule of generalship. That a victorious army of seven hundred should not spare a glance for the castle it had just seized, but vanish uncannily from the field—the tale swelled in the telling as it ran through the provinces, and reached Nobunaga's ears, as such news will, along with the report of the defeat.

"Do things not of this world dwell in Iga?"

Into Nobunaga's long, slit eyes came a glow like a firefly's, such as only the truly obsessed possess.

To the eyes of this born rationalist, the ninja of the Iga basin must have looked like so many goblins of the hills and streams.

He hated, above all things, what he could not understand. His monstrous cruelty toward the Ikkō sectarians at Ishiyama and Nagashima, the burning of Mount Hiei—there is in every one of them an excess that cannot be understood without reckoning on the fierce hatred, deep in Nobunaga's mind, of everything irrational.

The religious authority of Mount Hiei, salvation through the nenbutsu, the concealment-arts of Iga—among these, perhaps, there is no common thread at all. Yet to Nobunaga's rational mind, by now grown almost fanatical, they were all of a kind: devilry. They looked to him like evil spirits that could live only in the dim, damp folds of the soul.

Without this, the words with which he ordered his subordinate corps, in the third month of Tenshō nine, to reduce Iga cannot be understood:

"Leave not one man of Iga alive."

It was the same turn of phrase he had used when he butchered the monks of Mount Hiei. This was no mere order to take the field; it might better be called an order of extermination.

The forces mustered from Ise, Yamato, Mino, Ōmi, and elsewhere came to more than twelve thousand. The Iga men numbered a bare thousand-odd.

Against that meager force he chose seasoned commanders.

The lines of advance:

From the Ōmi road, Gamō Katahide and Asano Nagamasa.

From the Yamato road, Tsutsui Junkei.

From the Ise road, Takigawa Kazumasu.

From the Yamashiro road, Niwa Nagahide.

The Oda army, filling the seven passes, deliberately chose fair weather with clear sightlines, shunned a night battle, and came down into the Iga basin all together a little after daybreak.

They killed even the farmers.

Unusually for the Oda army, in this land they slaughtered women and children too.

The Oda troops ringed the basin and drew the noose steadily tighter, leaving behind them a vast litter of butchered dead. That they made no distinction in the killing was, no doubt, from fear that the "apparition" ninja were hidden among the rest.

In this war the Iga men never once formed a united command. They fought almost every man for himself, appearing under cover of night at the camps of the several armies, firing them and cutting the soldiers down.

In Niwa Nagahide's force the losses from these raids are said to have run to fifty-odd men.

But the last day came at length. —On the hills of Kashiwara, in what is now Naga district, there are the remains of a small fort.

This Kashiwara Castle became the last stronghold the Iga gōshi held, handed down to them from their forefathers.

"What—we're to die with this fort for a pillow? Is that what Lord Tsuge Kiyohiro says? Is that why he sent the circular round the province, and gathered us all here like this?"

Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon, who had come by night from Tsuge through enemy-held ground into the Kashiwara fort, climbed over its timber palisade, seized one of the gōshi already there, and cried out in a high, shrill voice.

The gōshi brushed off Jirōzaemon's clutching hand and, with a put-upon look, said:

"I'm not Lord Kiyohiro. But look at these fields heaped with the dead and you'll see it for yourself. Another ten days and there'll not be a living soul left in Iga. Rather than be butchered for nothing, Lord Kiyohiro means to make the most of this one fort still standing—to steel every man to die fighting and show the Oda what the honor of an Iga warrior is. That, I take it, is what's in his mind."

"Honor. A common enough thing to say. Where is there any honor in an Iga man? To hold no honor at all—that is the Iga man's honor."

"Ungh—"

"What's the matter?"

"Dead, are you?"

As the man folded and slumped against him, Jirōzaemon propped him up with an air of annoyance. A stray ball seemed to have punched from the back of his skull down through the neck below the jaw, and blood was spilling out all around.

He flung the still-warm corpse to the ground and ran off through the dark toward the hall.

The hall stood in the shadow of a great camphor. A double palisade ringed it; before the palisade an abatis bit into the ground; and before that a moat, brimming with water, that on this night at the full flush of spring gave back a keen, clear, star-crowded sky.

A warrior came pushing out through the line of the abatis, made out Jirōzaemon, and—

"Ah—Master. It is you, Master, is it not?"

"Gohei?"

Kazama Gohei came running up. Armored though he was, his thin cheeks, flushed with the color of blood, had a beauty—like a young shrine-maiden's—that struck one, all at once, as almost uncanny.

"And you yourself, Master."

"What of Jūzō?"

"He was fighting at the Tatsunokuchi fort. Since it was burned, no one knows where he's gone."

"He'll not be dead. A ninja of his quality does not lose his life though you pour a rain of arrows and shot on him."

"Only—"

"Mm?"

"Jūzō's sick father, Lord Tsuzura Tarōbei, and his mother, fleeing from the Tsuzura house at Ayama to the Tatsunokuchi fort, were cut down on the way by Niwa Nagahide's men. Only the younger sister got through to Tatsunokuchi and reached her brother at the fort, they say—but that same night she took her own life. She had likely been shamed in the hills, and could not live with it."

"Nothing rare in that. I've had my fill of watching men die. More to the point—have you seen Lord Tsuge?"

"In the great hall, I think—"

"A council again, is it."

Jirōzaemon started off, then, as though it had just come back to him, turned to Gohei:

"Your own father is dead too, you know."

"What—"

"..."

"On my way here, by Haburo Pond at Yubune, I saw the body."

"..."

Kazama Gohei stood stock-still in the dark.

Jirōzaemon's figure walked on a dozen paces ahead and passed into the hall.

The earthen room stank of blood and pus.

Straw mats were spread on the earth, and a great number of wounded men lay tumbled across them, most of them still in their armor.

When he slid open the cedar door of the great hall, there was no lamp within; in the dark he felt the presence of several men. One of them, sprawled on the boarded floor right where he stepped in, rolled heavily over.

"Shimotsuge's man?"

"Mm. Momochi, is it? What are you at?"

"A council of war."

The man—Momochi Shinnojō, who lived at Tatsunokuchi—answered wearily.

"Lying flat on your back?"

"No use in it. Once you're cornered this far, sitting up to talk it over won't squeeze out any better wisdom."

"Well—what have you decided?"

"That—ask over there."

Momochi Shinnojō pointed toward the seat of honor. There, arms folded, silent as an ornament set on a shelf, was the aged shadow of Tsuge Tarō Kiyohiro. The shadow stirred, and said flatly:

"We've decided to die fighting."

"Have you, now. A fine man we chose to lead our army."

"What was that?"

"Now hear me. If a ball or an arrow finds this body, I'll die fighting whether or not a council solemnly resolves it. The samurai of the provinces I leave aside; but for an Iga shinobi-warrior, to die fighting is a disgrace."

"The thing to do is plain—scatter and flee. Let each man run to whatever province will take him, and so long as he keeps alive, though he chew grass to do it, the day will come to make the provinces feel once more how an Iga man is to be feared. There's no saying we'll never repay the Oda for this."

"And the wounded? The women and children?"

"It can't be helped."

"You'd have them killed?"

"Once the able-bodied are away, let the rest surrender. If the attackers cut them down, that's their luck. If they let them live, that too is their luck."

"That is a cruel thing."

"The cruel one is you."

Before he had finished, Jirōzaemon turned sharply toward the cedar door at the entrance. He had caught something black—something like a killing intent.

"Jūzō?"

Tsuzura Jūzō stood there. Thick lips pressed shut, still in his warrior's straw sandals, he stood dark before the cedar door. No one had marked the moment he appeared; but that his presence was no common thing was plain to every man in the room who turned to look. Slowly he unfolded his arms.

"I will not scatter and run,"

he said.

Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon waved a hand, irritable, as though to brush away the strange force that came off the man's body.

"Back with you. This is no place for you to come."

"That is discourteous, Master. I learned the shinobi arts from you, but I am not your genin. The Tsuzura are samurai of the Taira line, and no mean ones. In this place I have every right to sit in the councils of Iga. And that house of Tsuzura, too—"

His voice trailed off. His face did not move, his lips stayed closed; only his two great eyes quietly filled, and soon soaked his cheeks with tears.

Jirōzaemon, watching with a glare that seemed to rake him from below, said:

"An ugly sight, Jūzō. What did you come here for? To weep? Is the death of your parents and your sister such a grief to you?"

"It is. They were not your parents who died, Master. They were mine."

"Heh. Clever enough with your tongue, at least. Listen well: what did I once teach you? Be as the earth, I said; be as stone, be as the wind, be as a leaf on a tree. Hold no ordinary human heart. Have you forgotten?"

"I have forgotten. And just now I feel no shame in it."

"Fool. Your father and mother are dead, and already turned to earth. You too—turn to earth while you yet live. Once you are earth, you stand level with the world of your dead, and there can be no more grieving."

"But my body is alive. So long as it lives, the tears come. The one thing this living man can do for the dead is to take revenge. I will not scatter and run."

"Stay on the battlefield, and do what?"

"Lay the torments of hell on the enemy. Farewell, Master. Live long and well, if you can."

"Wait."

Jirōzaemon's hand shot out and caught the sleeve of Jūzō, who was turning to go. Keeping his hold, he turned back to the assembly.

"You've all heard him. We can't well let a good shinobi throw his life away here for nothing. I've decided to bend my earlier plan a little. As we scatter, we'll set fire-charges in the enemy's camps, treat them to a full feast of fire and arrows, and then quit the province."

"How many men?"

"The fewer the better. The time—tomorrow night. There'll likely be a cold rain from dusk."

When he had finished, Jirōzaemon looked round at Jūzō.

"And mark me—make no mistake of it. Don't imagine I spared you out of any tenderness."

He fixed him with a cold, white stare. And yet it seems that this man, a very apparition of the shinobi, could not, in the end, bear to watch a rare vessel he had raised with his own hands spill itself away in a pointless death.

Two streams, rising from the mountain mass of Uda in Yamato, run one to the east and one to the north, and come to enclose the hill of Kashiwara that bears the fort. They join, and run a further ri to the north.

There stands the hamlet of Ōyado.

From of old it was called a crossroads of Iga: the Nabari highway, the Aoyama Pass road, and two others—four roads met about here. What Sun Tzu's book calls "ground of intersecting highways" must be a place like this.

Naturally the attackers set their headquarters here, and the hamlet—a bare fifty or sixty houses—filled up with Niwa Nagahide's men.

There was no moon, but the black sky was swept clear. And on the earth, more numerous than the stars that twinkled above, a vast host of watch-fires burned.

The fires were not confined to the yards of the houses. Down both sides of the highway they were ranged, a few ken apart, on and on; and even along the barley-field paths and among the reeds of the riverbank the braziers spat sparks and drove back the darkness of the earth.

The brightness of the fire, it seems, was thought their one charm against the eerie comings and goings of the Iga apparitions.

But when, past the third watch, five more fires were added to that teeming host of flames, there was—for all the light—no way to notice.

No. There were soldiers who noticed.

(What's that...?)

The man, who had come out of the barracks with his long spear still in his hand to relieve himself, peered from the embankment toward the reeds on the sandbar.

Several of the watch-fires among them seemed to move, ever so slightly—drifting toward the barracks.

(A trick of the eyes?)

Screwing up his nerve, he went down the bank, and found nothing amiss. The watch-fires, some tall, some low, all burned on of themselves, throwing off their flames.

But then he saw it: several of them were not the ordinary basket kind. It was a dish, like a great black pot, that caught the fire and burned.

The basket kind lit their tripod legs to the base in a red glow. This unfamiliar pot kind was the opposite: the stronger the fire on the dish above, the blacker the darkness it laid beneath.

(Ah—the shinobi!)

By the time he tried to cry out, it was already too late. A hand had come round from behind, and a blade, blacked over, had driven clean through the man's throat.

It was Kazama Gohei who swiftly stripped the fallen man's armor and put it on. Without so much as a flutter of his lovely lashes, with the blank face of a practiced clerk, he finished dressing the corpse—madder-red there in the firelight—in a shinobi's garb; then he laid his face to the ground and let a low call, the shinobi's own, escape his throat, pitched to be heard by nothing but the insects in the earth.

"Jūzō. The Master hasn't come. Nor has the rain. With watch-fires all over the ground like this, there's no way to move."
