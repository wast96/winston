## Chapter 40

Mei Yingzi's coming to see me was a thing I fretted over, a thing I dreaded, but at the same time a thing I longed for. Waking the next day, I had within me a kind of tense readiness—a tension that was less a worry over the hard problems Mei Yingzi might set me than a worry over the hard problems I had left for Mei Yingzi. I believed she must now be in a state of not knowing what to do: had those two packets of documents already been returned to Bai Ping? And how had they been returned? From Philip's mouth I had already learned of Bai Ping's lie about the course of my wounding; this lie, for Bai Ping, was perhaps only to make it convenient to summon Dr. Philip to save me, while for my own part, because Philip spoke of it entirely in the manner of idle chat, and because I feared everything might be brought to a deadlock, I had not corrected it truthfully. But as regards the work, thinking of it now, might it not have become a barrier between Bai Ping and Mei Yingzi? Philip did not know the true facts of my wounding, and Mei Yingzi naturally could not know them either; then ought I to tell Mei Yingzi the truth? And if I ought, should it be today? If Bai Ping's charge against me—the so-called reason for shooting me—was a stand taken on conscience, then she should not be our enemy; and then it seemed only I could link her with Mei Yingzi, and Bai Ping might become Mei Yingzi's best fellow-worker. But if Bai Ping's charge against me was only a form of words and a screen, then what should my attitude be? And if I confused these two, the true and the false—whether I took the true for false or the false for true—it would be a calamity and a sin; and the judging of true from false, how hard it is to be sure of...

The weather was fine, and my spirits were fine too; I had health enough to sustain all this thinking, but not wit enough to solve all the questions; I hoped Mei Yingzi, when she came, would bring her full, keen-witted optimism. So I could only wait anxiously for her to arrive—I waited for her as one waits, in a first love, for one's sweetheart.

At last Mei Yingzi came.

She brought her own peculiar fragrance, her own peculiar colour, her own peculiar radiance. All this was already beyond my expectation, and she brought besides her own peculiar gaiety—the gaiety she wore in the wide arenas of society.

She told me that my being wounded had not been let known to a single outsider; the newspapers, of course, had not been allowed to leak a word of it, and among our friends too the secret was kept. As for the apartment house, and Honsa Jiro's side, she had already given it out on my behalf that I had gone back to my native place; and as for my family, Mrs. Manfield had already told them that I had gone to Qingdao with her daughter.

She told me that after Philip received Bai Ping's telephone call he had telephoned her, and that for a while she had forgotten everything and only worried over my health; it was not until she saw me at the hospital, and learned from Dr. Gaolang and Dr. Philip that my danger was wholly confined to the matter of crippling, that she was at ease. But I told her that for me crippling would sooner be death—at which she laughed, and said:

"I should have thought a crippled left arm would surely do your learning and your career good."

"But what of our work?" I said.

"How does it compare with death?" she said.

We chatted idly a long while, without touching the work at all. I did not believe she had no hard problems in the work; then was it because, while I was recovering, to speak of it would do the work no good either? I could not endure it, and so I asked:

"You already know the course of my wounding?"

"I came to know two versions, neither of which could make me certain; but now I know a third, and this question may be counted settled." She smiled in triumph.

"The first version was Bai Ping's account?"

"No," she said. "It was Philip's account."

"The second?"

"Was my own conjecture," she said. "After I returned the documents to her in your name."

"Returned them to her in my name?"

"I sent a man round, saying only that they came from the Gaolang Hospital."

"And she?"

"She was not at home; the things were left there, but afterwards she made no sign at all."

"Then how did you conjecture?"

"I conjectured that, after Bai Ping's documents were lost, she took counsel with the Japanese officers. Since it was you they suspected, they meant to kill you. Bai Ping, though in her feelings unwilling to harm you, could not in the end stop them, and so the moment she learned you were wounded she telephoned Dr. Philip."

"And why could this conjecture, in turn, not be made certain?"

"It was the origin of that pistol."

"And so...?"

"It cost me a great deal of effort to trace, and not until last week did I learn it was of Chinese Government origin."

"And so...?"

"And so, early the day before yesterday, I went to call on Bai Ping."

Then she told me the course of her meeting with Bai Ping—a scene that comforted me, that thrilled me, and that solved for me every question of doubt and worry and unease.

At seven that morning the day before yesterday, Mei Yingzi, in light clothes and soft-soled shoes and a large, loose overcoat, with Bai Ping's pistol hidden in her pocket, drove her red car to call on Bai Ping.

It was Ah Mei who opened the door; she said Bai Ping was not yet up, and settled Mei Yingzi to sit a while in the drawing-room; but Bai Ping's bedroom door stood ajar, and as Ah Mei was leaving, Mei Yingzi took off her gloves, thrust both hands into her coat pockets, and softly pushed the door open and went in.

Heavy curtains barred the daylight, and the room glimmered with silver ripples; Mei Yingzi shut the door and stepped softly to Bai Ping's bedside. Before the bed a long-haired bearskin was spread, and so she sat down on the edge of Bai Ping's bed; this stir did not wake Bai Ping, and Mei Yingzi, reaching out, turned on the bedside lamp and called in a low voice:

"Bai Ping!"

Bai Ping woke with a start, then, putting on a drowsy smile, said:

"It's you?"

"Forgive me," said Mei Yingzi.

"Do you need me to get up?" Bai Ping asked.

"No." Mei Yingzi pressed her down and said intimately: "Will you let me put my hand under the covers?" So saying, Mei Yingzi slipped her hand in. Under the quilt Bai Ping took Mei Yingzi's cold hand in her own warm one and said:

"What is it that brings you to see me so early, and braving the cold?"

"I have come to give my hand into yours."

"Thank you," said Bai Ping. "Will you turn on the electric heater, take off your coat, and sit on the sofa and talk with me?"

So Mei Yingzi drew the sofa near and turned on the heater. Bai Ping said:

"Will you have a cup of hot coffee?" And she leaned up to call Ah Mei.

But Mei Yingzi stopped her. She took off her coat, and, drawing the pistol out of her pocket as she did so, sat abruptly on the sofa and said with a smile:

"Not a sound. I hope you'll be willing to tell me a few things."

"What do you mean by this?" Bai Ping seemed at first to start, but then said calmly: "Whatever concerns you, I am willing to tell you faithfully; as for what does not concern you, you have no right to ask me."

"Whatever concerns Xu concerns me."

"Then you two are of one party." Bai Ping sneered. "Very well—ask me."

"First I must know who it was that shot and wounded Xu."

"You wish to know?"

"I mean to avenge Xu."

"Truly?"

"Of course."

"Did he ask it of you?"

"Never you mind that."

"But this question—would it not be easier and surer to ask Xu than to ask me?"

"He does not know the man's name."

"Then he knows his face?"

"I won't hide it from you," said Mei Yingzi. "Xu is still in his wounds, and I have not seen him. I think this matter need not go through him; I mean, when Xu can receive callers, to go and call on him with the astonishing news."

"That is to say, you mean to avenge him first, and then go to see him."

"Yes."

"But suppose he himself does not wish for revenge?"

"Do you think so?"

"Yes," said Bai Ping. "He seems to have a broad enough heart to forgive people."

"But he would not forgive his enemy."

"Perhaps this enemy is a misunderstanding; perhaps this enemy is, on the contrary, one who loves him." The tone of these words of Bai Ping's carried a rueful sorrow. This made Mei Yingzi realize all at once that her former assumption had been wrong; she looked at the pistol in her hand, let out a clever smile, and said with conviction:

"Then this gun is indeed yours." Mei Yingzi handed the gun to Bai Ping, and said further: "Please do not think I use the gun to threaten you—I only give the gun back to you, that is all."

Bai Ping did not take the gun; Mei Yingzi laid it by her pillow and said:

"I do not know why you meant to shoot him. Having shot him, why again did you go to save him?"

"It is just as you a moment ago pointed the pistol at me, and now give it back to me."

"Do you think so?"

Bai Ping did not answer, and mused a long while; then suddenly, quick as lightning, she leaned up and stretched her right hand out from under the quilt—there had been a gun in her grip all along—and, aiming it at Mei Yingzi, said:

"Mei Yingzi, today we should settle our accounts once for all."

"Speak, then." "You once searched my room; you once took note of my movements; you made use of Xu to keep watch on me, and even had him steal my things.... Is that not so?"

"That you need only ask yourself—whether you have any background worth my noticing." "My question is not there. I am not in the least afraid of your noticing," said Bai Ping. "The question is under what name you were making use of Xu; he might have died without ever knowing what he was doing."

"But you did not let him die."

"That is because, even at the point of death, he still did not know he was guilty toward the nation."

"The nation?" said Mei Yingzi. "I recall that you too are Chinese."

"But what of you?"

"I am American," said Mei Yingzi. "I think we are the allied peoples of the two shores of the Pacific."

"Then, to be plain, Mei Yingzi—if you cannot prove that what you say is true, you need not think to walk out of here." So saying, Bai Ping unfastened the gold chain at her neck with her left hand and tossed it to Mei Yingzi, saying:

"Open that heart-locket; this is my identity." Mei Yingzi opened the heart-locket, on whose end Bai Ping's name was engraved; inside was a photograph of a woman of some fifty-odd years, evidently Bai Ping's mother.

"Beneath the photograph," said Bai Ping.

Just then Ah Mei knocked outside, and Bai Ping, changing to a gentle tone, said:

"Ah Mei, get us a little coffee and some refreshments; we'll be out presently."

By now Mei Yingzi was at ease; she gave the gold chain back to Bai Ping just as it was, and then took from about her own neck a string of pearls, chose out one bead, unscrewed it from the middle, and showed Bai Ping the cross-section of that pearl.

Bai Ping looked at it closely, then laid down the pistol and, like a little bird, flew into Mei Yingzi's arms; she clasped Mei Yingzi's cheeks and kissed her little bird-like lips. They embraced each other, and for a good while said nothing, hot tears welling from the eyes of each.

Mei Yingzi told me this course of things in a low, calm, harmonious voice; at the last, her eyes glinting with tears, but smiling, she said:

"It was your wounds that bought our light."

"If that is true, then it is my glory."

"Bai Ping and I have already exchanged a good deal of valuable intelligence."

"I ought to boast that this is the price of my wounds," I said, laughing.

Then Mei Yingzi showed a peaceful, lovely smile, as if granting my words, and gently asked after my hurts.

The tense worry that had gathered and knotted in my heart while I awaited Mei Yingzi had now long since vanished like smoke; what floated up in my heart in that instant was the joy of victory, the calm of peace. I looked at Mei Yingzi's smile, showing its almond-tinted front teeth, at her bright forehead, her fine bold brows, her radiant eyes, and I seemed to have a foreboding that, after she and Bai Ping had joined forces, how many seeds of victory would blossom into flowers as lovely as Mei Yingzi herself.

We began to talk at our ease—the first such frank and natural talk in all our friendship. I learned many things I had not known, saw solved the questions that had until now gone unanswered, saw confirmed the correctness of many of my guesses and corrected many errors in my suppositions.

It turned out that Stephen's original plan had been to find, among the dancing-girls who came close to the Japanese, one or two usable people. But because of the barriers of language and of race, on a certain occasion he made use of me.

After he discovered Bai Ping, he took her for a usable person; yet in her doings he suspected her of being an important talon of the enemy, and so first came the work of surveillance—the evening party at Mrs. Stephen's house was precisely the stratagem to lure Bai Ping in deeper. Naturally Mrs. Stephen was only a name for the work in Shanghai, and bore no marriage-tie to Stephen; and after this, the greater part of this work was handed over to Mei Yingzi.

Mei Yingzi at first made use of me, but later, seeing that Bai Ping and I were on too good terms, and fearing that I might on the contrary be used by Bai Ping, she deliberately drew close to me and, on the contrary, urged me to leave Bai Ping. After her journey to Hangzhou, her work of watching Bai Ping had turned into counter-espionage work. Several times Mei Yingzi had found on Bai Ping the very records of American naval intelligence, and later found the records of Japanese naval intelligence too, and so concluded she was an operative of the Japanese Army Ministry, with something of jealousy and opposition toward the Japanese Navy Ministry as well; Bai Ping's being shot at was perhaps the work of the Japanese Navy Ministry's hand—though at first Mei Yingzi had supposed it the work of Chinese patriots. The next day Mei Yingzi took the chance of Bai Ping's absence to search her room, but found nothing at all. It turned out that the few things Bai Ping meant to keep were entirely inside those two trunks of so-called old books and shabby men's things—the ones set in the alcove off the room I lived in, and labelled as another's goods left in deposit. On Christmas Eve, just as Mei Yingzi was about to risk stealing the document she wanted, she suddenly found the document in Bai Ping's handbag; this document was by no means an easy thing to come by, and Mei Yingzi supposed it something the Japanese Navy Ministry, or Umetake in his own person, had had Bai Ping secretly carry to someone in the Army Ministry. In fact the document Mei Yingzi had seen in Bai Ping's hand was a false one—Bai Ping had copied the form and content of that kind of document, meaning to exchange it for the real one, so that the enemy would not discover the theft. This real document Bai Ping later, of course, obtained through the exchange with ease; by the time it fell to me to steal it, it was of course already the genuine article—

Now, all of all this, every veil between Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping, had been wholly drawn back; it was as if the cloud-layer between star and star had been shone through with light, as if the frost-fog between the void of heaven and the great sea had been struck open by thunder and lightning; now was the time to watch how these two orbs would meet and merge and circle through the universe, how the void of heaven and the great sea would mirror each other and shine clean through the breast.

At last Mei Yingzi told me that Bai Ping, over the matter of shooting and wounding me, felt very guilty, and so did not wish to come and see me together with her, but meant to come alone tomorrow to apologize to me. She said that in this Bai Ping was still a woman possessing all the marks of a woman—words that made a deep impression on me. After Mei Yingzi had chatted idly a long while and gone, it dawned on me suddenly that what Mei Yingzi had shown today was likewise a woman possessing all the marks of a woman—and this was something I had never felt in the past at all.

I smoked a cigarette, without being aware that I was, all alone, smiling. Until the nurse reminded me:

"Mr. Xu, what are you smiling at, all by yourself?"
