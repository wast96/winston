## Chapter 52

Through all these perils and turns and hardships my mind had never once settled; I had no memories, no longings; no thoughts, no reckonings. But now, as Mei Yingzi and Cishan talked together, I recalled how, a few hours before, we had argued at Bai Ping's, and set out from there, and driven straight on in the car — the pistol — Bai Ping — the car door — a white shape and a black shape — the gunshots — the cry — all leaping and flashing by in an instant in my mind, my hand trembling on the gun in my pocket. I began to think of Bai Ping. She was dead! Was she dead? It was a true question; I could not believe it, and yet could not but believe it. Might she still be alive in that silver room? Might she never have come out at all? A thing once done is done, with no way to undo it, no way to turn time back and let us do it over — but Bai Ping might not be dead; she might be wounded, might even now be crying out in agony in the enemy's hands. And then I saw Stephen: his deep-purple lips, his lightless eyes, his stiff and gaunt frame — and before I knew it my hands and feet were trembling, my cheeks burning; I wanted to weep aloud, to wail, but again I held myself down. An unspeakable fire in me set me shaking, and at last I cried out:

"Bai Ping! Bai Ping!" In an instant hot tears burst from me, and, covering my face with my hands, I could not hold back my grief.

After a long while Mei Yingzi suddenly gripped my arm and, shaking it, said:

"Be strong!"

But that voice, too, was caught in sobs. I seemed to have wept out a little of what had been stored in my breast, and, raising my head, I saw the tears still hanging on Mei Yingzi's cheeks. Cishan and her mother watched us from either side, seeming to want to console us and yet not daring to. I began to pull myself together and wiped my eyes with my handkerchief. But somehow Mei Yingzi leaned against the gunwale, her eyes closed, her brows lifted, tears stealing down from her long, soft lashes; she showed not a trace of expression, and made not a sound. I could find no way to comfort her, and only said:

"Mei Yingzi, it's full daylight now — we ought to be thinking about how to get away."

Mei Yingzi made no answer; and somehow I suddenly saw that Cishan, too, was weeping in her artless way, and that her mother was wiping away tears with her handkerchief. A human heart can sometimes answer another so naturally as this — and sometimes it can be utterly numb. Mei Yingzi wiped her tears with her handkerchief, but still did not stir, her eyes still closed. She said:

"Let me be still a while."

So I asked Cishan's mother whether their boat needed loading, or had to go elsewhere. She told me they had unloaded their cargo the day before and had meant to pick up whatever trade they could today and go back, but that now they were entirely at our disposal; and I asked her to let it to us for the time being, saying that whatever the day's hire, we would give them rather more than the usual rate. Just then I noticed the money on the table had still not been taken away, and I said:

"Why hasn't this money been put away?"

And I handed it to her, placing it in her hands. The old woman took it, saying:

"Even to hire our boat, you don't need all this money."

"Keep it, keep it," I said. "And presently buy us a little something to eat."

"Cishan," said the old woman, "you go and cook some rice porridge first, and I'll go and buy some things."

But Mei Yingzi now suddenly roused herself and said:

"Wait a moment." And then, to Cishan: "You may cook us a little porridge first, but don't buy anything for now."

Cishan nodded, but glanced again at her mother.

Mei Yingzi rose, drew Cishan's mother down to sit beside her, and said:

"Granny, you've been so good to us that we'll never forget your kindness; but if you truly mean to save us, you must do exactly as I say in everything."

"Of course, of course," said Cishan's mother.

"Truly?"

"I'm no Japanese, after all, and you've been so good to us, so…"

"Thank you," said Mei Yingzi. "So: when you go ashore, first, you must on no account tell anyone you have guests here; second, whoever speaks to you, whatever they say, you simply feign ignorance; third, when you buy vegetables, do it just as usual, and don't buy anything special — this is not mere politeness; you must understand that if you buy too much, someone may take it into their head to question you; fourth, when you go up, walk about a good deal, don't ask this and that, but best go and see for yourself whether there's any road not sealed off; and last, buy us two sets of the cloth-padded jackets and blue cloth trousers you ordinarily wear — one man's set for him to wear, one woman's for me — mind our sizes first — and two pairs of cloth shoes as well." So saying, she opened her handbag again and gave her money: "This is for the clothes."

Cishan's mother took the money and said, with great openness:

"Set your mind at rest — I'll do everything as you say. I understand already. Set your mind at rest."

So Cishan's mother took up a basket and went off over the stern, and we watched her recede, stepping along the gunwales of the neighboring boats. Mei Yingzi grew dejected again; she was silent, not a sound out of her, sitting speechless. By now there were confused noises all around, and the boat rocked a little now and then. I was drowned in a welter of feelings, memories, plans, and suppositions — a thousand kinds of emotion twisted together, grief, worry, buried resentment, anger — until Cishan set the porridge on the plank table and called us to eat, and only then did Mei Yingzi rouse herself again. Cishan seemed unwilling to eat with us, but Mei Yingzi pulled her to it by force.

Big bowls of porridge, little bowls of dried salted turnip. Mei Yingzi surprised me: she ate a full bowl as if quite used to it. I was not hungry, but the heat of the porridge seemed to give me warmth and courage, and I ate a bowl and a half; Cishan too ate a bowl and a half.

After the meal Mei Yingzi and I began to have a little spirit back. Mei Yingzi asked Cishan for hot water and scissors, and had me cut off her hair.

Cishan brought out a big tin that had once held a hundred cigarettes, of the Great Britain brand, so old by now that a red woolen cord was bound around it — the same color, I noticed, as the cord on her braid — and inside were her sewing and washing things. Opening it, she took out for us a mirror and a pair of scissors. The mirror's frame was made of tinplate, and set into the back was a colored stage photograph of Mei Lanfang in "The Heavenly Maiden Scatters Flowers." Mei Yingzi took it and looked at it, then set it on the table and handed me the scissors, directing me in the mirror where to begin cutting.

In all my friendship with Mei Yingzi we had never been too distant, in our work or in our fellowship, but a situation like today's was the first of its kind. Pressed close behind her, I saw her lovely face in the mirror; Cishan's mirror was not flat enough, and in its wavering odd expressions kept surfacing, and we both laughed in understanding. A strange feeling rose in me, as though the distance between Mei Yingzi and me had, in an instant, shrunk a great deal. Looking back on it afterward, I felt the cause lay in this: that in the past, even when the two of us were alone, whether at work or at play, our moods had never been so at one, never met in a matching so clear and free of dust. In the past our thoughts had not been so simple, our aims had never been wholly the same, our responses to the world and our ties to people had not stood on quite the same ground; but now it was as though, for a little while, we had come utterly loose from the world, and the perils we had been through, the pain and terror and grief we felt, were now wholly the same, and what we now sought — how to come safely out of danger — was one and the same too; and it was these that gave us the feeling that our lives were bound together — a thing that came, between Mei Yingzi and me, just that once, that one time only, and that never came again after.

Following Mei Yingzi's directions, I cut away the curled and permed hair at the back of her head. She began to wash her face and hair, and at last combed it out; glancing at Cishan's fringe, she trimmed her own brow with the scissors too, and then I saw her let down an even fringe, in one rhythm with her lovely brows. — Mei Yingzi was beautiful through and through, I thought.

Then, somehow, she found in Cishan's dressing box a pair of gilt-worn silver earrings, and asked to borrow them; Cishan made her a present of them, and she thanked her, bent the wires to pierce them, and fixed them at her ears; then she told me to look and say whether she was no longer Mei Yingzi. But though she had wiped off the paint and powder, though she had let down a fringe, though she had put on the earrings, she was Mei Yingzi still. I noticed that at the top of these silver earrings were two characters for "long life," and by some foreboding, or superstition, or perhaps mere association, the phrase "lucky earrings" came quickly to my mind.

At once I recalled the jade-green drops at Bai Ping's ears, and how well they had matched the jade-green trim on her black dress; yet the moment she appeared, they had caught the eye and stirred in me an unspeakable unease. And now Mei Yingzi's earrings matched her clothes just as well, yet did not catch the eye at all, as though what she had done was precisely what I felt it ought to be; and the two characters for long life satisfied some innate need in me, as if they were a charm against calamity and misfortune. There are many in the world who are not superstitious, yet who will have nothing to do with any unlucky occasion, and this, I think, comes of the same innate need I have — a need with no reason to it, only an instinctive ease with a first impression. Just then I very much wanted to cry out, "Lucky earrings!" — but at the thought of Bai Ping's voice I swallowed it down at last. I said:

"For me, Mei Yingzi, even were you to melt into a liquid or dissolve into a gas, by my feeling alone I'd know you were always Mei Yingzi."

"Well then." Mei Yingzi smiled, showing her small almond-tinted teeth, and said: "It's a mercy you're not my enemy."

It was already past ten, and Cishan's mother had still not come back. We sat on her bunk, our feet and legs wrapped in her cotton quilt. In the boat's awning there was nothing to do, and the cold felt keener, and we were deeply afraid something had happened to Cishan's mother; a myriad unease filled our hearts, so that we could raise no appetite for talk, and if now and then we said a word, it was only to break the dreadful loneliness, with no special meaning to it. When Mei Yingzi had said, for the fifth time, "Why hasn't she come back yet," she closed her eyes to rest. She had been tired enough all through the night, and ought to have had some rest; but I believed she could not sleep, no more than I could. I thought hazily of Bai Ping — in my faith she was not, after all, dead; she seemed still to be living in the silver room, in the lavish, splendid world of society, at the very heart of a crowd of ugly enemies. I thought I could go to her at once; if only I had a pair of wings, I could fly to her; I longed to go straight to her house — but had the enemy already searched it? And Ah Mei? Carried off? Put to the torture? Then I would be marked by them at once — I had stayed at her house, I had often come and gone in her company, and they would want me straightaway; I was desperate to learn Ah Mei's whereabouts first, I must go, I must go — but Cishan's mother had still not come.

Many of the boats at the bow were moving about, and, fearing the people outside would notice us, I asked Cishan to draw that awning up a little. It did not block my view outward, though: the sky so overcast, the water so muddy, the far bank a ragged jumble of grass sheds, and crowds of people, filthy-headed and grimy-faced, in tattered clothes, coming and going over the rubbish heaps to left and right. Cishan told me these were all takers of white powder. A poisoned crowd, they had come to be no better than refuse, unable to labor with their bodies or their minds, without a single desire — no place too filthy would seem dirty to them, no food too dainty would seem anything but ordinary; but they must have eight jiao a day, four jiao in the morning and four in the afternoon, waiting for the white-powder peddler to come. The peddler came twice a day, always at a set time, and if now and then he was an hour late, a whole great crowd of them could not hold themselves together; day after day they waited for the white-powder peddler as one waits for a god. When the peddler came, his bag was full of four-jiao packets of white powder — no bigger, a packet, than a thumb — and at the sight of him the addicts swarmed forward; only in that instant did they still show the courage of men, still show a human will to live, for in their whole lives this was their one desire. Once they had breathed in that one packet of poison, they had no further meaning to their living: they would not be robbers, nor thieves; at best they were pilferers with a four-jiao desire, and beggars with an eight-jiao desire. Their lives held only the present — no past, no future; no memory, no hope. Now and then they picked two dice off the rubbish heaps and gambled with those too, but only to make up the sum of four jiao; money above four jiao they did not know what to do with, and money below four jiao they counted as worthless — and so they lived, day after day!

Within my sight, now, they stirred over there, in heaps and clusters, hands drawn in, waists bent, backs hunched, legs crooked; dragging broken shoes, wearing little caps; some lying, some sitting, some standing, some squatting, some hunting for cigarette ends — but no jesting, no talk, no dealings between them —

And this too is called a life! This too is called a life!

A little after eleven, to my anxious longing, Cishan's mother came. Mei Yingzi roused herself at once too. Seeing Mei Yingzi in her made-over guise, the mother stared a moment, then laughed despite herself. Mei Yingzi asked her how the blockade stood, and she told us that on the through-streets there was nothing but barbed wire, with no way past at all; but that in front of the shops there were only rope barriers, and though Japanese soldiers kept watch, there were always moments of slackness — one could slip through a rope barrier and cut through a shop, in at its front door and out at its back. This was the best plan, but for now it was quite impossible, for so soon after the incident the enemy's watch was extremely strict; her thought was that it would be best, two or three days from now, for her to lead the way and beg the use of someone's shop.

"But we can't possibly wait that long," said Mei Yingzi.

"But on the roads there are only Japanese soldiers, and armored cars going back and forth; the moment you set out, they'd see you and question you."

"Then what about at night?"

"At night the people in the shops are all asleep — who would open a door for us?"

"Then," said Mei Yingzi after some thought, "might we trouble you, granny, to find a shop first and make contact — give them a little money and have them leave a door on the latch at night?"

"I've no shop I know well, either." Cishan's mother hesitated a moment, then said: "And to make contact like that would only rouse suspicion — they'd think you were no ordinary travelers but must be fugitives, and then, never mind their fear of trouble, they might even go and inform on you. People's hearts these days really can't be relied on!"

So we fell silent; we granted, without a word, that Cishan's mother was right, and could only think it over slowly.

But Mei Yingzi began to sink into dejection again, and I did not know what she was thinking.

"I think you'd best stay on the boat a few days," said Cishan's mother. "Set your mind at rest, at rest about everything — I'll stake this old life of mine to save you, that's all."

Her words moved me greatly, but I had no fitting words to show my thanks. She said again:

"I can go and look every day, and when they slacken off a little we'll be able to slip across."

By now Cishan had long since taken her mother's bamboo basket and bundle of clothes. The bundle was wrapped in only a single sheet of newspaper, which did not cover it fully, and was tied around with a hemp cord; Cishan was untying it, and she took out the woman's clothes and said to Mei Yingzi:

"Best try the clothes on."

Seeing agreement in Mei Yingzi's face, I moved over to the bamboo stool on the other side; Mei Yingzi had Cishan hold up the cotton quilt as a screen, and I knew she was changing her clothes over there.

Just then I badly wanted a smoke; I had not had a cigarette since leaving Bai Ping's the night before, and did not know whether I still had any on me — but luckily there were four in my pocket. So I lit up and waited.

Only when I had finished the cigarette did Cishan take the quilt away, and there appeared a girl in a black jacket and blue trousers. The trouser-legs were a touch too short, and I saw she still had on her old silk stockings. But she seemed to have noticed it herself already, and said:

"I forgot to buy stockings." At that Cishan eagerly hunted through her own bundle for stockings to give her, and I watched her sit at the outer edge of the bunk to change her stockings and put on her shoes — a pair of black cloth shoes a little too big.

At last she was fully got up. She came and stood before me, seeming to feel herself quite new too, and for an instant her spirits were bright. But what impression did she make on me? Mei Yingzi was Mei Yingzi still, and all the clothing in the world seemed but an adornment to her. I said:

"Too beautiful — any cloud only sets off the brightness of the sun!"

She smiled, said nothing, and sat down on the bamboo chair, taking up the mirror on it to look; but I saw the platinum watch still on her wrist, and I said:

"There's something too much still, isn't there — what is it?"

She seemed to notice it herself too, gave a little smile, and quickly took off the watch and tucked it into an inner pocket; then she asked me for a cigarette. I handed her one, and lit another myself, and said:

"There's one left now — I won't smoke it until I have to."
