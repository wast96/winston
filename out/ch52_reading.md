## The Battle of Katsunuma

Toshizō, a single rider, threading the mountain paths, splashing across the valley streams, tearing through village after village like a gust of wind, rode for the Nappa-tai headquarters at Kanagawa.

"A plea for reinforcements."

{j} Beyond this there was no way to win in Kōshū.

(Will Kondō hold out until then?)

{j} No, Kondō would manage it. Somewhere in Toshizō there lived the conviction that, when it came to fighting, in all the present age there were none so strong as himself and Kondō. The history of the Shinsengumi in Kyoto would prove it.

The night deepened, and before long morning drew near.

Toshizō rode for his life.

Luckily it was through snow. The view was pale and white, and even without a lantern there was no great inconvenience.

As he crossed the Kobotoke Pass, all around went suddenly white. The sun had risen.

At that same hour Kondō, who had put up for the night with the Komakai headman's house for his headquarters, came out at his ease into the morning sun.

He began to stroll in the garden.

The headman who gave him lodging,

{j} (An hatamoto by the name of Ōkubo Yamato appears in no register of arms, but he is a company's commander for all that,)

{j} marveled to himself, so it is said.

After making a circuit of the grounds, Kondō gathered ten troopers, handed each a writ of the same wording, and,

{j} "Go to the villages hereabouts and raise soldiers with all speed,"

{j} and sent them off.

On the writ, in Kondō's own hand, it read:

Those who exert themselves for the sake of the House of Tokugawa shall, upon the restoration of its fortunes, be rewarded as they merit.

Ōkubo Yamato Masayoshi

so it ran.

Kondō believed in the restoration of the Tokugawa's fortunes, and had not yet given up the dream of a million koku in Kōshū.

It seemed there were many spirited men of the same dreaming cast as Kondō in the farm-villages of Kōshū too, for by dusk some twenty strapping-looking fellows had gathered.

Among them was a young man of a distinctly wary eye, and the other Kōshū men held back before him with great deference.

"Who are you, then?"

{j} Kondō's eye fell on him at once.

"Amemiya Keijirō,"

{j} he answered, brazen-faced.

"Are you permitted a surname?"

"I am indeed,"

He was the son of a small village headman of Higashi-Yamanashi in Kōshū.

Kondō asked further.

"Seeing your crest, a circle enclosing the character for 'above,' it is a house-crest I do not know; is there some lineage to it?"

"My house-founder was Amemiya Yamashiro-no-kami Masashige, a general under Takeda Shingen; after the fall of the Takeda we hid in the countryside three hundred years and served as risei, that is, as village headmen. Now, meeting this time of strife under heaven, I mean by all means to win renown, raise up my house, and lift my ancestors' martial name,"

It was a fearful Kōshū accent.

"That is admirable,"

{j} and Kondō, too, drew himself up. He seemed to be coming, little by little, into the mood of a warring-domains commander himself.

"We of the Kōyō Chinbutai are entrusted by the house of the former shogun (Yoshinobu) with the disposal of a million koku in Kōshū. Drive off the Western army and cut it away in fine style, and you shall have, each according to his work, a full reward,"

"A grateful thing,"

"I would make you captain of the Kōshū band; the rest of you gentlemen have no objection?"

"None,"

{j} they chorused.

This Amemiya Keijirō, at this time, seems to have thought in earnest of a Kōshū plunder.

In the years after, his fortunes turned about. In the thirteenth year of Meiji, judging that the demand for bread was sure to rise, he built a wheat-flour mill at Fukagawa in Tokyo and made a great profit; then, thrusting his neck into every sort of speculation, he succeeded at nearly all of them. He was jailed, it is true, in the graft scandal over the Tokyo city water-pipes, but after his release he ran about laying the city tramways, and, mixed up with the Kawagoe Railway, the Kōbu Railway, the Hokkaidō Colliery and the like, gained a vast fortune. He died in the forty-fourth year of Meiji.

"Now then, Amemiya, to business,"

{j} and he pointed to Katsunuma on the map.

"Here I would have you set up a barrier with your unit,"

Katsunuma was a post-town where the road came down from these Komakai mountains into the Kōfu basin, a bare three ri off.

Kondō meant to make this his forward line of defense, and, as soon as Toshizō's reinforcements arrived, to push out from Katsunuma to Kōfu Castle, five ri beyond.

Amemiya and his men piled the timber for the palings onto a cart, shouldered the Minié rifles Kondō had given them, and went down the mountain with a grand and stately air.

"Tcha,"

{j} Harada Sanosuke clicked his tongue at Amemiya's too-stately retreating back.

"A fire-looter," he must have wanted to say.

Kondō further decided to push his headquarters a little forward, and, seeing Kashio (now taken into the town of Katsunuma) as a fastness, resolved to throw up field-works there.

Kashio was a mountain village along the highway, looking down now on the Kōfu basin at its very feet; a fastness indeed.

He set his position on the hill east of the village (Mount Kashio), took the water of the Shinganzawa for a moat, and cut away the highway bridge.

Further, he hauled two cannon up onto the hill so as to rake the highway below at a slant, and planted abatis here and there along the road.

***

Meanwhile, into the hands of the Imperial commander Itagaki Taisuke, who had entered Kōfu Castle, intelligence came thick and fast.

"At Kashio the eastern army keeps showing itself,"

{j} was the word.

"That fellow, the one called Ōkubo Yamato,"

This name he had looked up in the register of arms and asked after among the old shogunal men at the surrender of Kōfu, but its true owner remained unknown to the end.

Itagaki and the Tosa men hated the Shinsengumi most bitterly of all. Were it known to be Kondō, there was ground enough not to let him off; for if you sorted by domain the men the Shinsengumi had cut down in Kyoto, there were more Tosa men, if anything, than Chōshū. Of harm to Satsuma men there was none at all.

By one report, Itagaki had heard the enemy general's name was,

"Kondō Yūhei,"

{j} as well. In fact Kondō used two different aliases; and even now Itagaki never dreamed it was Kondō Isami.

In any case, Itagaki chose five of the Tosa domain's picked commanders and sent them forward:

Tani Moribe;

Kataoka Kenkichi (later a man of the Freedom and People's Rights Movement and Speaker of the House of Representatives);

Ogasawara Kenkichi;

Hase Shigeki;

Kitamura Chōbei.

Tani Moribe, this being the first enemy the Imperial army had met since Toba-Fushimi, took a cautious line and loosed scouts busily to feel out the enemy's state; but there were rumors that they numbered a thousand men, and rumors again that some tens of thousands were coming up behind, and he could make nothing certain of it.

All of it was false report that Kondō had flung out toward the villages.

"In any case, the thing is to strike them,"

{j} said the gun-captain Kitamura Chōbei.

And so, with Kitamura's artillery at the head, they began to advance toward Katsunuma. In this age the guns had a short range, so it was the common thing for them to go at the head of the army.

By now the sky had cleared, and the snow of hill and field was beginning to melt.

When they entered the post-town of Katsunuma, Kitamura Chōbei, boldly taking five or six men, rushed the two cannon forward and came out into the center of the town.

At the center of that highway stood the hastily-made barrier. Its garrison was Amemiya Keijirō and some ten men of the Kōshū band.

Kitamura Chōbei, his red shaguma streaming, walked up to the paling.

"Who was it set a paling across this public highroad of the realm? Open it,"

{j} he said slowly, as though passing the time of day.

Within the paling Amemiya came forward and,

{j} "It cannot be opened,"

{j} he said flatly.

"Oh? And why is that?"

"I hold this by my captain's order. Without my captain's order it cannot be opened,"

"And your captain's name?"

"I do not know it,"

"So. Then there is no help for it,"

Kitamura, to the gun behind him,

{j} "Ready to fire,"

{j} he flung the command, and, springing under the eaves of the inn on the left, ordered: fire.

A roar—

{j} and the four-kin mountain gun spat fire.

When the smoke of the discharge had settled, there was no one inside the paling: far off, Amemiya Keijirō and his ten were fleeing head over heels. The shell had passed over their heads and burst beyond them (Amemiya, it seems, simply kept on running like this and went to Yokohama).

Kitamura and the rest threw open the paling and sprang in, and searched the whole post-town of Katsunuma, but there was not a single enemy soldier left.

Asking the townsfolk,

{j} "The defenders were only that lot,"

{j} they said.

This firing at the Katsunuma post-town was, as it turns out, the first cannon-shot of the eastern campaign.

Tani Moribe came up and,

{j} "The enemy are on Mount Kashio. They will have built the paling with this Katsunuma for an outpost line. And if that was a mere ten men or so, the main body at Kashio can be no more than twenty times as many,"

{j} so he reckoned. It was near enough the mark.

At once, he advanced.

Kondō was up on Mount Kashio.

"Here they come,"

{j} and Harada Sanosuke craned up.

"Kondō-sensei, if it's red shaguma, they're the Tosa lot,"

Satsuma black, Chōshū white, Tosa red, so it was fixed.

All three domains had gone over to the Western style, yet even so their ways of fighting had their marks: with the same rifle-drill, Chōshū fired lying, Satsuma fired standing, and Tosa broke off firing at once and went in with the sword.

"Tosa, is it,"

Kondō had no particular knowledge of the enemy, but what came back to him was Kyoto.

"At the Ikedaya I cut down a good many Tosa fellows. Tokoroyama Gokichirō, Ishikawa Junjirō, Kitazoe Yoshimaro, Mochizuki Kameyata…"

"So we did,"

{j} Harada Sanosuke too wore a dazed look, thinking of the old days.

"And then, first man up Tennōzan,"

{j} put in Nagakura Shinpachi, at his side.

In the Hamaguri Gate affair of the first year of Genji, the Chōshū army fled, and a band of their rōnin shut themselves up on Tennōzan. The shogunal army surrounded it, and the Shinsengumi were first to go charging up.

But there was nothing there save the suicide-corpses of seventeen loyalists, Maki Izumi among them. Of these the Tosa rōnin were Matsuyama Shinzō, Chiya Kikujirō, Nose Tatsutarō, Andō Shinnosuke.

"How the times change, and change,"

Kondō wore the look of a man not yet woken from a dream of the old days.

"Nagakura. At the Ikedaya, at the start, I went in with only five, you and the rest of us. And thought nothing of it, even so,"

Now it was different.

In those days there were three thousand watch-troops of the domains, mustered by the Kyoto Protector, and within that cordon of guard the Shinsengumi could cut in to their hearts' content.

In those days they could work because they rode the current of the times; now the other side had come riding it.

To put it in the terms of Kondō's Tennen Rishin-ryū, the difference in the two sides' "spirit-set" was great.

(Well, then, will it come to a fight or no?)

The hastily-raised soldiers had mostly fled, and those that remained clung to the mountainside and would not stir.

"Ogata,"

{j} Kondō called to Ogata Shuntarō, who had drawn himself close to the roadside below.

"The enemy are drawing near. It's about time we set the far side of the bridge alight,"

"Understood,"

Ogata had some ten farmer-soldiers take up torches, rushed the far side of the bridge, and in a trice set fire to several houses.

With a whoosh several columns of fire rose, and a rolling white smoke began to cover the front of Kondō's position. It was a tactic of the old school: it served as a smoke-screen, and it kept the houses from being used by the enemy's gun-troops.

Seeing this white smoke, Tani Moribe and the rest made out roughly where the enemy position lay.

"Let us split the men into three columns,"

{j} said Tani Moribe, gazing off at the lay of the enemy ground. The other commanders agreed.

Tani himself, with fifty men and two cannon, would go straight up the main highway.

Kataoka Kenkichi and Ogasawara Kenkichi, with five hundred men, would cross the Hikawa before the enemy front, climb the mountain on the right, and advance.

Hase Shigeki would climb the mountain on the left and press forward, raking the enemy on the height and on the highway.

"Then—"

{j} at Tani Moribe's nod, the commanders ran off in all directions to their own units and advanced at once. This nimbleness is the strength of organized domain troops.

Presently, reaching the east bank of the Hikawa, the two sides opened a furious exchange of fire.

Kondō stood bolt upright on the height.

(Is Toshi not back yet?)

He glanced round behind him, but unless a man were a demon-god he could not make the round trip to Kanagawa so soon as this.

"Fire, fire,"

Kondō was at the unaccustomed work of directing the shooting, but the hastily-gathered marksmen were in such a sorry state, loosing one round from the Minié and then running ten paces, that by no reckoning was it the posture of men making war.

"There's no help for it. Charge in,"

Kondō bellowed. But the old Shinsengumi officers had all become directors of gun-squads, scattered here and there, so that they made no knit body of close-fighters.

At Kondō's side, as his "household men," were plain troopers from the Kyoto days: Mishina Ichirō, Matsubara Shintarō, Sakuma Kensuke and others.

These drew their swords together.

On the ridge in front the enemy had already crawled up, so near that their very eyes and noses could be plainly seen.

"Charge!"

Kondō ran. His right hand being useless, he held his sword in his left.

The Tosa unit they collided with was Ogasawara Kenkichi's. Being only the van, they were but a dozen and more.

On the height it became a wild melee.

Kondō, left-handed though he was, worked fearsomely, cut down three Tosa soldiers in a moment, and raged on.

(Who is this?)

{j} thought Ogasawara Kenkichi. Ogasawara was called a marvelous hand with the spear, but of course he had not brought a spear onto the field.

He drew his sword and fought.

Trying to close with Kondō, he was blocked by one trooper (Matsubara Shintarō, perhaps).

Springing up, he cut Matsubara's shoulder. As Matsubara staggered, Imamura Wasuke, deputy captain of Ogasawara's unit, cut him down from behind and gave the finishing stroke besides. Examining this Matsubara's sword afterward, the strike-marks were all within five sun of the guard, which speaks of a fierce fight hilt to hilt.

Kondō, hard put to it as the enemy's numbers swelled and swelled,

{j} "Fall back,"

{j} at his single command, dove swiftly into the pine wood behind and began to retreat further, toward the Sasago Pass.

At the Sasago Pass he gathered his beaten men, meaning to deal one more blow to the enemy climbing to the attack, but Harada Sanosuke, with an uninterested face,

{j} "Let's not,"

{j} he said.

"So. Fall back to Hachiōji, is it,"

By the time they had drawn back to Hachiōji, the soldiers were down to a bare fifty.

"No good. Let us go back to Edo,"

{j} and here they disbanded the Kōyō Chinbutai, and the Shinsengumi, each changing into plain clothes, resolved to slip away to Edo by threes and fives.

About that time Toshizō, too, was hurrying toward Edo down the Tōkaidō.

Refused his reinforcements at Kanagawa, he had thought that now he would go back to Edo, appeal to the former shogun Yoshinobu, and borrow troops directly.

Of course, Toshizō did not dream that in Kōshū Kondō was already in headlong rout.
