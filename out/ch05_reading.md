## A Brief Study of the Early Organization of the Green Gang

{d} Li Shiyu

Of all the popular secret societies that have spread through China since the early Qing, the Green Gang was the most widespread and the most far-reaching in its influence. Except in its early days, and in certain special circumstances where it too played a progressive part, it was, generally speaking, always a great source of harm. In the Republican period above all, every reactionary faction at home, and the imperialist aggressors as well, had with it that murky relationship of mutual dependence in which each propped up the other's crimes. After the First Revolutionary Civil War it went further and became an important social pillar of Chiang Kai-shek's regime, so that, in the phrase of the day, "with the party, the government, the army, and the gang, every road runs through." In many cities of that time it would be no exaggeration to say that a "Green Gang world" had come into being. In the study of modern history, then, the Green Gang cannot but be treated as a subject of importance; and to study the modern Green Gang, one cannot but trace back the various features of its early organization. It is for this that I have put together the present essay from what I have seen and heard, limited as my competence is, and I do not know whether I have it right.

### I. The Early Organization of the Green Gang

How the early organization of the Green Gang arose, when and where it began, how it was organized, where its name came from, and who started it, are questions on which the histories leave no clear or systematic record. Yet among the *tongcao* handbooks that circulate within the gang there is no shortage of inquiries into its origins. Their compilers were for the most part men within the gang who knew a little of letters and put on airs of refinement, and the aim of their researches was no more than to father the gang on some ancient worthy and so make its lineage look long and deep; but some of their materials, once sifted and cross-checked, are still trustworthy enough to cite, or at least to draw upon.

The early organization of the Green Gang was originally a guild-like secret society among the boatmen of the Qing grain-tribute transport, and one must begin with that transport. Grain transport was the system by which, in feudal times, the land tax—grain—was carried to the capital; the boatmen called this levy the "tribute grain" or the "imperial grain." Under the Qing, the rulers gathered the grain squeezed out of Shandong, Henan, Jiangsu, Anhui, Jiangxi, Zhejiang, Hunan, and other provinces and shipped it, mainly by the Grand Canal, to Beijing, to be consumed by those rulers, their armies, and the parasites of their ruling clique. So both before and after it was called the "Green Gang," the gang always grew up with the great wharves along the grain-transport waterways as its base.

The volume carried was enormous: under the Qing, over 4 million piculs of grain went to Beijing each year, the grain boats regularly numbered more than 12,000, and the boatmen came to some 100,000 households, perhaps 200,000 or 300,000 people, who with their dependents made up the "millions who looked to it for their bread." To manage and control these boats and boatmen effectively, the authorities, following Ming precedent, set up a Governor-General of Grain Transport, who held both administrative and military powers. The troops he commanded were called the "transport garrison" or the "guard army," and their sole purpose was to hold the boatmen down and keep the transport secure. The transport organizations of the various regions were divided into many "guards" and "posts"; below the guards and posts were "links," and below the links, the gangs. This whole apparatus was also known collectively as the "grain gangs" or "grain-rice gangs." Each guard and post had a name—the Su-Song Guard, the Hangzhou Guard, the Yanzhou Post, the Jin-Qu Post, and so on. The names of the links below them seldom appear in the records. The gangs took the names of the guards and posts they belonged to—the Jiangxi Guard's Fortieth Gang, the Jiang'an Guard's Fifty-second Gang, and the like—though some went by gang names of their own, such as the Jiang-Huai-Si Head Gang, the Xingwu Sixth Gang, and the Jia-Bai Head Gang. The gangs were further distinguished, according to the prosperity of their region or the weight of the duties they bore, into "high gangs" and "low gangs." Each guard and post had its garrison commander, each gang its battalion officer; besides these there were military licentiates who "served with the gang," and every boat had its bannerman—variously called gang aides, transport aides, battalion officers, or bannermen—all of them petty rulers or their claws and fangs, though rather closer to the boatmen than the rest.

The members of this ruling apparatus made common cause and cheated in concert: they concealed and falsified their reports to those above, docked pay and levied extra charges on those below, and stopped at nothing in theft and smuggling, until the boatmen cried out day and night and the people could not bear the harassment. Problems of this kind had by then become a recognized dossier of their own, known as the "transport abuses." About these abuses the Qing authorities did not mince words. In the fourth year of Yongzheng (1726), for instance, an edict declared: "In the last several years, memorials from officials within and without on the lawless doings of the bannermen have run to no fewer than several hundred sheets." One may judge from this how grave the abuses were. Below I excerpt a few of their memorials to show something of the whole.

For a single grain boat to load its cargo and carry it to Tongzhou, it had to pass who knows how many tax stations and endure extortions beyond all rule, and this one drain alone would run to several hundred taels of silver:

"By what I have heard, the bannermen suffer at the loading-point, suffer in crossing the Huai, and suffer on reaching Tongzhou. What is the suffering at the loading-point? First, the vicious custom of 'buying the gang.' Gangs are high or low: in a high gang the men are well-off and easily make up their quota, in a low gang the men are poor and always fall short. When the boats are inspected for loading, a well-off bannerman bribes his way into a good gang, at a cost of two or three hundred taels… this is one hardship. Second, the corrupt fees of the minor stations. When a guardsman takes up a transport run, there are the standing gratuities to the guard officers and gang officers… under dozens of headings, too many to count… so that before the boat has even left its station he has spent fifty or sixty taels—another hardship."

"That the transport men run to lawlessness is all brought on by the squeeze of the officials and bannermen: at the time of transfer, the grain intendant issues the cash and grain and docks it at will… and the transport men's commandant, and the assistant prefect who oversees the transfer, extort still more; and once the boats set out, the military officers along the way find a hundred pretexts, in the name of hurrying them on, to demand more."

So heavy was the exploitation that at times the cargo of grain would not reach its destination before the carriage fee they had drawn was wholly spent. On the empty return they might even run clean out of food, and had no choice but to sell off their own belongings.

"Take the Ganzhou gang, always reckoned well-to-do, yet poor to the very bone. When it reaches Hexiwu for lightering [that is, where the water is shallow the grain must be shifted into small barges, and the hire of the barges must be borne by the original large boat], the rice is already loaded but the lightering charge cannot be met, and the battalion officer has to advance it for them, only so is the public business not delayed. On the empty return they have neither wages nor rice to eat, and there is nothing they will not sell… and the case of the gangs is much the same all round."

"I received the imperial charge to hasten the transport, and made careful inquiry. For several years now the returning empty boats have been frozen in… and having ruined themselves and it still not enough, they add their sons and daughters to the reckoning—their toil and misery are beyond words, and deeply to be pitied." With their goods all sold, they had even to sell their own children.

"For the garrison men lead the grain transport out in winter and back in winter, crossing lakes, river, and canal, spending themselves in toil and peril, hard beyond all measure… and the boatmen too are men in dire poverty, each getting a bare six taels of wages a year, poling, steering, and hauling, through mud and water, wind and rain, spending every sinew of their strength." Beyond the economic exploitation, then, the sheer physical toil was extreme.

The men who served as boatmen were mostly peasants who had lost their land and craftsmen thrown out of work, "men in dire poverty"; and "since the grain boats had a great many boatmen, all of them a bold and fierce sort, quick to answer one another's call and easily roused to act as one." Facing the grave conditions described above, they were not going to keep silent.

To form a secret society, then, to protect their own interests and struggle against the authorities, was the most natural thing in the world. Their societies were built on the very grain-gang organizations the authorities had set up in each locality, and kept the same names; anyone who had originally belonged to one of the grain gangs was entitled to join this secret society.

Such societies are said to have begun first among the grain gangs of the Anqing region, and only later to have spread gradually to the other grain gangs. Now the prefecture of Anqing was an important rice-producing region, comprising at that time six counties and having sixteen grain gangs, so that its being organized first is entirely likely; and the time this kind of society appeared can hardly have been later than the early Yongzheng years. For the earliest case-file now to be found in the archives concerning the suppression of secret societies among the boatmen dates from the fifth to the seventh years of Yongzheng (1727–1729).

After a fairly long stretch of development, by about the Jiaqing period the grain-gang boatmen of Anqing prefecture went on, alongside their original grain-gang organization, to found independently a secret society of their own called the "Anqing Fellowship of the Way"—the "Anqing Fellowship," "Anqing Gang," or "Qing Gang" for short. This was a more fully formed society than the earlier ones built on the local grain-gang structure. Its inner workings can no longer be known with any certainty today; we can glimpse only their broad outline from scattered notices in certain histories, checked against the relevant material in the gang's own *tongcao*.

Their leaders, for instance, were no longer called garrison commanders or battalion officers and the like, but "housemasters," "masters," "elders," "forebears." They fabricated a whole "line of transmission," dragging in various not necessarily relevant men as patriarchs, and even ranging the successive patriarchs of Buddhism among them, and solemnly claimed to be a branch of the Linji school of Chan Buddhism—which is where the gang's alternate name, the "Linji Association," later came from. Those who joined the Anqing Fellowship were also ranked, by the order in which they entered, into twenty-four generations, namely: "Qing, Jing, Dao, De, Wen, Cheng, Fo, Fa, Neng, Ren, Zhi, Hui, Ben, Lai, Zi, Xing, Yuan, Ming, Xing, Li, Da, Tong, Wu, Xue." In places where the boatmen gathered or lodged—in hermitages near the riverbank, and sometimes aboard the boats—they set up a kind of "Buddha-hall," with an incense table and the spirit-tablets of the patriarchs (of which more below), which they called an "incense hall"; a boat that carried one was called a "terrace boat," an "old-hall boat," a "general's boat," or a "peace boat." They laid down rules and observances, such as the "Ten Commandments of the gang" and the "Ten Prohibitions," which were regularly kept. They devised a set of rites, and a man joining had to go through a kind of quasi-religious baptism in the incense hall. They also invented many passwords, hand-signs, and secret marks by which gang men could communicate in secret, which they called "cut-mouth" argot and "watchwords." All of this naturally grew more elaborate as time went on.

The rise of the Anqing Fellowship was a great event in the history of the Green Gang. But the Anqing Fellowship was not yet the whole of the Green Gang's early organization; it can only be taken as one typical representative of it. In the years after the Anqing Fellowship was founded, the once merely local grain-gang secret societies joined one after another into this new organization, and its strength and standing grew by the day. It was only by about the Guangxu period that the grain-gang secret societies of the various regions—said to have numbered 128 and a half in all—were unified under it.

To suit the needs of unification, the name changed with it. The "Anqing Fellowship of the Way" became the "An-Qing Fellowship of the Way" or the "An-Qing Fellowship of Righteousness," and its short forms, the "Anqing Gang" and "Qing Gang," changed accordingly to the "An-Qing Gang" and the "Clear Gang," some also calling it the "Clear Sect." The shift from *qing* ("Qing/celebrate," as in Anqing) to *qing* ("clear/pure") plainly came about by a change in sound, but it also carried the sense of the "Qing dynasty" or "the Great Qing." As for the claim, made in some *tongcao*, that they were called the "Clear Gang" or "Clear Sect" because "Clear" (*qing*) stood first among the gang's twenty-four generation-names ("Qing, Jing, Dao, De…")—the view taken in *The Anqing Compilation*—or the claim that the term "Clear Sect" derived from the "Clear Water sect" of Wang Lun in the Qianlong reign—the view taken in *An Inquiry into the Origins of the Clear Sect*—these are pure contrivance. The word "An-Qing" ("pacify the Qing") is in fact of a piece with "uphold the Qing" and "aid the Qing," and the gang's passage from "Anqing" to "An-Qing" was much like the Boxers' turn from "oppose the Qing" to "uphold the Qing" and "aid the Qing." This is the usual course of popular secret societies and secret religions: once their organization has grown to a certain size, they inevitably wish to pass from the illegal to the legal. The earliest record using the name "Clear Gang" dates from the late Qing—for example, the *Report on the Investigation of Xu Huaili* of the twenty-sixth year of Guangxu (1900): "Among the salt-smugglers there have always been two kinds, the Clear Gang and the Encircling Gang; the Clear are the An-Qing Fellows of the Way." That was precisely the period of the Green Gang's great expansion.

As for why the "Clear Gang" (*qing bang*) then came to be called the "Green Gang" (*qing bang*), it was first of all a matter of homophone substitution, and possibly also a term set against the "Hongmen"—also called the "Red Gang"—which had appeared earlier and long coexisted with it, giving "Green Gang versus Red Gang," or "Green-and-Red Gangs" for short. Another possibility is that from their grain-gang secret-society days they had already called themselves *qingpi* ("green skins"), a word met with often in the archives—as in what the Manchu official Sebumo'e wrote in the sixteenth year of Daoguang (1836), in his *Report on the Arrest of the "Green-Skin" Grain Boatmen Returning Empty Who Blocked the Road and Seized Boats to Make Trouble*: "Further, the acting governor-general in charge of Huai matters, Lin Zexu, forwarded an imperial edict citing the censor Liu Menglan's memorial: the habitual bandits of the grain routes are styled 'green skins'… there being both boatmen fierce by nature and 'green skins' habitual in banditry." Having "styled themselves green skins" in the Daoguang years, in the Guangxu and Xuantong years they styled themselves the "Green Gang." Now the word *qingpi* carried the sense of a bold vanguard, a hero and a stout fellow, synonymous with what the common people called a "green-headed blade" or a "third green son." Spread by the gang's use of it, the word came into wide currency, appearing not only in documentary sources but in literary works, and even Mr. Lu Xun once discussed the "green skin" question. The word "Green Gang" appeared no later than the late Qing. In the third year of Xuantong (1911), Zhu Jiabao, in his *Memorial Requesting That the Green and Red Gangs of Anhui Be Punished on the Precedent of Hubei and Sichuan*, wrote: "In former days the various Green and Red gangs of society-bandits were everywhere and had long been a great scourge; and though they have been repeatedly hunted down and punished without the least leniency, the evil has its roots, and its stock and stump have not to this day been wholly rooted out."

The names "Anqing Gang," "An-Qing Gang," "Green Gang," and "Clear Sect," appearing as they did in different historical periods, did not replace one another in sequence but were used side by side; even the earliest term, "Anqing Gang," was still used by certain local gang heads after the appellation "Green Gang" had appeared. This can also be seen from the titles of the *tongcao* the various gang heads compiled and printed—as Lu Binsheng notes in *A Panorama of China's Dark Curtain*: "There are also the so-called 'Pan brothers'… in the north the 'Rivers-and-Lakes Eastern Sign,' in the south the Green Gang, the Anqing Gang, and so on." Examples are *The Anqing Compilation*, *The An-Qing Observances*, *An Inquiry into the Origins of the Clear Sect*, *The Green Gang's Compendium of the Grain Transport*, and the like.

The unification under the banner of "the Green Gang" was of course no true unification; the grain-gang organizations of the various regions still kept a relative independence. This can be seen not only from the confusion of *tongcao* titles cited above, but from the wide divergence of their contents. Their factional lines, their lineages of transmission, their argot and watchwords especially, were for the most part arbitrarily invented to magnify themselves and set themselves apart from the rest. This is also why Green Gang men, right down to recent times, still carried both a gang-head affiliation and a generation-rank to mark their standing. The Shanghai Green Gang boss Huang Jinrong, for example, had the Green Gang standing of "head of the Xingwu Sixth Gang, of the Tong generation." In truth the Green Gang and the grain-rice-gang organization had by then grown as far apart as heaven and earth, and the meaning of these gang-head affiliations and generation-ranks was, to the Green Gang men of recent times, quite beyond their ken.

As for who exactly founded the early organization of the Green Gang: at the very first, before the name "Anqing Fellowship of the Way" appeared, the early organization was simply the natural coming-together of the various gangs' boatmen on the base of the official grain gangs, with no unified leading body and no independent name of its own; it is therefore hard to say who founded it. But in all the Green Gang *tongcao*, when they come to the gang's origins, they say without exception that it was founded by three men—Weng, Qian, and Pan—in the Kangxi, Yongzheng, or Qianlong reign. And they say not only that these three founded the Green Gang, but that they "led the gang and set the grain transport in order," as though the early-Qing grain-transport system itself had been created by the three of them. Every kind of *tongcao* likewise says that the Green Gang's "ancestral shrine" stands outside the Wulin Gate at Hangzhou (the "ancestral shrine" meaning the hermitages built by Weng, Qian, and Pan when they preached), that Weng, Qian, and Pan each built a hermitage, and one kind of *tongcao* is even titled *The Precious Mirror of the Three Hermitages*. Every *tongcao* also says that the master of Weng, Qian, and Pan was surnamed Lu, that Lu's master was Patriarch Luo, the founder of the Luo sect, and that Luo's master was surnamed Jin. The spirit-tablets set up in the Green Gang's incense halls were not uniform from one gang head to another, but ran roughly to "Heaven, Earth, Ruler, Kin, and Teacher," "the Dragon Kings of the Four Seas," "the Old Official Masters," "Patriarchs Jin, Luo, and Lu," "Patriarchs Weng, Qian, and Pan"…; but the one that could never be left out was "the Spirit-Tablet of Patriarchs Weng, Qian, and Pan." Of the three, Pan was the chief, and there had always been a rule within the Green Gang that its members called one another "the Pan brothers." One kind of gang head even laid down that when gang men asked one another their surname, a man must not give his own surname directly but had to answer, "At home [that is, within the gang] my surname is Pan; abroad my surname is X [his real surname]." Another kind of gang head prescribed the answer, "Pan on my head, real surname X." It would seem, then, that the account of the three men Weng, Qian, and Pan founding the Green Gang's early organization may well have some basis in fact.

Now the three men Weng, Qian, and Pan really did exist, and are mentioned in a good deal of archival material on the suppression of the Luo sect among the boatmen; the fullest account is that of Cui Yingjie, Governor-General of Fujian and Zhejiang, in his *Deliberation on the Case of the Arrested Luo-Sect Hermitage Men Zhu Guanghui and Others*, of the thirty-third year of Qianlong (1768):

"The area of Gongchen Bridge, outside the Beixin Barrier of Hangzhou prefecture, has always been a mooring place for grain boats. In the late Ming, three men—one surnamed Qian and one surnamed Weng, both of Miyun, and one surnamed Pan, of Songjiang—came as sojourners to Hangzhou and together founded the Luo sect, each building a hermitage there, enshrining Buddha-images, keeping a vegetarian diet, and chanting the scriptures; hence the names Qian Hermitage, Weng Hermitage, and Pan Hermitage. Because the place lay hard by the grain-boat moorings, boatmen and others lodged in them, and so over time they took to the sect one after another… Each year when the grain boats returned empty, the idle boatmen all lodged in the several hermitages; the habit grew and was passed on and taken for a matter of course—this is the origin of the boatmen's taking to the sect… The boatmen lodging in the hermitages were not all of them Luo-sect men, but each year on a safe return they would each put up five fen of silver to buy incense, candles, and vegetarian offerings, and give thanks to the spirits at the hermitage. The keepers of the hermitages had always chanted a few scrolls of scripture a day, and those boatmen who had taken to the sect and chanted did as the keepers did, while those who could not chant merely burned incense and made obeisance with the other boatmen; there was nothing of gathering by night and dispersing by dawn, or of inflaming the people. Such is the present state of affairs, whereby Zhu Guanghui and the others of the twenty-two hermitages took to the Luo sect and gave lodging to the boatmen."

What this memorial records makes the relation between the Green Gang's early organization and the Luo sect perfectly clear. The meeting of the boatmen and the Luo sect was fortuitous, and at first confined to Hangzhou alone; there was no such thing as Weng, Qian, and Pan "leading the gang and setting the grain transport in order." There is a view in the historical field that the Green Gang was a branch of the Luo sect, and this memorial can also serve to clear that up.

Here, then, drawing on what this memorial records and comparing it with some of the Green Gang's internal legends, I shall try to reach conclusions on the question of the Green Gang's founders that come reasonably close to the facts. First, that the three men Weng, Qian, and Pan preached and built hermitages at Hangzhou is a fact; what they transmitted was the Luo sect, and boatmen were among its adherents. Second, the Luo sect did good service in linking and uniting the boatmen, and Weng, Qian, and Pan may indeed have organized the adherents into a religious body of a kind; the authorities, to control the boatmen's activities, brought pressure to bear on them under the pretext of suppressing the Luo sect. Third, the memorial does not mention that Weng, Qian, and Pan ever built a Green Gang early organization (meaning, of course, one before the Anqing Fellowship of the Way appeared), but neither can the possibility be ruled out—say, of a religious body linked by the Luo sect turning into a secret society of the boatmen. Fourth, if Weng, Qian, and Pan really did build a Green Gang early organization, then the legend's placing of it in the Kangxi or Yongzheng years is closer to the facts, and it cannot have been as late as the Qianlong years. In sum, checked against the official records, the Green Gang's internal legend of Weng, Qian, and Pan founding the gang is, apart from particular points not precise enough and still open to doubt, basically credible.

### II. The Mutual Aid and Resistance of the Green Gang's Early Organization

When the grain-gang boatmen organized themselves in secret, their first aim was to protect their own interests and to fight back against the exploitation and bullying of the authorities. They held closely together—in small things helping one another in daily life and work, in large things gathering in crowds to make trouble. Whether in official and private documents or in the gang's own *tongcao*, what is recorded shows this same spirit.

At Hangzhou, for instance, to solve the problem of the boatmen's board and lodging on the empty return, they set up hermitages of their own, some by taking over old temples and some newly built, with farmland outside the hermitage whose harvest went to the keeper's keep; there were also charity graveyards, where a boatman was buried when he died; and one grown old and unable to work could enter a hermitage to see out his days.

"The grain-boat boatmen are all people from Shandong, North Zhili, and other parts, who on the empty return had nowhere to lodge and, when sick or dead, nowhere for burial; hence the founding of the hermitages… Those now dwelling in the hermitages are all men grown old and sick, unable to serve as boatmen, who accordingly live in and keep the hermitages."

"In their idle, off-season time, because the halls take no rent but only a charge for meals, they all seek the convenience and come to the halls to lodge."

"…and so the hermitage became the boatmen's own property. And because there was not room enough to house them, they pooled funds and built more, to as many as several dozen hermitages. Outside each hermitage they set aside spare land, for the keeper's daily needs and as a charity graveyard for boatmen who died."

The boatmen's solidarity and mutual aid also showed in the "contract-transport" system they used in carrying the grain, which to a degree secured their livelihood (though the contract-transport system was a harsh instrument of exploitation and did great harm—of which more below). When a boatman committed some fault and was dismissed or put on the wanted list, they would take one another in and shelter one another.

"[A boatman] dismissed and put on the wanted list at the battalion officer's request—yet the garrison commander lets him be dismissed from this boat only to enter that one, dismissed from this gang only to enter another. Such being the abuse of the boatmen's contract-transport, how is it ever to be checked?"

As for this way of theirs of sharing hardship together, even after the name "Anqing Fellowship of the Way" had appeared, records survive in the documents that damn with faint praise. The following passage, for instance, records the state of the Anqing Fellowship in the Xianfeng years.

"They take those who entered earlier as seniors and those who entered later as juniors, already reaching to the fifth, sixth, and seventh generations. No one knows how many men there are to a generation, but all rely on passwords and generation-names as their token, and make contact wherever they go. Arriving anywhere with not a cent on them, they can get lodging and food; when something happens they all rise to it together, and where the password carries, help comes running at once, though they were never before acquainted."

Because the authorities' surcharges on the boatmen were too heavy, they banded together to demand higher carriage fees:

"They know that the prefectures and counties collect surcharges of as much as five or six tenths extra, and so find pretext to demand more. Transport aides and crooked bannermen make common cause. The prefectures and counties, fearing only a delay in the transfer, cannot but submit to their extortion, so that the gang fees have actually risen by stages to five or six hundred taels, even seven or eight hundred [where the original was only one or two hundred], and Su-Song is the worst of all."

If the trouble was fiercer still, it took the form of a petition (which the authorities called "suing the transport") or a riot (which they called "storming the granary" or "raising an uproar"); and because the scale was great, it sometimes left the rulers scorched of head and scalded of brow, sighing without end.

"The bannermen and boats seize on this to demand transfer fees, and the sums are vast… What is more, they play on the prefectures' and counties' weakness for surcharging and share in the fat of it; the moment they are crossed, they sue the transport in a body, or even gather in crowds to storm the granary."

"…At the least thing not to their liking, they even band together to bully the officials. Take the case of 'the Wujiang uproar' set down by the transport official Zhou Bushi—not only was the people's fat drained dry, but the dignity of the state was gravely wounded. When the transport comes to this, is there anything more to be said?"

In the Daoguang years the Green Gang's early organization took two forms, one on the boats and one on shore. Both often joined with the local drifting, propertyless men (whom the authorities called "roving-gang bandits") and with the lower petty clerks of the localities to set themselves against the authorities.

"The lairs within the gang are the boats where the old official masters entrench themselves, called 'lookout boats,' which hold scriptures and holy images; they take in disciples and gather money, coerce the bannermen, and are hand in glove with the roving-gang bandits—truly a bane within the gang… Their lairs along the way are all in league with the boatmen through the constables and river-runners of the riverside market towns, and among them are men of the Old-An [hermitage] and Pan-An [hermitage] sects mixing in, colluding with the boatmen and roving bandits and taking their cut, while the boatmen 'green skins' rely on them as a talisman of protection. When the local officials try to arrest the bandits, they cannot but charge the river-runners and constables with it—meaning to have them make the arrests, but instead being covered for by them—which is all the more a hidden peril within the gang, and ought most strictly to be forbidden."

The Green Gang's early organization sometimes also joined with "greenwood heroes" (whom the authorities called "worthless desperado bands") to raise revolts. When Wang Lun rose in revolt at Linqing in Shandong in the thirty-ninth year of Qianlong (1774), for instance, he drew on the strength of the grain-transport boatmen:

"[Wang Lun] moved back and forth through Shandong, and the worthless desperado bands he summoned grew by the day… [On the twenty-sixth of the eighth month] he took Tangyi and then Yanggu in succession, in each case plundering and abandoning the city and fleeing, and split off toward Linqing and Dongchang, meaning to block the transport route, several thousand strong."

"On the eleventh of the ninth month it was learned that the empty grain boats which had been hauled into the lock-canal from the sixth of the ninth month were all pressing on day and night… and among the boats passing Linqing there were some with coerced trackers following along, so that the bandit crowd gathered by the day."

"[On the thirteenth of the ninth month] the empty grain boats that had already passed Linqing came to sixty-eight gangs, over 3,400 boats in all, the rest mostly north of Linqing."

"[On the fourteenth of the ninth month] the empty grain boats blocked the bridge-lock so that [the government troops] could not get through, and the rebels in turn coerced the trackers into attacking the city, and lashed the grain boats together into a pontoon bridge, meaning to cross to the north."

Because Wang Lun and the grain-gang boatmen went through such an episode together, it was later told within the Green Gang that Wang Lun too was one of their senior "forebears." In the original preface to the famous Green Gang *tongcao* *An Inquiry into the Origins of the Clear Sect*, for instance, its author notes that "after Wang Lun of the Clear Water sect [a branch of the White Lotus] raised the banner against the Qing together with the various secret societies in Shandong and failed," "the grain-rice gangs of the several ports were all merged together, and the disciples of the Clear Sect increased from this day on"—and he offers this, moreover, as proof that the name of the Green Gang (Clear Sect) is bound up with the Clear Water sect.

Again, in the early Jiaqing years, Fang Rongsheng, a White Lotus leader of Chaoxian in Anhui who worked as a boatman, preached through the provinces of the lower Yangtze and was fomenting a revolt when the plot leaked and he was arrested and put to death, in the twentieth year of Jiaqing (1815). The records of this event show no connection with the Green Gang's early organization, yet *An Inquiry into the Origins of the Clear Sect* lists Fang Rongsheng too in its "genealogy," calling him a "forebear," "head of the Xingwu Fourth Gang, of the Lai generation."

At about the same time, one of the noted later leaders of the Taiping army, Zhang Zongyu, chief of the Western Nian, having fought fierce battles against the Qing forces along the Grand Canal on the borders of Hebei and Shandong and finally fallen there, may also have acted in concert with the grain-transport boatmen. Vague as the relevant records are, *An Inquiry into the Origins of the Clear Sect* likewise puts him into its "genealogy," calling him a "forebear," "head of the Zhenqian Gang, of the Xing generation."

There are even some leaders of peasant revolts who, by the historical record, could not possibly have had anything to do with the grain transport or the boatmen—Leng Tianlu and Ran Wenyuan, leaders of the Sichuan peasant army in the White Lotus rising of the Jiaqing years across Sichuan, Hubei, and Shaanxi, or Wang Zhengji, leader of the Clear Tea Gate (a White Lotus branch) rising at Huaxian in Henan—who are nonetheless made out to be heroic figures of grain-gang origin and listed in the Green Gang "genealogy." But there are also risings that, by the historical record, very probably had to do with the Green Gang's early organization, yet whose leaders were not listed in the Green Gang "genealogy." After the Nian army entered Shandong in the tenth year of Xianfeng (1860), for instance, the "Fu Army," led by the boatmen Liu Shuangyin and Sun Huaxiang, built stockades and rose in revolt at Yungu Mountain in Yixian and at Qishan respectively, acting in concert with the Nian and once taking Zouxian. The reason the boatmen joined the revolts was that during the Taiping revolution the grain transport was forced to shift to the sea route, and the former boatmen, for the sake of a living, had no choice but to run desperate risks.

"The people of Xu, Hai, Pi, and Su in Jiangsu, and of Tan, Lan, Teng, and Yi in Shandong, who followed the transport as a livelihood, together with the idlers who hired out to haul boats, came in all to tens of thousands. They regularly tore lengths of cloth into headbands to mark off their bands, privately lording it over one another, and had their factions in secret… In the spring of the third year [of Xianfeng], the south was overrun by war, the southern transport was commuted to payments or shifted to sea carriage, and the trackers and drifting men, hundreds of thousands of them, had nothing to look to for food… The weak turned to the ditches, the strong sank into banditry, and pillage grew ever fiercer."

"Since the diversion of the river and the halting of the transport in the early Xianfeng years, with the Guangdong disturbance raging, the jobless drifting men, left to be dismissed and disbanded, formed themselves into bands; having no means of livelihood, they could not but drift into banditry. In the Nian rebels' depredations, men of Huai, Ying, Xu, and Su were the majority. That the bandit bands multiplied in former years was never unconnected with the grain transport's being blocked, which drove them to this."

Another source states still more plainly that those who joined the Taiping and Nian ranks were "An-Qing Fellows of the Way," and points out that only a part of this organization's members joined the rebel armies, while others drifted piecemeal into society.

"[In the Daoguang years]… fierce, drifting, idle-eating men sprouted up among them, the so-called green-skin party of An-Qing Fellows of the Way, who called their kind together and gathered in crowds, holding on by force of numbers and forming a knot that could not be untied. When the Taiping and Nian risings broke out, this sort slipped in among them. Once north and south were pacified, they were all but ground away. Though such names still exist in the market towns, there is no great den to harbor them, and the odd cluster of five or ten a good magistrate is well able to handle."

The records cited above concerning grain-transport boatmen taking part in revolts are of a very mixed character. Some are noted as the doing of the Anqing Fellowship of the Way; some are loosely ascribed to "bandit parties," "bandits," or "crooked bannermen"; some may have had to do with grain-transport boatmen and some cannot have; some are entered in the "genealogy" of the Green Gang *tongcao* and some are not. We may therefore hold that, of these records, apart from those known for certain to have nothing whatever to do with the grain-transport boatmen, all—whether entered in the "genealogy" or not—may be regarded as the doing of the Green Gang's early organization, of which the Anqing Fellowship of the Way was the representative. The reason is this: since these records all concern revolts by grain-transport boatmen or men connected with them, and since these revolts cannot have been without organization, given that they ran readily into the thousands and tens of thousands; and since the fashion for secret societies among the grain-transport boatmen of the day was so fierce—"each setting up his own sect, taking in many disciples, and binding them into a death-sworn party," "forming bands," "the bandit parties multiplying"—of which the Anqing Fellowship of the Way was one typical representative; then, given the very nature of organizations like the Anqing Fellowship, it would be unthinkable that they played no part in these revolts.

### III. The Destructive Activities of the Green Gang's Early Organization

Besides the aspects of solidarity, mutual aid, struggle, and even alliance with peasant revolt described above, the activities of the Green Gang's early organization had another side to their character as well—the destructive, and indeed criminal and reactionary, side.

First, however much the aim of the Green Gang's early organization was to defend its own interests, those interests were in the end narrow ones, while its bounden task was still to carry the "imperial grain" for those rulers, which objectively played the part of "supplying the enemy"—so that it is not to be spoken of in the same breath with anti-Qing revolutionary organizations of the sort of the Heaven and Earth Society, which had appeared a little earlier. As Tan Renfeng put it in 1921, in a memorandum he submitted to the Beijing government from the standpoint of a revolutionary society: "The Clear Gang arose from the grain transport; with the flag 'Rightful Tribute to the Imperial Granary' flying high on their masts, they ravaged our people to feed the invader's grain—alas, and to be counted our fellows!" We can therefore regard organizations of the Green Gang kind only as mutual-aid bodies of political and economic struggle, and cannot call them revolutionary bodies. In more recent times, under the backing and indulgence of every sort of reactionary faction, the Green Gang turned into a gangster clique of society doing every kind of evil, and finally developed into a secret-service and traitor organization—which in fact had its very source here.

Second, the Green Gang's early organization had no strict discipline and was very loosely organized; still less did it have any clear objective of struggle, and it acted very blindly. It could of course sometimes do good, but at other times it did nothing but harm. Even where some of these harmful acts can be counted a spontaneous struggle, they belong among the negative means. Thus *The Collected Statutes of the Great Qing*, recording the state of the grain-transport boatmen, says of them:

"They steal and sell the tribute rice, water it and mix in sand, or privately carry passengers' goods, cross barriers and fords by stealth, or harass civilian boats and make violent trouble, or run their boats out of turn to evade inspection, or take on extra cargo midway and slip off home with the boat…"

There is still more on record about their brawling and fighting, their robbery and wrongdoing, their harassment of residents, and their reckless spending, opium-taking, and gambling in daily life; and in what is called the Anqing Fellowship period, this showed itself more conspicuously than before:

"Some years ago the grain boats of Zhejiang and Hu-Guang, out of private grudges, fought one another, taking up arms and loosing arrows, and the killed and wounded were many. Yielding to their ferocity, they acted without the least restraint. It is also heard that the winter before last, when the grain boats were frozen in in Shandong, they went so far as to seize and plunder and harass the residents. And on last year's empty return, it is heard, they forcibly took the common people's clothing—all of this a gross breach of the law."

"The An-Qing Fellows of the Way have long been a scourge, their name beginning with the grain boats of the Anqing gang. In the Jiaqing and Daoguang years, only when the grain boats passed through would their party go ashore to make trouble, thieving and stealing, doing every evil. Later, when the grain boats were laid up and disbanded, their kind had no way to live, and scattered through the various prefectures and counties, setting up what they called 'wharf stations,' gathering desperadoes together and defying the law to the people's ruin. At first it was only around Huai and Hai, in bands of hundreds and thousands; now it has spread through the prefectures and counties north and south of the Yangtze, and there is no place without it. They set up generation-names, mark off senior and junior, give free rein to their ferocity, and bind themselves into a death-sworn party."

"The An-Qing Fellows of the Way are mostly porters, boat-people, peddlers, and craftsmen, and drifters and disbanded soldiers of that sort, who make their lairs in opium dens, gambling houses, teahouses, and pawnshops, robbing and doing wrong, and their reach is very wide."

In this way they brought much trouble on themselves, and the officials, "whenever the transport men were mentioned, all gnashed their teeth"! The offenders among the boatmen were always to be "strictly examined," "punished with severity," "dealt with to the full rigor of the law," "to make a shining warning." In the sixteenth year of Daoguang (1836), for instance, Qishan, Governor-General of Zhili, memorialized: "From the end of the fifth month to late in the eighth month, we have taken over 500 offenders in all." At about the same time Entehenge, Governor-General of Grain Transport, memorialized: "This year, within Zhili, Shandong, and Jiangsu, we have taken in succession no fewer than four or five hundred men." At about the same time Tao Shu, Governor-General of the Liangjiang, also memorialized: "We have taken the vicious boatman Wang Qi, and duly requested the imperial warrant to execute him at Qingjiangpu… There remain Yan Dahan and some dozens of others… Guo Yunde and two more, three in all… Zhang Maofu and over forty others, whom we have ordered examined and confronted and dealt with severely, that none may be wronged or let off." On the one hand suppression this harsh; on the other, they repeatedly tightened the screening and inspection of boatmen at hiring, even laying down the method that each boat's "ten boatmen were to be hired by the transport army, with the ten boats standing surety for one another, so that if any made trouble or broke the law, all would share the punishment." All of this greatly hemmed in many of the boatmen's rightful actions as well.

It was precisely because of these negative doings that plain, simple working people, once they had become the state's boatmen and joined the gang, were gradually stained with many bad habits. These bad habits they carried on them the whole way, and down to recent times, when the Green Gang had become a reactionary gangster clique of society, they still used these same tricks to harm society and to harm the revolution.

Third, the "housemasters" and "elders" among the Green Gang's forerunner organization were for the most part rotten to the core. Using their standing in the gang, they often formed a kind of "labor boss," exploiting and squeezing the boatmen hand in glove with the authorities. Yang Xifu's memorial cited earlier already speaks of "the abuse of the boatmen's contract-transport." Having contracted a task from the state, they would then hire boatmen at a low price and pocket the difference. A *Board of Punishments Circular* of the seventh year of Yongzheng records that Meng Youde, labor boss of the Seventh Gang of the Jiang-Huai Guard, paid the boatmen he hired only three taels in wages while drawing six taels from the authorities on their account, and this quite apart from his other exactions from the boatmen. Beyond the economic squeeze there was also bodily cruelty: their "incense hall" was at the same time a private tribunal, with its "house law"—that is, private punishment—which the elders could apply at will. That same *Circular* also records that Liu Bashi of the Jiaqing gang, on the pretext of some trifling matter, had his disciple's nephew Zhao Yu inflict private punishment on Yan Huisheng, a boatman on Meng Youde's boat, cutting off his left ear. The *Circular* comments: after the boatmen of the various gangs formed their parties, "all was left to the direction of their 'sect-master' [the elder]—binding and burning, cutting off ears and severing sinews, with no scruple whatever, doing exceeding harm. Liu Bashi preached the sect and deluded the crowd, Zhao Yu defied the law and gave rein to cruelty, and Meng Youde joined the party in doing evil—all of them wicked men, and the fashion is not to be let grow." One may see from this the ferocious face of such labor bosses. And this same fashion was kept up all along: even in the later Green Gang's *tongcao* this "house law" system is still much emphasized. Consider the following three verses:

{p} "The house law is stern, and gods and ghosts take fright; / Qianlong himself bestowed the rod we hold. / Since you have broken the law, you must be beaten; / break it again, and next time you burn."

{p} "In the master's hall the command goes forth; / house law in hand, he shows no mercy. / Whoever breaks the rules of the gang, / old or young, is dealt with the same."

{p} "The ancestral rules of the gang are ten great articles; / cross reason, betray the sect, and the law will not spare you. / Today before the hall you meet a warning; / break the law once more, and it's the iron anchor for you."

Truly fearsome! No wonder, then, that the Green Gang elders of recent times still flogged their disciples with lawless punishments at the least provocation. After the victory in the War of Resistance, for example, the four brothers of the Wang Jiabao family, Green Gang bosses who acted as labor bosses of the Shuangyicheng porter guild in Tianjin and were styled the "Four Tyrants," when the workers protested that they extracted as much as 90 percent of their wages and often withheld them, "took six workers—Liu Baoyu, Old Yang, Yao Mingshou, Cui Xiaodong, Zhao Tianbin, and Da Qun—bound them hand and foot, hung them upside down in the stable, and rained blows on them with whip and rod until they had all but lost their senses. Only after the workers' relatives had knelt and begged again and again, and He Er had put in a word, did they swear to repent, and only then were they untied and their lives spared." Plainly this was still the surviving fashion of men of the Liu Bashi sort in the Yongzheng years, who "bound and burned, cut off ears and severed sinews."

But one thing must be pointed out here: these labor bosses were not necessarily all of them pure working people. Some were themselves petty rulers or their claws and fangs within the grain-transport system or in the localities, and some were boatmen who had been bought over by the authorities. In the early Guangxu years, when the Anqing Fellowship of the Way spread beyond the bounds of the grain transport into society at large, this was truer still:

"[The An-Qing Fellows of the Way] were at first men without a trade, drifters and the maimed and the bandit sort; but now they are actually licentiates and stipendiaries of the military examinations, sons of officialdom, runners in the yamens—brazen and unafraid of the law, taking themselves for heroes, glad to sink to the dregs, and thinking it nothing strange at all."

We can see this fact, too, from the rolls of some several thousand elders and their like—the "Roll of Forebears," the "Roll of Fellows of the Way," the "Roll of Those Known in Recent Households," and so on—listed in the *tongcao*, of whom about 10 percent were garrison commanders, battalion officers, military licentiates, secretaries, and the like from the grain-gang era. From the Republic on—that is, when they formally began their great expansion under the name of the Green Gang—recruitment was carried on more and more from the upper ranks. From those same rolls we can see, too, that noted warlords and bureaucrats—Wu Peifu, Li Jinglin, Zhang Zongchang, Chu Yupu, Jiang Chaozong, Yuan Kewen, Jiang Guiti, Yang Yuting, Chen Diaoyuan, Wang Zhanyuan, Zhang Zhijiang, and others—were at the same time noted Green Gang elders. Even those who had started as ordinary Green Gang men, once they became elders, without exception ended by fawning on the upper ranks. It was impossible for a Green Gang elder to get on without a warlord, a reactionary faction, an imperialist, or a local military-political chief behind him. The example everyone knows in modern history is the Shanghai Green Gang boss Du Yuesheng, who from the time Chiang Kai-shek launched the "April 12" coup formed an unbreakable bond with them, later swore brotherhood with Dai Li, and in the end, boosted by Chiang Kai-shek and propped up by the imperialists, became a "party-state notable" of no small weight. All this, of course, belongs within the range of the Green Gang's later history; I raise it here only to make the point more forcibly: in studying the Green Gang, the doings of these elders are to be strictly distinguished from those of the ordinary gang men.

### Conclusion

From all the above, it can be seen that the Green Gang's early organization was a guild-like secret society of the Qing grain-transport boatmen. Such societies arose no later than the early Yongzheng years, first taking shape among the grain gangs of Anqing prefecture. The Green Gang's own legend that its founders were the three men Weng, Qian, and Pan is basically credible. At the time it had no name of its own; after the Jiaqing and Daoguang years it was for a while called the Anqing Fellowship of the Way, and the name "Green Gang" was probably fixed only in the Guangxu period. Its organizational forms were at first based on the official grain-gang structure, and grew gradually more complex thereafter; thanks to the handing-down of the *tongcao*, these forms were preserved down into the Green Gang of recent times.

The first aim in founding the Green Gang's forerunner organization was to protect the boatmen's interests. They set great store by solidarity and loyalty, and besides helping one another in daily life and work, they sometimes banded together to struggle against the officials who oppressed them, and even to raise revolts. But they gained nothing thereby; what they drew on themselves was, instead, a still crueler oppression. And because the very enterprise they were engaged in was one that served the feudal rulers, because their organization was very unsound and their objective of struggle very unclear, because certain of the boatmen's negative actions made up many of their crimes, and because of the manipulation and control of the labor-boss figures—some of them former petty officials, some working people who had degenerated—the Green Gang, in the era of its early organization, was thus a society of lumpen-proletarians that subjectively attached itself to the feudal rulers and objectively defended the feudal rulers' interests. Once their development passed beyond the bounds of the grain transport, they degenerated the faster into a still more reactionary social clique that did every kind of evil, and became an important tool, used by every sort of reactionary faction and by the imperialists, against the revolution and against the people.
