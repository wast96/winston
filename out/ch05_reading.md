## Chapter 5. The Two of Them

"Yaa! Yaa—!"

The great cry came down from the keep. Even the soldiers of the attacking force drew back their swords and spears in spite of themselves and looked up.

"Men! Now comes the last hour of Shibata Katsuie! Watch how a warrior opens his belly, and take it for your model!"

With that Katsuie drew back from the window a moment. He took up the short sword held ready and drove it into Oichi's left breast. Her face gone white, she looked at him; and with the Hirokuni blade in his hand he struck off her head at a single stroke. At the same instant the twelve or thirteen waiting-women who had stayed beside her to the last, and would not hear of leaving the castle, drove their blades as one into breast and throat and fell dead.

Katsuie showed himself at the window once more.

"This is the end of Katsuie! Let it be your lesson!"

So he cried; and he drove the blade, dark with Oichi's blood, into his left side, drew it across to the backbone on the right, then turned the edge and cut down from below the chest to beneath the navel, and dragged out his entrails. As he did, Bunkasai, behind him, struck off his head. In that same hour more than eighty men followed their lord into death. Bunkasai, the last of them, threw fire into the powder laid ready, and in a moment the nine-storied keep was swallowed in flame.

By then it was five in the afternoon.

Goemon had already slipped from the keep, and now, from among the trees of the forest on Mount Atago, he watched it burn and fall. He stood downwind, and the reek of scorching flesh came drifting to him, thick and strong.

The realm of the Asura, he thought. The realm of the Asura. Every man alive is an Asura.

He felt the very will to live draining out of his body. He longed, with a sharp bodily ache, to be back in Maki's arms, to make sure at least of the one happiness that was his here and now; and he quickened his step and went south, and ever south.

Already, along that same road, messengers and spies were hurrying south and ever south. They were men sent out by the warlords of every province, to learn the outcome of this war at the earliest hour they could and to fix their own course by it.

Back at Hamamatsu, he gave Hanzō the whole account, first to last. Katsuie's speech on that last night he said over word for word, the tears standing in his eyes. From Hanzō's lips it passed to this man and that, was set down even in the letters of Padre Frois, and so came to be known abroad.

The telling ran on a long while, and toward the end Hanzō was swallowing his yawns. Goemon, carried away with the excitement of his own story, noticed neither this, nor the steadily more high-handed manner Hanzō was falling into.

When it was done they turned to the sake. Hatsuko waited at their side. Something was working in her: she kept stealing looks at Goemon, and each time, as though she could hardly keep from laughing, her eyes would go wide, or narrow to a squint; and the moment he turned her way she would glance off and put on a face of perfect innocence.

Presently, when Hanzō got up to go out to the privy, she came out with it.

"Tell me, Lord Saeki Dennai, don't you think that, in the little while since you last saw him, my man's grown terribly full of himself? A month ago he got a rise of fifty koku. He couldn't say why himself; he sat there tilting his head over it. But the moment the rise came through, he took to giving himself airs like this. What can be in his head? A grown man his age—" here she let out a shrill peal of laughter, the tears standing in her eyes "—just because he's climbed to a bare two hundred and fifty koku! Like a child, you'd say, and that would be kind; what a small, pinched little soul it really is. It's so pitiful it brings me to tears. What it comes to is, the samurai's spirit has soaked clean into him. Or perhaps he was born that sort from the first."

Just then Hanzō came back. "What sort of man did you say I was?"

"Only that you're the steady, dependable kind. The kind who, with every little rung he climbs on his slow plod up in the world, can't help being pleased as a boy with himself."

"Hmph. A slip of a girl, seventeen years old, handing down her verdict on a grown man. If ever there was a word for reckless, here it stands."

So even Hanzō, secretive and sly past all measure, was wound helplessly round this girl's little finger.

Then, without warning, Hatsuko changed the subject.

"I've met your wife, Lord Dennai."

"What?"

The astonishment of the two men she ignored altogether, and went on as though it were the most natural thing in the world.

"At the Doll's Festival I'd nowhere else to take it, so I carried a plain-grilled eel round to your house, Lord Dennai, and called while you were out."

"Haven't I told you not to go roaming off on your own without a word?"

"And I went about it properly, mind, face muffled and a hat pulled low, and off I went. Well, the shock I got! I've never had such a shock in all my born days. I gave one little 'Oh,' and after that not another word would come."

Her way of telling it was so droll that even Hanzō was drawn in.

"And what gave you such a shock?"

"Why, Lord Dennai's wife is the very image of me. Two peas in a pod. Like an elder sister, almost. Not that I've a sister, mind; I'm an only child. But if I had one, I fancy she'd be just so. Maki, of course—forgive me, you don't mind, do you, if I call her Maki?—Maki is far the prettier of us. But the eyes, the nose, the mouth: how two faces ever came out so alike I cannot think. In their natures, though, we're nothing at all alike. Different as day and night, the very opposite. Maki, not like me, is gentle, and settled, and wise; and for all that, innocent as a child. Every wild fib I told her she took for gospel truth."

"Hmph. A hopeless baggage."

"Anyhow, I do adore her. So I've pushed my way round to see her time and again, and these days we heat the bath together and take a good long soak."

"What?"

"She has a lovely body, that one. Telling you is telling the man who knows it best, but I'll say it all the same: dressed, you'd never guess, only she isn't bony like me, she's soft all through. Smooth as silk, with the faintest sheen of oil to her, so you could fairly eat her up. And then her breasts—"

Goemon, feeling as though Maki were being stripped bare before the whole room, waved a flustered hand. "No—that's enough—"

"Oh—very well, I'll leave off. You'd do well to keep her locked safe away. You're a lucky man, Lord Dennai."

"But now the master's home again, I shan't be able to come as often as I did. I'd only be in the way. What a pity," she said; and all at once her face turned sorrowful.

In those days love between men was a common thing. But that such feeling could pass between one woman and another was something Goemon had never known. In the women's quarters of a great lord, where there were none but women, it must have happened; only, word of it never reached the world outside.

So it was no wonder that Goemon thought Hatsuko, more than ever, a strange, unaccountable girl he could not make out.

Back home, Goemon asked Maki about her.

"I had a shock of my own. She's so very like me—more like me than Kazue, even. Kazue, my little sister; I've spoken of her, haven't I? Though she's nine years the younger, so she was barely seven when we parted. Not like me: a fine high nose, and big round staring eyes. She'd be seventeen now, that child, the same age as Hatsuko. I wonder how she does. And Father, and Mother, how are they now, I wonder? When they sent me off to Sakai the two of them doted on me so; they made such a pet of me, both of them. When I was small I'd sleep tucked in between Father's legs. Mother had the baby, my little brother, to hold in her arms at night, you see."

"Do you want to go back home, to your own country?" Goemon asked.

"Oh—if it were with you. But no, it's no good. There'd be nothing to eat if I went back. Our place has but five tan of field. The rice hardly comes at all, it's barley and more barley, so we ate rice only a countable few times in a year. When we did, we made it into chagayu."

"Chagayu? Now there's a dainty word for it."

"Chagayu, now: first you boil the water, and drop into it a little cotton bag, sewn up about two sun square and stuffed with tea leaves. The tea bushes we grow on the ridges between the fields. Once the water's on the boil you put in the rice. Two shō of water to two gō of rice. Only two gō, mind, a tenth of the water. Then you salt it to taste. And when the rice runs short you make do with one gō, and roast dried broad beans for the other and stir those in. That was what passed with us for a great feast. You'd never bear it, not you."

As she worked at the stove, Maki sang a song of her old home. The talk had drifted homeward, and Hatsuko was forgotten.

{p} Hush now, hush, at Negoro,
{p} on Kakuban Hill, yo,
{p} the doves are calling, yo,
{p} tojiyori, come you now.

It was a song almost without a tune: low, and level, and slow.

"What is Kakuban Hill?" Goemon asked.

To speak of home was, for Maki, a pleasure laced with a grief that caught at the chest. Her account ran as follows; what Maki did not know, the author supplies.

Maki's home was the hamlet of Nagao. It lay a league and a half to the southeast of the point where the Kino River, crossing Kishū from east to west, takes in the Kishi River running up from south to north; on the west bank of the Kishi, at the foot of Mount Tokika. Nowadays the name Saiga has shrunk to a little cape south of Wakayama city; but once, the whole west bank of the Kishi went by the name of Saiga.

When Maki, in the storehouse behind the Honjuji at Sakai, was asked by Goemon where she was from and answered, "A place called Hosono, far up the Tomobuchi River," it was her true birthplace she named. Maki was born at Hosono. But at five she lost her home to the torches of the warrior-monks of Mount Kōya; the family was driven out, and crossed the Kishi to settle in the hamlet of Nagao.

Now, a ri and ten chō north of where the Kino and the Kishi meet stands an old temple called Negoro-ji. It is an offshoot of Mount Kōya.

At the start of the ninth century, Kōbō Daishi came home to Japan. He was thirty-three, with something over two years' study in Tang China behind him, and he set about spreading the esoteric teaching of Shingon. The old spells of India had once been ordered and codified within Brahmanism; Buddhism took them up and built from them its rites of incantation and prayer. These passed into China, where they stayed a thing of the imperial court's own worship and never came down to the people; but a great many of the Shingon sutras out of India were put into Chinese. A part of all this had reached Japan earlier still, in the seventh century, carried by En no Ozunu.

I visited Negoro-ji in June of 1963. In a corner of the broad precincts stood an old ascetics' hall. It was where the yamabushi did their austerities: a small hall, three ken square, a goma altar at its center, the whole inside smoked black with soot. Looking at the black iron plates about the altar and pinned to the ceiling, I seemed to see before me the yamabushi who for centuries had blackened themselves at their prayers; and a chill went through me that stood every hair on end.

Picture a man who stakes his whole life on a belief that can bring no result whatever, who drills at it in deadly earnest, stops his ears to every truth, and sets hard as stone in his conviction. To stand before such a figure is to feel the flesh crawl at the sheer waste of the vast energy pouring out of him for nothing. And is not a great part of the human race, even now, in just that state?

Goma, then, is a Sanskrit word: a burning, a fire-festival. In Shingon it means burning up the faggots of earthly passion in the fire of wisdom, and the rite is performed with great fires before Fudō Myōō. But in the place of honor in this hall stood no Fudō Myōō. There sat, instead, the image of one strange personage.

It was a wooden figure some six shaku high, a staff in the right hand, a scroll in the left, its feet in high two-toothed clogs; a gentle face, framed in hanging white hair. This was En no Ozunu.

That Ozunu was no single man, but a body of anti-court mountain-dwellers, or of wandering rootless power, seems to be the settled view of historians of late. Another view makes them descendants of the Chinese, or the Korean, immigrants who poured in between the close of the sixth century and the opening of the seventh. By the legend, he hid himself in the deep mountains of Katsuragi, which rise three ri to the northeast of Negoro-ji, and there, through every rigor of ascetic hardship, mastered the secret art of pressing the gods into service. So he founded that peculiar religion called Shugendō, whose banner was the oneness of god and Buddha. And because he spread it among the common people, the government's troops persecuted him as a heretic; so, of necessity, he put to fighting use the art of war of Sun Tzu, brought over from China not long before. Thus he came to be reckoned both the father of Shingon esotericism and the father of ninjutsu.

Shugendō is a religion that looks, through its secret spells, for benefits in this present world; and to make that power one's own, a man must train in deep mountains and hidden valleys cut off from the human world. Such places, moreover, are held to be holy ground, where gods and Buddhas come and go. So the ascetics of old ranged the deep mountains of the whole country in search of them.

En no Gyōja, the legend runs, had two demons, Zenki and Goki, who at his command worked wonders beyond the course of nature. Behind that, surely, lies a plain fact: the ascetic had a great many yamabushi under him, whom he organized and set to every kind of work. The three powers he commanded were, it seems, the art of the war-cry, the art of hypnosis, and the art of healing. Since Buddhism forbids the taking of life, they used no swords; their whole fighting art was the war-cry and the staff, together with a mountain warcraft full of every stratagem.

Kōbō Daishi fused this Shugendō with the Shingon esotericism that had come by way of India and China, gave it a fresh grounding in theory, founded a Japanese Shingon of his own, and fixed on Mount Kōya as its holy ground.

That is exactly why the thing I heard on Mount Kōya rings true. I called on Soeda Ryūshun, the present abbot of the Renge-jō-in there. The portraits of the whole line of abbots survive, he told me; and down to only three abbots back, every one of them is shown in the dress of a yamabushi, the tokin cap on his head. The abbot himself, in his younger days, had trained in such feats as firewalking; as taking ten or so lit candles, flame-end into the mouth, holding them and drawing them out again; as thrusting the hand into boiling water and coming away unscalded.

Among the monks of Mount Kōya there were, from of old, three lineages: the gakuryo, the gyōnin, and the hijiri. The gakuryo were monks given wholly to doctrinal study, and closed off in their membership. The gyōnin were monks who mixed widely with the ordinary world, and so were the hijiri; and these last were the "people of the valley," the outcasts of their day. Even now these "people of the valley" live in the deeper valleys beyond the Inner Sanctuary of Mount Kōya, each cluster gathered about an Amida hall, with a Kōya-hijiri as the central figure who guards it. Their faith, they say, runs very deep, and they hold fast together.

When I think of these people, ground down through the long centuries, seeking their last refuge in faith and huddling on in deep hidden valleys out of the world's sight, I cannot help feeling, even now, how deep the sin runs of a humankind that has lived by grinding others down.

Of the three kinds, the one that kept the Shugendō, the yamabushi strain, most strongly was the gyōnin—the Renge-jō-in already named among them. In the first years of Meiji, finding the yamabushi more than they could manage, they sold the overlordship of them, for two hundred and fifty yen, to the Sanbō-in, the largest of the sub-temples of the Daigo-ji in Kyoto. And so today it is the Sanbō-in that stands as the head temple of yamabushi Shugendō.

Shugendō has two streams, the Tendai and the Shingon; but they differ only in such things as the color of the tokin and the size of the oizuru, and their mudras and spells are eight parts in ten the same. The Tendai yamabushi, it is thought, became through their tie to the Mii-dera the Kōga school of ninjutsu; the Shingon yamabushi, in the line of the Ōmine ascetics, became the Iga school. Put it from the Shingon side, and ninjutsu is what you get when you take Shingon esotericism, throw away everything in it that is inward and of the person, and develop instead what is outward and heterodox.

Now, among the gyōnin of Mount Kōya there appeared, at the beginning of the twelfth century, a monk of rare gifts, the Venerable Kakuban. He set himself against the gakuryo. To put it in a word: where the gakuryo held that "Kannon preaches the Law in her own person," Kakuban maintained that "Kannon is no more than the personification of an idea, and it is a second- or third-order person of hers that does the preaching." The latter view is, granted, the more philosophical; but the difference is one of surface only. At bottom it was the gakuryo's move to crush the swelling power of the gyōnin yamabushi.

Left no choice, in 1131 Kakuban raised the Daidenpō-in within Mount Kōya, and there, independent, taught his doctrine. One day the gakuryo fell on the Daidenpō-in. At that Kakuban turned himself into a Fudō Myōō the exact double of the hall's own, and stood beside it on the dais. Not knowing which was the true Myōō, they thought to test it with a drill, and set the bit to the groin of each and bored. From the one, blood flowed; from the other, none. So the one that bled must be Kakuban: they bound him hand and foot and, from the Great Gate, flung him down to the bottom of the valley below.

I went last year, in June, to see this Great Gate. On Mount Kōya today the old road that comes in by it has fallen into disuse. Everyone rides up by cable-car, from the Nyonin-dō side, so few people come to the gate at all; it stands abandoned. The present gate, burned again and again over the years by lightning, was rebuilt in 1709; yet it is a grand old pile for all that, and being abandoned it looks the grander.

Only, oddly enough, the ground straight before the gate falls away in a sheer cliff, as if it had been sliced off; lean over, and old cedars come thrusting up one after another, far down below. The road reaches the gate from the side, dead level, along its flank. Was it over this cliff, I wondered, that they flung the wooden Fudō Myōō with the blood dripping from its groin? And a cold shiver ran down my back as I peered over.

Now, as it fell out, the one that did not bleed was the real Kakuban; and after the gakuryo had hauled the other off with shouts of triumph, he showed himself again, came down off the mountain to Negoro, and founded the Negoro-ji. A forefather of ninjutsu, after all: it was no more than you would look for in him. By and by he went to the foot of the cliff, sought out the wooden Fudō Myōō that had stood in for him, brought it to the Negoro-ji, and enshrined it. The image is there to this day, revered as the Kirimomi Fudō, the Drilled Fudō.

The Kakuban of Maki's song, "on Kakuban Hill, yo," is this man, the founder of the Negoro-ji. What the "tojiyori" of the next line, "tojiyori, come you now," means, no one knows. Some say it stands for toshiyori, the old folk; others, that a fort once stood there, and it is that toride, the fort.

Maki sang, too, in her thin small voice, a song like this:

{p} Kishū: the Kino River, Arakawa, Kokawa;
{p} what wraps the oman is bamboo sheath.

Kokawa is a town two ri east of the Negoro-ji, and there stands an old temple, the Kokawa-dera. It was built forty-seven years before even the founding of Mount Kōya, a great place of some five hundred halls and pagodas. Being of the Tendai sect, it too is a temple not lightly bound up with ninjutsu. It is the third of the Thirty-three Places of the Western Country, and its familiar pilgrim-hymn is one our own ears know well:

{p} Deep too the blessing of father and mother;
{p} Kokawa temple, the Buddha's vow, a hall to trust in.

Seventeen chō up the hill behind the temple, on the mountainside, here too stands an ascetics' hall. In it, they say, En no Gyōja himself once dwelt, in the far past; and there is enshrined a very old wooden image of the ascetic, two shaku high.

In the age of the great estates, the court and the high nobles made over vast manors to the temples at every wish that took them: a prince to be born to them, the empress's sickness healed, a hated enemy cursed to death, long life, victory in war, a place in paradise after death. The vaster the estate, the greater the merit was thought to be, so they gave the vastest they could. In those days the precincts of the Kokawa-dera alone ran to more than fifteen thousand tsubo. Kōya, Kokawa-dera, Negoro-ji, the domains of all of them swelled in no time. The Negoro-ji's holdings came to reach across Settsu, Kawachi, Izumi, and Kii: seven hundred and twenty thousand koku, and two thousand seven hundred halls, pagodas, and cloisters.

Grown thus enormously rich, a temple like the Negoro-ji is said to have kept thirty thousand warrior-monks. Not that all of them were warrior-monks by trade. For the most part they were peasants, who at need, on the day some crisis came, would rush to the temple, borrow arms from it, and turn warrior-monk. And even the professional warrior-monks were not proper monks, but gyōnin. The proof is that they all bore cloister-names. They seem to have lived in great luxury; their armor and weapons, it is said, were worked all in gold and silver. Being Shugendō gyōnin in the line of En no Gyōja, they were nothing like the warrior-monks of Mount Hiei and such places: they let their black hair grow long and streamed it down their backs like a mane.

Mount Kōya holds a place of great scenic beauty. A mountain railway and a cable-car have been run up to it, and for all that it lost its vast temple lands, it flourishes now more than ever. There is even a bank open on the summit, and the monks go trooping in with their deposits. The Kokawa-dera and the Negoro-ji, by contrast, lie off the tourist route, and travel a steady road of decline.

The Kokawa-dera above all. By the account of a temple priest I met on my visit last June, the postwar land reform stripped it of forty-five of the fifty chōbu of arable it had held from of old, and left it a bare five chō, and that five all precincts and scrub. Every field is gone; not a grain of rice comes in; the old storehouses have lost their use.

"Time was, the temples down on the flat were the rich ones," he said, "but now it's the temples up in the hills that have come off best. A place like Mount Kōya owns mountains, so it has only to sell timber. Single trees worth hundreds of thousands of yen apiece, more of them than a man can stand to count. Here with us there's the Kokawa-dera festival, on the old calendar's eighteenth of the sixth month. The parish wards each bring out the danjiri they own, the festival floats, and there's the owatari, the procession of the god. Time was, the young men counted it an honor to haul and shoulder them; now no one will do it unless he's paid. And the parts where a man shows his face, no one will take at all. So we've no choice but to fall back on folk from the old people's home, or students earning pocket-money; there's no spirit in it, and it costs a shocking amount. All over Japan, it seems, every shrine and temple is in the same trouble. At the Gion festival in Kyoto, and the Tenjin festival in Osaka, the parishioners have grown unwilling to shoulder the mikoshi, so now they have to hire laborers for it, I'm told."

So the temple priest ran through his litany of complaints.

But that is the present day. In the old time of Tenshō 11, the year 1583, the Kokawa-dera and the Negoro-ji alike stood at the very height of a fierce, swelling prosperity.

By what Maki told Goemon, the house she was born in stood in a hamlet called Hosono, south of the Kino River, east of the Kishi, at the foot of Mount Kōya. Three or four generations back the family had come over from Saiga and settled there. But in the year her little sister Kazue was born, the warrior-monks of Mount Kōya seized house and land without warning; the family fled across the Kishi and settled in the district behind Mount Tokika, the place they call the hamlet of Nagao. With great labor they broke in a patch that was all stones and scrub and rough woodland, and by the third year had made of it a field of some six tan. Whereupon the warrior-monks of the Kokawa-dera came across the Kino River and told them that this too was temple land, and they must render the yearly rice-tax.

Maki was sixteen the year she was sold to Sakai. Her family then were five: her father Yagoemon, her mother Kiino, her elder brother Gisuke, her little sister Kazue. Gisuke was three years older than Maki, Kazue four years younger. That year's yield, counting the barley and the buckwheat grown after it as a second crop, came to four koku two to. Reckon the three children as two grown mouths, and the year's eating ran to four koku; the surplus was a bare two to. On a samurai's domain, land newly broken in was commonly let off the rice-tax for five or six years. Yet the warrior-monks of the Kokawa-dera came in the third year and demanded a land-tax of one koku eight to, and nine to of corvée-rice besides. Pay that, and the year's shortfall came to two koku five to; plainly it was more than the family could ever render. And, as ill luck had it, they had been Ikkō believers all the way back to their Saiga days, and to an enemy of its own faith the Kokawa-dera would show no mercy. Pay up or clear out, the monks pressed them.

There was the field they clung to, won with sweat and blood, and driven from it they had nowhere on earth to go. Weeping, they promised to pay; and after that they grew sweet potatoes and hemp as well, and hired out by the day at dredging the Kino River and cleaning out ponds and all the rest of it; and still it was not enough, and in the end they let Maki go.

Today the name Saiga survives only in Saigazaki, a little peninsula thrusting into Wakaura Bay south of Wakayama city; but once it seems to have covered rather more, the whole plain of the lower Kino. Its fishermen have always been reckoned first-rate, famed as the finest hook-and-line men in the land. They fished as far west as Nagasaki in Kyushu, as far north as Maizuru in Tango, as far east as Katsuura in Chiba, and south from Kumano to Shikoku; once out, they would not be home for three or four months. So the men were away much of the time, and the women were bred to hard, heavy work.

The fishing village of Saigazaki stands packed close on a slope that climbs sheer up from the sea, so there is no carrying water-pails and the like slung from a shoulder-pole. Naturally, then, they came to bear their loads on their heads. Even the night-soil for the little fields at the top of the cape they carried up on their heads. Fifteen or sixteen kan, fifty-seven or fifty-eight kilograms, they would shoulder without turning a hair.

The villages sit on hard bedrock, and getting water is a labor. You must bore four or five meters down through the rock before you strike a vein, and even then the flow is very slight. To fill one water-barrel, sixty centimeters across and a meter high, takes as much as three hours; so not a drop of it can be wasted, to the last. First they wash the rice; in the cloudy rinse they wash the vegetables; after that they wash their faces; and last of all they wipe down the furniture and the floor. Even water fouled pitch-black they will not throw out unspent, but turn to some use. To catch even one drop more from the bottom of the well, they keep a cloth fixed there and, working a pair of cords, sweep up what water is left.

With labor never enough, however much they had, and to make sure of it, this district marries terribly young. A partner is settled at seven or eight; it is nothing strange for a body not yet twenty to have two or three children. So even when those children are grown, the parents are still young, and the tie between them is less parent and child than brother and brother. Cases where the parents' youngest was younger than the eldest son's child were, from of old, nothing rare at all. And so the eldest and second sons had no great need to "look after their parents," and mostly set up branch households; by the time the youngest reached marrying age, the parents themselves would just be coming into old age. So it was the youngest who inherited the house. This is the custom of the last-born's inheritance, general in the district from ancient times. Even now, they say, at the primary schools of Saiga a child in the second or third grade already has a marriage partner arranged, and a child without one feels small, and mopes.

The fish chiefly taken along that coast were sea bream, butterfish, black porgy, red grouper, wrasse, young amberjack, aisu (the grown amberjack), sea bass, octopus, and the like. These they took not only on hook and line: their boats were fitted with special live-wells, and there seems to have been some particular secret to their art of bringing the catch home alive. To keep those peculiar methods and secret skills from being stolen, the Saiga folk might take a wife from another village, but never, on any account, would they take in a son-in-law from outside; and so marriage among close kin was commoner there than elsewhere. Anyone who let slip to an outsider the arts of the line, or how the tackle was made, was, without a word of debate, put to the village ban.

Maki had never been to Saigazaki or Wakaura. But she remembered well the tales she had heard from childhood from her grandfather and grandmother, her father and mother, and could tell them to Goemon as though she had lived there herself.

There is today, on the Wakayama city tram line, a stop called Akibayama. Just east of it a little hill, Gobōyama, swells up thick with trees. In the fourth month of Tenshō 8, when Ishiyama castle fell and the Venerable Kennyo, abbot of the Hongan-ji, withdrew into Kishū, it was to this Gobōyama that he first repaired, and then removed to the Sagi-no-mori close by, in the same Wakayama. That one fact alone shows how many Ikkō believers there were in that country. Gobōyama being a place of powerful natural strength, the men of Saiga, looking ahead to any emergency, had raised a fort there beforehand; and the women of Saiga, it is told, carried the stone and the rest of the building materials up on their strong heads.

A single unit was made up of ten-odd farmers or fishermen, with a headman set over it; and village by village they formed the bodies called the sō, or the ikki. Above these stood the landholding officers who spoke for the villages, the otona, the chūrō, the jakunen-yori, and it was they who managed the villages' affairs. One of these otona was a man called Saiga Magoshichi. In the sixth month of Tenshō 10 came the Honnō-ji Incident; and when the glad news reached them that Oda Nobunaga, the sworn and mortal enemy of the Ikkō faith, was dead, the Saiga believers all sprang up dancing for joy. Magoshichi above the rest, lamed though he was by an old gunshot wound, leapt up on his one sound leg, and up again, and danced himself into a frenzy. The look of that dance had something so queerly comical to it that it came down as the "lame dance," and at gatherings of the faithful they would dance it together at the least excuse.

"Have you ever seen this dance?" Goemon asked.

"How could I have? I was never down there myself."

"And this Saiga Magoshichi, is he living still?"

"At Gobōyama, they say. When the harvest's in and there's a lull in the farm work, the farmers hereabouts all go on pilgrimage to the Sagi-no-mori. You'd be sure to see him then."

Though Kennyo had quit Ishiyama castle, his son, the new abbot, the Venerable Kyōnyo, held on there stubbornly; but in the fourth month after, on the second day of the eighth month, he yielded the castle at last to Nobunaga and removed into Kishū. Fearing pursuit by Nobunaga's men, he did not go to the mansion at Sagi-no-mori where his father was, but hid instead in a cave at the very tip of Saigazaki, a place called the Hawk's Nest. It is a cavern set midway up a cliff that rises some thirty meters sheer from the sea: six meters high, and, they say, more than thirty meters deep. There was no reaching it by land; and it is told that one of the Saiga faithful, a man named Idomotoya Genbei, took the abbot out in a small boat, came at the place from the sea, and hid him in the cave.

At that time, they say, Kyōnyo composed this poem:

{p} The parent bird, hidden away in the hawk's nest,
{p} dreads even the child that dwells in the heron's wood.

By which it seems the two abbots, father and son, old and new, were on as bad terms as ever. When it came to taking Kyōnyo in at the Sagi-no-mori, Kennyo, fearing Nobunaga's pursuit might fall on his own head too, refused him entry, and Kyōnyo bore him a grudge for it. But be that as it may, with both abbots come to settle there, the power of the Ikkō faith in the Saiga country swelled up all at once.

Now, Hideyoshi, having destroyed Shibata Katsuie at Kita-no-shō, turned the very next day straight for Kaga, and entered the Oyama castle at Kanazawa. To Maeda Toshiie, as the reward of his betrayal, he made over the two Kaga districts of Ishikawa and Kahoku, which had been Sakuma Morimasa's holdings. Sasa Narimasa, the lord of Toyama castle, had been Shibata's man through and through, but to hold Uesugi in check he had sent no troops to this last war; and now that Shibata was destroyed and the two provinces of Kaga and Noto had passed wholly into Hideyoshi's hands, he made his submission and had his own domain confirmed to him. Swift as a gale and a thunderclap, Hideyoshi settled the rest of his dispositions too, and passing through Nagahama on the fifth of the fifth month and Azuchi on the seventh, by the eleventh he had halted at Sakamoto.

Nobunaga's third son, Nobutaka, had meant to close on Hideyoshi from two sides together with Shibata; but on the seventeenth of the fourth month it was he who came under attack, from Hideyoshi, and after that Shibata, his one hope, was destroyed. His spirit broke; his captains and men fell over one another to surrender to Hideyoshi or slip away, until few were left. Then Nobunaga's second son, Nobukatsu, egged on by Hideyoshi, laid siege to the isolated, unsupported Gifu castle, and sent in a messenger bidding Nobutaka come to Owari. Nobutaka came out of the castle, took a boat, and had got as far as Utsumi on the Chita peninsula when Nobukatsu sent his retainer Nakagawa Kan'emon to press him to take his own life. There was no help for it now; and Nobutaka, resigning himself, made an end on the second of the fifth month. He was twenty-six. He was a somewhat better man than his elder brother Nobukatsu; and so Hideyoshi had borrowed the elder brother's hand to be rid of the younger.

So it was that within a bare year of Nobunaga's fall at the Honnō-ji, in the early summer of Tenshō 10, all the provinces Nobunaga had once conquered lay at Hideyoshi's free disposal. His relations with the other provinces, too, grew very good. Even his powerful enemy the Mōri bowed to him and sent a hostage; Tokugawa Ieyasu, by his envoy Ishikawa Kazumasa, presented him the Hatsuhana tea-jar; Ōtomo Yoshimune opened friendly terms with him, and Uesugi Kagekatsu likewise sent a hostage. In short, in this one short year Hideyoshi had become not only Nobunaga's unquestioned successor, but far mightier than Nobunaga himself had ever been.

So he chose Osaka as the fittest place from which to command the realm, began raising his castle there in the fifth month of Tenshō 11, and by the eleventh month had moved in. The castle, to be sure, took more than three years to finish. At the start they set thirty thousand men to it, day and night, without pause, and later the number grew to sixty thousand. So when he moved in that eleventh month, only a part of it can yet have stood, on the burned ruins of the old Ishiyama castle.

Of this castle there survives a report Padre Luis Frois sent to the General of the Society of Jesus, dated at Nagasaki the first of October 1585, the eighth of the intercalary eighth month of Tenshō 13. It goes on from there to report the battle of Nagakute; so the passage seems to describe the work about a year after it was begun.

"As to Osaka: as I told in a former year, after they had besieged it six years, the bonze"—that is, Kennyo of the Hongan-ji—"withdrew with all his wealth, his soldiers, and his ships to Saiga, and the city, the temple, and the castle were straightway destroyed and burned to ashes. But this being the best place among all the provinces of the Gokinai, that is, the provinces bordering the capital, Chikuzen-dono has built here a new city and raised a palace and a castle; and it is not to be compared with the building Nobunaga set up on Azuchi hill to show forth his greatness, but far surpasses it. Chikuzen-dono has first raised on that spot a castle exceeding vast, and in the midst of it a tower exceeding high, and set about it fences, walls, and bastions. The bastions are like towers of themselves; at the gateways are gates great and small, and the gates are sheathed in iron. This is his dwelling, and the lodging of his most intimate officers and servants. Here he keeps his treasure, and here he has raised great houses for powder and for provisions. All this is built within the walls and moats of the old castle; but the old parts too have been rebuilt throughout, with bastions and towers added, so that in vastness, in fineness of work, and in beauty they match the new. The tiered tower above all is adorned in gold and in blue, seen from afar, and shows a still more majestic face. In the inner court of this new castle there is a garden. It answers to our own gardens; its making is ingenious, and it is set with natural stones, with green trees of the four seasons, and with much else from nature. And in a very good situation there are several chambers that serve for the kitchen. There is, too, a fair house for the tea, and beside it a garden, whose green lends it beauty. On a height at the other side of the garden stands a chamber exceeding fair, all decked in gold. From here one looks out over green fields; and the chamber is hung with many paintings, among them natural subjects and scenes from the ancient histories of Japan and of China. There are good dwellings for the castle guard. Chikuzen-dono, though a warrior, is of no high lineage, and it was not by his blood that the kingdom of Japan fell to his hand. Being of no rank, but fortunate, he has succeeded Nobunaga, the most famous lord and captain that Japan has brought forth in all her history; and therefore he strains every nerve to add to his own authority, to draw all men's hearts to himself, and to gather to himself the world's regard for Nobunaga's splendor, his person, and his rule; and to that end he has used every means. So the castle and city of Osaka, with the mansions the lords of Japan have raised there, surpass by common judgment, and by far, the fair city and castle of Azuchi; and the city already measures more than one legua"—five point five seven two kilometers—"across. The above is what one Padre, who had the chance to see all these things during Chikuzen-dono's absence, has written to us." (From the translation of Mr. Murakami Naojirō.)

If this report is to be trusted, it would seem that Hideyoshi set about his castle less for war than to display his own majesty, and gave that the first place.

In October of the following year Frois wrote again, in a report he sent from Shimonoseki to Valignano, the Provincial for India, as follows:

"The moat is now being dug about the castle of Osaka. Sixty thousand men are ceaselessly at work on it, and they are neither miners nor masons, but the lords and great men of all Japan. The moat is forty tatami wide and seventeen deep. Just as Your Reverence"—that is, Valignano—"saw at Azuchi, it is built up wholly of stone from the foundations; and so these men by day must lay out on their allotted ground their proper measure of stone, and by night a great number must bail out the water that wells up; they labor day and night. By what Ukon-dono"—Takayama Ukon—"and the other Christian nobles say, there are lords there whose daily outlay, over and above the laborers brought from their own domains, in wages to hired men, runs as high as a hundred and thirty cruzados"—a cruzado being ten monme. "What amazes everyone is that so vast a quantity of stone has been gathered, some of it great, some small. Osaka having no stone of its own, the lords for twenty or thirty leguas about have all been ordered to send, each according to his income, ships laden with stone every day; and the levy laid on the city of Sakai is two hundred ships a day. From our house at Osaka the river can be seen, and the Padres have watched a thousand stone-laden ships come in of a day, and sometimes above a thousand. So that in the afternoon, going up the river with a fair wind, one can see nothing but ships; and in the unloading all is done in perfect order, and if any man steal so much as a single stone to add to his tally, his head is struck off on the instant. And to keep the work brisk and swift, whoever fails in what is appointed him at the castle is banished at once, and forfeits his income and his rank. Within the ramparts raised about the castle"—seen from afar, their roofs all overlaid with gold—"seven exceeding great palaces and other buildings have been raised. To flaunt his power"—he says himself he wishes nothing but to leave a great name behind him—"he is raising three wonders in the capital; two are already finished, and the third was begun about a month and a half ago."

These three wonders were, the first the Jurakudai, the second the Sentō Gosho, and the third the Great Buddha Hall of the Hōkō-ji.

Last year I went to Shōdoshima, the island made famous by Tsuboi Sakae's Twenty-Four Eyes. There, on the shore of a little-visited spot on the north side called Komi, I saw eight hundred and nine squared granite blocks, each about six shaku by four by four, said to have been cut and got ready for shipping when Osaka castle was building, and then, for some reason, left behind on the beach. Close by, on the shore at Kobe, there was a place called the windlass ground, where they say the stones cut and hauled down from the hills were loaded, by a crane-like contrivance, onto ships and rafts. On Shōdoshima there is another quarry, on the east coast near Tōnohama, facing the Harima Sea, and there too stones remain, left from the building of Osaka castle. Historians hold that these are not stones from Hideyoshi's building but from a later one; yet numbers and marks are faintly cut into them, and it seems entirely likely that from here they lashed together great rafts, or floated the stones bound to countless casks, and carried them by sea.

Now, to Hideyoshi, risen to such a height, the thorn in his side was Ieyasu. Hideyoshi had been one of Nobunaga's captains; but Ieyasu had stood as Nobunaga's guest and ally, with no earthly reason to place himself under Hideyoshi. So the two of them fell, past helping, into a case where they must fight. Nobukatsu was a dull, foolish man; but to ally with him gave Ieyasu the just and public cause of upholding Nobunaga's own surviving son. So in the first month of Tenshō 11 Ieyasu met Nobukatsu and struck a secret alliance with him. Nobukatsu, for his part, was deeply aggrieved that Hideyoshi had given him but the single province of Owari.

Now, do you remember, reader, that near the beginning of this tale a young man named Torii Moriichirō appeared? If you have forgotten, you must call him to mind.

The Kōga ninja were bent, by whatever means, on ferreting out the secrets the Christians held of the musket, the cannon, of powder and shot. On that secret errand, last year, just after the Honnō-ji Incident, three young men had got themselves in among the students of the seminary at Takatsuki. Kōga chūnin, all three: Shinbei, second son of the lay monk Tarao Dōka; Ukon, third son of Taki Shirobei of Aburahi; and Moriichirō, heir of Torii Kihachirō of Ōhara. The first two had been eighteen then, so this year they are nineteen; Moriichirō, the last, is twenty.

This Moriichirō was a man with sunken eyes, the whites of them yellow, and the forefinger of his right hand gone from the root. At the seminary chapel, during the compisan, the gathering for confession, a Sakai gun-trader confessed that off the Saiga coast he had traded in secret with a Dutch pirate ship, selling sulfur and buying muskets, shot, and fire-arrows; and Moriichirō set himself to follow the man. He had served, too, as the go-between linking Hanzō with Oyu, the kunoichi of Hanzō's who had passed as one of Nobunaga's waiting-women. He tried to force himself on Oyu in the grove behind the Gion shrine, and was stabbed in the side for it; but in the uproar the men of Katō Toranosuke, on their round of the city, seized him, and because he gave away the secret, poor Oyu was cut down and killed. Moriichirō, found to be a student of the Takatsuki seminary, was let go. On his way to Hamamatsu to report, on the east shore of Lake Hamana, he ran slap into Hanzō. "Come to my house and rest a while," he was told.

There we had left this uncanny young man, and lost sight of him for a time. Now it falls out that we must meet him once more.

After that confession, he had followed the Sakai gun-trader, gone with him as far as Sakai, and learned that the man was one Ikenaga Kurō Chōa, who kept a great warehouse there on Ebisujima. He talked Father Organtino round, and on the pretext of winning this man to the faith, with the Father's introduction, moved into Ikenaga's house to live. Since his confession Ikenaga had given up his smuggling with the Dutch pirate ships; but the licit trade, at the daimyō's request, he had not given up. For that a trade in muskets and powder made a man a merchant of death was a thing the Christians of that day had not yet dreamed of. Far from it: the Padres themselves, when they judged it might win them favor with the rulers and serve the mission, would freely make presents of muskets and powder, and lend a hand in the trade besides.

It is reckoned that the first musket to reach our country came in Tenbun 12, the year 1543, when a Portuguese ship put in at Tanegashima and a merchant aboard her had a strange thing about three shaku long. Some sixty years later, in the account called the Teppōki, written by a monk named Bunshi: "Within it is bored a hole, and without it is straight. Bored though it is, the bottom is stopped with a plug. Beside that is a little hole, and this is the touch-hole, that lets the fire through. You put the fine powder down the barrel, and a small lead ball after it. Then, bracing the body firm and narrowing the eye, you set fire in at the little hole, and on the instant there is nothing it does not strike. When it goes off it is as the flash of lightning; when it sounds, as the crash of thunder; and none who hears it but covers his ears."

On that island the aged Tokitaka, father of Oribe-no-jō, the lord of Nishimura, paid a high price and bought two of the guns, and set his retainer Shinokawa Koshirō to learn the compounding of the powder. Tokitaka, having got the two, was beyond measure delighted; he prized them, polishing them of a morning and cleaning them at evening, and gave them the names Furusato, "Old Home," and Koshizashi. The guns were handed carefully down ever after; but three hundred and thirty-five years later, in the tenth year of Meiji, at the time of the Seinan War, they were lost to fire, it is said.

About that first time, a man named Tsuda Kenmotsu Kazunaga, head of the Suginobō of the Negoro-ji in Kishū, was staying on Tanegashima for the trade with Ming China and the Ryūkyūs. Hearing of the guns, he begged earnestly for one, and had it made over to him. So the gun, no sooner come to Tanegashima, was carried off at once to the Negoro-ji in Kishū.

As I said before, the Negoro-ji was a temple that had grown up in struggle against Mount Kōya; and having amassed a vast domain, it came to raise a very powerful army. Its rough warrior-monks outnumbered its men of learning, and it had a corps of gyōnin some five thousand strong. The three thousand to four thousand five hundred cloisters were governed by six: the Koikebō, the Chishakubō, the Senshikibō, the Iwamurobō, the Suginobō, and the Aizenbō. Four of these were cloisters of gyōnin captains; under them were the standard-bearers, each over a company of twenty-seven, who served as unit commanders and made up the army. The Suginobō, of those four, held a specially leading place; so, hearing of the gun's power, it spared no money and had one made over to it. An army such as this kept, for the making of its weapons, a Sakai smith named Shibatsuji Seiuemon; and taking that gun for a model, he set about producing them.

Gaspar Vilela, a Jesuit, came to Japan in 1559, seventeen years after the gun's first arrival, preached in Kyoto, Sakai, and Nara, and returned to India in 1570. In a letter he sent from Goa to Portugal he wrote thus of the Negoro monks and their guns:

"These too, like the brethren of a knightly order, have war for their calling; and because there is much war among the provinces of Japan, they are hired for money. Above twenty thousand men they keep already, trained to nothing else; and though many fall in battle, these monasteries fill the vacancies again at once. Each man's task is to make seventy arrows a day for the war; every week they hold trials of the gun and the bow; they prize the arts of war and are forever at their practice."

Meanwhile, a little behind Negoro, a Sakai merchant named Tachibanaya Matasaburō learned the making of guns from Tanegashima, began to produce them at Sakai, and came to be called Teppō Mata, Mata of the Guns; and Shibatsuji Seiuemon too returned to Sakai in time and took up the trade. So the gun-making of Sakai, with these two at its heart, developed with great speed. Sakai, a free and independent city that belonged to no lord, made guns in quantity on its ample wealth, and sold them the length of the land as a matter of pure commerce.

Oda Nobunaga, with an eye to the gun's power, bought them in quantity, devised his own peculiar gun-tactics, and with them shattered the Takeda at the battle of Nagashino. But in Eiroku 12, the year 1569, threatening the townsmen with the destruction of the whole city, he stripped Sakai at last of its rights of self-rule and made it his own domain. So the guns of Sakai came to serve Nobunaga alone, and the lords within his provinces, and became the foundation on which he built his supremacy.

In the Sakata district of Ōmi there was a village called Kunitomo, where from of old a great many smiths had lived who forged swords and blades. Once Nobunaga had destroyed the Azai, he brought these Kunitomo smiths under his hand, set them making guns, kept the guns for his own use, and strictly forbade their export to other provinces. These are the famous Kunitomo guns; they too served Nobunaga's rise to supremacy.

Some hold that the early guns were cast; but the founding of that day could never have made a thing to bear the force of the powder's blast, and it seems rather that the barrels were built by winding ribbon-steel round and round a core-rod and forge-welding it. The barrel they could make by this winding method; but the stopping of the breech was more than they could work out. Merely welded shut, it would be blown clean off when the powder fired. So Tanegashima Tokitaka, the year after the gun first came, that is, in Tenbun 13, when a Nanban ship put in again with a smith aboard her, set a metalworker named Yasaka Kinbei to learn the way of it from the man. But the man would not teach it, whatever was tried. In the end Kinbei gave his own daughter Wakasa, seventeen years old, to the ship's captain, and so, they say, learned the making.

The method was this: first to file a male screw out of steel; then to cut a matching female thread into the end of the barrel; and to screw the one home into the other. Simple enough, once you see it. But to learn it a man had to give up a daughter. The Kunitomo smiths, too, for all their pains, worked the same thing out for themselves, and long kept it a guarded secret.

Shibatsuji Seiuemon had by now come back from Negoro to Sakai and was busily making guns; so Moriichirō attached himself to him, to learn the trade. It was in the forty-first year after the gun's arrival that Moriichirō moved into the house of Ikenaga Kurō.

That gunpowder is made by mixing saltpeter, sulfur, and charcoal was known in China by the middle of the eleventh century. But there the saltpeter came to less than forty-seven percent; it was a powder for blasting, not the propellant, rich in saltpeter, that fires a shot. What the Mongol army brought over and startled the Japanese with, at the time of the invasions, was a bursting shell like a hand grenade. That by the middle of the fourteenth century cannon of iron and of bronze were already made in China is plain from excavated barrels bearing dated inscriptions; but the small-arm, the musket, was not yet invented.

The primitive cannon passed from China to Japan, and the ninja marked it early, making powder chiefly at the village of Aburahi in Iga. But these were wooden tubes, not for firing iron shot: no more than beacon-smoke for signals, or a means of frightening the enemy; at most a kind of flame-thrower, likewise for frightening, and a kind of grenade. Of the powder's three ingredients, sulfur Japan produced in such plenty that it exported it to China; but saltpeter it had none of in nature, and the art of making it came only much later, so that then it had to be brought in from China and Java. So the daimyō ran mad after the getting of saltpeter; and there survive many documents to prove that the ninja, who sought to wheedle round the Padres and the ship-captains, had marked well the importance of powder. That the ninja, lacking the daimyō's resources, saw the importance of powder and yet could make so little use of it, was for this reason.

Moriichirō got the precious saltpeter from Ikenaga, cheaper than it would fetch elsewhere, and passed it in secret to a courier Hanzō sent at set intervals. The courier was Etegi, the fat and powerful one. Several times over Etegi went the two hundred and fifty kilometers of road between Hamamatsu and Sakai and back, the saltpeter loaded on his horse's back.

For Ieyasu, with the reckoning with Hideyoshi close before him, had to see to his arms.

"Forty-two is a man's great unlucky year. If the crossing of Iga two years back was the year before, this is the year after. Unless I gird my loins and go at it, my number's up, and the rent falls due."

So, in the first month of Tenshō 12, Ieyasu, now forty-three, said to Honda Tadakatsu, half in jest. But the corner of his lip was twitching; and plainly, with the reckoning with Hideyoshi before him, he was in no joking humor at all.

Nobukatsu, once Katsuie was destroyed and he himself had killed Nobutaka, felt for the first time badly forlorn. He saw at last that his greatest enemy was Hideyoshi, and finding no one left to guard the house of Oda, thought to lean on Ieyasu. Ieyasu, for his part, had held himself in check for a year and a half since Nobunaga's death, giving himself wholly to the ordering of Kai and Shinano; and that was because he had made up his mind to a decisive battle with Hideyoshi, the man who would settle who should succeed Nobunaga. He married his second daughter, Tokuhime, to Hōjō Ujinao of Odawara and made peace with him; and with his rear now secure, he raised Nobukatsu as his brocade banner and committed himself to the fight. He set about drawing the whole anti-Hideyoshi party together, laid a plan to hem Hideyoshi in on every side, and made overtures to Chōsokabe Motochika of Shikoku, the Negoro-ji of Kishū, the Ikkō sect of Saiga, Sasa Narimasa of Etchū, and others.

There survives a letter, dated the twenty-first of the third month, sent under the joint names of Ieyasu and Nobukatsu to the Negoro-ji and the land-chiefs of Saiga. "Hashiba Hideyoshi has of late done just as he pleases; and to bring him to account we have joined hands with the men of the western and the northern provinces, and are laying our plans. Do you too, then, with all haste, gather your forces and set them marching toward Izumi and Kawachi. Whatever you may wish, we will confer with you in any way. In this matter, loyalty is the one thing that counts above all." So it runs.

Hideyoshi, for his part, read the situation, and ordered Nakamura Kazuuji of Kishiwada in Izumi to hold Negoro and Saiga in check. Ieyasu's ninja were kept busy, sent this way and that as secret envoys, and dispatched to sound out the state of the various daimyō.

At such a time Hideyoshi's stock method was bribery. He would offer a price to make a man's eyes start, and reach out his buying hand with a brazen, pushing persistence. Call it directness at best, or an iron face at worst, it was Hideyoshi's very own, and no one else could hope to copy it. There was nothing for it but to call it a gift of the man. He always went straight for the men nearest the enemy's heart, the most crucial of all, the very ones you would have thought past all winning. This time too, among the Oda retainers, he went at a most crucial man: Ikeda Tsuneoki, lord of Ōgaki castle in Mino, and his son-in-law, Mori Nagayoshi, lord of Kaneyama in the same province. He bought them both off with the promise of three provinces to the one, Mino, Owari, and Mikawa, and two to the other, Tōtōmi and Suruga.

He reached out his bribing hand, too, to Nobukatsu's three chief elders: Tsugawa Genbanojō, lord of Matsushima in Ise; Okada Nagato-no-kami, lord of Hoshizaki in Owari; and Asai Tamiyamaru, lord of Kariyasuga besides. Nobukatsu got wind of it and put all three to the sword, then sent word of it to Hideyoshi, which was as good as a challenge to war. That was on the sixth of the third month. Hideyoshi, judging his hour come, set out from Osaka on the tenth and pushed forward to Sakamoto in Ōmi.

Meanwhile many a man, pulled hard from both sides, hung wavering, unable to tell which way to go. The Tamon-in Diary, about the twenty-second of the third month, writes: "The colors of upheaval show over the land. What is to come of it? A fearful thing. There is nothing for it but to leave all to the will of the gods, and pass one's days and nights in the dark."

Look at the forces the two sides could put in the field. Hideyoshi's sphere spanned twenty-four provinces: six million three hundred thousand koku, and a soldiery of a hundred and fifty or sixty thousand. Ieyasu and Nobukatsu had two million five hundred thousand koku, and something over sixty thousand men. The latter's strength, that is, came to no more than a third of the former's. In a field battle it is the rule that the greater force wins. The Komaki-Nagakute campaign now to come was a war that broke that rule indeed; and ever since, many a tactician has racked his brains to reason it out. Because Ieyasu's side had the just and righteous cause, they say, and Hideyoshi's had none, and so no unity of will; or because Ieyasu had taken in the Takeda soldiery and had, just then, crack troops seasoned to war. Many such reasons are given. But I wish to press one new point that none of them has noticed.

It is the work of the ninja corps on Ieyasu's side, with Hattori Hanzō at its head. Hideyoshi's side, of course, used a great many spies of the ninja sort. But these were kamari, not ninja truly trained; they had no organization of their own, and belonged, scattered, to this commander and that. On Ieyasu's side, by contrast, some two hundred ninja of Iga and Kōga were already organized as the Shinobi-gumi, the Stealth Corps; and the chain of command ran one and unbroken: from Ieyasu straight to Hanzō, and from Hanzō straight to the ninja. Here lay the reason Ieyasu forestalled his enemy at every turn in this war. Hanzō, too, poured his overflowing energy into the hour as though it were the one chance of his life, and threw himself into the work for all he was worth.

The battlefield was the plain ten kilometers north of present-day Nagoya, on the Meitetsu Komaki line, about midway between Inuyama and Nagoya. Since the end of last year already, Hanzō's men had been turning up all through this district, disguised as every kind of person. Goemon too was mobilized; and Karasumaru, ordered out for the first time, dashed about in high spirits. Save for those on other duties, like Moriichirō and Etegi, every last man was at work up and down this quarter.

By the beginning of the third month, the families and retainers of the three elders Nobukatsu had killed, in the southwest of Owari and in Ise, raised the flag of revolt. And while the combined army of Nobukatsu and Ieyasu was reducing the castles they held out in, the seat of the war shifted all at once to northern Owari.

Ikeda Shōnyū, as Tsuneoki now styled himself, was a foster-brother of Nobunaga's, and bound to Hideyoshi besides by the marriage of his daughter to Hideyoshi's nephew Hidetsugu. Until now he had worn the face of Nobukatsu's ally; but now he turned on him and seized Inuyama castle, which Nobukatsu's retainer Nakagawa Seizōsu held for him. Inuyama castle stood on a strong site above the Kiso River, which runs between Mino and Owari. It lay at the northern edge of the battlefield Ieyasu had marked out, and so, of course, within the range of his ninja's operations; but that Ikeda would turn traitor no one had dreamed. The doings of the Sengoku captains were beyond even a ninja's imagining.

The bait Hideyoshi threw was this: he would give over his third son, Nagayoshi, to Ikeda in adoption, and grant him besides the three provinces of Owari, Mino, and Mikawa. Ikeda snapped at it at once; and, for the first of his exploits, crossed the Kiso and took Inuyama castle.

Hearing this, Shōnyū's son-in-law Mori Nagayoshi, chafing to win an exploit no less than his father-in-law's and earn Hideyoshi's praise, led three thousand men on the sixteenth to Haguro, thirty chō south of Inuyama. He burned the villages round about, and, with Komaki-yama a hail away, pitched camp in the Hachiman wood. This was a little under two ri from Komaki-yama. The war was drawing at last toward the ground Ieyasu had marked for it.

Komaki-yama is a round little hill two hundred and eighty-four shaku high, eighty-six meters, running somewhat longer east to west, its long axis about six chō, six hundred and fifty-four meters; and from it a man commands the whole country round. Ieyasu had drawn a great double circle here on his map.

Yet how came it that the ever-shrewd Hideyoshi overlooked this spot? It can only have been that his mind was taken up with the Negoro and Saiga leagues. On the nineteenth, just as Hideyoshi was making to bestir himself, the two leagues showed a threatening move on Osaka, by sea and by land. A fleet that set out from the cape of Saiga made by way of Awaji for Izumi; and on land a force of twenty-three thousand, split in two, fell on Sakai and Kishiwada. Hideyoshi's army met them and beat them all back; but it delayed his own march.

Now, Ieyasu, hearing from Hanzō that Mori Nagayoshi had thrust forward alone and unsupported into the Hachiman wood, ordered Sakai Tadatsugu to fall on Mori's camp at dawn on the seventeenth. By then Goemon had lain hidden since the night before, in the grass on the bank of the Kanare River, which runs hard by the Hachiman wood.

The Mori men, spying Sakai's banners through the morning mist, were thrown into alarm; and when one of their captains, Nabeta Naitōsuke, rode down to the water to scout the near bank, Goemon let fly. A single crack, and his aim did not err: Nabeta, shot clean through the breast, pitched headlong from the saddle and died. The guns of that day were said to carry true only to two hundred paces. But Goemon, back when he sought Nobunaga's life, had drilled long at the gun in the mountains of Tarao, in the early summer of Tenshō 6. In those same hills he had once aimed, from a hundred and fifty paces, at a ninja carrying to Azuchi a secret letter from Kuki of Ise that told how the iron ships were coming on, and, without touching his body, had knocked the sedge hat off his head and left him senseless with the shock. And in the eighth month of Tenshō 8, from a wood near the Yodo River ferry, he had aimed at Nobunaga, two hundred paces off, Nobunaga on horseback, and had passed the ball within two or three sun of his ear. A fair hand with a gun, then, one must allow.

Not letting the moment slip, Sakai's troop crossed the river and fell on them. The Mori men, set upon in their turn, broke and fled toward Inuyama in an instant, leaving above three hundred dead. A warm sun shone; the heat-haze shimmered; and the deutzia bloomed in wild white profusion over the whole flat of the plain. Ieyasu set Sakakibara Yasumasa to begin that day raising ramparts on Komaki-yama, and threw up several more forts nearby. He camped that night on the hill, and the next day returned to Kiyosu castle, a ri and a half off.

That Hideyoshi at last set out from Osaka on the twenty-first of the third month was reported first of all, and at once, by Moriichirō, who was in Osaka. That his force was a hundred thousand; that he came by the Ōmi road into Mino; that on the twenty-sixth he left Gifu and reached Unuma, threw a bridge of boats across the Kiso and crossed, and by noon on the twenty-seventh had entered Inuyama castle: all of it reached Ieyasu as clear as if held in his hand, through the reports of Hanzō's ninja, as close and ceaseless as the teeth drawn one by one through a comb.

"This moment Hideyoshi himself has left Inuyama castle and is coming down to the south." "He has but ten horsemen with him." So the reports came thick and fast to Hanzō from his men. It was not yet an hour since Hideyoshi had entered the castle. To go out himself before a battle, to the very front, and take a look, was Hideyoshi's constant way. Ordinarily he did it days before the enemy took up his ground; this time he was late. So dozens of kamari were scattered before him and behind and on either hand, on strict watch; and even Hanzō's ninja, for all their skill, could not get near.

Hideyoshi climbed Ninomiya-yama, some two ri south of Inuyama castle, and looked out over the plain below. Komaki-yama lay a little over two ri to the southwest. Then he came down and rode on to Futatsubori, a bare twenty chō in front of Komaki-yama. He paced out for himself the places to fortify, marked them—here, here, here—and rode back to Inuyama. Hearing the report of it, Ieyasu, the next day, the twenty-eighth, saw to his own defenses; and Nobukatsu came out to Komaki-yama on the twenty-ninth. By the fourth of the fourth month a rampart stood between Futatsubori and Iwasaki-yama, twenty-odd chō of it, one ken two shaku thick and three ken three shaku high; and Hideyoshi left Inuyama and moved his headquarters to Gakuden, a ri and a half behind Komaki-yama.

And so, nose to nose, a battle-line more than two ri long stood opposed. By night the watch-fires of the two armies ran in a single line across the whole face of the field, a stern sight; but by day the spring sun lay warm and mild, yellow and white butterflies danced, and the scene was all peace.

Both Hideyoshi and Ieyasu were men who, from the first age of understanding, had spent their days at nothing but war. Their experience of commanding great armies was past counting. And each knew the other through and through: his temper, his tactics, his strategy. Each knew that one slip could bring on a crushing defeat. So both armies held themselves at full draw, and it began to seem that neither would move at all.

What broke this deadlock was Ikeda Shōnyū. He had taken Inuyama castle and won himself credit; but his son-in-law Nagayoshi had come to grief in the Hachiman wood and made them all a laughingstock, and Shōnyū, in his shame, laid a plan before Hideyoshi. It was this: for this war Ieyasu had come out with every man mobilized, so his home province of Mikawa must be standing wide open. Seize that opening, slip secretly round to the east, and strike at Okazaki.

The adversary being Ieyasu, Hideyoshi thought it too risky and long withheld his leave; but he gave way at last to Shōnyū's zeal, and consented. On the sixth of the fourth month Hideyoshi mounted an attack in front of Komaki, to draw the eyes of Ieyasu's army his way; and at midnight Shōnyū set out from Inuyama with twenty thousand men, marched south, entered the hills at Ninomiya, and pushed on by Ikeuchi and Ōkusa.

There, as they ate their midday meal, in the scrub of the hill that overlooks the hamlet in that valley, a small ninja in Iga garb crouched watching the column intently. He was small as a child, but frighteningly quick in his movements. Yet the little ninja seemed wholly unaware that a second ninja, in the same garb, was stealthily shadowing him.

Look at the eyes showing through the second one's mask—sunken, the whites of them a sickly yellow—and you, reader, are bound to think: hullo, I know this one. He who had lodged in Ikenaga's house at Sakai learning the making of guns had been called up in haste, it seems, for this war. That is all very well; but the disquieting thing is that this man, like a hunter with his eye on a quarry, keeps his gaze fixed less on the enemy than on that other small ninja.

No ninja of Hanzō's should yet have worked his way to this point, a good ri behind the enemy's front. So this Ikeda force was surely meant to fall on the far right of Ieyasu's line; and if so, the moment their meal was done they would be at the attack, and to carry word of it was a matter of seconds.

Yet the little ninja, though the Ikeda force had finished its meal and showed signs of moving off, made no move to go. On the contrary, he stretched out flat in the grass, as though content to let the whole column pass by under his eyes. For twenty thousand men to file past took two full watches and more. All the while the little ninja would now and then raise his head snake-like to look down at what went by, and then lie back again. And—wonder of wonders—at last he seemed to fall asleep. The faint sound of sleeping breath could be caught.

The great column had gone off to the south. Inch by inch Moriichirō crept toward the sleeping ninja. Keeping his quarry to his left, his left arm across his face to still the stir of his own breath, he crawled forward crab-wise. When the gap had closed to some eight shaku, he sprang, and threw himself full upon the sleeper.

Ninja that the other was, the body rolled sharply to the left. But in that instant Moriichirō's left hand caught the collar, and the cord tied at the breast tore with a rip, and the front fell open wide. And there, sure enough, was a pure-white back, and black hair fallen loose about the neck.

"Mm—a woman..."

Moriichirō's blood took fire all at once. Cursed from birth with a nature that flared into heat at the bare fact of a woman, he had, from the moment he first glimpsed this small ninja at a distance, already caught the scent of one.

The ninja garb was of the Iga school, and the Iga school could surely have no women in it. Perhaps it was a boy. And once the thought had lodged, he could not rest, though it cost him his life, until he had made certain what this was. When he knew it for a woman, he raised with his whole soul a shout of triumph, bared his fangs, and fell upon her.

"Ha—you sham ninja, you!"

In his mind a justification stood ready on the instant: that he fell on her not because she was a woman, but because she was an impostor. For a man who acts always at the push of a twisted impulse has always his excuse laid ready to hand.

Two years before, behind the Gion shrine in Kyoto, Moriichirō had once fallen upon Oyu, a kunoichi of Hanzō's. And at the last hair's breadth, caught by Katō Toranosuke's city patrol, he had sold her identity away, and she had been butchered for it. That time too his justification had stood ready: "Oyu was sent as Hanzō's kunoichi to spy about Hideyoshi. Yet she has lost her heart to some fellow, this Yoichirō, and gone clean off her wits. The shortest road back to her senses is to take her body."

Moriichirō gripped the collar in his left hand, set on no account to let go, and hauled. The other, light of body, came dragging toward him. He pinned her down beneath him; and then, to make doubly sure it was a woman, he seized the breast of the ninja garb, wrenched it open, and thrust in his right hand. And there, past all expectation for so small and slight a body, was a full, rich breast.

And at that, just like any young street-tough of our own day three hundred and eighty years on, all reason went clean out of him. By now the forces the two armies had actually gathered on this plain were, they say, a hundred thousand for Hideyoshi and seventeen thousand for the allied Ieyasu and Nobukatsu, near a hundred and twenty thousand men in all, drawn up face to face; and the Ikeda column that had just passed below him was about to touch off the decisive battle. All of that, too, was forgotten. Under the pouring light of a serene spring day, a pitiless scene of human lust was about to play itself out.

But at that instant something sharp drove up into his side from below. He looked up; and with a great thud, before his eyes, a man in ninja garb came dropping out of a tree. Were he truly a ninja, no such noise would come of his landing. And the man who had dropped with a thud that shook the ground was of a huge, overwhelming bulk.

Two years before, at just such a moment, he had been stabbed by the very Oyu he had pinned, with her short knife. The memory flooded back; he clapped both hands to his side and rolled away to the right.

Hemmed in by the two sham ninja, Moriichirō was at his wits' end. These three years he had been now a seminary student, now a gunsmith's apprentice, and had done no proper ninja training. He had no confidence in his own arts, and that want of confidence unmanned him. He went rolling down the slope, tumbling toward the hamlet of Ōkusa where the Ikeda force had halted only just now, and, getting to his feet at the foot of the bluff, bolted headlong to the southeast, straight for Taraga, the far right of Ieyasu's army.

Feeling at his side as he ran, he found the knife had torn his clothes and barely grazed the skin. It was a bare ri to his own lines at Taraga; he should soon have overtaken the Ikeda force that had gone before him, yet not a sign of them was to be seen. Somewhere the twenty thousand of the Ikeda army had vanished away. But Moriichirō, without stopping to make sure where they had gone, ran at full tilt into Matsudaira Nobutada's camp at Taraga.

"A report, sir. Torii Moriichirō, of the Shinobi-gumi under Hattori Hanzō. A while ago I lay hidden on the bluff above Ōkusa village, a ri to the east, and I saw an enemy force of some twenty thousand come down from the north, take their midday meal at Ōkusa, and then, the whole of them, march on southward. So I hastened straight here to report it."

One of Nobutada's captains asked, "And this enemy force, marching south, which way did it go?"

"That, sir—somewhere on the road from Ōkusa to here, it vanished away."

"Twenty thousand men, vanished away? What are you saying? Why did you not follow at once and see where they went?"

"Sir, the fact is—"

"The fact is what?"

"I fell in with two low sham ninja—"

"What? Sham ninja?"

"Yes, sir—and while I was grappling with the two of them, I lost sight of the enemy troops."

"And these sham ninja, what became of them?"

"To my regret, sir, they got away."

"How did you know they were impostors?"

"One was certainly a woman; and the other a great giant of a man, thirty kan if he was a pound."

"What? A woman ninja? No such creature exists."

"That, sir, is why I call them shams—"

"And how did you know it was a woman?"

"Ah—" Moriichirō stuck for words.

"What did her face look like?"

"She—she had a ninja hood on, sir, so—"

"You never saw her face. Then how did you know she was a woman?"

"That was—while we were struggling together, I—"

"Fool!"

And so a feat that should have been to his credit came to nothing. Nobutada at once sent out scouts to find where the vanished enemy had gone. They found that the force, on leaving Ōkusa, had borne off to the left at once, marched straight south for the Shōnai River, and, at the bend of the Shōnai, halted at Kamijō village and begun to throw up a rampart there.

This was reported to the headquarters at Komaki. Hanzō at once ordered out Goemon and ten men to watch what the force would do next. The Ikeda force lay that night at Kamijō, and stayed there the whole of the eighth. Wasting a full day here was to bring decisive consequences down on Shōnyū and his men. They had come a bare five ri, over no road hard enough to need the rest; if they meant to take the enemy by surprise, they should have pushed straight on into Mikawa. No doubt they reckoned that Ieyasu's army, its whole attention fixed on the enemy in its front, would never notice this flanking march. By the reach of scouts and spies in the warfare of that day, it was a reasonable enough assumption; but they were bound by the common assumption, and never reckoned with the powers of Ieyasu's Iga ninja.

At ten o'clock on the night of the eighth they set out. The first and second divisions crossed the Shōnai at Ōdome village, the third from Noda village, the fourth from Matsukawado. Then, after pushing east a while along the Seto road, they turned sharply right just short of Seto, forded the Yada River, marched straight south, passed Nagakute, and before dawn on the ninth reached Iwasaki castle, a ri south of Nagakute.

The castle's lord, Niwa Ujitsugu, had marched off toward Komaki, and his younger brother Ujishige was holding it in his absence with a bare two hundred and thirty-nine men; so it was wiped out in a moment by the first and second divisions of the invaders. The third division halted a little to the north, at Kanehagihara, the fourth further north still, at the Hakusan wood on the north bank of the Kanare River, and there they thought of taking their breakfast. From here it was another seven ri to Okazaki.

But Ieyasu, who through the reports of Goemon and the other ninja knew the Ikeda force's every move as if he held it in his hand, had, with Mizuno Tadashige for vanguard, sent that vanguard out of Komaki by ten on the night of the eighth. It crossed the Shōnai and entered Obata castle, between the Shōnai and the Yada. At that same tenth hour the Ikeda force set out, bore a little north from Kamijō, and crossed the Shōnai; so it was Ieyasu's men who had crossed the river first.

Ieyasu himself, with Ii Naomasa's Red Guard of one thousand eight hundred and a personal guard of one thousand two hundred, three thousand men in all, left Komaki a bare hour behind the vanguard, crossed the river, and by midnight had entered Obata castle. About then the Ikeda force was still in the very midst of crossing, a ri or so upstream. They call it the Red Guard because officers and men alike wore red helmets and red armor, and even their weapons were lacquered red.

The invaders were in high spirits, sure they had not yet been marked by Ieyasu's men. But Ieyasu's side had got a step ahead of them, stuck fast to their flank, and, through the ceaseless reports of the ninja, knew their every move while it bided its moment to strike. With things so, however great the difference in numbers, the issue was a foregone conclusion.

A little past that midnight, Goemon, who had been trailing the enemy's rearmost, fourth division, reported to Obata castle.

"The fourth division is resting at its ease in the Hakusan wood. They have set sentries, and the rest are all fast asleep."

"Good. Go back to the Hakusan wood once more, and if anything changes, bring me word."

Goemon, in the haze of the moonlit night, was making his way back to the Hakusan wood, a ri and a half off, when in the pitch-dark of the wood along the way he came on two suspicious ninja. Suspicious, because they were not using the arts a ninja naturally uses when abroad by night. One was big and fat, the other small and slight. Both wore ninja garb and looked the part, up to a point; but there was a carelessness about them that no true ninja would ever show. Goemon had no wish to have it out with such as these, and made to let them pass and go on.

Whereupon the small, slight one suddenly called out in a shrill woman's voice: "Master Goemon—too grand to notice us, are you."

Even Goemon started, and stood stock-still. Only two women knew him for Goemon. If it was not Maki's voice, it was Hatsuko's; and the giant beside her would be Karasumaru, fifteen years old.

"Hatsuko—?"

"That's right."

"What are you doing in a place like this? Does Lord Hanzō know?"

"Heavens, no. If he knew I was out here, he'd faint dead away."

"And that's Karasumaru with you?"

"—Yes, it is." It was the voice of a boy utterly at his wits' end.

"I see. So you couldn't stop this one from having her way, could you?"

"No."

"And so, in a fret, you've been trailing along at her heels?"

"Yes."

"A hopeless wife, this. Well, go straight home, the pair of you. A terrible battle is about to break hereabouts, any minute now. This is no playground."

At that Hatsuko's eyes drew up sharp and narrow. "A playground, you say? The very idea. I'm doing ninja's work out here, I'll have you know."

"Oh? And what work is that?"

"Scouting the enemy's movements, what else?"

"And what have you found out?"

"I've been following the Ikeda force since morning, that's what. So I know just where it went and what it did, every bit of it."

"And have you reported it?"

"What are you talking about? It's a secret from the master"—Hatsuko always called Hanzō the master—"if I report it, he'll find out I'm here, won't he."

"Then however much you learn of the enemy's movements, it's of no use to a soul, is it?"

"Whether it's of use or not is more than you can say yet."

"What are you saying? The fighting is about to open at any instant."

"Just so—that's why I'm in such a hurry. The enemy's first, second, and third divisions are a ri and twenty chō off, down south by Iwasaki castle. What's here is only the rearmost, the fourth division of eight thousand under Miyoshi Hidetsugu; and a third of that is baggage-train."

"That I know already. What's left is to make out, as early as I can, just when they mean to finish their breakfast and move off, and to carry the hour of it to Obata castle."

"So you're going to sit here and wait till then?"

"That's right."

"How tedious. I'll drop off to sleep."

"Sleep? Out of the question. The battle may start any moment. Very well—you keep watch here. I'll just slip round to the east of the enemy and see how things stand."

Hatsuko, pleased to be given a task of her own, said, "Very well, then," and settled down in the grass. In a moment Goemon was gone from sight.

East of the road where Miyoshi Hidetsugu's fourth division was encamped rose a little hill, an old shrine called the Hakusan-gū on its crest, and woods all about it. Skirting its north side, Goemon crossed the Yada River and came to Inaba, a small hamlet on the Seto road that runs from Obata to Seto. He turned west along the road and made for Obata castle.

A plan of attack had taken shape in his mind. Split the force in two, he thought: one column driving up the road he had just come by, to take the fourth division from behind, the other pushing east along the left bank of the Yada; close on it front and rear, and the fourth division would be a mouse in a bag. Reaching Obata, he laid the plan before Hanzō. "Yes—that's good," said Hanzō, and put it up to Ieyasu at once. Ieyasu adopted it; and by two in the morning Sakakibara Yasumasa was moving along the Seto road, while Mizuno Tadashige and Ōsuga Yasutaka crossed the Shōnai at once and began pushing east along its left bank.

Going on ahead of Mizuno's men, Goemon came back to where he had left the other two, and found Hatsuko sleeping soundly, with Karasumaru beside her at his wits' end. "Come, it's battle now. Up you get—up you get—" And herding the two of them before him, he climbed the hill to the south.

Just as the east seemed to be paling toward dawn, and the cooks of the Miyoshi division had at last woken and begun to send up their white smoke, a single gun cracked from Mizuno's men on the left; and at that signal, bows and guns were loosed all together.

The Miyoshi men, who had never dreamed the enemy was near, fell at once into wild confusion—guns! matchcord! bows! arrows!—and while they were still shouting, they were cut down in heaps. No sooner had they got their weapons in hand and set themselves to face the attack than, this time from dead behind, came Sakakibara's assault.

"Quick—get word to the main body!"

"Across the Kanare, past Nagakute—they'll be a ri and more south of here. Call up the reserves, quick!"

And in a moment a great many broke and ran to the south. Two of these soldiers, thinking, no doubt, to slip away in the confusion, came crashing up into the wood on the hill where Goemon and the others lay hidden. A small ball was flung at their feet: a sharp report, and it burst into a blue fireball. With a shriek the two of them went sprawling. A woman's laugh rang out, shrill and piercing—ha-ha-ha-ha—and a giant ninja stepped out, stripped the two of them bare, bound them hand and foot, and strung them up on a tree-trunk. The pair promptly fainted away.

At this Hatsuko fell into a wild pitch of excitement. Shrieking with laughter, she went racing down the hill, sprang out onto the road where the couriers were running for the main body, and pelted them with flash-balls and smoke-balls, one after another. The soldiers, who had never met such things before, took her for a demon sprung up out of the ground, and scattered like spiderlings, fleeing back the way they had come or off down the side-roads.
