## Chapter Five

A day passed.

The news that Shuangqiao had fallen took up a few lines in one corner of the Shanghai papers. Of late there had been too many such things; the papers could not carry them all, and, for the sake of keeping the public calm, judged it best to carry few; and people, too, had grown used to them, just as they had to the kidnappings for ransom that cropped up again and again in Shanghai itself, so that a reader's eye, having glanced once over the item, felt nothing rise in him but the thought, "There it goes again," and, at the same time, a thankfulness that the place that had come to grief was, as luck would have it, not his own home town.

The civil wars that ran on year after year, and grew fiercer as they ran, and the risings in the countryside, had in a certain sense strengthened the rich in their composure — though the dread that a mine might any moment go off beneath their very feet was, by the same measure, strengthening in them too.

But the state of Wu Sunfu's mind when he came upon this news was not so simple a thing. He had just finished his breakfast and lay sprawled along the sofa-couch with the paper; in a chair across from him sat the Wu young mistress, an inexpressible sorrow and wistfulness graven deep at the corners of her brows and eyes. All at once Wu Sunfu flung down the paper and gave a short, cold laugh.

The young mistress's heart gave a great leap; steadying herself, she looked at her husband, and the colour of her face changed a little. Being of an over-wrought nerve, she took this cold laugh of his to be aimed at her, and then, as though her secret had been spied out, her face, after paling a moment to a greyish white, flushed suddenly red.

"Peiyao! — What ails you? — Hmph. The thing that had to come has come at last!"

Wu Sunfu said it coldly, seeming to fight down some bursting rage; his sharp glance darted flashing on every side, and passed back and forth several times across the young mistress's face — a dreadful look, that seemed to tear the very heart to shreds.

The young mistress's face at once went white again, and her heart shook and pounded, pit-a-pat; but at the same moment something within her breast seemed to snap, and of a sudden she hardened her heart, bracing herself to lay everything bare, bracing herself for a scene played out to the life. Her look grew strange and terrible to see.

But Wu Sunfu, his whole mind bent upon the fall of his home town, had taken no notice of the strangeness in the young mistress's face; he rose, took a few paces, swung his arm hard, then stood still again, and, looking at the drooping fair neck of the young mistress, said as if to himself:

"Ah, the thing that had to come has come at last! — Ah, Shuangqiao! Three years ago, my dream —"

"Shuangqiao?"

The young mistress suddenly raised her head and asked. She felt now that Sunfu's cold laugh, and this "thing that had to come," pointed at something else altogether, and her heart seemed to lighten a little; yet she felt ashamed as well, and a blush rose in spite of her to her face, and in her eyes was a look at once bashful and guilty. She felt that her husband was too much to be pitied; and had he shown her, at that moment, one step more of feeling, she would fain have thrown herself into his arms and told him everything, and repented, and sworn to be his faithful wife for ever.

But Wu Sunfu came up to the young mistress and only laid his right hand on her shoulder, and said, flat and dry:

"Yes. The peasant bandits have broken into Shuangqiao — our own home town! Three years of my heart's blood, the blood I spent to make our home town a model town, is by the look of it all thrown away this time! Peiyao, Peiyao!"

Those two warm cries of her name ran like a current of electricity, and filled the young mistress's heart to its inmost folds with warmth; but lifting her face to look at Sunfu, she caught at once the flavour of it — that this ardour was not for her, but for Shuangqiao, for the "dream of the model town" — and her heart cooled again by half. She was on the very edge of tears.

"Two or three months back I foresaw that the town could not escape the bandit scourge — and now the thing that had to come has come at last! . . ."

Wu Sunfu went on; of the young mistress's torn and tangled feelings he felt not a thing. He glared savagely up into the empty air, and of a sudden turned to fury:

"I loathe them, that pack of blackguards! What are they there for? A whole battalion of men, two machine-guns! Have they all sworn never to draw a trigger? Bah! — And that blackguard Fei the little-moustache, is he dead? I wired and got no answer; a whole day has gone by and still no report from him! Not till it came out in today's paper did I so much as know! We are kept in the dark, waiting for others to come and cut our throats! Waiting to have our throats cut!"

Stamping his foot of a sudden, Wu Sunfu flung himself down in a rage upon the sofa-couch, knotted his brows at the paper beside him, and then looked at the young mistress. Her silence, too, he was now much displeased at. He softened his tone a little, and said, with a note of demand in it:

"Peiyao! Why do you never open your mouth? What are you thinking of?"

"I was thinking — that a man's dreams must fail, sooner or later!"

"What talk is this! —"

Wu Sunfu shouted it as though he would scold her; but after his eyeballs had rolled up once in majesty, he said nothing further, and picked up a sheet of the paper to screen his face — not that he read it in earnest, for he was still nursing his fury. And this fury, as he himself was convinced, was conduct that accorded with "reason." Hard, stubborn, and full of self-confidence, he knew well by what means to stamp out an enemy; he could be cruel, and he could be subtle and soft; at such times he might roar, but it was not fury in the true sense of the word. Only when he saw how muddled and useless his own people were — as the day before yesterday, when Mo Gancheng brought word that things at the mill were unsteady — only then would he fall into a real rage, a rage most hurtful to his health. And now, over this business of Shuangqiao's fall, his wrath was the greater precisely because the iron hand of his power could not reach directly to the man who was answerable for it.

At the same time his mind passed from the man answerable for order in Shuangqiao to those answerable for a county, a province, and so up to the highest in the whole nation, and his thoughts and feelings grew more tangled still. He flung down the paper and stared at the carpet under his feet with its new-fashioned design, and at the finely-inlaid parquet floor that showed at the carpet's edge, and, like a statue of stone, neither stirred nor spoke.

Only the sound of the parrot in its cage, ruffling its feathers, went rustling on in that exquisite drawing-room.

The manservant Gaosheng quietly pushed the door open and put in his head; but the grave air that filled the little drawing-room at once pressed down under his tongue the words he had meant to say. He would not draw back, and dared not come in, but stood stiff by the door, and could only make round eyes at the young mistress.

"What is it?"

The young mistress asked it as though she too were angry, and swept her pretty, coaxing glance across her husband's face. Wu Sunfu lifted his head as though startled, saw at a glance the two name-cards in Gaosheng's hand, and, with a wave of his hand, gave his order in a steady voice:

"Very well — show them into the big drawing-room!"

With that he rose and took a few paces, and before a great mirror looked to see whether his face had come back to its usual cast; and at the last, standing before the young mistress, he patted her shoulder very tenderly and said:

"Peiyao — these two days you seem to have much on your mind, listless, unable to rouse your spirits. Don't fret over such things! I shall always find a way to deal with them! Your health was ever a frail one."

He caught up the young mistress's hand and pressed it gently a while, as though he would pass his own courage and self-confidence from his palm into hers. Then, without waiting for her answer, he let go of her hand of a sudden and strode from the room.

The young mistress leaned back in her chair, her head against its back, and her eyes brimmed with tears. She understood Sunfu's meaning, understood his every word; but she felt at the same time that her own heart's secret could never, most likely, be made plain to this husband of hers, who had buried himself head and all in his "enterprise." A strange savour welled up in her, and she could not tell whether it was bitter, or sweet, or sharp.

When Wu Sunfu walked smiling into the big drawing-room, Tang Yunshan was the first to come forward to meet him, saying, as if in the deepest lament:

"Sunfu! To think your home country should have sunk into a bandit tract — the incompetence of the provincial authorities stands wholly exposed!"

"We only learned of it today when we saw the paper. You, Sunfu, will surely have had a full report? What has it come to by now? — I hear the troops quartered in your town were no small force, a whole battalion of them; how could they let it slip through their hands!"

Wang Hefu put in as well, shaking Sunfu's hand very warmly, and heaving a sigh, as though in deep sympathy.

Wu Sunfu smiled and bade his guests be seated, then answered calmly:

"Bandits so rampant as this — it is a monstrous thing peculiar to China alone! — I too only knew of it just now, from the paper. All word is cut off, and I cannot make out the true state of things over there, so that I feel there is no place to lay a hand to it — but where is Sun Jiren? Why has he not come?"

"Jiren was held up by some matter. He asked me to stand for him."

So said Tang Yunshan, lighting a cigarette, the words coming half-drawn, half-puffed out; the smoke caught in his throat, and he coughed several times running.

"The hour we had fixed on falls out badly — it lands just as this trouble has broken in your home country; and since you, Sunfu, have only now had the news, you must be laying plans to meet it, so that this morning you will surely be much occupied. Our own affair had better wait for another day."

Wang Hefu said this, looking at Wu Sunfu with a broad smile — words that showed how well he understood the ways of the world. But Tang Yunshan, not waiting for Wu Sunfu to say yes or no, cut in ahead to object:

"It is no question at all, no question at all! Hefu, I'll warrant Sunfu will never agree to that proposal of yours! Sunfu is a man cast in iron, quick in his dealings and seasoned besides; I have never once seen him take half a day over any piece of business! Much less over so trifling a matter as this — he has only to knit his brows and the whole plan is there! Let us not waste time in idleness; let us get straight to our meeting in due form!"

Tang Yunshan brought out the very tone he had always used in running party affairs and political departments, and set both Wang Hefu and Sunfu laughing. Whereupon Wu Sunfu drew the talk round to the business proper before them:

"As for Zhuzhai, I have spoken with him twice. He can join, in the main. But he cannot settle it in form until the Dragon-Boat Festival is past. — The man is over-cautious to a fault, and that too is a merit in him. Looking at it as it stands, we may be eight parts sure of him! The night before last, did we not settle on the principle of 'better a gap than a makeweight'? If we hold fast to that principle, then Zhu Yinqiu, Chen Junyi, Zhou Zhongwei and their sort had best not be called in at all; but how it is to be in the end, that I must leave to you two, Hefu and Yunshan, to decide."

"But will that not leave us too few men?"

Tang Yunshan hurried to put in the question, and burst, for no reason at all, into another loud laugh.

Wu Sunfu smiled and did not answer. He knew that the hot-tempered Tang Yunshan had but one thought — to draw together enterprisers great and small into a single body to be worked for political ends — while as for the intrigues and hidden struggles within the world of enterprise, Tang Yunshan was, to tell the truth, a rank outsider. Wu Sunfu, who had travelled in Europe and America, was of course no old-fashioned man of the "in trade talk trade" stamp; but an enterpriser he was, whatever else, and though he kept one eye upon politics, the other was forever turned toward the play of interest in enterprise, and watched it without ever tiring.

Just then Wang Hefu, stroking the two thin wisps of his moustache, sat nodding on one side with a smiling face; and seeing Wu Sunfu smile faintly and give Tang Yunshan's question no answer, Wang Hefu said:

"Yunshan's meaning is that he fears some other house may come and carry them off, I take it? — But that is no cause for over-anxiety. I had thought at first that Zhou Zhongwei and Chen Junyi, being of compradore origin, could hardly be too small in their means, and so had a mind to draw them in; but afterwards, when brother Sunfu made it plain, I saw that the two of them have nothing but an empty signboard. Whether we set up a bank, or whatever else, we must go by the true facts of the case, and can have no dealing in the buying and selling of wind. — Well, Sunfu, am I not right?"

"Enough! I bow to the majority. — Sun Jiren has a draft here; shall I bring it out?"

Tang Yunshan cut in once more to say it, ran his eyes in a circle over the faces of the two men, Wu and Wang, and then opened his document-case and drew out a large envelope.

This so-called "draft" was but a single sheet of paper, a few short lines, holding three main heads: first, a capital of five million yuan, one-third to be called up to begin with; second, plans for several new enterprises — textiles, long-distance motor-coaches, mines, applied chemical industry; third, the rescue of several enterprises already standing — a certain silk filature, a silk-weaving mill, a steamship office, and so on: all of which they had already talked over at their last consultation, and which Sun Jiren had now merely set down in writing.

Wu Sunfu took the "draft," and as he read there rose up for him out of the paper a mighty fabric of longed-for things: tall chimneys standing thick as a forest, belching black smoke; steamers riding the wind and cleaving the waves; motor-cars speeding over the open plain. In spite of himself he smiled a little. And this dream of his was not, perhaps, wholly castle-in-air. Rich in practical experience, he knew well that an undertaking might begin in a small way, but that the scale planned for it could not but be large. Three or four years back, when he had been ardent to develop his native place, he had taken just this policy. In those days he had meant to build up a "Shuangqiao Kingdom" on the foundation of a single power-plant. Nor had he been without a fair measure of success; but a Shuangqiao of but one hundred thousand souls — what room did it give a man to turn in? Set against the present plan, it was truly the little wizard in the presence of the great.

Thinking thus, Wu Sunfu came to feel that the fall of Shuangqiao was, after all, no such tremendous blow; he grew so heated that the pimples on his face steamed one after another, and, laying the "draft" down on the table and looking at Wang Hefu, he was about to speak, when unexpectedly Tang Yunshan brought out a few odd words more:

"Did we not say just now that Zhu Yinqiu and the rest were not to be called in? And yet, under the head of 'rescue' in the 'draft,' the three men's factories are entered all the same — is there not something a little self-contradictory in this? — Ha, ha, I'm an outsider, but a thing once it strikes me I must always ask."

Tang Yunshan lowered his voice, with something furtive in his air; as though, not wholly seeing into the mystery of it, he had yet in some part caught its drift.

Both Wu Sunfu and Wang Hefu fell to laughing. They exchanged a glance, and then looked at Tang Yunshan's face, that seemed at once cunning and honest. Tang Yunshan himself laughed out loud. Reckoning that he was not likely to get an answer, he thought to turn the talk another way, and gravely took up the "draft" from the table, hoping to find in it some matter to speak upon.

But Wu Sunfu snatched the "draft" away with one hand, and said to Tang Yunshan:

"Yunshan, that question of yours has much in it, and since you are no outsider anyway — for by and by, when we have our bank or whatever it is to be, we shall ask you to come forward as its manager, and you must have some grasp of every matter — we do not hold that Zhu Yinqiu and the rest should come into our company, because they have no real strength, and coming in would be a matter of names on a list and no more, and could do nothing to help our company thrive. But their enterprises are, after all, Chinese men's industry, and now that they can keep them up no longer, they must sooner or later come to the happy close of shutting their doors; and that too is a loss to Chinese industry. Should they end by making the concerns over to foreigners, then the power of foreign industry in China is increased by so much, and Chinese industry is the worse off for it. So, reckoning for the future of Chinese industry, we must 'rescue' them after all! Every one of the several enterprises set down in this 'draft' as marked for 'rescue' has been fixed on in accordance with this principle."

Breaking off sharp, Wu Sunfu, with righteousness written on his face, raised the forefinger of his left hand and struck it once, hard, against the edge of the table. This speech of his, so earnest and so clear, made Tang Yunshan feel that his own guess of a moment before — that there was some mystery beneath it — had been anything but fair and above-board. Not Tang Yunshan alone; even Wang Hefu, the smile never leaving the corner of his mouth, grew grave. In his heart he admired how truly well Wu Sunfu ordered things, and could not forbear to bring out some sentiments of his own on loyalty to the public good and love of country:

"Just so! The Third Master's words are truly a saying to save the nation! Reckoning it up, China has been at the work of industry for fifty or sixty years now; leaving aside the state-run undertakings of Li Hongzhang, Zhang Zhidong and their fellows in the former Qing days, there have been no few run by merchants besides; but where are the fruits of it? Is it not all for want of good management — losing money, closing down, and ending for the most part in foreign traders' hands? — You must understand, Yunshan, that to put an enterprise into the hands of some fleeced simpleton who cannot run it is a thing truly to be grieved and sighed over! To the man himself, and to the nation, it brings not the least good. In the end it merely lines the foreign traders' pockets. So on this head our company must by no means be slack — for though a man be our nearest kin or dearest friend, we do better to counsel him to spare himself the vexation, and hand the thing over, clean and plain, to one who has the ability to run it — how much better that would be!"

Wang Hefu had not yet done speaking when Tang Yunshan had already burst out laughing again; a shrewd man after all, he had by now taken the whole of it in.

So the three of them went on to discuss the several new enterprises planned in the "draft." Now Tang Yunshan was no longer an "outsider," but had almost taken on the air of an "expert." He recited off, several passages at a stretch, the words on "construction in earnest" from the late Premier Sun's bequeathed work, the Fundamentals of National Reconstruction; he said that once the present war was over, true democratic government would come to pass forthwith, and then the "Great Eastern Port" and the "Four Great Trunk Lines" that the late Premier Sun had foreshown must surely be brought to completion before long, and that therefore the sites his company proposed for its investment ought to lie near the "Great Eastern Port" and along the course of the "Four Great Trunk Lines." As he spoke he opened his document-case again, drew out a map, dotted a good many black points on it with a pencil, and went on with a flood of explanation; and at the end, letting out a breath like a man who has brought some great affair to its close, he said to the two enterprisers, Wang and Wu:

"Do you approve? Sun Jiren thought very well of it. Presently I can even work up this talk of mine into a detailed plan in writing, and by and by, when the bank opens and we set about calling for shares, have it printed alongside the share-prospectus — would that not be a fine thing!"

Wang Hefu had nothing against it, but neither did he say so outright; he only fixed his eyes on Wu Sunfu's face, and waited for this resourceful and decisive "Third Master" to give his opinion first.

And yet — how strange. Wu Sunfu, who was ever of no common mettle, forever taking the broad-axe to a thing, now set his face and pondered. In his eyes the "Great Eastern Port" and the "Four Great Trunk Lines" seemed to wear something of the look of the Three Fairy Isles upon the sea. He was a man of dreams, and a man of the practical at once; he held that everything must have a large-scale plan for its opening draft and its final ensign, but that if this large-scale plan were itself built upon yet another large-scale plan that was empty air, then that was a thing he would not take to. He pondered a while, and at last said, laughing:

"Good! It may well be approved. A grand signboard we must have too. But let us divide the plan into two parts: what Yunshan speaks of is the outward, the open part, and may be called our final goal. As for Sun Jiren's original 'draft,' that is the inward, the part not made public, which we shall set our hands to in the near future. Thus our company has business in plenty to do for the present, and by and by, when the 'Great Eastern Port' and the like are brought to completion, our undertakings will be more still. Wang Hefu, what do you say?"

"Excellent! The Third Master's reckoning can never go far wrong! Ha! Ha!"

Wang Hefu gave his whole-hearted assent, won over root and branch.

Just then the manservant Gaosheng suddenly ran in and said a few words at Wu Sunfu's ear. They all saw the muscles of Sunfu's face give what seemed a twitch. At once Sunfu rose, hurriedly begged leave of the two, Wang Hefu and Tang Yunshan, to "desert them a moment," and ran out.

The two men in the big drawing-room did nothing for the while. Only Tang Yunshan's bald crown gave off its oily gleam, and the rings of white smoke he puffed from his cigarette came from his mouth one after another and rose up, like the bubbles blown by a fish. Presently he tossed the half-smoked cigarette into the ash-tray and said, as if to himself:

"A capital of five million, one-third called up for the present — about one and a half million; and that, why, that — what sort of things can it be enough to do?"

He glanced at Wang Hefu. Wang Hefu seemed to have heard nothing at all, and sat with his eyes shut, resting his spirit — though perhaps he was casting up some reckoning of his own. Yunshan took up the "draft" once more to look at it, and counting over the new-enterprise plans set down there, found them no fewer than five, with heavy industry among them besides; so that even to his "outsider's" eye a capital of five million seemed by no means enough, let alone a bare one and a half million. Forgetting himself, he cried out loud:

"Aiya, aiya! Here's a great flaw! A great flaw! We must wait for Sunfu and talk it over in full, there's nothing for it!"

Wang Hefu started violently, opened his eyes, and, seeing Tang Yunshan's grave air, could not help laughing. But Tang Yunshan, who was the readiest of all to laugh out loud, did not laugh now. He only cried, over and over:

"Look, look! Is five million enough?"

It happened that Wu Sunfu came back just then. At a glance he saw Tang Yunshan's air — the forefinger of his right hand pressed straight down like a brass rule on the second head of the "draft" — and heard him crying over and over, "Is five million enough?"; and Wu Sunfu understood the whole of it. But because Zhuzhai's telephone-call just now had reported that the state of the bond market was none too hopeful, and his own heart was heavy, he let Tang Yunshan fret there for nothing.

Happily Wang Hefu had understood by now how the matter stood, and explained it to Tang Yunshan very simply:

"Yunshan, the thing goes step by step; these several new enterprises are not to be opened all at one time —"

"Then why, the day before yesterday, did we already talk of going at once to the Ministry to take out the licence?"

Tang Yunshan cut across Wang Hefu's explanation, his eyes on Wu Sunfu.

"To take out the licence first is like booking our seats before we go to the theatre."

It was Wang Hefu again who answered, seeming to grow a little impatient of Tang Yunshan's being "too much the outsider."

"And to speak plainly, once our company is set up, the very first piece of business is not to start the 'new' at all, but to 'rescue' those tottering 'old' enterprises. Only the new seats, too, must be booked betimes."

Wu Sunfu spoke too, sinking heavily into a chair. But Tang Yunshan at once came back with another question:

"Just so — rescue! And if the men are unwilling to take our 'rescue'? Will not the one and a half million of capital then lie dead on our hands?"

"We shall make it so that they cannot but be willing!"

Wu Sunfu said it flatly, a savage smile rising on his face.

"Yunshan! Silver is always a live thing. Put into the bond market on the exchange, a paltry one and a half million — what is it worth!"

"To be sure! Since our company is a financial house, to work the 'bond arbitrage' is one line of its business too."

Wu Sunfu carried on to put Wang Hefu's words upon a reasoned footing. This only muddled Tang Yunshan the more. Scratching his smooth bald crown, he cast a glance at the two, Wu and Wang, seeming to own his "outsider's" part; but in his heart he still felt that their talk was straying ever further from the matter in hand.

Just then the door of the big drawing-room opened, the manservant Gaosheng standing sideways outside it, and after him a man came striding proudly in — and it proved to be none other than Sun Jiren, his whole face aglow, so that one saw at a glance he had good news. Tang Yunshan, catching sight of him first, sprang up and cried:

"Jiren — you come in the very nick! I'm not equal to it! I lay down this office of deputy here and now!"

Sun Jiren was quite taken aback, thinking some unlooked-for turn had come in the affair; but Wu Sunfu and the others burst out laughing, came forward to exchange greetings with him, and told him that the matter was in the main settled, and wanted only the fixing of a day to set it going.

"Were you not too tied up to get away, Jiren? How is it you have contrived to come after all?"

"A friend had asked me to go and talk over some small matter with nothing to do with us; and by the sheerest chance, without meaning it, I lit upon a thread for our own company —"

Sun Jiren, answering Wang Hefu as he settled into a rocking-chair near by, wagged his long thin neck and turned his face round with a great air of triumph to say:

"Sunfu, guess what thread it was? Our company can be established within three days!"

This was no small shock of excitement! Every face lit with joy, yet no one opened his mouth; all bent their questioning eyes fixed upon Sun Jiren.

"To open a bank we must wait on the Ministry of Finance's approval, and the days drag on; go under the name of some banking syndicate, and there is business we cannot do. Now the thread I have come upon is this: there is a trust company ready-made that is willing to work together with us — 'work together' we call it, but in truth it is we who seize the reins! I have slipped away to come here just to talk it over with you all, and see how it is to be done. If you all feel this is a road we may take, then let us settle the terms and lay them before the other side."

Sun Jiren still spoke in his slow, deliberate way, but his little head wagged the faster for it.

And so a crossfire of questions, answers, weighings, and plannings sprang up all at once, and the air grew lively and strained as it had not been before. Wu Sunfu, it is true, felt some difficulty over the one clause that within a week he must pay in two hundred thousand yuan of capital — for of late, having joined that secret organisation of Zhao Botao's for playing the bonds long, he had already shifted close upon a million among the banks and native banks he dealt with, and how great his losses from the upheaval in his home country might be was as yet undecided, so that in the matter of money he too was beginning to feel that he "could not turn round in it" — yet in the end he resolutely and decidedly agreed to the proposal of Sun Jiren and the others: that, once the trust company had taken the terms of the partnership, the three of them, the backstage bosses, should each within a week pay in two hundred thousand to begin with, so as to set to work in earnest without delay.

They further settled that the first piece of business should be to advance a loan to "rescue" the two enterprisers Zhu Yinqiu and Chen Junyi.

"Sun Jiren shall treat in earnest with the trust company's side! This is a business we must trouble you to take on, Jiren."

So said Wu Sunfu, greatly stirred, holding the sure conviction of victory, like a great general on the eve of the decisive battle.

"Then are we to call for shares no more?"

Tang Yunshan asked this once more at the last, his whole face full of hope.

"No! —"

Three voices answered all together, very firm.

Tang Yunshan forced a smile, but felt in his heart a touch of dampened spirits; that fine essay of his on the grand design of industry would, by the look of it, get no chance to show its face in the papers. But this lasted only an instant, and straightway he was chatting and laughing merrily again.

When he had seen his guests off, Wu Sunfu paced the big drawing-room a while, well pleased with himself. It was now ten o'clock, the very hour at which, by his custom, he went to the mill to attend to business. He went first to his study and drew up the drafts of two telegrams, one to the county government and one to be sent, by way of the county, on a "seek-and-deliver" to Fei the little-moustache; then he pressed the electric bell to summon the manservant Gaosheng in, and gave his orders:

"If the son-in-law master telephones by and by, ask him to be put through to the mill. — Send Li Gui to dispatch these two telegrams. — The car!"

"Yes, sir! — Is the master going to the mill? There's a man surnamed Tu come from the mill who's been here a good long while, and is still waiting in the gate-lodge. Will the master see him or no?"

Only now did Wu Sunfu recall that he had summoned this Tu Weiyue to be questioned — and this was already the second time; the first, he had let the man wait a whole evening for nothing, and this time again some business had got in the way. He pondered a moment, and then said, as though much put out:

"Send him in!"

Gaosheng went off on his errand. Wu Sunfu sat there, turning over the mill's roll of employees and trying the while to call to mind what manner of man this Tu Weiyue was; but it was all very vague. The little clerks at the mill were too many, and even one so sharp as Sunfu could not keep every one of them clearly in his head. Little by little his mind turned again to how, the day before, he had gone to the mill to reason the women workers round, and to Mo Gancheng's several reports — everything looked to be going smoothly, and with a little more management the whole disturbance might, most likely, be laid to rest.

His heart grew light, so that when this Tu Weiyue came in, there was on his often-stern, purple face even a shadow of a smile.

"So you are Tu Weiyue?"

Wu Sunfu asked it, leaning his body forward a little, his pair of sharp eyes circling flashing over the young man. Tu Weiyue bowed, but said nothing; without a trace of timidity in his bearing, he looked frankly back at Wu Sunfu; the pose in which he stood was easy and free, his chest thrown straight; on his clean, fresh, high-spirited face not a flicker of feeling showed, save that his two eyes glinted, faintly, with a light natural and alert.

"How many years have you been at the mill?"

"Two years and ten days."

Tu Weiyue answered, very composed and very exact. That "exact" above all stirred a note of approval in Wu Sunfu's heart.

"Where are you from?"

"From the Third Master's own country."

"Oh — Shuangqiao too, then? Who is your guarantor?"

"I have no guarantor!"

Wu Sunfu was startled, and his right hand went to turn open the roll of employees on the table; but Tu Weiyue went on:

"Perhaps the Third Master still remembers — at the outset I came with a letter from your honoured house's late old master. Afterwards I was set to general duties in the mill's counting-room, and to this day no one has ever spoken to me of wanting a guarantor."

The muscles of Wu Sunfu's face moved once, in something between a smile and none. He remembered at last: this Tu Weiyue too was a "man of parts" the late old master had thought well of, and this Tu Weiyue's father, it seemed, had even been a good friend of the old master's, and a disciple besides of the old Vice-Minister of a generation further back. Sunfu, who had always borne a distaste for his father's life and cast of thought, of a sudden turned the good impression Tu Weiyue had just made on him into loathing. His face fell, and his questioning went straight over to the true business for which he had called this young clerk in to talk:

"I have a report here that it was you who let slip the news that the mill meant to cut wages, and that this is what has stirred up the present go-slow!"

"Quite true. I did say that wages were to be cut before long."

"Bah! So fond of loose talk as this! In this you have broken my rules!"

"I recall that the Third Master's Rules for Factory Management lay down no such article as this!"

Tu Weiyue answered, without the least meaning of fear in him, looking very composed and natural at Wu Sunfu's angry face.

Wu Sunfu glared savagely at Tu Weiyue a while. Tu Weiyue stood there, easy and free, without a shred of cramped unease about him. A clerk who could stand up to that sharp, savage glare of Wu Sunfu's was truly the first Wu Sunfu had ever met; and he could not but marvel to himself. He liked such a composed and daring young man, and his face smoothed a little. He changed his tone and said:

"Be that as it may, you ought not to have spoken. See now — you have brought a calamity down on us!"

"That I cannot allow. Since there was the fact of a wage-cut, the workers must sooner or later have known of it. And besides, even had the Third Master not cut wages at all, the go-slow, or a strike, would have broken out all the same — was bound to break out!"

"What do you mean by that?"

"My meaning is — the workers have come to know too that the futures-silk the Third Master has sold off is no small quantity, and that we are now pressing on with the reeling to make delivery; and so they would take this chance to stir a little, and turn it to their own small profit."

Wu Sunfu's face changed of a sudden, and, grinding his teeth, he cried:

"What! The workers know too that I have sold out the futures-silk? The workers know even this? Was it you who told them that as well?"

"Yes! When the workers, having heard it elsewhere, came to ask me of it, I could not tell a lie. The Third Master knows, of course, that a man who lies is not to be relied upon!"

Wu Sunfu gave an angry shout, brought his hand down hard on the table, and sprang up:

"You blackguard! You would curry favour with the workers!"

Tu Weiyue did not answer, but bowed with a smile, still very natural, very composed.

"I know that you are carrying on with that Zhu woman worker — you would buy the people's hearts!"

"Third Master, I beg you will not drag in a man's private affairs!"

Tu Weiyue said it very steadily and with a stubborn set, his alert eyes now showing a faint anger, fixed hard on Wu Sunfu's face.

Wu Sunfu's face and glance had altered again as well; they were cold now, and fixed — and more dreadful than when he had been raging and roaring. From that face, from that glance, Tu Weiyue could read what his own end would be; and yet he was not afraid. He was clever and able, and had daring too; but he was stubborn withal. Of the mind to "give himself to his work and take joy in it" he was not wholly without; but to force him to learn the methods of Mo Gancheng and his sort, and win the favour of this stern master, that he could not do. He stood there smiling, and composedly awaited Wu Sunfu's final disposal of him.

A death-like silence pressed down upon the study. Wu Sunfu put out his hand to press the electric-bell button on the wall — plainly Tu Weiyue's fate was to be settled in that one press; but just as he was about to touch the bell, Wu Sunfu's hand of a sudden drew back, and he turned his face to gaze at Tu Weiyue without stirring his eyes. Alertness, composure, daring — all were set out upon this young man's face. Given but the right ordering, such a young man could do good work; and it seemed to Wu Sunfu that many a clerk at his mill was no match for this Tu Weiyue before him. But was the young man to be relied upon? In these times, the abler a young man, the more force and daring he had, the more some unsteady notion was in him. And that was not a thing to be seen at a glance. Wu Sunfu pondered, and pondered again, and in the end sat down in his chair; his face too was no longer so dreadful as before, but still he barked sternly at Tu Weiyue:

"Your conduct is nothing less than setting the workers on to make trouble!"

"The Third Master should understand — this is no affair that any man could set on!"

"You are inciting the labour unrest!"

Wu Sunfu was again all sternness of face and voice.

No answer. Tu Weiyue threw his chest a little straighter, and smiled faintly, cold.

"What is that cold smile for?"

"Did I smile coldly? — If I did, it is because I was thinking that the Third Master ought not to fail to see this: that whoever a man may be, he must live, and would live the better for it besides! And that is the fiercest inciting force there is!"

"Pah! Idle talk! The workers see more clearly than you; the workers know how to have a care for the whole, know the concord of labour and capital; when I went to the mill yesterday and explained matters to them, did not the unrest quiet down a great deal? Is the union not warmly upholding my proposals, and straining every nerve to find a solution? I know too that among the workers there cannot but be some dangerous elements — there are those at work stirring and inciting them, who with their mouths speak of seeking the workers' good, but in truth are smashing the workers' rice-bowls; of all this I have my inquiries here, my detailed reports. And I know very well that these people too are being made fools of, and have strayed into the wrong path. I stand for peace; I do not care to use high-handed measures; but in the mill I am, as it were, the head of a household, and I cannot suffer such a horse as harms the herd. I have no choice but to lay bare the guilt of such men, and let the workers see it for themselves, and rise of themselves to deal with this horse that harms the herd! —"

"Has the Third Master called me in twice only to say this speech to me?"

In the moment that the edge of Wu Sunfu's talk faltered, Tu Weiyue put the cold question back at him, his face still betraying not a flicker of joy or fear.

"What! Do you have some further hope in you, then?"

"None. I only thought the Third Master must, rather, have something further to say."

Wu Sunfu stared blankly at the young man. He began to half-suspect that the fellow was no more than a lunatic, and cried out very angrily:

"Go! Leave your brass tally, and go!"

Tu Weiyue, not in the least flustered, brought out his clerk's brass tally, easy and free, and laid it on Wu Sunfu's desk, then bowed with a smile and turned to go. But Wu Sunfu of a sudden called him back:

"Stay! Come to the mill along with me. I'll have you go and see once more how peaceable the workers are, how careful of the whole!"

Tu Weiyue stood still, turned back, and looked at Wu Sunfu's face, smiling without cease.

It was plainly no lunatic's smile.

"What are you smiling at?"

"I am smiling — because before a great thunderstorm there is always a spell of calm, so calm that not a breath of wind stirs!"

Wu Sunfu's face changed of a sudden, but on the instant turned cool again. His experienced eye had made out at last, from this young man's bearing, certain marks out of the common, and judged him for certain no lunatic, but a prodigy of a man; and on the contrary he asked, very civilly:

"Is Mo Gancheng's report not accurate, then? Would the union dare fall in with the workers to set itself against me?"

"I have no knowledge of what Mo Gancheng may have reported to the Third Master, and I know too that the union dare not go against the Third Master's wishes. But the Third Master must surely know the union's true standing and strength?"

"What? You mean —"

"I mean that this thing, the union, may in the Third Master's eyes seem to have a little strength, but on the workers' side it is a wholly different matter."

"No strength?"

"It is not so simple as that. If they could win the workers' faith, then of course they would have strength; but if they are to help the Third Master, they cannot win the workers' faith, and this so-called union of theirs is then no more than an empty signboard — no, I should say they can no longer keep up even the empty signboard they used to have. The Third Master doubtless knows well that an empty signboard, empty though it be, has yet some measure of a drugging power. Now that the workers are up in so fierce a way, these paper tigers are put to shame; they must at once obey the Third Master's bidding and keep up their signboard — I had better speak it out plainly: they mean to get the Third Master's understanding in secret, while on the face of it they still make a show of speaking for the workers."

"And what am I to understand?"

"That the monthly merit-bonus be raised by half a tithe, and that at the Dragon-Boat Festival there be, besides, a special reward of two dollars a head."

"What! The merit-bonus raised by half a tithe? And a special reward on top of that?"

"Yes — they are even now spreading this cry among the workers, meaning by it to talk down the workers' demand for a rice-allowance. If they cannot manage so much as this, the workers will smash their signboard; and since they are a so-called 'union,' they must needs play out this whole conjuring-trick!"

Wu Sunfu of a sudden set his face like a tiger's, and swore in a burst of temper:

"Such a thing as this! How is it Mo Gancheng brings no report of it — is he sleeping himself stupid?"

Tu Weiyue smiled faintly, cold.

After a while Wu Sunfu's face grew calm; measuring Tu Weiyue carefully with his eye, he asked of a sudden:

"Why did you not come and tell me of this sooner?"

"But neither did the Third Master ask me sooner. And besides, I judged that a petty clerk on twenty dollars a month, doing odd jobs, had no call to report such things. Only just now the Third Master took back my brass tally, and that made the case a different one; reckoning upon the old friendship between my father and your honoured house, I thought that to speak of the union, and of how things stand at the mill, as two friends might in chatting, would hardly rouse a man's jealousy again, or be reckoned toadying and back-biting!"

Tu Weiyue said it coldly, a look of proud and self-regarding disdain showing in his eyes.

Feeling the barb in the words, Wu Sunfu forced a smile; he felt now that this young man, admirable though he was, had also something to be feared in him, and at the same time was ashamed in himself that such a man should have been at the mill a full two years and he never once have marked him. He changed his tone and said:

"Your temper, it would seem, is a hard and unbending one?"

"Just so; I have nothing else to pride myself upon, and so must needs pride myself on this hardness."

Tu Weiyue smiled again as he said it.

Seeming to take no heed of this further barbed remark of Tu Weiyue's, Wu Sunfu tilted his head and thought a moment, then of a sudden said loudly:

"The merit-bonus raised by half a tithe, and a special reward besides? I cannot agree to it! See now — even without agreeing, I mean to have this unrest brought to an end!"

"Not to agree will serve too. But then it ends in another fashion."

"Would the workers dare rise in revolt?"

"That depends on how the Third Master handles it."

"By your account, then, I must give something, more or less — is that it? Well! Then I'll play out the union's conjuring-trick for them!"

"If the Third Master is pleased to do it so, that will serve."

Wu Sunfu, put out, threw a hard look at the smiling Tu Weiyue.

"You have, I take it, some other way in mind."

"Let the Third Master consider — if we go by the union's way, how much money must be spent?"

"Some five thousand dollars, I should think."

"Just so. Five thousand is no great sum. But at times a sum smaller than five thousand can bring about a better result — if only a man knows how the money ought to be spent."

Tu Weiyue said it still in his cold way. He saw the thick brows of Wu Sunfu give what seemed a movement. But on that square, purple-brown face not a flicker of feeling showed. Little by little the two sharp glances bore straight in upon Tu Weiyue's face — glances like bullets that could pierce any solid wall — so that even one so able to keep his composure as Tu Weiyue felt some slight unease.

He lowered his head, and set his teeth lightly upon his lip.

Suddenly Wu Sunfu stood up and asked loudly:

"Do you know what the workers are doing now?"

"I do not. The Third Master will see when he reaches the mill."

Tu Weiyue raised his head to answer, and drew his body straighter still. But Wu Sunfu laughed. He knew that a thing this young man had made up his mind not to speak of lightly, he would by no means speak of; he was a little put out by so excessive a stubbornness, yet he admired such firmness too, and the wish to win this young man over as a right arm gained the upper hand in Wu Sunfu's heart. He caught up a pen and, standing just as he was, wrote a few swift lines on a sheet of note-paper, then turned and handed it to Tu Weiyue, saying with a smile:

"Just now I took your brass tally from you; now let me give you this in exchange for it!"

On the note-paper were these few words: "Mr. Tu Weiyue's salary is raised by fifty dollars even, from the present month. This for the attention of Mo Gancheng. — Sun. The nineteenth."

When Tu Weiyue had read it, he laid the slip on the table and said not a word, his face still without any expression at all.

"What! Are you not willing to work for me?"

Wu Sunfu cried out in astonishment, and looked at the young man without stirring his eyes.

"I thank the Third Master for his kindness. But I cannot accept it. With this one sheet of paper, nothing can be done."

For the first time Tu Weiyue spoke with something of excitement in his air, and looked frankly back at Wu Sunfu's fixed gaze.

Wu Sunfu said nothing, but of a sudden put out his hand and pressed the electric bell on the wall, took up the pen, and added a line to the note-paper: "From Mo Gancheng downward, all inspectors, overseers and the rest at the mill are to obey Tu Weiyue's ordering, and are not to trifle with it!" He flung down the pen, and said to the manservant Gaosheng, who had come in:

"Have the car take this Mr. Tu to the mill!"

Tu Weiyue took the note-paper again and glanced at it, gazed at Wu Sunfu a good while longer, and only then bowed and said:

"From today, I count myself as working for the Third Master."

"A man of ability I always deal fairly by. I know that in these times there are among the young not a few able men; the pity is that I am pressed with business, and cannot talk with the young as often as I would. — Now go back to the mill first, and tell the workers I shall surely find a way to content them. — Whatever comes up, come and take counsel with me at any time!"

Wu Sunfu's whole face was flushed with the glow of satisfaction; in his sharp observing and reckoning he had made it out for certain that the labour unrest at the mill could be brought to an end before long.

Yet a man of his stamp would never let the victory in any single matter puff him into complacency and let him rest content with it. Pacing with measured tread, he brooded a good long while, and of a sudden fell to doubting his own "ability": had he not always been close and careful in his heed, and set each man to work by the measure of his gifts? And yet in the end he had all but lost this Tu Weiyue; had failed, moreover, to foresee this present unrest; had, no longer ago than yesterday, still not rightly gauged how mighty the workers' strength was. He had been blinded and cheated by those useless running-dogs, and threatened besides by those overbearing workers! True, he had now the certainty of settling this bout of unrest — and settling it, too, to his own advantage — but he could not help laying out, over and above, a sum of secret money; and that, to him, was a grave defeat still!

To spend two or three thousand dollars the more did not greatly grieve him; there were times when, in the mood for cards at the club, he would lose above that sum over eight rounds of mahjong. But that the uselessness of his own underlings should oblige him to dip into his purse — such a habit must on no account be let grow! The foreign enterpriser had indeed the grand, far-reaching mettle and the hand of iron, but he had faithful and able subordinates too, and so could meet every turn with ease, and could not but prosper wherever he set his hand. In an industrially backward China there were simply no such "subordinates"; and what were your factory clerks but the hangers-on and spongers at the gate of some great country landlord — good only for shirking, good only for flattery, with no notion how to bring a thing off well. — Thinking thus far, Wu Sunfu could not but grow pessimistic, and felt there was little hope for the future of China's callow world of industry; taking the lower grades of management alone, society had laid up no store of them, to say nothing of the rest.

Men of Mo Gancheng's sort were fit only to gather rents and dun for debts out in the country; the overseer Wang Jinzhen and the inspector pockmarked Li were at bottom nothing but rogues, whose whole stock in trade was to brag, to be jealous, to beat the workers, and to pocket their allowances; and how should Wu Sunfu not have known it? And yet he had gone on using them until now, for no other reason than that "men of talent are hard to come by"; and besides, with Wu Sunfu's own pair of sharp eyes keeping watch above, there should surely be no going wrong — who could have foreseen that they would all but wreck the great affair? For the workers were no longer the workers of former days!

The more Wu Sunfu turned it over the heavier his heart grew, and he did nothing but circle round and round in the study. He never let others see that he too had such times of gloom and dejection; even the Wu young mistress had no chance to see it. It was by this means that he had always built up men's faith in him and their worship of him. And he was convinced besides that it was the best of all ways to temper one's bearing. But it had one flaw: that whenever he shut himself in to brood, he felt always his own loneliness. He was an able and outstanding "great general," but he had no aide-de-camp or chief of staff to whom he could trust his inmost heart. Just now he had taken well to Tu Weiyue, and had at once picked him out for use and laid a heavy charge upon him; but now of a sudden he wavered a little: Tu Weiyue's talents he had gauged aright, and the one thing over which he could not be without misgiving was this young man's cast of thought. In these times, the clearer a young man's head, the more daring and ability he had, the more some unsound notion was in him; the "heresy" of communism had already bewitched this whole company of gallant youths!

This one dreadful misgiving all but brought Wu Sunfu to utter dejection. The old and the middle-aged, of Mo Gancheng's stamp, were out-and-out weaklings, and the young were not to be relied upon; and with a master's single pair of hands, could he keep the great wheels of the enterprise turning? In spite of himself Wu Sunfu's face changed. He set his teeth, took up the telephone from the table, and called into it as though in a rage; he had made up his mind to have Mo Gancheng keep a secret watch on Tu Weiyue.

But when the line was put through and he heard Mo Gancheng's cringing, halting voice, Wu Sunfu of a sudden changed his plan; on the contrary he gave his stern command:

"Have you seen my note in my own hand? . . . Good! All of you are to obey Mr. Tu's ordering! No shirking or shifting it off! . . . As for money? He will want to draw a little secret expense. However much he asks, you are to pay it! . . . This account he shall render up to me himself hereafter. Do you understand me plainly?"

When he had set down the receiver, Wu Sunfu gave a wry smile: he could only take the risk of trying out this Tu Weiyue, and could only use his own pair of eyes to keep watch on this lovable and fearsome young man; and he could not but keep up, besides, his own firm and resolute front, and could not let his underlings know that he too had a wavering, unsteady heart in him — that having picked a man out for use, he should yet be there mistrusting him.

He hurried out of the study, rounded a covered gallery, and came to the big drawing-room.

His own private car — fitted with steel plating and new-fashioned bullet-proof glass — stood beside the stone steps before the big drawing-room. The chauffeur and Old Guan the bodyguard stood there passing idle talk.

The door of the little drawing-room stood half ajar. The gay, bright laughter of young men and women came floating out through it. Wu Sunfu knit his brows and, half without knowing it, went to the door of the little drawing-room to look — and there were the Wu young mistress and Lin Peishan, and Fan Bowen besides, their three heads gathered close together. Wu Sunfu had never been one to meddle much in their idle affairs, but now of a sudden he was mightily displeased, and, giving a deliberate cough, walked into the little drawing-room with anger in his face.

The young mistress and the rest started apart in surprise, and only then was it seen that there was the Seventh Young Master, Ah Xuan besides, wedged in between the young mistress and Fan Bowen, still with his head bent low over some book or other.

Wu Sunfu took a step forward, his eyes sweeping the room in majesty, and coming to rest at the last on Ah Xuan.

Feeling it too, it seemed, Ah Xuan raised his face and, listlessly, set down the book in his hand. Lin Peishan moved to sit in the corner of the room by the glass window in front, tittering behind her hand. Wu Sunfu, who at first had meant only to make some small show of his displeasure, was now truly a little angered; yet he held himself in, and picked up, offhand, the book Ah Xuan had set down to look at it — and it proved to be Fan Bowen's new collection of verse.

"New poetry! You young people are the ones for this sort of stuff!"

Wu Sunfu said it with a smile that was no smile, cast a glance at Fan Bowen, and, turning a page offhand, had four lines of verse leap into his view:

Gone is the Su Causeway, poetic, its waist a girdle of tender green,

and only beetle-like motor-cars roll up a pall of yellow dust;

the bourgeois' foul and vulgar foreign villas

everywhere defile the Lady of the West, so plain, so pure, so unadorned!

Wu Sunfu could not help laughing. Fan Bowen's habitual argument — that the vulgar, boorish bourgeois had no understanding of the highest, the supreme, the sacred art, and so forth — came welling up all at once in Wu Sunfu's memory. In former days he had merely found it laughable, but now, jarring as it did against his mood, he found it hateful. Your young men of today were just so: if not romantic and decadent, then extreme and going to the bad; and Wu Sunfu passed swiftly in his mind from this poet Fan Bowen before him to the Tu Weiyue who was the crux of his trouble. But to read Fan Bowen a lesson was, after all, hardly convenient, and so he must make Ah Xuan the peg to hang his sermon on:

"Ah Xuan! Who would have thought that in a bare three days in Shanghai you should have learned to be an 'elegant man'! But a romantic poet takes a man of talent to make one, and you, I fear, will not do yet!"

"But there is a famous saying: genius or idiot, either one is a poet. In Ah Xuan I see the very flash of the poet. He is, at the least, far and away more full of promise than the Mammon enthroned in his hall of gold!"

Fan Bowen cut in of a sudden, coldly, and at the same time made a sign to Lin Peishan out of the corner of his eye.

Because this was a very clever pun, not only did Lin Peishan fall to tittering again and could not stop, but even the Wu young mistress began to laugh; only Ah Xuan and Wu Sunfu did not laugh. Ah Xuan lifted a blank face, and Sunfu knit his brows. Though no "poet," Wu Sunfu understood very well the meaning of this remark of Fan Bowen's; he hated such witticisms that made a parade of a little cleverness, and held that only the most trifling of men would think to win a laugh from the women by such small tricks of the tongue. He threw Fan Bowen a fierce look and turned to go, when unexpectedly Fan Bowen spoke again of a sudden:

"Sunfu, I simply don't see why you must run a silk filature. Are there not plenty of roads to riches?"

"The one Chinese industry that can stem the draining-away of our money abroad is silk, and silk alone!"

Wu Sunfu answered as though with little will to it, in his heart a hundred and twenty parts displeased with this romantic poet.

"Indeed! But Chinese silk, once gone abroad and woven into satins and silks, is sold back into China all the same. What that Sister Yao and Sister Shan wear on their backs was never silk of Chinese make! Last month I went to Hangzhou and saw that of every ten weaving-looms, nine and more were using Japanese rayon. This year Shanghai has taken in over eighteen thousand bales of Japanese rayon, worth better than nine million eight hundred thousand silver dollars! And now, with the European sale of filature silk at a standstill and the New York market snatched away by Japan besides, you all hoard your silk in the warehouses. On the one hand a great outcry that filature silk has no market, and on the other our own weaving turns round and uses foreign rayon — is this not the very contradiction of China's industrial future!"

Fan Bowen suddenly launched into this discourse, as though he would wash away the shame of his romantic "poet's" name.

But Wu Sunfu did not on this account soften his unfriendliness; on the contrary he felt the more displeased. Being an enterpriser, he could of course take no satisfaction in these shallow arguments about native goods. The enterpriser's aim was to develop the enterprise, to multiply the number of his chimneys, to widen the market for his sales; and as for his products passing through a foreign silk-mill and coming round to be sold back into China again, that was another question, one for the proper government department to seek some remedy for — the enterpriser could hardly give up eating for fear of choking!

"This is all the stale talk of the old pedant, no more."

Wu Sunfu, with a cold laugh, delivered this judgment lightly, shrugged his shoulders, and walked out. But no sooner had he stepped through the door of the little drawing-room than he turned back and called the young mistress out, and, taking her into the large dining-room opposite, charged her very gravely:

"Peiyao, you really must take Ah Shan's affair to heart, and not leave her to make sport all day long like a child!"

The young mistress looked at her husband, at a loss, not well understanding the meaning behind his words.

"Bowen is a clever fellow, to be sure, and can turn a witty phrase, but there is no great force in him. For Peishan to be forever in his company will not do at all. — And what is more, Second Sister once spoke to me of it: she would like to make a match for her with their sixth brother, Xueshi. To my mind it is rather Xueshi — the study of poetry — that will one day amount to something with a name to it."

"Oh — so this is what it's about? Let them please themselves!"

The young mistress looked at her husband a good while, and only then answered coolly. She did not, it is true, much favour Fan Bowen — this was the sudden change in her of the last two or three days — but neither did she favour Du Xueshi; she had a fond folly of her own besides.

Wu Sunfu, put out, knit his brows a moment, but said no more; he cast one glance at the young mistress, her eyes lowered in thought, then hurried out of the large dining-room and sprang into the "insured" car that stood before the steps of the big drawing-room, and, with a note of vexation in his voice, gave his order in four words:

"To the club!"
