## Bubaigawara

The masked man stood rigid, unmoving.

There, on the veranda.

He had turned on them a pair of huge, gilded eyes that blazed with light, and he did not stir.

(Hmph—trying to scare me.)

Toshizō sipped his soup, without so much as a glance at the masked man.

Call it cool nerve if you like; but Toshizō had a perverse streak that came out at moments such as this. With the look of a child in a sulk, he went on sipping his soup, spitefully.

"Hijikata,"

{j} said Okita Sōji, unable to hold back,

{j} "you've a caller."

"Ask him his business. He'll answer you, no doubt, in a Jōshū accent oddly crossed with Edo talk."

That it was Shichiri Kennosuke, Toshizō had guessed by instinct.

Ever since he had heard that waiwai-tennō were roving in bands through the Tama villages along the Kōshū Highway,

{j} (They're hunting for me,)

{j} he had known it. The Kōgen Ittō-ryū men of Hachiōji, with Shichiri Kennosuke at their head, were surely plotting to avenge Rokusha Sōhaku the moment they laid hands on him.

(A bold fellow, though.)

Toshizō was half-impressed, too.

This Satō residence (the Satō house still stands today in Hino, in the western reaches of the Tokyo metropolis, though its head is now the postmaster and the old mansion has been pulled down and replaced by a trim brick post office) was the grandest headman's seat along the whole Kōshū Highway, a rural samurai's mansion with a stoutly built long-house gate and high walls. Clerks, menservants, and hired hands lived within it in numbers; it was no place to be slipped into easily.

"What is it you want?"

{j} Okita said to the waiwai-tennō.

The full moon of the fifteenth night hung over this masked man's right shoulder, and the pine in the inner court shone in its light.

"I must trouble you to come,"

{j} the Sarutahiko mask spoke for the first time. The voice, sure enough, was Shichiri Kennosuke's.

"Trouble us—to what?"

"Come along with me, and no talk."

"To where?"

Okita, being well brought up, spoke well. He had a beauty one might have wished to make a page-boy of.

"You are Okita Sōji of the Tennen Rishin-ryū, I take it."

"You know me?"

{j} Okita beamed. This youth, too, seemed to keep his nerve in an odd place.

"As luck has it, two masters of your school are gathered here together. We of our school have a grievance against yours. I mean to settle it."

"And who are you?"

"The one working his chopsticks over there—Hijikata Toshizō—will know."

He called men by the suffix -kun.

It was a fashion lately spreading among the loyalist rōnin who swaggered about the country, and it seemed that Shichiri, for a stiff-necked Jōshū man, might after all have a quick ear for what was new.

"Medicine-seller,"

{j} now he called Toshizō by that name.

"The proof of the killing of Rokusha is in hand. If I lay a charge before the intendant's office, the matter is closed. But we of the Hiruma hall do not, out of mercy, do so. Rest easy on that head."

"..."

To note, in passing.—

The land of Bushū (the Tokyo metropolis and parts of Saitama and Kanagawa), Edo included, ran to some three hundred and ninety square ri in area.

Reckoned in assessed yield, 1,280,000 koku.

Almost all of it was tenryō, land held directly for the shogunate. It was governed by shogunal officials—the Kantō intendant at Edo, the Nirayama intendant in Izu (the Egawa house), and the like—but compared with the domains of the various lords the rule was lax to the point of seeming a fiction: taxes were taken no higher than the fixed rate, and the keeping of order was loose. The farmers, too, held themselves proudly:

{j} —We're no dirt-farmers of some lord. We're the shogun's own farmers, direct.

{j} and their love of the Tokugawa house had been three hundred years in the growing. This was in the blood of Kondō and of Toshizō alike.

Then, too, under intendant's rule the eye of authority reached but poorly, so that gamblers naturally coiled in the post-towns, and in the fields the village swordsmen swaggered and lorded their strength. Of all the sixty-odd provinces of Japan, one saw this in none but Bushū and Jōshū.

That Shichiri Kennosuke should have charged nothing before the intendant, but chosen to settle sword with sword, was the way peculiar to the swordsmen of Bushū, and Toshizō understood it well enough.

"Sōji, see him to the gate,"

{j} Toshizō said, drawing the rice-tub toward him,

{j} "and mind you learn the place and the hour, exactly."

He ate one bowl more than usual.

About the time he had finished, Okita Sōji came back.

"The place is on the bridge at Bubaigawara. The hour, the lower half of the hour of the Dog, when the moon comes to the top of the sky. Their number, they say, is two, the same as ours."

"Ah."

Toshizō lay down, but at once sat up again and looked to his sword.

The nicks left in the edge when he had cut down Rokusha were past counting; the thing was useless.

"Sōji—do you think this will cut?"

"Well, now, I wonder. I've never killed a man, the way you have, Hijikata."

{j} He laughed teasingly with those pretty lips. Silent about it though he was, he had heard of the killing of Rokusha from Kondō, it seemed, and knew.

"But that's a wretched edge,"

{j} Okita said, peering close,

{j} "will it cut, I wonder?"

Toshizō ran off at once to the shed, hunted out four or five kinds of whetstone, and with them ground the edge at the well. Being deft with his hands, given time and pains he could pass for a middling sharpener.

The moon went behind cloud. Presently, when it came out again from a gap in the cloud, there was a soft pat-pat of straw-sandaled feet coming up behind him. When the steps stopped, a figure crouched at Toshizō's back and to one side, and seemed to be peering intently at what his hands were doing.

(...)

Supposing it was Okita, he went on grinding away without heed, when—

"Why are you sharpening a sword at this hour of the night?"

It was the master of the house, Satō Hikogorō.

At the risk of saying it once too often: this was Toshizō's brother-in-law, husband of his elder sister Onobu, and five or six years his senior.

The Satō were an old house that had come down since the age of the warring states, and generation on generation they had loved a martial style. Hikogorō's late father in particular had been a great lover of swordsmanship: it was he who backed Kondō's adoptive father, Shūsuke, with money, who tore down one wing of his own long-house gate to build him a training hall, and who arranged the adoption of the Kamiishiwara farm boy Katsuta by Shūsuke, and so made the young swordsman Kondō Isami. Without the Satō house, one might say, the Tennen Rishin-ryū would never have flourished in Tama, nor Kondō Isami come into the world.

The present head, Hikogorō, was still young. He too, an even greater lover of the martial arts than his father, had already been granted the mokuroku by Isami's adoptive father.

A man born with the air of a great householder about him, mild in temper; yet it was from this man that the funds came, in later years, when the Shinsengumi was formed.

"..."

Toshizō ground on in silence. Hikogorō, in a coaxing sort of voice,

{j} "Give it up, this quarreling."

"There's no quarrel. Wild dogs have been about here and making a nuisance, so I'm going to see to them."

"Ah, wild dogs. That kind you can't cut with the lie of the coat—cut against it, so,"

{j} and he mimed a cut with his hand,

{j} "that's how you cut them. Did you know that?"

Hikogorō—whether by breeding or by nature—did nothing but smile away, and it never occurred to him to doubt a man's word.

Which was the very reason that even so hard a pair as Toshizō and Kondō held this genial great householder in respect and deferred to him; Kondō had gone so far as to exchange the cup of sworn brotherhood with him.

"Brother, I've a favor to ask."

"What is it?"

"South of Bubaigawara there's a little bridge called Bubai Bridge, is there not. I hear there are many wild dogs thereabouts, so I'll lay the ones I cut down together by the foot of the bridge. Come morning, send a servant to clear them away."

"Right you are."

Toshizō went back to his room.

What he had asked of Hikogorō was, of course, about his own and Okita's corpses.

***

To Bubaigawara was two ri.

By night the road took time. Toshizō and Okita slipped out of the Satō house at Hino early. It was a splendid clear moonlit night, and the road showed white before them. Toshizō blew out the lantern he had gone to the trouble of readying,

{j} "The other side really is two men, then?"

"Well, this is a surprise."

Okita was cheerful as ever.

"Why a surprise?"

"You're an unexpectedly trusting soul. It'll be a crowd, of course—bound to be. Can you really imagine that vicious Hachiōji lot coming as two, the way they promised?"

"There's something in that."

True enough: hard up even for a disguise, they dressed as waiwai-tennō; they meant to eat their way into the Tennen Rishin-ryū's ground—do what they might, it was low and base. Under color of a vendetta, if only they cut down Okita and Toshizō, the Tama villages would pass, they reckoned, into Kōgen Ittō-ryū ground.

"Still,"

{j} Toshizō gave a grin,

{j} "which do you prefer, Sōji—few against many, or many against few?"

"Many. Though only by night, mind."

By night, if a small party cuts its way in, the larger one cannot tell friend from foe and does nothing but flounder in a panic, so that they are cut down all the faster. Okita, it seemed, had that kind of cunning.

"You know a great deal, you."

"Cunning I picked up from the storyteller at a variety hall. Lately, since the world's grown troubled, oddly enough the audiences there run to samurai. And because they run to samurai, the performers take to reading the Taiheiki, or the Three Kingdoms. You ought to look in now and then yourself, Hijikata—you'd make no mean strategist."

"Hmph."

Strategy, he held, was a thing one was born with. Toshizō had a secret confidence in it. If he were to pass his whole life without using this gift, he could not die content, not though he died.

When they came along the Kōshū Highway to about where the Nishifu farmers' cooperative now stands,

{j} "Here—let's turn off right,"

{j} and he stepped onto a footpath between the paddies. They were nearing the ground he had marked for the fight, and to go strolling along the main highway was to risk an ambush—to be cut down in the dark, or spotted by a scout, so that their whole line of approach would be known.

"We throw them off."

Soaking wet with the night dew, treading through the grass, they worked south and south again; and after some fifteen or sixteen chō there was a graveyard set in the fields. It is there still; the name of the field-temple is the Shōkōin.

Toshizō knew an old man here, the temple servant Gon. For all his years he was fond of a small wager, and once, when he had been beaten black and blue over a game at a gambling den in a nearby village, Toshizō—who happened to be in that village teaching fencing—had rescued him.

He roused Gon and led him out to the graveyard,

{j} "Go over toward Bubai Bridge. Carry the temple lantern, so no one thinks it odd,"

{j} and, coaching him so, made him his scout.

Bubai Bridge lay some five chō east, beyond this darkness, and all the way to it was paddy, with here and there a puddle glittering in the moonlight.

The graveyard was deep in grass.

Among the gravestones and the wooden grave-tablets Toshizō sat down, and made Okita sit too.

"Sōji, put a light in the lantern."

He had the ground lit.

Scratching at the ground with an old chopstick, Toshizō deftly drew a map.

"This is the country around Bubai Bridge. Can you see?"

On the map the roads ran fearfully tangled together.

This Bubaigawara, for all that its name carries the word for a riverbed, lies far from the actual bed of the Tama River, which is well to the south; two or three hundred years since, it had become paddy, with villages dotted here and there.

From of old it had often been a battlefield, and even now armor-fittings, swords, and human bones come up now and then out of the fields—as Okita, too, knew.

Knew, and more: the Taiheiki the storyteller had read the other day fell just at this passage. Long ago, in the age of the Northern and Southern Courts, in the fifth month of the third year of Genkō, Nitta Yoshisada of the southern side, pushing in from the direction of Kumegawa, fought the Kamakura host here at Bubaigawara and got the worse of it; he fell back for a time to Horikane and, while he was raising troops from all quarters, Miura Yoshikatsu came in from Sagami at the head of six thousand-odd horse. Yoshisada, overjoyed, split an army of one hundred thousand horse into three columns, fell on the Kamakura lines at Bubaigawara, and broke the Kamakura host utterly. This is what the world calls the Battle of Bubaigawara.

"This Bubaigawara is what the art of war calls a ground of highways,"

{j} Toshizō explained.

A ground of highways is a point where the roads come crowding in from three or four sides and meet. Because armies move easily there, such places have from of old been the sites of great pitched battles. So it was with Sekigahara in Mino.

Besides the Kōshū Highway and its branch roads, the Kamakura Highway, the Shimokawara Highway, the Kawasaki Highway and others meet here, or pass close by.

"And this is Bubai Bridge?"

{j} Okita peered in. To tell the truth, in his heart Okita was impressed. For a task of cutting their way in, just the two of them however many the enemy, that Toshizō should draw a map every time and work out a plan of battle—that impressed him.

(This man is no mere ruffian,)

{j} he was thinking, when old Gon came back.

"A fine to-do,"

{j} the old man said, sitting down beside Toshizō.

"It's night, so I can't say for sure, but counting the men posted about here and there, there must be twenty of them, I'd say."

On the bridge itself, though, there were two, said old Gon.

But at the foot of the embankment, in the shadows of the eaves of the ten-odd farmhouses nearby, they lay hidden, three and four at a time, holding their breath—so it seemed to him.

"Which quarter has the most men?"

"The north end of Bubai Bridge. They're swarming below the embankment, in the shadow of the zelkovas and such."

"So they would be."

"Can you tell that, sir?"

"More or less."

He had no wish to boast to the old man, but it was just as Toshizō had imagined. The enemy reckoned that he and the rest would leave the Kōshū Highway short of Fuchū, turn onto the Kamakura Highway, and come south to Bubai Bridge. That was the sensible route.

"A good thing, too,"

{j} Okita began to hum a tune.

"Stop the singing."

"Don't be angry. Toshizō, you're a fine strategist—that's what impressed me. If we'd gone on just now by the ordinary road from the Kōshū Highway, we'd have been surrounded north of that bridge and cut to ribbons."

"Gon,"

{j} Toshizō pressed a certain point on the map. It was south of the bridge.

"This will be thinly held, I fancy."

"Just so. There was only the one figure stirring there."

"Hm."

Toshizō glared at the map and thought a while; then, some wild notion striking him, he thrust a hand into his breast, pulled out a purse, and

{j} "Gon—keep it. And this business—not a word, though your mouth be torn open."

"I understand."

Gon vanished into the dark.

"Sōji, we cut in along the river. You from upstream, I from down; we close in little by little and meet under the bridge. From there we run up the embankment and cut down the men hidden in its shadow."

"I see."

Okita, being sharp, understood at once. That way, they took the enemy off guard.

More than that: the men hidden in the shadow of the embankment ought to be the weakest. That is, imagining the enemy's array, the ablest hands were on the bridge. One would be Shichiri Kennosuke, no doubt; the other, the hall's master, Hiruma Hanzō. These two were serving as decoys as well. And by the disposition of the men, the bridge was the command post.

The next ablest were the crowd hidden at the north end of the bridge, whose task was to close in and make the kill.

Seen so, the men below the embankment were, as it were, the reserve, and beyond doubt the least useful of the lot.

When a small party attacks an enemy line, there are two ways of it. There is the case where the best course is to strike straight for the commander, fell him, and flee; and the case where one breaks up the weak face of the line and so deals the enemy a blow in numbers.

Toshizō took the latter.

"They'll never dream, the men on the bridge, that we come up out of the river. We break up the men nearest to hand; and if, once broken, they prove unexpectedly brittle, we cut down either Hiruma or Shichiri. If their guard is firm and it can't be done, we cut down four or five and flee."

"And where do we meet?"

"This graveyard."

Toshizō pointed to the cloth-wrapped bundle set beside him,

{j} "A change of clothes is in this. Our things will be soaked to a pulp with blood, so by daybreak they'll be past walking in. We change here, and go straight back to Edo just as we are."

Then, handing Okita a whistle,

{j} "If we're parted, and I blow this, take it for the signal to draw off. If you blow, it means you're in danger, and I'll come to help at once."

The two set out.
