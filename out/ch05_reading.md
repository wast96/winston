## Chapter 5. The People of the Day Know Not the Cloud-Piercing Tree

It was completely unexpected.

Muci realized that he was making a grave mistake. An unforgivable mistake.

There must be no fresh complications!

After half a minute’s thought he drew on his gloves and made himself grow calm. First he wiped down the edges of the wooden crate on every side, then laid a thick sheaf of ink-printed newspapers back inside in neat order, shut the lid, and last of all locked it. Then he returned the crate to its place. He had scarcely finished when he heard footsteps beyond the door and the voice of Rong Chu.

“Lao Du, listen to me—this pocket watch of mine is solid gold, you’d never lose by buying it. Lao Du, Lao Du… wait a moment. Wait.”

The door opened. Muci was lying idly on the bed, reading a newspaper.

“You’re back?” Muci greeted him politely.

Du Luning swept his eyes over the whole cabin and said coldly, “We’re almost there. Best get ready early.”

Muci nodded.

“The three of us shared a boat by some happy chance—let’s part as good friends.” Somehow the situation in the room struck Rong Chu as uncanny, and the words came blurting out of him.

A faint, barely perceptible smile showed at the corner of Du Luning’s mouth. “What’s this? Reading the paper with your gloves still on?”

Muci said nothing more. He stretched lazily, and then simply spread the newspaper over his face and went to sleep.

When the boat reached Hangzhou, the three men parted at the Hangzhou station, shaking hands in farewell—“parting as good friends,” after all.

Following the address he carried, Yang Muci soon found the Hangzhou Police School. At the gate he inquired of a guard, who directed him to report to the sentry post on Third Avenue.

Two large trucks, sheeted over with black tarpaulins, stood at the Third Avenue sentry post; armed guards were everywhere, and a good many new students like Muci were registering in turn and answering the instructors’ questions. Muci saw that every student who passed the checking instructor’s scrutiny climbed up onto one of those two tarpaulin-covered trucks—no one knew how many people were packed inside, or where these people were bound.

Yang Muci joined the line. Ahead of him stood a young woman, a little past twenty, lovely of face and slender as a willow. Soon they drew near the instructor in charge of registration.

“Name?” the instructor asked.

“Xin Lili,” the woman answered.

“Admission notice?”

“I’ve been transferred over from the Seventh Branch School.”

“The Seventh Branch—which class?”

“The telecommunications class.”

“Papers and letter of introduction.”

Xin Lili produced her papers and her letter of introduction. Muci watched the instructor finish his review and hand her a special pass stamped with an embossed seal, telling her: “Second truck, intelligence group.”

Dragging her luggage, Xin Lili passed the checkpoint without trouble. It was Yang Muci’s turn.

“Name?”

“Yang Muci.”

“Papers.”

Muci handed over his papers.

“Your admission notice?”

“I—I lost my admission notice on the way here. I’m truly very sorry.”

“In that case, until your admission notice turns up, I can’t let you through. My apologies. Next.”

“Instructor!” Muci’s hand came down on the table.

“What do you think you’re doing?”

In an instant four or five guards with loaded rifles closed in on every side. Muci drew his hand back. “I meant no offense,” he explained. “I really and truly did lose that form. If I can’t report in on time today, I’ll be sleeping in the street, for there’s no money left in my pocket. Please, you must help me.”

Just then a telephone rang in the sentry box, and a guard called the registration instructor to the gate to collect a document. The instructor stepped out of the box, and after a while he came back.

“Your name is Yang Muci.”

“Yes,” Muci answered.

“Your admission notice has been delivered.” The document in the instructor’s hand was none other than Yang Muci’s admission notice. It genuinely startled him; looking closely, he saw that it was indeed the very form he had lost, and his mind clouded over, thicker and more bewildered than before, as he watched the instructor issue him a special pass.

“You’re on the first truck, action group.”

“Thank you, Instructor.” Muci dragged his luggage through the checkpoint. In front of the first truck he was told that his luggage would be held for the time being in the school’s keeping—confiscated, in effect, for now. Empty-handed and nimble-footed, Muci was helped up onto the first truck.

The truck was full of student-looking people, none of them saying much. Muci seized the lull to go over, carefully, everyone and everything of the past two days, sorting out with care each link that touched him closely. Just which link had gone wrong?

Had Lao Yu’s people been secretly protecting him all along—had they found this “admission notice” for him? No. From the moment he took that form in hand, his connection up and down the line to Lao Yu was, for the time being, at an end; barring the most extraordinary of circumstances, he and Lao Yu were to have no contact whatever. That was discipline.

Had the police at the Shanghai platform lent a hand? That too was unlikely, for if the police had come by the form, they would have put it straight into the “lost passenger property office,” and at most mailed it on to him—whereas this “admission notice” had reached Hangzhou at the very same time he did.

Was it Rong Chu? There was no reason for it; by the time he met Rong Chu the form was already lost—which is to say Rong Chu had no notion the form even existed.

Was it Du Luning? A wallet once lost, a fellow passenger, a brand-new American-made wireless transmitter, even a face of unfathomable depth? The blurred lines had already sketched themselves into a clear picture…

Two hours later the trucks full of students rolled slowly out through the gates of the Hangzhou Police School. Where would fate carry them? None of them knew—only Muci knew that he was moving up to the very front line of his own war.

The true site of the Central Police Academy’s Special Police training class lay in wild, densely wooded hill country; the school was for all the world like a bathtub, ringed on every side by mountains. The two trucks jolted the whole way in, and once inside the school Muci and the rest jumped down one after another and went readily to help the women students down—mostly half-lifting, half-hauling them—and some of the students, growing acquainted, began to laugh and joke, so that the mood lightened a good deal. Muci noticed that among the students on his truck were two who seemed to be lovers; they walked together and never left each other’s side, their faces grave, without a trace of a smile.

Muci took in the layout within the school and without: sentry posts were spread all around the grounds, each one commanding a high point, the guards armed with live rounds, the watch severe. Barbed wire was strung thickly outside the walls; at first glance the place looked more like a prison. This band of unarmed students was like a company of “travelers” fitted with invisible handcuffs and leg-irons. One could only hope their journey would not be too long.

“Will the new students please collect their uniforms from the instruction office, and assemble on the parade ground in half an hour to await orders. Will the new students please collect their uniforms from the instruction office, and assemble on the parade ground in half an hour to await orders…” The school’s loudspeakers repeated the same message over and over, and the students followed their leading instructor to the instruction office.

Half an hour later the two groups—something over a hundred people—stood in neat uniform on the empty parade ground, the instructors drawn up in ranks to welcome them.

At a shouted command of “Attention!” Yang Muci caught sight of Du Luning.

He stood on a platform run up then and there on the school parade ground, his head held high, dressed in a crisply pressed uniform and a pair of snow-white gloves, his eyes very cold, his face severe and empty of any needless expression.

Faced with this sudden turn of events, Yang Muci found his mind grown all at once extraordinarily clear.

“Welcome, new students, to the Special Police training class of our Central Police Academy.” Du Luning led off the applause, and a wave of answering clapping rose across the parade ground.

“The Master said: Is it not a joy to learn, and to practice in season what one has learned? Is it not a delight to have friends come from afar? Is he not a gentleman who feels no rancor though others take no note of him? And what does this mean? It comes down to two words: to learn! Your purpose in coming here is to learn. The aim of learning is to work better in the days to come, to serve society better. To have friends come from afar; to feel no anger though others take no note of you—which is to say, here you have need of no renown whatever. The more obscure you are, the better you will be at infiltration and concealment.” Du Luning’s gloomy gaze swept the whole field. “But I must remind you all: this is no ordinary school, nor is it a common military academy. It is a secret, wholly sealed ‘espionage’ school!” A few uneasy cries of alarm went up from the crowd. “I am this school’s supreme executive officer—Du Luning, Major-General and Chief of the Intelligence Section of the Juntong Bureau! Here, my orders are iron discipline; you will carry them out unconditionally. Director Dai has authorized me, here, to use any means whatever to uphold that iron order, and here, in all things, my word is final!” Du Luning’s eye fell on Yang Muci. “This, of course, requires your complete cooperation. I must warn certain persons of hidden designs: do not try to challenge my authority, or to disturb your fellow students’ learning. Any act of feigned compliance with my orders will be severely punished—no matter who the offender is, no matter how strong his backing. Seeing that this is a school and you are all students, here every instructor, myself included, is your teacher; from now on you may simply address me as ‘Teacher.’ I have finished. If there is anything you do not understand, you may afterward speak with any instructor, myself among them. Dismissed.”

Ten minutes after the dismissal, Muci was taken directly by an Instructor Li to the school’s disciplinary office, where he was told that Du Luning wished to see him.

Muci had braced himself for the worst and readied his best response; but when he actually reached Du Luning’s office, he realized he might have to find some new way out of his difficulty, for the room was too dark—the heavy floor-length curtains shut out all the light of spring—and at close quarters Muci could feel the murderous air about Du Luning. The killing air was too heavy.

“Reporting, sir! Action-group trainee Yang Muci comes at your order, and awaits the Teacher’s instruction.”

“When did you arrive?” Du Luning asked.

He knew the answer perfectly well. “I came ashore on the same boat as you, Teacher.”

Du Luning swung a fist that landed full and solid on Muci’s face. Muci lost his footing and was thrown to the floor—but his body sprang up as though flung back by a spring, and he stood before Du Luning without so much as a quiver.

“When did you arrive?” Du Luning asked again.

“Twelve o’clock, noon today,” Muci answered.

“You didn’t go and look at the streets?”

“No.”

“You didn’t go and stroll round a print shop?”

“No.”

“You didn’t go and forge documents?”

Muci did not answer.

“Speak.”

“I don’t do things that break the law.”

Du Luning laughed. “Have you read the ‘Sayings of Master Zeng’?”

“I have.”

“Master Zeng said: Each day I examine myself on three points.”

“In planning for others, have I been less than loyal? In dealings with friends, have I been less than true? Have I failed to practice what was handed down to me?”

Du Luning clapped his hands lightly, in appreciation. “Do you know where you differ from the other trainees? ‘To practice in season what one has learned’—they have come here to learn. ‘Have I failed to practice what was handed down to me?’—you have come to review the old and know the new. Is that not so?”

“It is not!” Muci denied it flatly.

“Not so? What was your course of study?”

“Financial administration, at Tokyo Imperial University in Japan.”

“What course?”

“Financial administration.”

Without the least ceremony Du Luning dealt Muci another heavy blow full in the face, and this time, before Muci could recover, followed it with a second punch, knocking him to the floor again.

This time Muci did not try to brazen it out. He paused a moment before slowly picking himself up. “I’m sorry,” Muci said.

“What?”

“I’m sorry, Teacher. I did not mean to offend your dignity.”

“But you already have.”

“No blame lies on one who acts in ignorance!”

“Well said!” Du Luning walked along the edge of the desk. “But there is one thing I still can’t work out. You say you graduated in financial administration from Tokyo Imperial University in Japan, and we have evidence enough that you did graduate in financial administration from Tokyo Imperial University—so the question arises: does a course in finance and economics teach a man, too, to pick locks and force doors? Answer me!”

Muci did not answer.

“Why don’t you answer? Is it that you can’t answer? Or that there is simply no answering it? Have you already studied our trade? Then why did you come at all?”

“I have not!”

“You do it like an old hand!”

“I have not!”

“You have!”

“You’re framing me!”

“Why should I frame you? You’ve only just arrived; you and I are still strangers. Why should I not frame someone else, and pick you out to frame instead? Give me one reason.”

“Because I can open locks. I meddled with your personal belongings without leave.”

“You dodge the heavy charge for the light one.”

“When I was studying in Japan, I once did work-study at a lock factory. I learned to open not only ordinary household locks, but safes as well.”

“The young master of a great Shanghai banking house—doing work-study?”

“Believe it or not, as you please.”

“You may say it lightly, and I’ll hear it lightly. Perhaps we’ll have our second real conversation only after the new round of investigation into you comes back.” Without turning his head, Du Luning raised a hand and pointed to the door. “Out!”

“Sir!” Muci went out, pulling the door shut behind him.

At that same moment the other door leading into Du Luning’s office was pushed open, and Yu Xiaojiang—a Juntong Bureau major, a female operative, a dossier in her hand—came in. She was plain of feature, with a shrewdness about the brow; the sort of person whose joys and sorrows, likes and angers, are never easily read, the sort who vanishes without a trace the moment she plunges into a crowd.

“You heard all of it?” Du Luning asked.

“Yes, Chief.”

“What do you make of him?”

“One would have to say he has perfect self-possession and a formidable will.”

“High praise.” Du Luning lit a cigarette. “What’s that in your hand?”

“The family material on Yang Muci supplied by ‘Shanghai No. 7.’ I’ve already asked our informant in Tokyo to investigate all of Yang Muci’s records abroad for us. In about a week we’ll have a complete analysis of Yang Muci’s identity.”

Just then thunder rumbled faintly beyond the window. Du Luning yanked the curtains open; the sky was heavy with dark cloud, with all the look of a storm about to break over the mountains, the wind filling the tower.

In the far distance the tall green trees of the mountain gorge all but blocked Du Luning’s gazing line of sight.

“The people of the day know not the cloud-piercing tree; only when it has pierced the cloud do they call it high,” said Du Luning. “If Yang Muci proves to be sound, then this is a student I will train myself.”

“Yes, Chief.”

The storm came in earnest.

In the week of overloaded crash-training at the special class, many students collapsed, unequal to the strain; the trouble was that students under training here had no right to sick leave, and so, in the cruel military drills as they neared their end, one young woman student drowned.

A single stone raised a thousand waves. The students’ fury may well be imagined; they stormed the school’s instruction office, and a fierce clash broke out between instructors and students, until in the end gunfire settled the matter.

Yang Muci, however, took no part whatever in this violent outburst. In what time the drills left him, he applied himself single-mindedly to learning, under Yu Xiaojiang, to receive ciphers and to strip down and reassemble a wireless set. Until one day, quite by chance, he overheard the conversation of a pair of lovers among the students…
