### 1. Throwing Himself into the Work: Many Battles Still Ahead
In 1948, once the People's Liberation Army had turned from the strategic defensive to the strategic offensive, every field army was winning brilliant victories on its front.
In the northwest, the First Field Army had, since going over to the offensive, taken one heavily fortified enemy city after another and liberated many small and middling towns. Peng Dehuai and He Long were now driving straight for Xi'an, the great northwestern stronghold. Xi'an could be taken any day, and the work of taking over the large and middling cities already liberated or about to be liberated had to be strengthened. So in March 1949 the Party Center appointed Chen Yangshan head of the Social Affairs Department of the Northwest Bureau of the Central Committee, in charge of intelligence work in the northwest and of the takeover of its cities. His first task was to prepare for the takeover of Xi'an.
Chen Yangshan had done intelligence and united-front work in Xi'an from 1936 to 1940. He knew its people, its ground, and its ways, and to make sure Xi'an came through undamaged he had, on taking up his post, ordered our underground armed personnel to organize a force of more than two thousand men, who dug out the explosives the enemy had buried at the railway bridges, the power plant, the factories, and elsewhere before they fled.
Xi'an sits in the middle of the Wei River plain, where more than ten dynasties had made their capital; it is one of China's six great ancient capitals. It stands on the Longhai Railway, the traffic hub linking the northwest to every province of the country, and its strategic value was of the highest. Chiang Kai-shek had sent his own trusted man, Hu Zongnan, known as "the King of the Northwest," to hold it. By now Hu Zongnan's forces were falling back step by step; the Liberation Army was already at the gates, and the Kuomintang troops milled about like ants on a hot griddle. Our intelligence personnel obtained the exact time of Hu Zongnan's withdrawal from Xi'an and the particulars of his retreat south — its routes, and the dispositions, unit designations, code names, garrisons, and equipment of each army and division — and got hold of a street map of Xi'an and plans of its defense works. The liberation of Xi'an was now a settled thing.
The northwest intelligence system had a solid base. The Social Affairs Department of the Northwest Bureau had already brought more than 130 intelligence hands into Xi'an, placed in the Shaanxi Kuomintang's civil, military, police, and secret-service organs and in the telecommunications and other key departments, and had gathered a great deal of important military and political intelligence. The system led by the Central Intelligence Department had also come to grasp the doings of the Kuomintang's Central Statistics Bureau secret service in Shaanxi and its plan, laid by the Xi'an Garrison Command, to burn the city down.
On the eve of the liberation of Xi'an, our underground intelligence personnel there used the enemy's own radio at the Xi'an Pacification Headquarters to make contact with a set we had specially rigged, and sent back more than ninety secret reports on the movements of Hu Zongnan's forces and of the upper ranks of the Kuomintang's party and secret-service men. The intelligence proved of great value to the liberation of Xi'an, and won the praise of the commanders Peng Dehuai and He Long.
To make the takeover go smoothly, the Xi'an Military Control Commission was formed before the city fell, and Chen Yangshan was named head of its Public Safety Department. On May 10, under Chen Yangshan's direction, the department drew up the list of targets and the plan for taking over the city once our forces entered, and it was decided that the department would take charge of all the personnel, property, arms, and files of the Kuomintang's party, police-and-gendarmerie, and secret-service systems in Xi'an, together with their open and secret front organizations.
On May 20, 1949, Xi'an, the northwestern stronghold of the reactionary Kuomintang regime, was liberated. Chen Yangshan led his public-security and intelligence personnel into the city at once and took charge of the takeover, of public order, and of the task of rooting out enemy agents and bandits. Public security sub-bureaus and a Xi'an Municipal Security Committee were set up, and the aims and tasks of public-security work were laid down.
On May 25 the Xi'an Municipal People's Government was established, and Chen Yangshan was appointed chief of the Public Security Bureau. To keep public order he issued in quick succession the public notices "Registration of Stragglers and Disbanded Soldiers" and "Measures for the Registration of Reactionary Party and Corps Cadres." His aims and tasks were clear and his measures tight and timely, and the work was highly effective.
By the end of June his men had cracked a case of Kuomintang deep-cover agents headed by Huang Xueyu, arresting ten of them and seizing one radio set, eight pistols, and five barrels and two crates of explosive materiel.
Early in July they broke another major bandit case, capturing seventeen bandits headed by Sun Miantian and Ding Lao'er, and turning up one short-arm, a forged seal of the Xi'an Municipal People's Government, more than ten sets of identity papers, and part of the loot.
Late in July they cracked yet another: the deep-cover case of Yao Huanwen at the Eastern Shaanxi station of the Second Section of the Kuomintang Pacification Headquarters, and a military-espionage case involving the Kuomintang's 30th Army.
Returning after nine years to Xi'an, the ground he had once worked, Chen Yangshan gave his own share to the safety of its people.
In September 1949, Chen Yangshan received notice from the newly established Ministry of Public Security under the Central Military Commission: he was to hand over his work in the northwest cleanly and come at once to Beiping for the First National Public Security Work Conference, which the Center was convening.
Chen Yangshan sensed that a new mission might again be waiting, and so he lost no time in briefing the Northwest Bureau, the Xi'an Military Control Commission, and the Xi'an Municipal People's Government in full before setting out for Beiping.
Reaching Beiping, Chen Yangshan was put up at the Six Nations Hotel on Zhengyi Road. There he met old comrades who had come for the conference — among them Li Shiying, Xu Jianguo, Wang Jinxiang, and Zhou Xing — parted for years and now gathered joyfully under one roof, all of them feeling the warmth and gladness of it.
Once settled, Chen Yangshan heard that his old chief Li Kenong was in Beiping too, and, stirred, he wanted to see this leader, elder brother, and comrade at once. That very evening he hurried to Gongxian Hutong to call on Li Kenong, whom he had not seen in two years. Li Kenong came to meet him warmly and said, half joking, "Our 'lucky general' is here — welcome, welcome!"
Chen Yangshan saw that Li Kenong looked ruddier than he had in the Jin-Sui days, though the lenses of his nearsighted eyes seemed to have gained a few more degrees.
After the first pleasantries, Chen Yangshan talked freely with Li Kenong for the better part of an hour, giving him a full account of the northwest and of Xi'an, while Li Kenong listened and nodded his satisfaction. They took the midday meal together, and then, not wanting to cut into Li Kenong's rest, Chen Yangshan rose to take his leave. Li Kenong told him two things: first, that Kang Sheng was now in Beiping, staying at the Beijing Hotel, and that Chen Yangshan should find time to look in on him; and second, that the Central Intelligence Department was summing up twenty-two years of intelligence work. Li Kenong invited Chen Yangshan to take part, and Chen Yangshan said he would be very glad to.
Chen Yangshan then sat in on two discussions of the draft summary of intelligence work. "This summing-up," he said, "has taught me a great deal. The work we did in the past was like a heap of loose sand; there was no summing it up, and one could see neither its successes nor its mistakes clearly. This time practice and theory have been brought together, the content is rich, and it is a first-rate chance to learn."
Before the public-security conference opened, Chen Yangshan found time to go to the Beiping Municipal Public Security Bureau to see Tan Zhengwen, an old comrade who had fought beside him in the Jin-Sui Border Region. At Tan Zhengwen's home he was surprised to run into Chen Long, who had come to call on Tan Zhengwen as well. Parted for years and met now by chance, they were overjoyed. Tan Zhengwen and his wife, Jiang Peng, received the two of them warmly. Afterward Tan Zhengwen gathered together several comrades who had fought in the Jin-Sui Border Region and now worked at the Beiping Municipal Public Security Bureau — Feng Jiping, Di Fei, Qu Rixin, Liu Yong, and others — for a reunion with Chen Yangshan.
The conference was called by the Center, and its business was of great importance; Mao Zedong, Liu Shaoqi, Zhou Enlai, Zhu De, and other central leaders gave important directives to it. But the circle of those attending was small, limited to the chief responsible comrades of the Xi'an departments of the greater administrative regions and the centrally administered municipalities. The conference made considerable changes in personnel: Li Shiying was moved to be head of public security of the East China Military and Administrative Committee; Zhou Xing to the same post on the Southwest Military and Administrative Committee; Tan Zhengwen to director of public security of Guangdong Province and concurrently chief of the Guangzhou Municipal Public Security Bureau; Chen Yangshan to member of the Shanghai Municipal Party Committee and deputy chief of the Shanghai Municipal Public Security Bureau; Luo Ruiqing to chief of the Beiping bureau; Chen Long to chief of the Nanjing bureau; and Xu Jianguo to chief of the Tianjin bureau.
On October 1, 1949, the founding of the nation was proclaimed. Chen Yangshan was invited to watch from the reviewing stand at Tiananmen in Beijing. New China had come into being; beside himself with excitement, he threw his whole heart into the work of building it.
In November 1949, on the eve of taking up his post in Shanghai, Chen Yangshan went to bid Li Kenong farewell. Li Kenong said to him: "Shanghai is a complicated place, with no end of problems. Imperialism, the feudal forces, Chiang Kai-shek's secret agents, the secret-society gangs — all of them are at work wrecking a city only just liberated. When we were in Shanghai in the twenties and thirties we knew it as it was then, and it has changed a great deal since. Learn all you can from the East China comrades, and never take it lightly." Chen Yangshan was deeply grateful for Li Kenong's counsel.
On reaching Shanghai, Chen Yangshan learned the enemy situation and the state of the city from the East China comrades — among them Chen Yi, Pan Hannian, Li Shiying, Wu Kejian, Liang Guobin, Wang Fan, and Yang Fan — and then plunged straight into the tense and crowded work. He found Shanghai, just as Li Kenong had said, extremely complicated. Complicated to begin with, it was more chaotic still now that the old order was giving way to the new: public order was in disarray and counter-revolutionary activity rampant. Chen Yangshan felt the full weight of what he had taken on, and he toiled day and night, not even resting on Sundays.
At the end of 1950 Chen Yangshan's aged mother, seventy-five, fell gravely ill and wished to see him one last time, but his work was so heavy that he could not once get home. Before long his mother died. Still Chen Yangshan could not get away to see to the funeral, and could only send his wife, Zhang Suzhen, back to their home district to bury her — and so the old woman left the world with that regret.
In September 1950, Chen Long, chief of the Nanjing Municipal Public Security Bureau, was moved to work at the Ministry of Public Security in Beijing. Chen Yangshan was transferred to be a standing member of the Nanjing Municipal Party Committee, deputy head of its Political and Legal Affairs Commission, and chief of the Nanjing Municipal Public Security Bureau, concurrently chief procurator of the Nanjing Municipal Procuratorate. Holding several posts at once, Chen Yangshan carried a still heavier load. On receiving his orders he handed over the Shanghai bureau without delay and went to take up his post in Nanjing.
At the Nanjing Municipal Public Security Bureau he met many old comrades from the Central Social Affairs Department at Zaoyuan in Yan'an, among them Chen Long, Hai Yu, Gan Lu, Ma Jingzheng, and Huang Gengfu. Reunited in Nanjing after years apart, they were overjoyed, and looked forward to the days of fighting together ahead.
Nanjing is known in Chinese history as the "sacred ground of six dynasties"; before liberation it was the capital of the reactionary Kuomintang government, and its reactionary roots ran deep. As the Kuomintang collapsed, to sow chaos for the Communists it turned loose more than three thousand criminal convicts it had held, and left many agents behind in hiding. Nanjing lay close to Shanghai, alike in its political and economic conditions and no less complicated. Because of this special position it was made a centrally administered municipality in the early days after liberation. To keep Nanjing safe was no light burden for Chen Yangshan.
In 1951 the nationwide Campaign to Suppress Counter-Revolutionaries was under way. The preliminary-examination section of the Nanjing Municipal Public Security Bureau ran into trouble while interrogating Huang Kai, a veteran secret agent and traitor of the Kuomintang's Central Statistics Bureau.
From 1930 to 1933 Huang Kai had taken part again and again in wrecking our Party's organizations in Wuhan, Shanghai, Nanjing, and elsewhere, arresting and killing many responsible comrades of our Party; his reactionary arrogance knew no bounds, and his hands were steeped in blood. Our Party's Central Committee member Yang Pao'an and his wife, Luo Qiyuan and his wife, the whole family of the Hubei provincial leader Chen Hua, the patriot Deng Yanda, and more than twenty other comrades all died at Huang Kai's hands.
The examiners of the section were all young men in their twenties, unclear about the enemy-and-us struggles of the thirties; the historical files had not yet been put in order and there was nowhere to check anything, so although they questioned Huang many times, they could not tell true from false in what he confessed, could not judge how far his candor went, still less test the historical events his statement would have to confirm. It was all without a thread to follow.
When Chen Yangshan heard of it, he telephoned the section himself and asked: "Is your section interrogating a prisoner named Huang Kai?"
The section chief, Qin Jie, said they were, and gave Chen Yangshan a brief account of how the case stood and where it was stuck.
Chen Yangshan told Qin Jie that he would examine Huang Kai himself the next afternoon.
The section's comrades were delighted to hear the chief would examine Huang Kai in person, and quickly made everything ready.
The next afternoon Chen Yangshan came to the section to examine Huang Kai, with Qin Jie and Lian Chengyi sitting in. At first Huang Kai dodged the heavy questions for the light ones and was far from honest. Seeing this, Chen Yangshan laid before Huang Kai the whole record of crimes committed by the Kuomintang's Central Statistics secret service in the thirties. At that Huang Kai realized this examiner was his very adversary of those years, and, knowing his crimes could no longer be hidden, made a full and honest confession.
The examination ran a good three hours, and Huang Kai gave a systematic account of the crimes he had committed during his time in the Central Statistics secret service — taking part in the seizure and killing of Communists and progressives in Wuhan, Shanghai, Nanjing, and elsewhere; his attempt in Wuhan to arrest Zhou Enlai, which came to nothing; and how, afterward in Shanghai, together with another Central Statistics agent, Zhang Chong, he had impersonated Wu Hao, Zhou Enlai's alias, to run a "Wu Hao Notice" in the Shen Bao and the Xinwen Bao, fabricating a declaration of withdrawal from the Party in Wu Hao's name.
Afterward Huang Kai wrote out a detailed statement. The case was examined a few more times and soon closed. This is how it had come about:
After Gu Shunzhang's defection, the men who had been planted inside the Kuomintang's secret service — Qian Zhuangfei, Li Kenong, Hu Di, Song Zaisheng, and others — were forced to leave their posts. Bao Junfu, who had been serving the Communist Party from within the enemy's secret-service apparatus, was seized and held by Chen Lifu.
With Gu Shunzhang's defection the apparatus was broken up, and the Party Center's room to move in Shanghai narrowed further still. At that time only four members of the Party's Central Politburo remained in Shanghai — Xiang Zhongfa, Wang Ming, Zhou Enlai, and Lu Futan — and the general secretary was still Xiang Zhongfa.
Before long a fresh blow fell. Xiang Zhongfa had always led a loose and undisciplined life, and Zhou Enlai had warned him again and again not to go out and about. On June 21, Xiang Zhongfa slipped out on his own and stayed away a whole night. The next day, hailing a taxi at the door, he was recognized and arrested by Kuomintang agents. Pan Hannian, only just transferred to the Central Special Branch, got word and reported at once to Zhou Enlai. Xiang Zhongfa knew Zhou Enlai's lodgings and had a key; if he turned, he would surely bring the agents to make the arrest. Zhou Enlai moved swiftly to the Metropole Hotel on Simalu, a place kept by foreigners, and to verify whether Xiang Zhongfa had really turned, he had Tan Zhongyu, head of the Special Branch's action section, set Red Squad men to keep a wonton stall near his lodgings. In the dead of night they saw a party of agents marching a man to Zhou Enlai's lodgings, and open the back door with a key. It was plain that Xiang Zhongfa had indeed defected. Xiang Zhongfa's defection did not deliver Zhou Enlai into their hands, but Xiang Zhongfa gave up Ren Bishi's wife, Chen Congying, and the agents at once arrested Chen Congying and Yang Xiuzhen, the woman living with Xiang Zhongfa. Yang Xiuzhen was a woman of the streets, yet like the Communist Chen Congying she faced the Kuomintang agents' torture and never let slip a word — while Xiang Zhongfa, general secretary of the Party's Central Committee, came forward to urge Yang Xiuzhen to tell the truth.
Once Zhou Enlai had confirmed Xiang Zhongfa's defection, he parted for a time from Deng Yingchao and took shelter at the home of Tan Zhongyu and Zhou Huinian. Zhou Huinian recalled: after Zhou Enlai came, he shared the big bed with Tan Zhongyu, while she moved a small bed out to sleep elsewhere. As the danger grew closer, Zhou Enlai could only lie low by day and move by night, going out in disguise only after dark. In disguise he looked like a Japanese. Once, changing clothes in a hurry, he put both feet into one trouser leg, to the delight of everyone present.
After Gu Shunzhang and Xiang Zhongfa had been arrested and defected one after the other, of the three-man Central Special Committee only Zhou Enlai was left, and Chen Geng, Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, Tan Zhongyu, and the rest left Shanghai one by one. On the proposal of the Comintern's Far Eastern Bureau, the Party's Central Committee decided to set up a provisional Central Politburo in Shanghai, made up of six men — Bo Gu, Zhang Wentian, Kang Sheng, Chen Yun, Lu Futan, and Li Zhusheng — with Bo Gu in overall charge. In early December, Zhou Enlai left Shanghai, taking ship by way of Hong Kong and going through Guangdong to the Central Revolutionary Base Area in Jiangxi.
Chen Lifu, having failed to take Zhou Enlai, was not only unwilling to let it rest but grew somewhat vicious out of shame. On September 1, 1931, he ordered a "reward for the arrest" of Zhou Enlai and others. From November into early December 1931, he further put out, in Gu Shunzhang's name, notices in the Shanghai Shibao, Shen Bao, Xinwen Bao, Shishi Xinbao, Minguo Ribao, and other papers offering a reward for the capture of Zhou Enlai and others.
When the Kuomintang's Central Statistics secret service under Chen Lifu found the manhunt fruitless and the rewards of no avail, it fell back at last on the tricks of slander and provocation, forging a so-called "Notice of Wu Hao and Others Withdrawing from the Communist Party." From February 16, 1932, the Shanghai Shibao, Xinwen Bao, Shishi Xinbao, and others ran the notice in turn. The Shen Bao, the most widely read paper in Shanghai, had received it on February 15 too, but its advertising office, on reading it, concluded that a notice claiming 243 people had left the Party yet naming only one, Wu Hao, must have something wrong with it, and decided to hold it back on the 16th. On the 19th the Kuomintang's press-censorship office demanded to know why the Shen Bao had not run it, and only then, on the 20th and 21st, did the Shen Bao print it. The full text ran:
{v} Notice of Wu Hao and Others Withdrawing from the Communist Party
{v} We are firmly convinced that the course now taken by the Chinese Communist Party — the so-called building up of the Red Army to hold the present government in check — is nothing less than the wasting away of China's strength to resist Japan, whose only end must be to make of China a puppet of Japan and to sink the Chinese nation into an abyss from which there is no return in ten thousand ages; it runs counter to our own original purpose in taking up revolution. Moreover the international line the Party follows is but the Soviet Union's policy of self-interest. The Soviet Union proclaims at every turn that it will oppose imperialism, yet itself compromises with imperialism. Consider how, with Japan now invading China, the Soviet Union not only fails to keep strict neutrality but lends the Chinese Eastern Railway for Japan to move troops, and has concluded a nonaggression pact with Japan, thus fanning the flames of its aggression. What is commonly called aiding the weak and small nations is all a slogan to deceive our countrymen. We, awakened in conscience, hereby withdraw from the internationally directed Chinese Communist Party.
{v} Signed, Wu Hao and Two Hundred and Forty-Three Others
"Wu Hao" was a pen name of Zhou Enlai's. He had used it for a long time — as far back as January 1920, when he published articles in the Awakening. Those who wrote for the Awakening in those days mostly used pen names, and Zhou Enlai and the others drew lots for numbers and made their names from homophones of the numbers. Deng Yingchao drew number one, so her pen name was "Yihao."
The Kuomintang secret-service apparatus under Chen Lifu had played a vicious card, meaning to sow confusion inside the Communist Party and break it up. But by the time this "Wu Hao Notice" appeared, Zhou Enlai was long since at the Central Revolutionary Base Area. To expose the enemy's plot and set the facts straight, the provisional Center in Shanghai struck back by several means.
After Gu Shunzhang's defection the Party's Central Committee had decided to move Chen Yun to head the Central Special Branch, with Kang Sheng, Pan Hannian, and others working alongside him. Chen Yun took up this heavy charge in a moment of crisis without flinching. Though he worked in the Special Branch only a few months before being moved to be secretary of the Party group of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions, he handled a good many grave matters, among them this great affair of Xu Enzeng's fabricated "Wu Hao Notice" slandering Zhou Enlai as having left the Party.
When Chen Yun saw this so-called "Wu Hao Notice" in the papers, he was furious at the base way the Chiang secret service lived by spreading lies, and he laughed too at how blinkered its chiefs were, not even knowing that Zhou Enlai had left Shanghai for the Jiangxi Soviet as long ago as last November. After talking it over with Kang Sheng and Pan Hannian, he reported the matter to the provisional Party Center, and Bo Gu and the other leaders decided at once to issue a statement in the Party Central organ Struggle, sternly pointing out that this was a new trick of Kuomintang reactionary slander, a shameful act, and that no amount of slander by the Kuomintang against the Comintern, the Chinese Communist Party, or individuals could save the Kuomintang from ruin. Seeing the provisional Center's statement, the mass of Party members saw the enemy's sinister tricks the more clearly and trusted the Party the more. In late February word of the Kuomintang secret service's slander reached the Jiangxi Soviet, and Mao Zedong, chairman of the provisional central government of the Chinese Soviet Republic, at once issued a solemn declaration: Comrade Wu Hao was serving in a leading post on the military commission of the Soviet central government, and not only had he certainly not left the Communist Party, still less would he ever utter the absurd and reactionary words in that notice. This was plainly the slander of Kuomintang partisans who butchered workers, peasants, and soldiers and sold China to imperialism. Mao Zedong said further to Zhou Enlai: why would the Chiang secret service get up a fake Wu Hao notice like this? Beyond slandering you, it seeks to set the leading comrades against one another and to weaken the Party and the Red Army. But such a plot cannot succeed. The refutations by the provisional Center and by Mao Zedong did much to expose the Chiang secret service's schemes. For all the enemy's noise and the foul air it stirred up, the mass of Party members stood the more firmly around the Party, and not one Communist left it.
At that urgent hour, besides reporting to the provisional Center, Chen Yun talked it over with Kang Sheng and Pan Hannian and judged that the most direct way was to publish a clear refutation in the press, stating plainly that the "Wu Hao Notice" was a lie someone had deliberately manufactured; but the newspapermen might well fear a Chiang reprisal and dare not print it. Just at this difficult pass, Mr. Tao Xingzhi, a democratic figure serving as adviser to the Shen Bao, was baffled to see in the paper that the Zhou Enlai he admired had put out a "Wu Hao Notice," and he asked Pan Hannian for the truth. When Pan told him it was the Chiang secret service's shameless lie, he was filled with indignation and denounced the crimes of the Chiang agents. When Pan raised the idea of publishing a statement in the press to set the record straight but feared no one would dare print it, Tao Xingzhi offered on his own account that, as an adviser to the Shen Bao, he would go to its chairman, Shi Liangcai, to work the matter out. Shi Liangcai was progressive in his thinking and supported the Communist Party's call to resist Japan and save the nation. He had not agreed to run the "Wu Hao Notice" in the first place, and had printed it only under duress from the Chiang agents; toward Tao Xingzhi's proposed refutation he took a positive view, though the method had to be weighed. After careful study, on February 22 the Shen Bao published a statement.
To refute the notice further, Chen Yun again worked out a plan with Kang Sheng and Pan Hannian, and judged that the only way to issue a clear refutation was to have a lawyer come forward to represent Wu Hao and publish in the press a statement denying the "Wu Hao Notice"; there was no other course. So Huang Mulan was sent to sound out the lawyer Chen Zhigao. Chen Zhigao thought a lawyer's published refutation was workable. But an ordinary lawyer would carry no great weight; a famous one was needed, and famous lawyers too feared that offending the Kuomintang secret service would cost them their living, or even that the Kuomintang would have them killed, and dared not make such a statement on another's behalf. There was one way left: to go into the foreign concessions and engage a foreign lawyer. Foreign lawyers enjoyed privileges in the concessions; the Kuomintang, a bully to the Chinese, cringed before foreigners, and once a foreign lawyer issued a statement the Kuomintang would swallow its anger and be at a loss — and there was precedent for it. Drawing on his dealings with foreign lawyers, Chen judged it best to engage the licensed French lawyer Ba He.
So the provisional Center in Shanghai paid handsomely to bring forward Ba He, the French lawyer and standing legal adviser to the Shen Bao, and on March 4 ran in the Shen Bao an "Urgent Notice by Lawyer Ba He on Behalf of Zhou Shaoshan." The full text ran:
{v} Mr. Zhou Shaoshan has come to this office and states as follows: that in submitting articles for publication he had used the alias Wu Hao; that a notice recently carried in the press, of Wu Hao and 243 others withdrawing from the Communist Party, has put relatives and friends at home and abroad to the trouble of writing and wiring their concern; that his name Wu Hao, apart from its use in his writings, was never used for any outward activity, so that this Mr. Wu Hao must certainly be another person; that the so-called withdrawal of two hundred and forty-three persons from the Communist Party at one time has in truth nothing to do with him, touches his personal reputation, and is apt to give rise to misunderstanding; that he further fears lest unworthy persons turn right and wrong upside down and make trouble on the pretext of it; and that he therefore specially entrusts your firm to make this statement on his behalf and to thank his relatives and friends for their letters and telegrams of concern. Accordingly, this is duly published on his behalf as above.
{v} The Law Office. No. 40 Damalu, Sixth Floor, Room 5.
"Zhou Shaoshan" was one of Zhou Enlai's aliases, well known within the Communist Party. Thanks to the Party organization's quick-witted counterstroke, the designs of Chen Lifu and his kind came to nothing.
What Chen Lifu never foresaw was that more than forty years later it would be Jiang Qing and her sort who, for purposes that would not bear the light, dug out again Chen Lifu's old "masterpiece" to slander and attack Zhou Enlai.
Afterward Qin Jie set down an account of Chen Yangshan's examination of Huang Kai:
{v} Comrade Yangshan examined Huang Kai himself, with Comrade Lian Chengyi keeping the record and me sitting in. Comrade Yangshan examined him a good two or three hours, and Huang Kai gave a systematic account of what he had done during his time in the Central Statistics secret service: taking part in the persecution of Communists and progressives in Wuhan, Shanghai, and Nanjing; his attempt in Shanghai to arrest Zhou Enlai, which came to nothing; and how, afterward in Shanghai, together with another Central Statistics agent, Zhang Chong, he had impersonated Zhou Enlai under the alias (Wu Hao) to run a "Wu Hao Notice" in the Shen Bao and the Xinwen Bao, fabricating a declaration of withdrawal from the Party in the name of more than two hundred members. After the notice appeared, not one Communist gave himself up. Before long came the fact of lawyer Ba He's refutation in the Shen Bao on Wu Hao's behalf.
{v} In the course of examining Huang Kai, Comrade Yangshan also questioned him about the related crimes of the Central Statistics agents in the thirties. Huang Kai had by now caught on that Comrade Yangshan was his adversary of those years, and knowing his crimes could not be hidden, he confessed in fair detail. From then on Huang Kai wrote out his statement in order. After Comrade Yangshan's examination this case, once taken up, had a thread to follow, and was soon closed.
{v} During the "Cultural Revolution," the "Gang of Four" used the "Wu Hao Notice" to frame Comrade Zhou Enlai. The statement obtained from Huang Kai in Comrade Yangshan's examination was just what powerfully proved Comrade Zhou Enlai innocent.
Word that Chen Yangshan had examined a prisoner in person spread and caused a stir, drawing a quick response from the officers and men of the whole bureau. People said to one another: Chief Chen Yangshan held several posts, his work ran in a thousand directions, and that a man so busy could still go down to the grass roots and help solve its difficulties was a rare and admirable thing.
This act of Chen Yangshan's set an example for the bureau's officers and men and taught them much; it moved them to improve their style of work, and every task showed marked results. For this, at the Third National Public Security Work Conference, Chairman Mao singled out the Nanjing Municipal Public Security Bureau and Chen Yangshan for praise.
In June 1952 the North China Administrative Committee set up a public security bureau. Chen Yangshan was transferred to Beijing to serve as a member of the North China Bureau of the Central Committee, deputy head of the Political and Legal Affairs Commission of the North China Administrative Committee, and chief of the North China Public Security Bureau. During his time in North China he saw chiefly to the organization and professional building of the public-security organs of the North China provinces, and helped the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region solve the problem of establishing large labor-reform farms.
In the autumn of 1954 the greater administrative regions were abolished and the North China Administrative Committee dissolved. Chen Yangshan was transferred to be a vice-minister of the Ministry of Justice of the Central People's Government and deputy secretary of its Party group. He had never done judicial work. To get to know it as fast as he could, he set himself to learn the trade, and every day, besides his work, found time to read law books and to seek out the noted jurist Minister Shi Liang for instruction, so that he might carry out the Center's aims and policies for judicial work in earnest.
In the autumn of 1956 the Chinese Communist Party held its Eighth National Congress, and Chen Yangshan was elected a delegate to it. When the Congress ended he joined the other members of the Ministry of Justice's Party group in a careful review, in the light of the Congress's resolutions, of the ministry's shortcomings in carrying out the Center's aims and policies for judicial work, and they drew up measures to put them right.
### 2. A Deep Comradeship: Li Kenong and Chen Yangshan
People called him "the general," yet he had never led troops or fought a battle; in his sixty-four years there was no grand scene of campaigning far and wide, and yet he was one of the brightest stars among the founding generals of the Republic.
Before National Day in 1955 he put on the gleaming rank of a colonel general, and Chairman Mao Zedong bestowed on him in person the First Order of August the First, the First Order of Independence and Freedom, and the First Order of Liberation. He was one of the fifty-two generals first raised to the rank of colonel general in the Republic — a "scholar-general," standing out remarkably.
Yet the life of this man of mystery ran, plain and solid, through nearly every great event: from the underground struggle in Shanghai in the 1930s to the counter-"encirclement" campaigns in the Central Soviet; from winning over Wang Yaqiao, the foremost assassin of the Republic, to intercepting the great traitor Zhang Guotao; from making contact with the Northeastern Army's commanders in the Xi'an Incident to the later Chongqing negotiations and the Panmunjom talks during the War to Resist America and Aid Korea.
Who was he? Receiving foreign guests once, Chairman Mao Zedong spoke of him and said: "He is China's great spymaster — only he is the Communist Party's spymaster."
When the American Central Intelligence Agency learned of his death, it was so overjoyed that it did the unheard-of thing of declaring a three-day holiday, to celebrate the passing of a formidable adversary.
He was Li Kenong — a name to command respect, a name touched with a certain mystery. His life, elusive and full of hazard and suspense, rich in the color of legend, was itself a splendid romance of Chinese intelligence history.
Li Kenong and Chen Yangshan stood in the relation of senior to junior, and were comrades besides who had weathered the same storms in the same boat. In Chen Yangshan's heart Li Kenong was forever his elder brother, forever the man who showed him the way.
{p} "Seeing a comrade off, setting out on the march — no words between us, and tears in both our eyes."
Time and again, when Chen Yangshan met with hardship, it was Li Kenong, this elder brother and comrade seven years his senior, who appeared before him.
He could not forget how, back in the days of the White Terror, when he was in the Central Special Branch and Gu Shunzhang defected, the Party organization warned him in time to move so that no harm might come to him. By then Chen Yangshan knew that the intelligence had been so accurate because our Party had several heroes planted inside the enemy who passed word to him in time, but only in 1940, going from Xi'an to Yan'an, did Chen Yangshan learn that Li Kenong was one of them.
From then on Li Kenong was forever the model Chen Yangshan studied, and Li Kenong's legendary story spurred Chen Yangshan on at every turn, and was living material for the education of the younger generation.
In mid-March 1989, Chen Yangshan, himself gravely ill, wrote for the ninetieth anniversary of Li Kenong's birth an essay, "Comrade Li Kenong, an Outstanding Leader of the Intelligence Front," recalling his years of comradeship with this legendary hero.
Chen Yangshan wrote in the essay: "Li Kenong joined the ranks of the revolution very early, and became one of the leaders of the local Party organization. All his life he bore great charges, risking his life and matching wits and words with every sort of adversary, so opening new political ground, widening the Party's many activities, and laying a foundation for the Party's intelligence work as well. Long in the practice of intelligence, he made outstanding contributions to the Party's intelligence cause. He is a model for the intelligence worker, and a founder of international intelligence work."
Before 1927 the Party had no intelligence organization. The April 12 counter-revolutionary coup taught Chen Yangshan a bitter lesson; after Zhou Enlai came from Shanghai to Wuhan (May 1927) he set up an intelligence organization under the Central Military Commission, and its grasp of the enemy situation let the Party counter Wang Jingwei's anti-Communist plot and cut its losses in the failure of the Great Revolution. After the August 7 Conference the Party Center moved back from Wuhan to Shanghai, and Chen Yangshan came to understand that the safety of the Party's central leading organs had to be guarded; under Zhou Enlai's direct leadership the Central Special Branch was set up, charged with the grave task of protecting the Party Center. Before long it took up intelligence work against the enemy's detective organs, sending trusted comrades deep inside; it was at this time that Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and others planted themselves in the Kuomintang's central secret service, got hold of the enemy's important secrets, and smashed the great plot by which Chiang Kai-shek's secret service hoped to net Chen Yangshan and the Party Central organs in a single sweep, building an immortal merit in the defense of the Center.
Later Comrade Li Kenong was forced to withdraw from Shanghai to the Jiangxi Soviet, where he served as executive head of the State Political Security Bureau and in other posts. In the winter of 1935 Li Kenong was moved to head the Central Northwest Liaison Bureau, handling united-front work with the outside, making contact with the Northeastern Army and the Northwestern Army, and laying secret courier lines through the White areas. In effect he had returned to intelligence work. Bearing a great charge, he took direct part in the negotiations with Zhang Xueliang, and at the time of the Xi'an Incident went with Zhou Enlai to work in Xi'an; afterward he set up Eighth Route Army offices in Xi'an, Shanghai, Nanjing, Wuhan, Guilin, and elsewhere, using the form of the united front to push the war of resistance forward. In 1941 Li Kenong returned from Guilin to Yan'an as a deputy head of the Central Social Affairs Department, and after the Central Intelligence Department was founded in 1942 he served concurrently as a deputy head of it, leading intelligence work across the country.
Looking at the history of the Party's intelligence work, the War of Liberation may be called its high point at home. At that time, in keeping with the character of the war, Li Kenong proposed in good season that intelligence work should serve the military struggle. Striking at the enemy's forces from every side and by every means, mounting broad political offensives, and boldly developing and using secret sources, the work won great results. It truly set in motion the intelligence organs across the country, gave full play to the drive of the intelligence workers, and gloriously carried out this historic task of supporting the War of Liberation. Beyond that, the local intelligence organs pressed the study of city work and policy, learning the state of the Kuomintang's party, government, army, police, secret service, industry and commerce, and society, and did much research toward protecting factories, funds, warehouses, and files in preparation for entering the cities, which also played an important part in securing urban order. Chen Yangshan had long worked in the Kuomintang areas and rarely saw the Center's documents, and knew little of the Party's line, aims, strategy, and tactics; Li Kenong's remarks opened Chen Yangshan's eyes and gave him a fuller grasp of the Center's spirit and of the revolutionary practice of the past.
After the rectification ended in 1944, on the strength of this deepened understanding Li Kenong called Chen Yangshan, Wu Defeng, and Luo Qingchang to a meeting to settle the differences between Chen Yangshan and Wu Defeng over Xi'an intelligence work. Little came of it, but that a man himself convalescing should take the trouble to help resolve Chen Yangshan's questions of thought moved Chen Yangshan deeply.
At the end of 1944 the organization decided to send Chen Yangshan to head the investigation bureau of the Jin-Sui Border Region. On the evening before he left, Li Kenong had Chen Yangshan and comrades Feng Jiping, Liang Hanbing, Nie Yuansu, and others to a meal at Zaoyuan, and there gave Chen Yangshan and the rest their charge: on reaching Jin-Sui they were to open up the work as fast as they could, to lose no time in settling the screening left over from the "Rescue Campaign," to adjust the organization in good season, to get to work, and to prepare for the counteroffensive. At the same time they were to reconnect as quickly as possible with the comrades earlier sent into enemy territory who had lost touch, and find ways to make fresh placements, and to press hard the gathering of enemy intelligence. After Chen Yangshan and the others reached Jin-Sui, things turned out just as Li Kenong had said, and following his instructions they completed the recovery of the work by May 1945 and then made a large fresh round of placements.
In June 1945, during the Seventh Congress, Chen Yangshan came back from Jin-Sui to Yan'an to report to Li Kenong on the recovery there. When he told how the screening of a great many comrades had been settled, how some cadres had already been sent into enemy territory, how contact had been made through couriers with some comrades who had lost touch, and how the buying-up of enemy and puppet books and papers had been widened, Li Kenong was well pleased at the swift pace of recovery. When Chen Yangshan raised the shortage of cadres in Jin-Sui and asked that the Center send more, the cadres were all in intensive study just then, and the Central Organization Department had not many in reserve either, only the Party school having some. Li Kenong put a word to Peng Zhen, and Chen Yangshan soon picked out a batch of cadres.
When Japan surrendered, Chen Yangshan was still in Yan'an. Four or five days later he set out back to Jin-Sui, and before going bade Li Kenong farewell; busy as he was, Li Kenong told Chen Yangshan that the objects of intelligence work had greatly changed, that all sorts of connections should be shifted by whatever means into the Kuomintang, and that this work should be strengthened, and that the Center would soon send instructions. Before long the Central Intelligence Department did send instructions, calling for the turbulent situation to be used to plant intelligence personnel inside the Chiang camp by every possible means. Chen Yangshan and the others carried out this new line without letting the chance slip.
In March 1947, Hu Zongnan attacked Yan'an and the Center withdrew; Li Kenong came to Jin-Sui and stayed at Linxian, coming now and then to the Jin-Sui General Public Security Bureau at Xingxian, and Chen Yangshan and the others asked him to speak to their public-security and intelligence personnel. Because certain leaders of the Jin-Sui Sub-bureau were then "Left" in their thinking — distrusting every public-security cadre of landlord or rich-peasant origin, some of whom were moved out of the public-security organs, and holding that intelligence connections of such origin could not be used either, but that one must rely on poor and hired peasants — this mistaken thinking was bound to do grave harm to intelligence work. When Chen Yangshan and the others put these facts before him, Li Kenong pointed out plainly that this was "Left" interference that would injure the intelligence workers and wreck the work. The people an intelligence organ uses, he said, apart from the leading core, must be used with skill — how else could the work be done? A security organ was another matter: its guards had to be loyal and reliable. These were two different natures, two different cases, to be sharply distinguished. Li Kenong's instruction served at the time to raise the cadres' understanding and to advance the intelligence work.
In the winter of 1947 Li Kenong called Chen Yangshan to Linxian to report, and Chen Yangshan stayed two days, reporting on public-security and intelligence work in Jin-Sui. Li Kenong pointed out: to do intelligence work well one must plant intelligence personnel secretly inside the enemy; what should be kept secret must be kept secret, but the work must not be made a mystery of either. Especially with the fine situation of the War of Liberation, enemy secret-service organs and some officials, looking to Chen Yangshan for a way out, were coming forward of their own accord to make contact with Chen Yangshan and pass him intelligence, and it was very important to develop them boldly. Li Kenong listened to the report with great care and attention, and raised some fresh questions besides, which Chen Yangshan found a real spur.
In September 1949, Chen Yangshan came from Xi'an to Beiping, where Li Kenong was just then organizing people to write a summary of the twenty-two years of intelligence work before the founding of the nation. It was discussed twice, and Chen Yangshan took part in both — a great education for him.
Two things moved Chen Yangshan most of all:
{v} In October 1949, after the Ministry of Public Security was set up, I asked to work in Shanghai, and the organization was just preparing to send me there. Before I left, Comrade Kenong talked with me. Shanghai, he said, was fairly complicated, with a good many problems; imperialism, feudalism, Chiang Kai-shek's agents, the forces of the underworld, were all at work wrecking a city only just liberated. We had been in Shanghai in the twenties and thirties and knew only the Shanghai of the past, and it had changed a great deal. He told me to take care to get on well with the East China comrades and to take care to keep the comrades united. Years later, thinking back on the picture of Shanghai that Comrade Kenong had given me then, and on his words about uniting the local cadres, and setting them against what I had felt for myself while working in Shanghai, I felt keenly how accurately Comrade Kenong saw things, and how he cared for his comrades.
{v} While I was working at the Ministry of Justice, I was said to have made a so-called "political mistake" in my work. Early in 1959 I was stripped of all my posts at the ministry and sent down to the grass roots for remolding. In the autumn of 1960, judged to have done well at the grass roots, I was moved back to the central organs after all. The Central Organization Department was sending me to the Central Investigation Department, and that department agreed to take me. Unexpectedly a problem arose over the assignment: they wanted me to do administrative office work. I said I had never done such work, had no experience of it, and would surely do it badly. I asked the organization to think again, but to no result. Comrade Kenong was in the hospital then with illness, and I had not meant to trouble him, but there was no one else to turn to. When I went to him, he asked me on his own what I thought of the assignment. I said I could not manage office administration, and would rather do work of a cadre-training kind. He had me move over to the organs at once, and it was through his care and intervention that I was assigned to the cadre short-course class. That so high a sense of responsibility should, even in illness, still trouble itself with the assignment of cadres moved me greatly.
Li Kenong's spirit of long and stubborn struggle against illness also heartened Chen Yangshan throughout. One saying of Li Kenong's rang always in Chen Yangshan's ears: "The body doesn't matter. We're pack donkeys; we're used to carrying loads, and it's not carrying them that feels wrong. Carry what you can, as much as you can."
This, Chen Yangshan said, was Li Kenong's noble character, working with all his heart for the Party's cause with never a thought for his own safety.
In 1957, not long after Li Kenong injured his brain in a fall, he was still handling official papers from his sickbed, which moved people greatly. Afterward the Center decided he should leave work altogether and give himself to convalescence. By 1961, when his health had improved a little, he wrote to the Center asking to take charge of gathering the historical materials, missing from Party history, on the Shanghai Central Special Branch's protection of the Party organization in the 1920s and 1930s, and proposing to go to Shanghai himself to investigate and interview.
In July 1961 Li Kenong talked with Chen Yangshan, and what struck Chen Yangshan most was his saying, "Though I worked many years in the Central Special Branch, what I know is still not enough."
Li Kenong had Chen Yangshan and Pan Fang take part in gathering the materials, which delighted Chen Yangshan. Li Kenong told Chen Yangshan too that he meant to go with them to Shanghai himself to do some investigating. "In Shanghai we must first find some of the people who worked in the Special Branch — who is still living? And we must gather a great mass of written material, and be clear about where to gather it."
In late August, Li Kenong, Chen Yangshan, and Pan Fang went to Shanghai and stayed in a villa on Yueyang Road that had once been Kong Xiangxi's. At first no one had a clear idea of what to do or could propose a definite method, and they only set about learning the circumstances of the Special Branch people still alive; the Investigation Department of the Shanghai Municipal Party Committee helped a great deal and supplied much. 
Short as the gathering trip was, Li Kenong was scrupulous to a fault, requiring that neither at home nor out on work should anyone use the chance for private business. When Pan Fang's spouse came to Shanghai without telling Li Kenong beforehand, he drew a rebuke; when relatives of Chen Yangshan came to see him, Li Kenong, holding that private matters should not be seen to in work time, rebuked Chen Yangshan too. Li Kenong kept the rules strictly himself and told them beforehand whatever he did. But over the work he gave them a free hand: what had been decided they settled themselves as the case required, and could give full and untroubled play to each one's drive and creativity. He also kept a tight grip, forever asking them to report, and whether they had it right or wrong they were praised or criticized on the spot. Chen Yangshan, Pan Fang, and the others were all glad to report to Li Kenong promptly, the better to put their shortcomings right.
In the course of the work, Chen Yangshan and Pan Fang found in some traitor files at the Shanghai Municipal Public Security Bureau how the Party had been broken up after 1932, and when they reported it Li Kenong had them go on gathering, to get to the bottom of the damage and draw the lesson. One more thing stayed always in Chen Yangshan's memory: Li Kenong's "conscientious and responsible resolving" of the political question of Xu Qiang. 
This was after 1932. Xu Qiang had worked in the Central Special Branch, and after the war of resistance was still in the intelligence line; in 1939 Kang Sheng moved Xu Qiang from Shanghai to Yan'an and, without a shred of evidence, threw Xu Qiang into prison, from which he was released only after Japan's surrender. After liberation Xu Qiang worked at the Shanghai First Commercial Bureau, but no rehabilitation finding had ever been made for him. Li Kenong heard out Xu Qiang's views on the finding, revised it himself, and settled Xu Qiang's case once and for all. For two martyrs, Li Kenong went in person to sweep their graves and met with their families, seeing to their difficulties in good season. Where he found an irresponsible thing done, he raised it sternly on the spot. In September Li Kenong wrote a report to the Center from Shanghai too, on the interviews and the gathering of materials, keeping strictly to the reporting rules.
Seeing Li Kenong's illness grow steadily worse, Chen Yangshan and Pan Fang urged him more than once not to go investigating or reading materials himself, though they could not wholly bring it off. In the evenings they made a point of not calling on Li Kenong, and saw to it that the lights were out before ten so that he might have sleep enough. They often pressed Li Kenong to go to the hospital for checkups too. Mindful that Li Kenong's wife, Zhao Ying, had died, and to keep his life from loneliness and dullness, in those days they would sit with Li Kenong after meals for a heart-to-heart talk — partly to learn of his work, his thinking, and his judgments on history, gaining a priceless treasure, and partly to lift Li Kenong's spirits.
While in Shanghai, Li Kenong, Chen Yangshan, Pan Fang, and Shanghai comrades such as Han Yangshan went to the Longhua Martyrs' Cemetery in Shanghai and swept the graves of Peng Pai, Lü Ming, Li Bai, and other martyrs.
Chen Yangshan had meant on the way back from Shanghai to do some further investigating in Nanjing, but in view of Li Kenong's state of health he went straight from Shanghai to Beijing. Back in Beijing, Chen Yangshan was busy the whole time putting the materials in order and did not think to make a record of an interview with Li Kenong — and so lost the chance, to his lasting regret.
Early in 1962, Chen Yangshan reported to Li Kenong and asked for his instructions. Chen Yangshan remembered it clearly: on the afternoon two days before Li Kenong died, he still called Chen Yangshan to talk, chiefly to ask how the work was coming along. Who could have known this talk would be their last meeting! When others told Chen Yangshan the news of Li Kenong's death, Chen Yangshan could not believe it. "How sudden, how grievous! Never again to hear his voice, never again to hear his instructions, never to have drawn from his storehouse the greater riches that should have been drawn — what a loss it is!"
Chen Yangshan said: "Comrade Li Kenong's boundless loyalty to the Party's cause, his firm carrying out of the Party's instructions, his valiant readiness to sacrifice everything for the revolution, were the root reason he could carry every task through to victory. Comrade Kenong had a lofty character too and a way of treating people as equals; he was good at uniting cadres, bringing out the strength of every quarter and the drive of the comrades, and he gave his last breath to the intelligence cause!"
### 3. Holding to Principle: Following the Party with an Upright Heart
In the twenties and thirties Chen Yangshan had done intelligence work in Wuhan, Shanghai, Chongqing, Xi'an, and elsewhere, but never in Nanjing. Still, he knew something of Nanjing's social conditions, and above all of the Kuomintang secret-service apparatus and its doings, which was a great help to him in leading the city's political and legal work.
The Campaign to Suppress Counter-Revolutionaries launched across the country in 1951 was work of a strongly political character. To lead the campaign rightly, the Nanjing Municipal Party Committee set up a Counter-Revolutionary Screening Commission. As deputy head of the committee's Political and Legal Affairs Commission and chief of the Nanjing Municipal Public Security Bureau and chief procurator, Chen Yangshan was naturally a principal member of it.
Nanjing was a city of fairly complicated enemy conditions. When the Party committee discussed and drew up the plan for the campaign, two views emerged. The party secretary, Ke Qingshi, held that, given the complexity of Nanjing's enemy and social conditions and the large numbers of counter-revolutionaries and deep-cover agents, it was best to take a "steady" line, to proceed with caution and avoid startling the snake by beating the grass. Chen Yangshan's view was that, many as the counter-revolutionaries in Nanjing were, the political and legal organs already had a basic grasp of the enemy situation, and it was best to take a bold and open line, making a great show of force: rouse the masses to a few large-scale suppression drives and the counter-revolutionaries would be shaken, forced to repent and give themselves up.
Because the two principal members leading the campaign had put forward different lines and methods, the final plan could not be fixed, and so it was decided to report both views to the Center and to act on its ruling.
The Center replied that the bold and open line was the correct one. So a great show of force was made in Nanjing, and a few large-scale suppression drives were carried out; many counter-revolutionaries and deep-cover agents, feeling themselves at a dead end, were forced to repent and give themselves up to the government, bowing their heads and admitting guilt and handing over their rosters, papers, weapons, and the like, so that the counter-revolutionaries and deep-cover agents hidden in Nanjing were basically cleared out.
After the campaign, the Nanjing Municipal Party Committee decided that the party-and-government relations of the public security sub-bureaus should be put under the district committees. The mass of officers and men of the sub-bureaus talked much about this decision, holding that public-security work in a large or middling city must be led centrally and uniformly by the municipal bureau and ought not to be split up. They had strong objections to the committee's deciding it so.
Chen Yangshan thought the officers and men were right and the committee's decision truly unsound. So on the one hand he instructed the sub-bureaus to carry out the committee's decision, and on the other reported to the committee and asked it to think again. But the committee would not take Chen Yangshan's view. He then, by the principle of organization, reported in his own name to the Center, asking the responsible central departments to step in and settle the matter.
The Central Organization Department and the Ministry of Public Security studied it and adopted Chen Yangshan's view. The two jointly wired the Nanjing Municipal Party Committee and the Nanjing Municipal Public Security Bureau, affirming plainly that Chen Yangshan's view was correct, and directing the committee to correct at once its decision putting the sub-bureaus' party-and-government relations under the district committees.
When the Center's telegram was passed on to all the officers and men of the bureau, everyone was well satisfied and felt keenly that Chen Yangshan was not the least bit careless on a question of principle and could argue his case on its merits, and they admired and respected him the more from the heart.
Chen Yangshan always urged seeking truth from facts in the handling of affairs. During the "Three-Antis" campaign of 1951, some leading cadres went in for formalism; to show how much they valued the campaign and how active they were, they set the grass-roots units a daily quota of graft cases to catch. Fail to meet the quota and you were criticized. The Gulou public security sub-bureau had no grafters and nothing to report, but the pressure from above was hard, and the sub-bureau chief, Li Delai, at his wits' end, telephoned Chief Chen Yangshan directly to ask what to do. Chen Yangshan answered: "Seek truth from facts." This was how the matter ought to have been handled; and yet in those days, to get that four-word instruction was a rare thing indeed. So Li Delai still could not put the matter out of his mind decades later.
Chen Yangshan could not stomach anything that went against history or twisted the facts. He never let chasing shadows on the wind pass unchallenged.
In 1987 a magazine called Celebrity Biography, published by a press in Zhengzhou, serialized an article, "Subduing the Demons on the Shen River," written jointly by Qin Dong and Li Zheng. Its main plot was largely lifted from Mu Xin's book General Chen Geng. Perhaps to draw readers, the authors had deliberately stuffed the piece with things spun out of nothing that had never happened at all, inventing and fabricating on a large scale about Chen Geng's deeds, and making up out of thin air a good deal of "history" about Zhou Enlai and Song Qingling too. When Chen Yangshan read this article, riddled with errors, he could hardly contain his anger, and was deeply indignant.
As it happened, that autumn Chen Yangshan was invited by the Zhejiang Provincial Department of State Security to lecture on the history of intelligence in Hangzhou, Ningbo, and elsewhere. Learning that the author of the article worked at the Ningbo Television Station, Chen Yangshan went there specially and questioned him to his face: what was his purpose in writing the piece, and where had the material come from? And he sternly reproached him for this fashion of reckless invention that departed from the facts and violated history, until the author had nothing to say.
Chen Yangshan was inward by nature, sparing of words, seasoned and grave in his bearing. Those who did not know Chen Yangshan took him for a man who put on official airs, and dared not approach this hero of the hidden front. It was not so: he treated people kindly and warmly, and juniors most of all. But in the face of right and wrong on a matter of principle he was upright, and brave too. On a question of principle he never spared anyone's feelings, never smoothed things over, but spoke out for justice and never gave way. Once, at a meeting of the leading members of the Supreme People's Procuratorate, a comrade quoted someone else's fact-twisting lie in a speech blaming another comrade, though the matter had already been cleared up. Chen Yangshan spoke out for justice, cutting in suddenly on the speaker: "The facts are not as you say." His few short words of interruption fell with weight, left the speaker dumb, and firmly stopped the spread of that lie.
In 1935, Chen Yangshan had done underground work in Chongqing and Chengdu with Chen Chang and Chen Kehan. After liberation, in a political campaign, Chen Chang was wrongly listed by the public-security organs as a suspected agent, expelled from the Party and dismissed from public office, and put under strict investigation. Worn down by his sufferings, he died under a false charge in 1960. When Chen Yangshan learned of it in 1961, he at once took up with Comrade Wang Shiying, the leader who had sent them to Sichuan in those years, the question of rehabilitating Chen Chang. On the testimony of Wang Shiying, Chen Yangshan, and Chen Kehan, this passage of Chen Chang's history was cleared up, and a rehabilitation finding was made with the approval of the Central Organization Department. And at that very time Chen Yangshan himself was under false accusation and blows.
### 4. Plain and Frugal: A Public Servant of Noble Character
Chen Yangshan lived frugally and kept himself clean all his life; whether in the years of war or in time of peace, whether in high office or relieved of his posts and idle, he was ever plain and thrifty. To the day he died, few of his clothes were without a patch, and few of the things in his home were worth much. His meals were always very simple, as a rule three dishes of vegetables and a bowl of soup, with a few meat dishes added only when guests came; he never ate or drank to excess. Chen Yangshan had no habit of putting on a show for guests; when relatives or old comrades called at his home he never took them to a restaurant to lay on a banquet, and when he had to do such entertaining he would always tell his wife to bring their own dishes and carry the leftovers home, so that no food should be wasted — a full showing of the Chinese nation's virtue of diligence and thrift.
High in office as he was, Chen Yangshan always looked on himself as a servant of the people and an ordinary Party member. He knew well how thousands of years of feudal privilege-thinking could tell on the raising of the next generation, and so in daily life he was not only clean-living himself but very strict with his children, forever teaching them: "Your mother and I are both old Communists, and you are the children of old Communists. Be modest and honest, harbor no notions of privilege, and above all do not use your parents' social standing to seek advantages of any kind." He said it, and he did it. Nowadays, for instance, the fashion of using power for private ends is rife, and some heedless high cadres often use their office to gain their families and children all manner of benefits; Chen Yangshan never did so. Well as he knew that his four children all had certain hardships in their lives, he never once used his social standing to put in a word for them.
Chen Yangshan's children, knowing their father's nature, never made any improper request of him either, and were used to finding their own ways, each of them, to overcome the difficulties they had.
In 1982, the two-year-old child of Chen Yangshan's eldest son suddenly came down with acute appendicitis and lay near death. His eldest son asked to use his official car just once to rush the child to the hospital. Chen Yangshan would not have it, but insisted they go by taxi, though afterward he gave his son two hundred yuan toward the grandchild's treatment. The matter was a lesson to his eldest son.
Chen Yangshan's youngest son had always stood at the top of his class, but failed the college entrance examination in 1958; unwilling to accept it, he wanted to have it out with his teacher. When Chen Yangshan learned of it he was very angry and reproached him sternly: "You think too much of yourself; you did not review in earnest, you failed the exam and still make a fuss — it is outrageous. Do not imagine that because I am a state cadre you may run wild and look down on everyone." Chen Yangshan did not lose his temper lightly, and this stern rebuke had a great effect on the rest of the boy's life. As it happened, a few days later a reporter from the People's Daily came to interview Chen Yangshan about the several high cadres' children who had failed the examination, asking how he planned to arrange his children's future. He answered the reporter plainly: he planned to send the boy to labor on a farm, to make up his lessons in the intervals and sit the examination again the next year. After the report appeared, the boy wept and stormed at home and flatly refused to go. To bring the boy round, Chen Yangshan patiently reasoned with him, and in the end got him to go to the farm gladly and of his own accord.
In 1989 Chen Yangshan's lung cancer worsened and he was hospitalized. Just then Chen Yangshan's daughter was assigned by the organization to a temporary posting for training in Shandong. Uneasy about Chen Yangshan's illness, she asked to put off the posting. When Chen Yangshan learned of it he said to his daughter: "You needn't worry about my illness; the work matters more, and my illness must not upset the organization's arrangements." After Chen Yangshan's daughter took up her post in Shandong, still uneasy, he wrote to tell her to set her mind on the work, to do real things for the peasants, and to use science and technology to help the peasants grow rich — showing his active support for his children in their careers.
Clean and upright all his life, Chen Yangshan, to have his children carry on the Party's fine traditions and the nation's virtues, left them no great fortune but a testament on how to be a person. To his children Chen Yangshan left these thirteen precepts:
1. Heed what the leading comrades say, submit to your assignments, and give your mind to your work.
2. In your work consult with the comrades and do not cling stubbornly to your own view.
3. Be wholly responsible in what you do; do not go through the motions, but see the task through.
4. Do not quarrel with anyone or curse anyone, still less strike anyone.
5. Do not look down on others or talk about them lightly, or make sport of them.
6. See another's strong point, and learn from it.
7. See another's failing, and examine yourself.
8. Keep strictly to the rules and system of your work, and never break them.
9. Keep discipline strictly; do not smoke and drink here and there.
10. Do not meddle in others' affairs, but keep yourself well in hand.
11. Be big-hearted; do not vie with others over who is abler, or wrangle.
12. In household matters too, act by consultation and do not decide on your own.
13. From now on the family must live in harmony, help one another, make allowance for one another, cherish one another, and never again quarrel, curse, or come to blows.
Chen Yangshan's son Chen Jianyu said: these thirteen precepts are the finest legacy Father left his children.
Because Chen Yangshan could be strict with himself in daily life and practice what he preached, on the score of clean living not one member of Chen Yangshan's family behaved improperly. Chen Yangshan was a worthy servant of the people, a hero of the hidden front, and a model to his children.
Plain and frugal, a public servant of noble character; thrifty all his life, his spirit shown in the anecdotes of his family's ways. From the anecdotes his comrades and family recall, we may see Chen Yangshan's style and spirit.
Anecdote one. In January 1945, after Chen Yangshan had taken up his post as head of the investigation bureau of the Jin-Sui Military Region, the region's headquarters once called a cadre meeting, and He Long, noticing that Chen Yangshan was not there, asked the convener whether Chen Yangshan had been notified. The convener answered: "The business of this meeting has nothing to do with the investigation bureau; there was no need for him to attend." He Long said angrily: "This is your looking down on him. I, He Long, think highly of him. In Shanghai in 1927 he saved my life — my, He Long's, life — and he is a remarkable man. Your looking down on him is looking down on me, He Long."
Once this got out, the comrades who heard of it all held Chen Yangshan in great respect, which brought the investigation bureau many conveniences in its work.
Anecdote two. In the early days after the founding of New China, when Chen Yangshan was deputy chief of the Shanghai Municipal Public Security Bureau, several relatives came from his home district to Shanghai to find Chen Yangshan; some claimed to have given his family relief in the days of the Great Revolution, some to have sheltered him when the enemy was hunting him, and they asked Chen Yangshan to help find them work in Shanghai.
In fact none of the reasons these people gave was true. But Chen Yangshan did not simply turn them away; he reasoned with them: "I am a cadre of the Communist Party, and the Communist Party is not like the Kuomintang. Under the Kuomintang, once a man got office, relatives and friends who came to his door could all be made officials. We Communists do not go in for pulling family strings. I think you had best go back home and till the land." A few words left them with nothing to say.
But in handling such matters there were exceptions too. In 1952, when Chen Yangshan was chief of the North China Public Security Bureau in Beijing, his wife's cousin Zhao Hongfei came to Beijing to ask his help in finding work. Seeing that in 1928, when the Kuomintang was after Chen Yangshan, Zhao Hongfei had braved danger to shelter him and brought him safe out of peril and away to Shanghai, Chen Yangshan recommended him to the Shanxi Provincial Public Security Department with a request that they help place him. As it happened the Shanxi labor-reform bureau was short of accountants, and since Zhao Hongfei had done accounting work he was taken on and set to keep the books there.
Anecdote three. In 1954, when Chen Yangshan was a vice-minister of the Ministry of Justice, his cousin came to Beijing to visit. In their talk his cousin told Chen Yangshan that the Party branch of the Huzhou Chinese Medicine Association meant to admit him to the Party, and he sought Chen Yangshan's view. Chen Yangshan said to him: "You have always been sympathetic to the Communist Party, and in the days of the Great Revolution you even did things of use to it; you are a fairly well-known doctor of Chinese medicine in the district, with a good base among the people. Things are only just liberated, and there is a great deal of mass work to be done. I think you would be of more use staying outside the Party than joining it. Or you might join a democratic party." Chen Yangshan's cousin agreed, and back in Huzhou, after talking it over with the Party branch, joined the Chinese Peasants' and Workers' Democratic Party.
Anecdote four. In 1962, after the "anti-Party clique" case of the Ministry of Justice's Party group had been reviewed by the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection, in 1965 the Center ruled that Chen Yangshan might be placed as a vice-governor in a smaller province.
In October 1965, Peng Zhen talked with Chen Yangshan and told him that the Central Organization Department meant to place him as a vice-chairman in Inner Mongolia or Ningxia, and sought his view on the assignment. Chen Yangshan said he suffered from fairly severe emphysema and that the climate of neither place suited it, and asked the organization to think again.
Peng Zhen said: "You are a Zhejiang man; you could work in your home province." Chen Yangshan said: "I know a great many people in Zhejiang, and it is awkward when they come to my door; better to place me in some other province." In the end it was agreed to await the organization's decision.
Before the Spring Festival of 1966, Peng Zhen again talked with Chen Yangshan and told him the Center had decided to place him as vice-chairman of the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region and wanted him to take up the post soon. Chen Yangshan had always obeyed the organization's decisions absolutely; he said not another word, and after the festival went to take up his post in Ningxia.
Anecdote five. Chen Yangshan was a man of strong loyalty, and never forgot those who had helped the Party, or helped him.
From 1928 to 1935, while he worked in the Central Special Branch in Shanghai and was rather hard up, a relative named Yu Shengzhang, then in business in Shanghai, often helped him with money and had sheltered him too. After Shanghai was liberated Chen Yangshan had made inquiries after Yu's whereabouts, but without success.
In 1977 he asked a member of the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress, who was going to inspect work in Shangyu county in Zhejiang, to inquire after Yu. As it happened the head of the organization department of the Shangyu county committee, Wu Jusheng, was a relative of the Yu family, and he passed Chen Yangshan's address and the news that he was looking for Yu to Yu Shengzhang's younger sister (Chen Yangshan's cousin's wife). Only then was contact made.
In April 1980, Chen Yangshan wrote to his cousin's wife and arranged to meet in Hangzhou that May. On meeting, Chen Yangshan first asked after Yu Shengzhang; learning that Yu Shengzhang had died, Chen Yangshan felt it keenly. Chen Yangshan praised Yu Shengzhang as a man of strong sense of justice, sympathetic to the Communist Party and one who had done things of use to it. He told how, while he was working in Shanghai, Yu Shengzhang had given him great help, and he was deeply grateful.
Anecdote six. Chen Yangshan was strict with himself to the last, and in the small things of life too was ever mindful, at all times and in all places, of his effect on the masses. During the War of Liberation, for instance, when he was chief of the public security bureau in the Jin-Sui Border Region, conditions were very hard, and the general-affairs office, mindful that his several children were still small, arranged for them to enjoy the middle-mess ration with their mother. When Chen Yangshan learned of it he stopped the arrangement at once. He would not have his children set above other people's.
In daily life he always kept public and private strictly apart, was pure in heart and few in wants, and never took advantage of the state or of others. When his unit or his colleagues brought him gifts, he insisted on paying the full price, and otherwise would not accept them. The official car assigned him he never misused beyond riding to work, still less let his family or children use.
In 1978, transferred back from Ningxia to Beijing to be given work, he was assigned by regulation a six-room apartment, but, thinking that some cadres were worse off for housing than he, he did not accept it. To the day he died he lived in a three-room apartment. The furniture in his home was very plain; apart from a bed, a desk, and a bookcase issued by his office, a sixteen-inch color television, and a single-door refrigerator, there was nothing else worth the name.
Anecdote seven. Chen Yangshan treated people kindly and warmly and was easy to be with, and his subordinates most of all. For instance, while lecturing on the history of intelligence at the Ningbo Municipal Bureau of State Security in 1987, he struck up a friendship in a few short days with the driver who drove him. Two years later, when this driver came to Beijing on business, he went specially to call on Chen Yangshan. Chen Yangshan received him warmly, still remembered his name, talked with him merrily, and had his secretary take the driver about Beijing for a few days' sightseeing. When the driver left the capital, Chen Yangshan had his secretary see him to the airport, which moved the driver greatly.
Wherever he worked, Chen Yangshan often went down to the grass roots to chat with cadres and the masses and learn the problems in their work and lives, the better to do all he could to solve them. During the War of Liberation, when he worked in the Jin-Sui Border Region, however busy he was he would always find time to see in person the intelligence personnel come back from enemy-held areas or the comrades come from the grass roots to report to the general bureau, talking with them of the work and opening his heart to them as well, and doing all he could to solve some of their real difficulties. This unassuming style of work won him the respect of cadres and masses alike.
In his long revolutionary life Chen Yangshan was praised and commended many times by the leaders. For instance: in 1928 he "drew out" the enemy agent Bao Junfu from the enemy's camp to work for our Party, which played an important part in protecting the Party Center, and Zhou Enlai praised him for it. During the War of Resistance and the War of Liberation, when he led public-security and intelligence work in the Jin-Sui Border Region, he showed marked results, and He Long, Peng Dehuai, and Liu Shaoqi praised him. In 1951, when he was chief of the public security bureau in Nanjing, his work showed results, and Chairman Mao praised the Nanjing bureau. Yet he thought nothing of this praise, never spread word of it to others or told his family of it. This indifference to his own merits was a full showing of his modest, prudent, fine style, and of the national virtue of not parading one's merits or presuming on one's high standing.
