## Rakshasa Valley

South of Higashiyama stands a maple-covered peak called Senzan. Deep in its folds a clear spring has welled up since ancient times. The summit is no great height; but the ravines run deep, and follow one of them and the shape of the land shifts and shifts again, until you might be a thousand ri from anywhere, lost in the heart of the mountains.

The springs that rise between the rocks gather into a single thin stream that winds from valley to valley. They call it the Otonashi-gawa. It is not five shaku across; it scarcely deserves the name of river. Once clear of the foothills and into the streets of Shimogyō, it runs a bare dozen chō and vanishes into the Kamo.

Where the stream left the foothills a small bridge crossed it, lacquered a dreamlike, gorgeous vermilion. About two of the old double-hours after Gohei had taken his leave of the Chinnō-in, two men set the planks of that bridge sounding and crossed toward Senzan.

"This is the bridge?"

"Just so. The world of the living, they say, ends here."

On Senzan stands the Sennyū-ji, which since the reign of the Emperor Shijō has been the burial ground of one sovereign after another. The vermilion bridge is called the Ukiyo-bashi. To cross it, one supposes, is to pass into the land of the dead.

"Kuroami."

It was Tsuzura Jūzō who had spoken. Kuroami had got himself up as a merchant. The road had already carried them into the mountains, where the dark was such that a man could not make out what lay a few shaku ahead.

"That light ahead—is that it?"

"That'll be a soul-flame."

Kuroami said it as though it were nothing. The mountain had a haunted air.

Great cedars pressed close at their shoulders; overhead, in the thin ribbon of sky, a few stars showed through.

All at once a huge, black wind came gusting up from beneath their feet.

"Master Jūzō—this is Rakshasa Valley."

With that Kuroami was gone. Jūzō looked over the edge: two jō below, the old man had lighted as neatly as a bird at the foot of a log bridge. Jūzō did not leap. He felt his way along the cliff path and came round the long way. In the old court days a demon that fed on the dead had haunted this valley. It craved the corpses of women; and when there were no corpses left it would go down into the capital, stand at the crossroads, and seize living women and devour them. As though that same demon had risen here, a human shadow crossed suddenly before Jūzō. Then, from the trees close by, another figure stepped out. The shapes slipped past under his very eyes, quick and smooth—no footfall, no word—over the log bridge and away into the dark beyond.

One look at the way they walked and he knew them for rappa of Iga and Kōga.

Jūzō slid down the cliff and crossed the log bridge at his leisure. To the shadows, no doubt, Jūzō and Kuroami were only two more shadows among them.

A little beyond the bridge he found a narrow track in the scrub of the cliffside, and clambering up it with a rustle of leaves he came out all at once on a level place the size of some two hundred mats. Great boulders walled it on three sides.

There stood a ruined hall of the Healing Buddha.

A faint light burned inside.

On an impulse Jūzō climbed the cliff that rose at the hall's back. A wind was blowing.

He turned, and there were the lights of the capital.

Below him lay the roof of the hall.

A small precinct floated pale beneath the starlit sky, and across that pale ground shadows darted, one after another, and were drawn in under the roof.

Kuroami came up the slope and whispered at his ear.

"A good many have come, it seems."

"How many?"

"A round twenty."

"Iga men only?"

"A few from Kōga. One from Harima."

"I'll stay up here where it's cool."

"Eh—you won't come down? But from tonight you're their chief."

"Better a chief keep out of sight.—What Buddha is enshrined in this hall?"

"Yakushi. The Healing Buddha."

"Then Yakushi will do for a chief. A face unseen—the word carries all the further for it. …The money, is it ready?"

"Two hundred pieces of silver."

"Scatter it evenly. No favourites."

"Understood."

Kuroami let go the grass he had been gripping and slid down the cliff.

He went into the hall. Every man inside turned to look at him at once. Most, as one would expect, were dressed as samurai, though there were pedlars and farmers among them too. All wore the look peculiar to the trade—lifeless, the skin a muddy yellow-green—and in the shadow of a low, guttering lamp only their eyes glittered. Kuroami spoke the moment he was through the door.

"I am Kuroami of Iga, the man who's hired you. Some of you will know me. As you see me now, I go by the name Iseya Kahei, and keep a whetter's stall near the Great Buddha in the capital. Mark my face well."

Without a word the men rose and settled themselves again in a ring around Kuroami.

"Of late Hideyoshi's realm looks safer than ever. No wars. And you—you've all grown lean, I'd wager."

Kuroami was lean enough himself. His small hand pointed now at a long-faced man whose thinness stood out even in that gathering, and—

"You—you'd be Natsumi-no-Mimiji of Iga, if I have it right. Where are you living now?"

"On the riverbed in the capital. I left Iga in the ninth year of Tenshō, and I've drifted the provinces ever since. The world has no more use for a rappa, it seems; there's no work worth the name. I've tried my hand at it all—puppeteer, temple drudge—and it's come to this: squatting on the capital's dry riverbed and begging my bread."

"Why not turn thief?"

"Alone? Hardly."

"As it happens, I've whistled up every rappa I could find tonight—a full twenty of you. Each a name in the place he was born. Band this many together and work Kyoto, Ōsaka, Sakai, and you could turn the realm on its head, let alone spare yourselves the beggar's bowl."

"The work—is it robbery?"

"Not robbery alone. There's arson too. But for the present it's no such rough business."

"Then what—"

"You are to curse the world, with all the strength that's in you."

And with that Kuroami drew out ten pieces of silver for each man, thrust them into their breasts, and—

"—set rumours flying."

He closed his mouth and let his eyes travel round the circle.

What Kuroami wanted of them was this: that they seize their moments and their places and breathe into the ears of the common people the word that Hideyoshi's realm had run its course.

"And yet, to look at it, this realm shows no sign of tottering. Court noble, warrior, peasant—every one of them seems to be singing the praises of the peace."

It was one of the older rappa who spoke.

"That, my friend from Kōga, is a shallow view."

Kuroami held up an open palm and bent down the thumb.

"First: Hideyoshi has no heir. There's the seed of every disorder. Next—"

The very things Kuroami was setting out below, Jūzō too was turning over where he sat on the rock above. Far beneath his feet, at the bottom of Rakshasa Valley, he could feel the midnight wind wheeling in eddies.

It was not many days since Jūzō had left Sakai and come up to the capital. A few days earlier he had called on the whetter Iseya Kahei—Kuroami—whose shop stood beside the Hōkō-ji. Kuroami had set his hands to the floor in an oily bow at the front of the stall and said:

"Your humble servant is Kahei the whetter."

"I'd like this done in twenty days or so."

"Oho—a Kunihiro, this."

Working the peg free, he studied the blade with a connoisseur's air, and—

"Hardly any curve to it—rare in a Kunihiro. An early work, by the look of it, and there's a young man's fire still in the temper."

Rattling on so, he sent the boy at his side off on an errand, then looked at Jūzō and all at once fixed his gaze.

"The gathering of the rappa you spoke of before—three days from now, at the evening Four."

"Where?"

"The hall of the Healing Buddha, in Rakshasa Valley."

"No priest in it?"

"None, of course."

"Good.—I want to hear how things stand in the capital."

"Not much to speak of, I'm afraid."

By "not much to speak of" Kuroami seemed to mean that life among the common people of the capital was perfectly calm, and that there was little opening for a rappa to work his way into this reign.

"No."

Jūzō gave a faint, doubtful tilt of his head.

"Look closer, and it may not be so at all."

"And how does Master Jūzō see it?"

Kuroami shut his small mouth and leaned in to listen.

A rappa was, you might say, a technician of the age of wars. Where, from which small tear, the fabric of the world would next begin to rip—for that first sign these men were forever watching. And these two meant to kill Hideyoshi. Not only to kill him: if, as it seemed, their paymaster wanted that killing to set the whole realm unravelling, then the work would go hard unless a cry of hatred against Hideyoshi were already rising among the townsmen of the capital.

"Take the plainest case. If Hideyoshi were to die now, there's one man who'd go wild with joy for it."

"Ieyasu, you mean?"

"No. The tens of thousands of labourers levied for the descent on the continent. The order went out to the provinces this past New Year: Shikoku and Kyūshū to send six hundred men, the Chūgoku provinces and Kishū five hundred, the Five Home Provinces four hundred; and in one village in Ōmi, so the talk runs as far as Sakai, eighteen peasants out of every hundred and ten households. They are to be mustered at Ōsaka on the twelfth and put aboard the ships—and those men, and their wives and children, waiting on that day, must be sick to the heart of life itself. The murmur of it cannot but be rising in the capital too, like a rumble underground."

"Is that so. And yet—I'd have said the very opposite."

Kuroami reached out and pointed to the swords hung thick along the wall of his workshop, and—

"See for yourself. Even a whetter nobody's heard of, newly set up in trade, has orders like these. And it's not the whetters alone. Four hundred thousand fighting men are arming for war. Just now it's as though a rain of gold were falling on every trade in the capital."

"Give that rain a year and it turns bitter. Four hundred thousand fighting men, you say—but the mountains of war-rice to feed them will be carried off across the sea. Before long prices climb, and warrior and merchant alike are driven to the wall. That is the hour disorder is born of. If Hideyoshi marches into Korea and the Ming and carves himself ten million koku of land over there, well and good; if not, the Toyotomi peace won't outlast three more years.—And this isn't my own saying. Today, on a whim, I changed my clothes and lay about on the riverbed, and even the wretches who live in the shacks there were talking so."

No sooner had he said it than Jūzō felt a doubt at the rashness of his own words. The very opposite thought—that Hideyoshi's world would not, after all, come down so easily—had begun of a sudden to take hold of him. What he was thinking of was the gold Hideyoshi held.

(So long as that vast hoard of gold and silver holds, the man Hideyoshi will keep men at his back a good while yet—let him be all the gossip paints him, a false beard pasted to his face, his teeth blacked, scarlet hakama on, caroling away without a care…)

Jūzō had never set much store by the loyalty or the solidarity of the commanders under Hideyoshi. The rappa's eye for the world, handed down father to son, held not a breath of moisture in it; he was wholly incapable of the folly of reckoning the world by men's warm feelings or a warrior's fidelity. But the thing that now rose up and blocked his thoughts had a hard, distinct shape and colour of its own. It was the gold and silver heaped at Hideyoshi's back—new-minted, in a quantity no ruler before him had ever commanded.

The Hideyoshi in Jūzō's mind had been blessed by a strange run of fortune. Not the fortune of rising in the world alone—had it been only that, a man who climbed from common footsoldier could have risen no higher than field commander to Nobunaga, and there stopped. In the sixth month of Tenshō ten, Hideyoshi avenged his dead lord at Yamazaki. In the same stroke he set aside the dead lord's own son, and Ieyasu, and Katsuie, and reached for the dead lord's place. As a usurper in all but name he was no whit different from Mitsuhide. Yet the dead lord's generals fell in behind this usurper, one of their own fellows, to a man. And that, Jūzō held, was owing less to Hideyoshi's power than to the power of the gold and silver in his hands.

At the moment the Hideyoshi of the surface world struck down Mitsuhide at Yamazaki, a miracle was stirring in the underground of Japan such as had never been seen before and would never be seen again. As if in answer to Hideyoshi's fortune above, gold and silver came welling up all at once from the hills and fields of every province. At once Hideyoshi took the mines of the whole country—Sado, Ikuno, and the rest—into his own single grip. And the gold he scattered, open-handed, among his newly won retainers.

This was a man who, back when he was only one of Nobunaga's junior officers, would press a fistful of gold on a subordinate the instant he brought off some feat. He knew to the bone how gold bewitches the human heart. Twice, in the thirteenth and the seventeenth years of Tenshō, he held the brazen ceremonies of the gold largesse; and as word of them passed among the peasants and townsfolk, the whole world slipped into the illusion that this was no longer Hideyoshi in the living flesh, but an incarnation of gold, a thing no hand could touch.

The gold heaped at the south gate of the Jurakudai, his villa, ran on the first occasion to five thousand pieces of gold and thirty thousand of silver; on the second, to twenty-six thousand of gold and silver together—three hundred and five thousand ryō in all, a staggering sum. He gave it by the fistful to the court nobles, the lords, and the grandees who came to pay their respects, and by it he did more than break the nerve of warlords hardened in the age of wars: he brought them—men forced to keep more retainers than their domains could feed—to their knees before his gold.

It was gold, Jūzō believed, that had won Hideyoshi the realm. And so, he reasoned, when that power of gold broke, the foundations of the upstart Toyotomi regime would break with it. Failing that—let a man appear in the world with gold to outweigh Hideyoshi's, and the greater part of those warlords who supposedly owed the Toyotomi everything, dazzled as they were by gold and silver, would surely run to the new man's side.

Was there such a man?

(Ieyasu?—)

He considered it; but great a lord as Ieyasu was, master of the Kantō and its two million five hundred and fifty thousand koku, he could hardly command wealth on that scale. Here, though, lay one grave possibility. The massed riches of the men of Sakai. Let the wealth of Sakai once stand behind Ieyasu's every move, and the realm would come to trust him. And in that hour, as Jūzō saw it, the house of Toyotomi would fall.

(So that is it—that is Sōkyū's design.)

With the sensation of waking, Jūzō read off the map inside the mind of the merchant Imai Sōkyū. Whether the scheme had been carried from Sōkyū to Ieyasu, or Sōkyū was one stone Ieyasu himself had laid, far in advance, in a long and careful game—either way, an upheaval of the world within a few years seemed certain.

(And the Iga gōshi that Nobunaga wiped out—perhaps, as such an upheaval ran its course in the years to come, they too might rise and stand in the world again.)

Reading his way this far into Sōkyū's heart, Jūzō felt his blood leap, a rare thing in such a man. For something had become plain of its own accord. Might not his own master, Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon, one of the chiefs of the Iga ninja, have struck a bargain with the powerful men about Ieyasu—the restoration of the Iga gōshi, in exchange for the success of this work?…

(The word of it must have been brought by Hattori Hanzō.)

So Jūzō guessed.

Hattori Hanzō came of the Hattori, a great house among the Iga gōshi. He had served Ieyasu since his father's day, and would later be granted a mansion in Edo, before the Hanzō Gate at Kōjimachi, take the title Iwami-no-kami, and hold a stipend of eight thousand koku—a rare rise for a man out of the Iga ninja. It was always Hanzō who handled the dealings between the house of Tokugawa and the Iga gōshi.

(That man would not do Iga a bad turn.)

To cut down Hideyoshi was, in the same act, to work for the revival of Iga.

For the first time a will to throw himself, body and life, into this work came bubbling up inside Jūzō.

The first task was to loose through the streets of the capital a cry of hatred against the Toyotomi regime. And for a beginning, that meant setting rumours flying, wildly magnified, of the discontent and the opposition among Hideyoshi's own daimyō over the sending of troops to Korea.

And now, beneath the roof of the Healing Buddha's hall under his feet, that very plan was being laid in secret, with Kuroami at their head, among the twenty professionals Jūzō had gathered.

When he looked down, the light in the hall had already gone out. Fearing the glow might be marked from outside, they must be carrying their council forward in a darkness stripped of every flame. When he looked up, the constellations of the Hour of the Rat glittered sharp overhead. The Battle of Sekigahara, nine years off—had it not, one might ask, already begun on this very night?

Time passed.

Kuroami came climbing up.

Jūzō sat silent on the rock.

"Have they scattered?"

"They have scattered."

Kuroami hunkered down at Jūzō's side like a carved kōshin monkey. For a while the two of them watched the stars in silence; then Jūzō spoke.

"What is it?"

"There's a woman. Kohagi, she's called."

"A woman already, is it? Your honoured father was just the same—you're a troublesome man yourself, master."

Kuroami said it looking into Jūzō's face, half in exasperation, half in reproach.

"A big woman, narrow-eyed, full in the cheek. Not that I'm in love with her. No—perhaps I am. I've lain with her once."

"There, you see."

"This is no jest. I've a feeling that woman may prove the most dangerous enemy our work will meet."

"What is she, then?"

"Sōkyū's adopted daughter—and, so Sōkyū himself says, the child of a certain noble house that came to ruin. …But unless my eye is badly out, she's a Kōga rappa in another shape. Sōkyū's been taken in. She's no friend to us. And if she's no friend—then from which quarter has she wormed her way in?"

"Before you go quibbling over the whys, best cut her down clean and have done."

Kuroami said it lightly, his eyes still on the dark beyond. Jūzō gave a small laugh, and—

"She's not one I can cut down. A woman I've lain with, even once."

"Heh. You talk like an ordinary man. A rappa should live with a little more iron in his spirit."

This man had worked the fields as a lowly retainer of the Tsuzura, the gōshi house, and had minded its children too. He had known Jūzō since Jūzō was in swaddling-clothes. There was a father's feeling in the weight he gave the words as he admonished him—then, of a sudden, he broke off, and—

"No—I shall cut her down myself, the moment I find her. You've no objection?"

"Rather that. I'd ask it of you myself."

"A good ring to that. That's the Tsuzura Jūzō I know."

Kuroami closed his mouth, his face like a bird's. He was past fifty now. Let this seasoned rappa fall silent, and from that instant he could snuff out his own presence so completely that even Jūzō, seated at his side, half fancied the old man had melted into the dark. Open his mouth, and he surfaced into the world again: there was a mastery in that long-drilled art that even Jūzō found, at times, uncanny. …After the silence—

"Master Jūzō."

The voice came, and with it Kuroami's presence bobbed up into the world again.

"What is it?"

"It's about Master Gohei—Kazama Gohei. Have you had word of him? Has he truly deserted our company, then?"

"To find that out, Master Jirōzaemon of Shimotsuge has a daughter on her way up to the capital, or should have. We'll know before long."

"If he's deserted, Master Jūzō, you know what it means. …It's you, who were his comrade, who'll have to cut him down. And a man of that skill—no one else could be the one to finish him."

"Kisaru said she'd do the stabbing."

"A woman's not to be relied on."

Kuroami spat the words out. This old man, the shinobi art made flesh, loathed above all things the entry of anything female into a shinobi's work. It was very nearly a creed with him, like the ritual abstinence of a mountain ascetic setting out to enter the peaks.

"When you meet this Kisaru, tell her to go back to Iga at once. It's ill luck to have a woman coming and going inside the bounds a ninja has drawn to do his work."

"What a stubborn thing to say. You're growing old, Kuroami."

"It's not my age that says it. In Iga they've handed the saying down these hundreds of years."

Jūzō and Kuroami parted there on the cliff. Kuroami pinched the edges of his sleeves between his fingers, spread both arms like wings, and dropped—drawn down as though the dark ground several jō below were swallowing him. Jūzō climbed on up the cliff, followed the ridgeline south, and came out on the avenue of pines along the approach to the Sennyū-ji. Dawn was still a good way off. He meant to walk the town until first light, and set the map of the night-time capital into his body.
