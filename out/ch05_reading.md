## Chapter 5

The sky had already gone a little ashen, a scattering of stars still lying sparse across it. The road was dead and empty, only the doors of a few all-night pawnshops kept open for the gamblers standing ajar. The street lamps flickered wearily, the roadside trees were desolate beyond words, and we walked treading their forlorn shadows; a light autumn-morning wind came, its chill going through one, and I said:

"Do you really mean to walk all the way to Xujiahui?"

"What?" she said. "Haven't you the spirit for it?"

"I?" I said. "I'm a man — don't you know?"

"Nonsense," she said. "I've found it's men who dread walking most at an hour like this."

"But I really am afraid you're too tired," I said with a laugh. "To tell you the truth, I'm a country fellow, forever walking first thing in the morning."

"Which is just why I picked you to walk with me," she said, laughing aloud.

The sky grew brighter than before — brighter, and bright as Bai Ping's own attire, the silver flower in her hair, the silver-grey of her dress. My interest in her deepened, and without thinking I put an arm about her; just then a gust of wind came and struck down a few leaves, and I felt Bai Ping shiver. Realizing then how thin her dress was, I took off my coat and laid it over her shoulders.

"Aren't you cold yourself?"

"I'm a man," I said, laughing.

"A man again." She touched my clothes with her hand and went on: "But you have more on than I do."

"Which is why I can spare you one."

She said no more, and walked leaning against me.

We walked to the end of Yuyuan Road, crossed Avenue Haig, and went on along Route de Sieyès, in silence; the sky slowly brightened, the wind no longer so cutting as before. My heart could bear the stillness no longer, and I began:

"What are you thinking of?" She seemed to have her answer ready, and replied without hesitation:

"Thinking that perhaps this is the first time you've walked so far with a woman."

"It is."

"Then I ought to feel it a great honour."

"For you, I imagine, it is not the first time."

"How do you know?"

"The work of your profession."

"Nonsense," she said, with a touch of pique. "Is my profession, then, to walk men from the gambling house to the church?"

"What of it?" I said. "If your profession were always to walk men from the gambling house to the church, would you not feel it an honour?"

"But that, perhaps, is the work of my soul," she said. "My profession is to dance with men."

Only then did it strike me that the one walking beside me was a dance-hostess; and whether some contempt for her stirred in me unawares I do not know, but I said nothing further.

Silence: I heard our footfalls, I heard our breathing; and so we turned into Route Pétain, where I saw the sun in the east, and the frost heaped on the scorched leaves of the thickets inside the roadside hedges beginning to melt, glittering with the freshness of morning. Before a rather open garden, Bai Ping suddenly pointed to a maple within, and said:

"Why, the leaves have turned red already."

"Yes," I said. "It is autumn."

"Would you pick me a red leaf?"

I did not answer, but turned in at the garden gate. There was not a soul within, and the grass was all frost; I trod across it and, from that maple, picked two whole red leaves. When I came back Bai Ping was standing at the gate, and welcomed me with an unexpectedly lovely smile. I handed her the leaves, and she said:

"Thank you, then." She took the two leaves, but at once gave one back to me, saying: "This one is for you — keep it, in memory of our journey from the gambling den to the church."

"Thank you." I carefully pressed it into my wallet, and asked: "Do you give it to me in all sincerity?"

"Of course." But as I was about to go, Bai Ping gave me back my coat. "Thank you," she said. "I've walked myself quite warm now."

The sun was up, the day seemed particularly fine, and I too had grown hot with walking, so I did not put the coat on but only hung it over my arm and walked on with her.

At the door of the church her manner turned suddenly devout, as though I were not beside her at all. Inside, she crossed herself with holy water and, her eyes fixed on the altar, advanced step by step, serene and solemn, while I followed softly behind. A good many of the faithful had already come; some knelt in prayer on the ground, some sat reciting their office. My heart began to grow pure and calm, and thinking of the excitement and strain of the gambling den the night before, I felt an unaccountable shame and emptiness.

Bai Ping knelt down before the altar, and I knelt with her; she laid both hands on the seat in front and buried her head in them, and I did as she did, closing my eyes despite myself. Suddenly she said, low:

"Pray for your truest wish."

So I prayed; without reflection I said within my heart:

"May the War of Resistance soon be won; may all who love be joined together; may I too have a heart as solemn and translucent as this."

I raised my head and looked at the candle flames before the altar, my thoughts rising and sinking in a haze; I felt the grandeur of the universe and my own smallness, the dimness of life and the impermanence of the world.

I do not know when Bai Ping raised her head; she was gazing at the altar, as though sunk in some deep reverie. I watched her from the side — the large round eyes, the thick long lashes, giving off now a strangely innocent light. Her coat lay strewn on the chair like a fallen leaf, and the pale-grey cheongsam, its silver buttons glinting, was drawn close about her fine, healthy body; before, it had struck me only as elegant, but now it struck me as pure. I did not disturb her, but gazed at her as she gazed at the altar.

At last the Mass began; Bai Ping covered her head with a white scarf and bowed over her hands, and only then did I move my eyes to the priest at the altar.

I listened quietly as the Mass went on, with feelings past telling in my heart — bewilderment, desolation, clarity and remorse.

When the Mass was over, Bai Ping and I came out of the church. She said nothing all the while, but only walked quietly at my side; and at the turning I could bear it no longer, and said:

"So you are a devout Catholic."

"Not really," she said. "But I love the air of this Catholic church."

We took a car from a nearby stand, and I saw her to her door, then went straight home to sleep.

I woke to find it already two in the afternoon; I had an engagement at four, and just as I had eaten a little and was setting out, I discovered in the pocket of my coat three wads of banknotes — four thousand dollars, the very sum I had lost at the tables the day before. How they came to be in my pocket — Bai Ping had put them there, of course. But in all the time Bai Ping and I had been together, what chance had there been for her to take the notes from her handbag and slip them into my coat pocket? On my way out, hand thrust in my coat, I turned it over and over — from seeing her carry the notes out of the den and come with me to the restaurant, to her putting the notes into her wallet, to her going to the cloakroom, when I took the pawn ticket for the ring from her wallet, to our walking together all the way to Mass at the Xujiahui church, and taking a car back when the Mass was done — and I simply could not think of a single chance she could have had that I did not see.

Turning it over and over, I boarded at the bus stop. Just as I was about to buy my ticket, I came upon the red leaf in my wallet, and it dawned on me all at once: when I went to pick the leaves my coat had been over her shoulders, and the moment I came out with them she had given the coat back to me, and after that I had never once put my hand into its pocket — so beyond doubt it was she who had planned it, sending me off to pick the leaf.

I got back about supper time, meaning to stay in that night and read the books I had bought at the secondhand shop the day before. But Stephen came.

I told him how the night before had gone, but kept back the matter of the pawn ticket and the money — as I had already decided to do on my way home.

Stephen was much put out at not having been found by me the day before, but not cast down; at once he said, all high spirits:

"Come — let's go and find Bai Ping again today."

"No," I said. "Today you should go alone."

"Why?"

"I really am too tired," I said — but that was a chance lie. The truth was that I did not wish to paint over again the impression of beauty Bai Ping had left on me.

Stephen still tried to rouse my interest, but I only kept urging him to go alone. In the end he gave in to me — the first time since our friendship began that I was not brought along by him, and the last time as well.

I called a car for Stephen, and as we waited for it an idea struck me, and I suddenly said:

"Have you any money? Could you leave me five thousand dollars?"

"What — is it for this that you won't come out?"

"No," I said. "This is another matter."

"Will a cheque do?"

"Just as well," I said.

He took out his chequebook and pen, and as he was signing the car sounded outside; he handed me the cheque and hurried off.

At twelve o'clock someone tapped at my window: it was Stephen.

"What?" I went out to open the door, and asked the moment I saw him: "Back so early?"

"Lucky fellow," he said, laughing. "Bai Ping is in love with you."

"Nonsense." I walked with him into the room.

"Because you didn't come, she wasn't the least bit happy."

"I imagine she, like me, was simply tired."

"No," he said, lighting a cigarette. "I wanted to take her out, and she refused."

"Had she some other engagement?"

"None," he said. "She only said she didn't feel like going out."

"Did you say anything to her of last night, between her and me?"

"No; I merely pretended we hadn't met just now."

"Good."

"What?" he asked. "Are you in love with her too, then?"

"Nonsense," I said. "With a dance-hostess?"

"That's not right," he said gravely. "Can one not fall in love with a dance-hostess?"

"That's not what I mean," I said. "I only mean to say that I have never loved — so you needn't be jealous."

"Now that is nonsense!" he said, laughing. "I hope you will love her, for she truly is in love with you."

People are forever making such jokes at a confirmed bachelor, and I was not surprised, so I did not answer. He went on:

"She's a very lovable person."

"She is," I said. "Then do you love her?"

"That is not love." He laughed, a little abashed. "My love is given elsewhere."

I did not press further, but tidied the books on the table and said:

"Care for something to eat?"

"Yes."

So I plugged in the stove to make coffee and toast bread, and broke off the conversation there.
