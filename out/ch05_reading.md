## Chapter 5. Into the Tiger's Den: CCP Intelligence Officers Go on the Offensive

The year 1941 may fairly be called year one of Communist intelligence work.

In the space of that single year came one heavy blow, the New Fourth Army Incident; two great reorganizations of the central intelligence apparatus; and three great victories in international strategic intelligence. Through the towering seas of a world war, Communist intelligence work pressed forward, and ran up into the front rank of the international intelligence race.

### 1. "The Great Darkness in the East"!

The hidden front fights in two main ways: intelligence and security. Intelligence is offensive work; security is defensive.

The Communists had built their intelligence and security system in the first place to protect themselves, and even when they gathered intelligence it was mostly the warning, protective kind. That defensive posture began to change in 1940.

On the anniversary of the Mukden Incident in 1940, the Party Center issued a directive: it would open up work in the great cities behind enemy lines, with Zhou Enlai as director of the Central Committee on Behind-Enemy-Lines Work and Kang Sheng as his deputy. On October 7 the Social Affairs Department issued its "Directive on Opening Up Intelligence Work Behind Enemy Lines," which held that opening up work in the great cities behind the lines was now the first task of security work.

Behind enemy lines meant the enemy's rear, the areas held by Japan and by the Wang Jingwei regime.

To move from bristling with defenses on first reaching Yan'an to pushing deep into the heart of enemy country: this meant that Communist intelligence and security work was turning from defense to attack.

No sooner had it moved to attack than it was attacked itself. Early in 1941 the Nationalists suddenly launched the New Fourth Army Incident, and the New Fourth Army was all but annihilated. The cruel fact drove the lesson home once more: guard against the surprise blow, strengthen the intelligence work.

As it happened, the Center had already deployed for offensive intelligence work, and now, in short order, one great report came in on the heels of another.

On March 8, the Japanese ambassador in Washington, Nomura, reached a "Draft Understanding" with the American secretary of state, Hull.

On May 19, the British foreign secretary Eden, the Chinese ambassador to Britain Guo Taiqi, and the Japanese ambassador to Britain Shigemitsu settled the terms of a peace plan, which Guo carried to the United States to sound out its views.

From Shanghai, Hong Kong, Nanjing, Chongqing, reports of these secret international talks came in by many channels, and the German friend Anna Wang, too, found something like a Munich conspiracy afoot in the Far East.

China's war of resistance had entered, and was still entering, the wider world war; China was being swept, unavoidably, into the storms of international politics. The way to escape being at their mercy was to read the great international trends correctly and fight for the strategic initiative.

Mao Zedong laid out the strategy from Yan'an. It was not Japan that was now courting America and the Soviet Union, he said, but America and the Soviet Union that were courting Japan, courting it not to widen the war onto their own ground. America had no intention of joining Britain, the Soviet Union, and China in a common resistance; the aim of its secret talks with Japan was to break up the German-Japanese-Italian pact and keep itself neutral.

Intelligence made all the difference. The Communists could now not only see through their opponents at home; they could see clear through the great powers abroad. On May 25, 1941, Mao issued an internal Party directive: "Notice on Exposing the New Far Eastern Munich Conspiracy." "A new Far Eastern Munich conspiracy, to make peace between Japan and America at China's expense and create an anti-Communist, anti-Soviet order, is now brewing among the Americans, the Japanese, and Chiang. We must expose it and oppose it."

In September 1938, the British prime minister Chamberlain and the German chancellor Hitler had reached an agreement in the small European city of Munich, and Britain consented to Germany's seizure of Czechoslovak territory. Emboldened by that indulgence, Germany the very next year invaded Poland and touched off the Second World War. And so Munich became Europe's byword for the appeasement of aggression.

Asia, in fact, had its own "Munich." From the Mukden Incident of 1931 to the Marco Polo Bridge Incident of 1937, China and Japan had already been at heavy war for years, and Chiang Kai-shek, still waiting on foreign aid, still did not dare declare war on Japan, while Britain and America were preparing to convene a "Far Eastern International Conference"—indulging Japan's seizure of Chinese soil.

This Far Eastern Munich was full of peril. If the Americans, British, and Soviets all made their peace with Japan, China would be left an orphan of nations; and if China's own Nationalists then came to terms with Japan, the Communists would be left the lone army of the resistance.

To the whole Party Mao put it in the starkest terms: "Be ready for a great darkness in the East."

Getting the intelligence did not mean the intelligence work was over; there was still the question of how to use it.

Through the war of resistance Japan was the great national enemy. The Communists' intelligence organs kept their eyes fixed on Japan, watching to see who in China might dare make a secret peace with it. Pan Hannian's network, working its way into the Japanese secret-service organs, found that the Nationalists had eight separate lines of contact with Japanese agents, and that one Juntong representative was passing himself off as a brother of Soong May-ling named Song Ziliang. Had these talks succeeded, China would have thrown up Wang Jingweis by the batch, great and small, and the united front of resistance would have miscarried.

How to stop a secret surrender?

The usual method was assassination: kill the negotiators on both sides and the talks could not go on. When Wang Jingwei fled Chongqing and issued an open telegram from Hanoi in Vietnam showing his wish for peace, Chiang Kai-shek flew into a rage and sent Juntong to kill him at once. To assassinate a famous man in a foreign capital—from setting up the base, to sending the men, to smuggling in the weapons, to the shadowing and the deed itself—Dai Li surmounted every obstacle, only to fall short at the last and kill the wrong man. Failing to kill Wang, he only goaded him into throwing off his last shred of face and fleeing outright to Nanjing, where he became a collaborator in the open.

Against the Far Eastern Munich conspiracy, Mao did not reach for assassination.

Wang Shiying, the Eighth Route Army's representative in Shanxi, convened a mass rally and laid bare the scheme of the Shanxi authorities to treat secretly with Japan, even naming the representatives of both sides. A conspiracy fears nothing so much as the light, and Yan Xishan had to break off his talk of surrender. By rights Wang Shiying was in a position to have Yan killed. Yan's close friend Zhao Daiwen was governor of Shanxi, and the governor's own son, Zhao Zongfu, was a loyal secret Communist; killing Yan would have been as easy as turning a hand. But the task Wang Shiying gave Zhao Zongfu did not include assassination, only intelligence—getting the evidence of Yan Xishan's secret dealings with the Japanese agents.

Within a united front, to assassinate an ally whose plot has not yet come to light wins no hearts. Within a united front, to expose an ally's treacherous scheming wins them in plenty. A single open dispatch from the New China News Agency could stop a plot in its tracks; Mao exposed them one after another, and lightly cut through eight secret lines of surrender.

This open kind of stratagem Mao carried over, too, into the international struggle.

Receiving foreign correspondents, the Communist leaders raised the point that America ought not to be supplying Japan with scrap steel and other strategic goods. The correspondents were astonished: America called itself neutral between China and Japan, and here it was propping Japan up by the back door.

Under the pressure of world opinion America halted its exports of steel, rubber, and oil and other strategic goods to Japan, and the secret Japanese-American talks could reach no agreement either.

Practice proved it: to break a conspiracy by exposing it, to win without fighting, gave an extraordinary return on the investment.

Practice proved something else too: only by getting the intelligence early could you escape being at the enemy's mercy and design your response.

The Far Eastern Munich conspiracy was stopped, and the premise of the victory was earliness—getting the intelligence early and holding the strategic initiative.

Look around the world of that hour: what was the most important strategic intelligence of all?

One prize was Germany's strategic intentions; the other was Japan's.

A great international race for strategic intelligence!

Every spy in the world was electrified, and threw himself into a contest for intelligence of unprecedented ferocity. Whoever could seize one of these two prizes would be a super-spy.

### 2. Who Warned Stalin of Germany's Invasion of the USSR?

The one most anxious about the intentions of Germany and Japan was the Soviet Union.

Germany's armies had already swept across Europe. In the west they were bombing Britain across the Channel; in the east they now stood on the Soviet border. About the only thing Hitler had not yet done, that he might, was strike the Soviet Union. Soviet military intelligence sent out a mass of agents to watch Germany, to watch whether and when it would open its attack on the USSR.

Japan was Russia's old enemy. Russia pressed east, Japan pressed west, and the two expansions collided in China's Northeast. From the battle of Port Arthur in 1905 to the battle of Nomonhan in 1937, Japan had always sought a chance to take up arms against the Soviet Union. Soviet military intelligence deployed early: as far back as 1929 it sent the strategic agent Sorge to Shanghai to watch from close by, and soon afterward slipped him into Tokyo, to gather intelligence in the Japanese capital itself.

Sorge was a top-flight spy, but with his fair hair and blue eyes he was, once in Japan, taken straight onto the watch-list of the Tokkō, the Special Higher Police. To get close to Japan one still needed yellow-skinned Asians.

The Communists were a member of the Comintern's intelligence bureau, and had long had a relationship of intelligence exchange with the Comintern. By the time of the Second World War the Comintern was no longer content to have the Communists hand over China-theater intelligence; it wanted networks of its own across China, answering directly to itself. The Comintern proposed to the Communists that they train a number of Chinese intelligence men to gather intelligence on Japan in the Far Eastern theater.

As it happened, the remnant of the Western Route Army that had reached Xinjiang was then undergoing training in modern military technique, and the Communists agreed to pick talent from among them. Chen Yun and Deng Fa chose twelve from these four hundred-odd men and sent them to the Soviet Union to train. Later these men were posted to occupied areas across China to do secret intelligence work, and made many contributions to the international anti-fascist front. Though these Communists were under orders to obey the Comintern and to keep no lateral ties with the Party organization, still, out of a Chinese patriot's love of country, some of them took the initiative to contact the Communist intelligence organs and provide the motherland with resistance intelligence.

Lanzhou, too, had a "Joint-with-the-Soviets Intelligence Group." To win Soviet aid for the resistance, the Nationalist government had allowed the Soviets to set up diplomatic and military missions in a number of Chinese cities. The deputy Soviet military representative at Lanzhou, Vladimirov (Sun Ping), was a Soviet intelligence officer, and he asked the Communists to help set up a "Joint-with-the-Soviets Intelligence Group." The two sides agreed that the Soviets would supply funds and a radio and the Chinese would choose the people. Yu Mingjiu (Xiao Bingshi) was made group leader. Yu opened a firm, the "Gaoji Trading Company," on Yimin Road (now Qingyang Road) in Lanzhou, taking the post of manager himself, with the underground Party member Qin Yulin as his deputy; the group's members included Luo Jingyi (a woman), Du Hansan, Lu Xizhen, Li Yongwen, Wang Hongzhang, Ran Ping, Chen Benshen, Liu Xinglan (a woman), and others. The group needed a man who could get inside the Nationalist forces. Wu Xiuquan talked it over with Sun Zuobin, secretary of the Gansu work committee, and they recommended a company commander in Deng Baoshan's forces, Wang Xinchao. In September 1939 Wang Xinchao was taken into the Soviet Communist Party (the CPSU) and put in charge of a secret radio code-named "Martyr," reporting directly to Moscow.

Yan'an had a Soviet intelligence group too, quartered at Zaoyuan, the seat of the Party Center and the Central Intelligence Department, operating under the name of the Soviet news agency TASS. Sun Ping, the deputy Soviet military representative at Lanzhou who had founded the "Joint-with-the-Soviets Intelligence Group," was transferred to Yan'an to head the Soviet intelligence group there. In March 1941 the group opened an intelligence training class in a small gully behind Zaoyuan. The Communists who came to study were told by the Soviet staff: "The organization has decided you are to be a Soviet agent." Three months later they graduated, and the whole class was sent to the Northeast to gather intelligence on the Japanese army.

From Yan'an to the Northeast one had to pass through Shanxi and Hebei, through Nationalist zones and enemy-occupied zones, and could not help traveling scattered. Yao Lun, a Party member since 1935, was a Northeasterner, and in 1938 he was county Party secretary in Lin County, Shanxi; he was studying at the Marxist-Leninist Institute in Yan'an when he was called up to be a "Soviet agent." His destination was Dalian, but he was stopped short at Yanbei. Luckily the Yanbei military sub-district had a contact, Cao Lanchi, a worker who had fled back from the Datong coal mines and knew the lay of the occupied areas. This Cao Lanchi ate and drank and made merry along the way, hardly like a worker, and later tried to lure a fellow traveler into deserting; Xing Xiangsheng saw through him. Yao Lun and Xing Xiangsheng consulted the local prefectural secretary and decided to arrest him. Under sudden interrogation Cao Lanchi admitted he was a spy sent by the Japanese, and that he had ties to the sub-district's own reconnaissance-section chief and mass-mobilization-section chief. The case was pursued further and implicated a dozen of their own people. Yao Lun and the others thus missed their window for the Northeast, and turned back to Yan'an.

International spies of a like kind were at work across the Northeast as well. Russia had held the Chinese Eastern Railway there, and the Soviet state, inheriting the rights of way, had recruited some Communists among the Chinese of the Northeast. After the war of resistance broke out, these Chinese agents in turn supplied the Soviets with resistance intelligence.

The Comintern, the Soviet Party, the Soviet army—all had tried to build intelligence systems of their own in China, and a number of Communists spent a spell as "Soviet agents" on that account. For a Communist intelligence service so recently created, this intelligence partnership, too, was a kind of seasoning.

Soviet Red Army intelligence had long kept organizations in China's Northeast, watching above all the Japanese army's dispositions against the USSR along the Sino-Soviet border, and many of its members were Chinese. During the Second World War the Soviets planned to spread a network against Japan across the whole of China, and borrowed cadres from the Communists.

Liu Hekong and 18 others, officers and men of the Western Route Army who had fallen back into Xinjiang, made the long journey to Moscow, where the Soviet Red Army intelligence general Antonov briefed Liu Hekong in person. The villa in the Moscow suburbs was cut off from the world, the living lavish—woolen overcoats and high boots, bread and butter and milk. Yet for all that comfort the Chinese Red Army men had no easy time of it: the coursework was crushing.

These Chinese Red Army men were of peasant stock and not much lettered, but they learned fast, and every one of them was a fighter. They soon mastered the whole trade of espionage—wireless, weapons, photography, drafting, driving, secret writing, making contact, parachuting, reconnaissance, cover identities, the counter-espionage services of the various countries, ciphers, call signs, and the rest. The Chinese are quick to adapt, too, and country bumpkins like Liu Hekong learned to wear a Western suit and eat foreign food, and could move in the life of the cities.

In April 1939 Liu Hekong flew from Moscow to Urumqi, and by arrangement of the Eighth Route Army office traveled by way of Lanzhou and Xi'an to Chongqing, then from Chongqing through Guiyang to Kunming to obtain a passport out of the country into Vietnam. Under the alias Wang Baohua, Liu sailed from Haiphong in Vietnam on a British Butterfield & Swire steamer, went north by way of Hong Kong, and reached his destination, Shanghai, by the middle of May. Having taken so roundabout a course, he was the last man the concession police would ever have guessed to be from Moscow. Following Moscow's instructions, Liu made contact with his superior at two in the afternoon on the 25th, at the Paris Cinema on Avenue Joffre. And following his superior's instructions, Liu enrolled in a wireless training class in Shanghai—which wiped away any trace of his having studied wireless in Moscow.

Liu Hekong was glad, of course, to be back in his own country, but the new work made him deeply uneasy.

A young man of twenty-five could scarcely keep a foothold without a family for cover, so the organization arranged a false wife for him, a twenty-one-year-old woman student from Beiping. Their landlady envied the young couple their closeness, never knowing that behind the shut door the two of them quarreled, their tempers past matching.

Their tempers did not match, but their work did, and for the cause of revolution the two of them shared the bitter with the sweet. A false marriage that stood together through life and death became a true one, and gave them a daughter. Hidden away in Shanghai, Liu had no chance to taste the pleasures of that glittering world; day and night he worked, head down, in a garret, his mind always on his Red Army comrades, and above all on the eighteen who had gone with him to train in Moscow. Where were they hiding? Had they met with danger? Could the "little sparrow" work on his own?

The "little sparrow" Liu Hekong worried about most, Sheng Xianchuan, was the youngest of the eighteen; on the Long March he had been Li Xiannian's bodyguard. Sheng was now lying low in Lanzhou, getting close to Nationalist officers under a merchant's cover.

The others too had returned to China, and lay hidden in Chongqing, Nanjing, and elsewhere.

Liu Hekong's work was that of a secret radio operator, and though he worked shut up in a room it was dangerous all the same. The Japanese ran a tight counter-espionage regime in Shanghai, with a wireless listening station on the roof of the Xinya Hotel and mobile direction-finding vans in the streets. To protect the set, Liu opened three shops and moved house six times, and was once forced to hide in the home of Fan Xing.

Liu Hekong sent and received wireless traffic constantly, but all he handled was numerical code; he did not know the substance of the intelligence. Even without knowing the direct use of his work, Liu obeyed to the letter; before he set out he had known his task was to gather intelligence, on the Nationalists and on Japan. Sharp-eyed, Liu also noticed that the stations receiving his traffic were not only Vladivostok but Chongqing too—proof that his intelligence was serving not only the Soviet Union but China's war of resistance. Never prying into secrets, Liu still learned a thing or two: his own controller, "Mr. Liu," was Chinese, and Fan Xing who sheltered him was a Chinese comrade too; both were highly capable, able even to reach into Nationalist Juntong.

The organization Liu Hekong belonged to, like Sorge's and that of his successor Walton, answered to Soviet military intelligence. Sorge withdrew, Walton was arrested, and men like Liu carried the work on. Soviet intelligence work in China never rested. Only, where it had once been run mostly by foreigners, it was now run mostly by Chinese.

For all the Soviets' care in training Chinese agents, these Red Army cadres were of country stock and could hardly break into the upper reaches of the great cities. So the Soviets turned again to the Communists for help. The Soviet ambassador in Chongqing, Panyushin, and the military attaché Roshchin went straight to Zhou Enlai to borrow men—intelligence men.

Zhou Enlai had no shortage of talent at hand.

In September 1940 the Party Center issued its notice on opening up work in the great cities behind enemy lines, and set up a Committee on Behind-Enemy-Lines Work with Zhou Enlai as director and Kang Sheng as deputy. The Southern Bureau, of which Zhou was secretary, took charge of pushing forward the work in the great cities behind the lines: Dong Biwu, deputy secretary and head of the united front department; Qin Bangxian as organization chief; Kai Feng as propaganda chief; Liu Shaowen as intelligence chief; Ye Jianying over military affairs and liaison; Deng Yingchao over women's work; Jiang Nanxiang over youth work; Wu Kejian as editor-in-chief of the New China Daily with oversight of intelligence.

These masters of clandestine work spread their working ties into every layer of society, and above all into the inner core of the Nationalist leadership.

Inside the confidential office of the Nationalist Central Party Headquarters, Shen Anna lay hidden, taking down in shorthand the secrets of the central standing committee's meetings.

On the Military Commission's advisory staff was Shi Yong (Sha Wenwei), whose elder brother Sha Menghai, a gifted man of Zhejiang, drafted documents for Chiang Kai-shek; through his brother's connections Shi Yong entered that high advisory body, where he could see the proposals people of every stripe laid before Chiang.

On Zhongtong's Sichuan Provincial Special Committee was Li Qiang, who caught wind in advance when Zhongtong was about to arrest underground Party members.

Even the senior Nationalist generals had Communists at their elbows: Chen Cheng's aide-de-camp Deng Dazhang, Li Zongren's secretary Liu Zhongrong, Bai Chongxi's secretary Xie Hegeng.

Inside Juntong itself, the greatest of the secret-service organs, there was actually a seven-man Communist Party branch, with Zhang Luping sent from Yan'an as its secretary.

The Nationalists' four great families each had Communists at their side too: at T.V. Soong's, Wang Bingnan; at H.H. Kung's, Ji Chaoding; at Chen Guofu's, Lu Xuzhang; and at the side of the supreme leader Chiang Kai-shek himself, Yan Baohang.

Yan Baohang was a Northeasterner from Haicheng, a senior aide to Zhang Xueliang. After the Mukden Incident of 1931 he fled inside the Great Wall, and with Gao Chongmin and Du Zhongyuan founded in Beiping the Northeastern People's National Salvation Association. In 1935 he petitioned Zhang Xueliang to stop the "bandit-suppression" and join with the Communists against Japan. When the Xi'an Incident broke out and Chiang Kai-shek was held by Zhang Xueliang, Soong May-ling urgently needed someone to mediate, and thought of Yan Baohang. Yan had studied in Britain and was head of the Northeastern YMCA; Soong May-ling entrusted this fellow Christian with going to Xi'an to talk their old Northeastern chief, Zhang Xueliang, around.

Yan Baohang shuttled between Xi'an and Nanjing, helped bring about Chiang Kai-shek's release, and from then on enjoyed the full trust of Chiang and his wife. But old Chiang did not know that in this same period Yan had also become a friend of Zhou Enlai. In 1938 Zhou personally approved Yan's admission to the Party, and arranged for him not to reveal his Communist membership but to go on, in his guise as a Northeastern patriot, doing united front work in the war of resistance, and above all opening up work among the Nationalist upper crust.

Yan Baohang was deeply trusted in Chongqing. When Chiang Kai-shek and Soong May-ling launched the "New Life Movement," Yan was its executive director. Familiar with English culture, he was even Soong May-ling's dancing partner, and high officials angling to flatter the First Lady had to go through Yan Baohang.

Yan Baohang was forever in the company of senior Nationalist officials. At one banquet the president of the Control Yuan, Yu Youren, and the president of the Executive Yuan, Sun Ke, discussed with visible delight a piece of news: Gui Yongqing said Germany was going to attack the Soviet Union.

Chiang Kai-shek had always admired German fascism. He wanted German advisers even for encircling the Red Army, and he still hoped Germany might mediate the Sino-Japanese conflict, so the Nationalists' ties with the German military were very close. One day in 1941 the military attaché in Germany, Gui Yongqing, was chatting with a German officer in Berlin, and remarked that the problem of northern China was hard to solve; the German officer boasted offhand: China's problem is easy—on June 22 Germany attacks the Soviet Union, and it all solves itself. This colossal piece of news raced back to Chongqing and fell, of all places, into the ear of the secret Communist Yan Baohang.

Yan Baohang reported it at once to both the Soviet embassy and Zhou Enlai. The Party Center decided on the spot to pass this top-secret intelligence to the Soviet Party. In the same period Pan Hannian, in Hong Kong, also obtained the date of the German attack on the Soviet Union.

The world balance of 1941 shifted from hour to hour.

On June 13 the Soviet TASS agency denied that a German-Soviet war was imminent. On June 14 Wang Jingwei flew to Japan on a "state" visit. On June 16 Zhou Enlai reported to Yan'an that he had obtained strategic intelligence of an imminent large-scale German attack on the Soviet Union. The Party Center at once relayed it to the Soviets.

Soviet intelligence had already, through several of its channels, obtained word of the German surprise attack, but Stalin would not credit it. The art of war holds nothing worse than fighting on two fronts; by common reasoning, Germany should concentrate its strength to take Britain in the west first, and only then open war on the Soviet Union in the east. It was hard to imagine Hitler fighting on two fronts against the Soviet Union now, unless he had lost his mind.

Besides, the organizations reporting on Germany's intentions worked mostly in Western Europe, and there was no guaranteeing they had not been penetrated by German or British spies. The international spy world was awash with contradictory reports—some that Germany would launch a great attack on the Soviet Union, some that Britain was planting false intelligence to sow discord between Germany and the USSR—and Stalin could not make up his mind.

Only when the Communists supplied their intelligence did Stalin believe it. The Communists had no ties to Germany or Britain, and so no way of being taken in. The frontline Soviet forces went onto a war footing one day early. That was a success of intelligence work; even one day would cut the losses greatly. And yet, for a war on such a scale, a single day's preparation was still too little, and the Soviet Union suffered a grave defeat all the same. That was a failure of intelligence work, a great failure bearing on the safety of a nation.

In the international race for intelligence on Germany's strategic intentions, who was the winner?

After the German attack began, Stalin sent the Communists a telegram of thanks: "Thank you for the intelligence of the German attack, which allowed the Soviet Union to go early onto a war footing."

Always before it had been the Soviets who aided the Communists; this time, for the first time, Stalin thanked them.

The Communists' intelligence ability was beginning to draw the world's notice.

### 3. Mao Zedong's Method of Intelligence Analysis: Investigation and Study

Having judged the German invasion of the Soviet Union exactly right, Mao Zedong, far from preening, gave intelligence work still more weight. In this half-year of 1941 the international situation was changing too fast; intelligence work had to keep pace.

Drawing further on the lessons of the New Fourth Army Incident, the Party Center promulgated two decisions: on July 1 the "Decision on Strengthening Party Spirit," and on August 1 the "Decision on Investigation and Study." Theory alone was not enough; there had to be an organizational measure too. The Party Center decided to build a Central Investigation and Study Bureau, charged with investigation and study of the enemy, ourselves, and our friends alike.

The powers of this bureau were of a scope and standing without precedent. Its subordinate bodies—the Central Social Affairs Department, the Second Bureau of the Military Commission, the Central Policy Research Office—took in every central-level intelligence and security organ and were not limited to them. Under the central bureau were four undisclosed branch bureaus as well: the First, in the Jin-Cha-Ji Border Region, to investigate the great cities of North China and the Japanese-occupied Northeast; the Second, Pan Hannian's network, for Europe, America, and the occupied areas; the Third, in the Southern Bureau at Chongqing, to study the great cities behind the lines in the south and the parties in the Nationalist zone; and the Fourth, in the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, for the interior of the Border Region and its borders.

Mao Zedong proposed himself as director of the Central Investigation and Study Bureau, and concurrently head of the Policy Research Office. This post, it may be said, was the one office in Mao's whole life bound up with intelligence and security work.

In the Yan'an years Mao's investigation and study rose from a personal habit into a systematic enterprise. The Center's decision on investigation and study went into the Social Affairs Department's Cadre's Handbook; the department set up a books-and-press section that published a journal of its own, the Books and Press Bulletin. Every month Yan'an set aside three hundred silver dollars to subscribe to newspapers from the occupied areas; the Jin-Cha-Ji social affairs department sent men into Beiping and Tianjin to buy them; the Jin-Sui social affairs department kept a transport team just to carry them the long road to Yan'an. The central leaders at Yan'an and the Japanese Communist leader Okano Susumu studied them closely, and out of them came important intelligence of strategic value.

The Japanese paper Asahi Shimbun carried this: "From the summary of the North China Army's overall results for Shōwa 18 (1943), it is fully clear that the North China Army, which once took the Chongqing armies as its opponent, has by now turned entirely to the sweeping-out of the Communists as its center." The Yan'an Liberation Daily reprinted it at once, running an article titled "The Eighth Route Army in the Enemy's Own Mouth," to refute Chiang Kai-shek's slander that the Eighth Route Army roamed about without fighting.

"Know the situation, and the resolve comes strong." Mao made investigation and study the ground of decision, raised it to the level of philosophy, and prized it beyond what most could imagine.

In 1940 Mao wired Chongqing from Yan'an, asking Zhou Enlai to carry out upper-level surveys in each province of the Nationalist-ruled areas—of the great landlords, the great bourgeoisie, the senior generals—several dozen to a hundred men per province. The Yan'an Public Security Bureau made a detailed survey of every household in the belt between town and country around Yan'an, producing the "Survey of Yan'an's New Township," to lay a foundation for social administration. During the running fight through northern Shaanxi, Mao moved among 300,000 enemy troops with only a few hundred guards; yet even within so small an establishment he added an investigation section. At every stopping place, however pressed for time, the section chief, Mu Fengyun, would carry out the investigation himself, to make clear the social lay of that village.

Before every strategic turn, investigate and study first.

During the War of Liberation Mao asked each strategic region to send the Center comprehensive reports at set intervals. Fighting was so hard-pressed—who had time to write essays? Lin Biao dragged his feet and did not report, and Mao wired him again and again to hurry, and praised Deng Xiaoping, who reported promptly. Mao took the occasion to remind the Party's senior cadres: "To think a good while on the whole and on what is held in common is something a leading comrade cannot do without. Lacking such thought, leadership work will fail."

Mao held firm and made it a rule: investigation and study was to be the basic skill of every Party cadre. With victory in the war and the cities to be taken over, cadres everywhere were put through rotating courses, and the study material was the survey reports. The materials the East China Bureau prepared on conditions in Shanghai ran to tens of millions of characters. Thanks to so thorough a preparation of intelligence, the country-bumpkin cadres entered the cities without a dazzled eye or a swimming head.

Intelligence work is a science too, and the root way to prevent an intelligence failure is a scientific attitude. Intelligence analysis includes the specific judgment made in response to a particular event, and also the basic research that grasps a strategic trend. Just as natural-science research divides into applied and basic science, investigation and study may be called a basic kind of intelligence analysis and compilation. Mao devoted himself to making intelligence work scientific, and after 1949 named the Center's intelligence organ the "Investigation Department." Note that this name differs from both the Soviet KGB and the American intelligence agency. The little books-and-press section of the Yan'an days grew, step by step, into a vast research establishment.

Formed over many years, investigation and study became Mao's way of life. When his illness grew so grave he could not speak, Mao still signaled with his hand: he wanted the reference materials on the Japanese prime minister Miki.

Investigation and study—Mao Zedong's method of intelligence analysis.

The Central Investigation and Study Bureau had been running only a month when Mao was dissatisfied again. In September 1941 the Party Center decided to establish a Central Intelligence Department as well.

The new Central Intelligence Department was the unified military-and-political strategic intelligence organ through which the Party Center and the Central Military Commission carried on investigation and intelligence work. It was formed by merging the old Central Social Affairs Department with a part of the Military Commission's General Staff, still under the Central Investigation and Study Bureau. In May 1942, when the Japanese launched their great mopping-up of the resistance bases behind the lines, the Military Commission's burden grew heavier, and most of the military elements of the Central Intelligence Department were parceled back to the General Staff.

The Central Intelligence Department and the Social Affairs Department were one body under two signboards. Their main task was the gathering and study of military and political strategic intelligence, though a smaller apparatus was kept to guide security work in the base areas: intelligence work was directed in the name of the Central Intelligence Department, security work in the name of the Social Affairs Department. This arrangement helped change the old habit of leaning toward security, and gave more weight to intelligence.

The old intelligence work had not weighed strategic intelligence enough. Now it was made plain: the Central Intelligence Department, as the unified military-and-political strategic intelligence organ of the Party Center and the Military Commission, had as its main task the getting of military and political strategic intelligence. Mao told the field: don't report tactical intelligence to Yan'an—how many troops garrison such-and-such a strongpoint, how many machine guns—I want only strategic intelligence.

This meant a high degree of intelligence self-awareness. From getting mainly warning, protective intelligence, the work turned to getting mainly military and political strategic intelligence. This too was a great strategic turn in Communist intelligence work.

The head of the Central Social Affairs Department, Kang Sheng, held the post of head of the Central Intelligence Department as well, with three deputies: the head of the General Political Department, Wang Jiaxiang; the deputy chief of the General Staff, Ye Jianying; and the deputy head of the Social Affairs Department, Li Kenong. Kang Sheng poured his main energy into the political campaigns; Wang Jiaxiang, ill, never took up the post; Ye Jianying came seldom; and the department's day-to-day work was in fact run by the deputy head, Li Kenong.

On the Center's instructions the Central Intelligence Department set up the view that investigation and study and intelligence work were closely bound together, and made clear that intelligence work served the Party's whole line, strategy, and tactics. It took the Party Center's "Decision on Strengthening Party Spirit," "Decision on Investigation and Study," and "Notice on Improving Intelligence Work" as the theoretical weapons of intelligence work. The tasks of intelligence work were made clear: on the one hand, general investigation work—systematically gathering open and semi-open material of strategic significance; on the other, secret intelligence work—collecting the enemy's military and political secrets of every kind. At the first departmental meeting Zhu De spoke: there was a great deal of material, he said, but the trouble was too little study, and the subjectivism among comrades in the past had come from men who called themselves Marxists. This meant that the Communists' intelligence work was not only an active practice but had been raised to the level of theory.

The Central Intelligence Department worked chiefly against three targets—the Japanese and their puppets, the Nationalists, and Europe and America—studying their politics, military affairs, parties, personalities, secret services, and social conditions. It divided the country into several regions: the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia region, the Jin-Cha-Ji region, the Hong Kong–Shanghai region, the Jin-Sui region, the Central China region, the Chongqing region, the Xi'an region. In each region it built intelligence radios, intelligence way-stations, courier lines, and other systems of liaison and command.

The old hands and able men of the Communist hidden front now stepped forward, one after another, onto the intelligence front line.

Zhou Enlai was posted at Chongqing, the Nationalist wartime capital, in charge of liaison with the Nationalist Center and at the same time directing the secret work of the Southern Bureau. Dong Biwu, Ye Jianying, Wang Ruofei, Bo Gu, Wu Yuzhang, Deng Yingchao, and others came and went at Chongqing from time to time.

Lin Boqu was at Xi'an, the Shaanxi provincial capital; Xie Juezai at Lanzhou, the Gansu capital. Their public posts were as representatives of the Eighth Route Army offices, but they also directed the underground work of the Northwest.

The Jin-Cha-Ji Border Region was the largest base area outside Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia; Du Liqing, who took the name Xu Jianguo, was head of the social affairs department of the Northern Branch Bureau.

The veteran security cadre Tan Zhengwen was transferred to the Jin-Sui Branch Bureau as head of its social affairs department and concurrently head of the general public security bureau.

The deputy head of the Social Affairs Department, Pan Hannian, went in person to Shanghai and Hong Kong, hid himself on the spot, and carried on his secret work.

Set up the points, string the lines, weave the net: the Communists' intelligence work soon formed a network covering the whole country.

The Center's settled guideline for underground work was: "Hide the picked and able, lie low for the long term, store up strength, and wait for the moment."

It is worth noting that in 1941 the intelligence work of both America and the Soviet Union was still parceled out among their army, navy, and air force, with no top-level intelligence organ. That famous American Central Intelligence Agency was not established until 1947.

By 1941 the Communists' regard for intelligence work was already the equal of any great power's.

### 4. Yan'an Strikes Out

Attack is the best defense, and intelligence the best reconnaissance. The best way to protect the Party Center's seat at Yan'an was still to carry the intelligence work outward. On the Central Intelligence Department's deployment, the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office threw itself into offensive intelligence work.

At the end of 1940, following the Social Affairs Department's "Directive on Opening Up Intelligence Work Behind Enemy Lines," the Border Security convened its first field-work conference, put forward the guideline "boldly and freely, actively opening up," and decided to build field posts in every district. As the Border Region's own intelligence and security organ, the Security Office's field work had a regional character: it centered on the Border Region's frontiers, working against the enemy directly across from it.

Northeast of Yan'an, the Suide and Mizhi posts faced Zhongtong's district offices and Juntong's stations in the direction of Yulin; south of Yan'an, the Fu County and Ganquan posts faced those at Luochuan; southwest of Yan'an, the Dingbian and Longdong posts faced those at Xifeng. The intelligence cadres at the Border Security's posts grew to more than 40; Ge Shen was sent to Sanbian, Chen Shiqi to Longdong, Qu Jixian to Guanzhong, and Bu Lu of the Border Security led men on rounds to guide the work.

The security sub-offices of the various prefectures and the county security sections had intelligence strength too. Every intelligence post took care to reach into the enemy's secret-service organs and build inside lines.

The Guanzhong sub-district was the salient thrust toward the Nationalist zone, and the intelligence work there was of special importance.

The Guanzhong sub-district's field organ went by the name of the prefectural commissioner's secretariat; the field group leader, Qu Jixian, held the post of chief secretary, and the deputy leader, Qin Ping, passed for the manager of a trading company. Qu Jixian personally held several inside lines to the west; Zhang Zhongping lived at Liulin and ran the eastern line; Chen Jian, under the title of united front chief for Chishui County, ran the dispatching work around Chunhua; Yang Zongyao worked under the title of deputy chairman of the Xinzheng County assembly.

Many people of the Guanzhong sub-district lived in the Communist Border Region but worked at their trades in the Nationalist zone, which helped the field group build its strength. In Yao County they recruited the higher-primary headmaster He Zhendong, the Hu brothers, the Meng brothers, the ward-chief Cheng, and others; in Fuping they used the Elder Brothers Society to win over an outlaw armed band under Fang the Fifth. In Chunhua County the underground Party member Shang Wanyu, under his open Nationalist Party identity, secretly built an intelligence group. In Xunyi County the underground member Li Shuzhen, whose home was in the Border Region, used the cover of a primary-school headmaster to build an intelligence group. Most striking of all, they sent Yang Zongyao's nephew Yang Hongchao into Zhongtong as a full-time investigator.

Yang Hongchao taught primary school in the border village of Xiaoyizi, a li outside which lay the Nationalist zone. When Yang received a letter of subornation from the Nationalist zone and did not know how to answer, he mailed it to his uncle Yang Zongyao. Yang Zongyao, a veteran Party member of the Guanzhong sub-district who had once given Xi Zhongxun cover in his underground work, knew that the man behind the subornation, Zhang Zhanying, was a turncoat with ties to the Nationalist Zhongtong branch in Xunyi County. Yang Zongyao reported it to the organization; the field group leader Qu Jixian saw an opening, and after conferring with the Guanzhong prefectural secretary Xi Zhongxun, they decided to turn the plan against its maker and use the chance to send Yang Hongchao into Zhongtong. Bu Lu, too, talked with Yang Hongchao himself, coaching him on how to win their trust.

By the organization's arrangement, Yang Hongchao reported false intelligence to Zhongtong, claiming he had recruited a special source in Li Ke, the Xinzheng county Party secretary; this won Zhongtong's trust, and he was made a full-time investigator. Yang was a steady man, and now and then he brought back some Nationalist intelligence; the Border Security in turn let him feed Zhongtong some false intelligence about the Border Region.

Yang Hongchao found that the secretaries of the Nationalist Xunyi county branch, Pu Suichang and Pu Youjie, were both full-time Zhongtong agents; he learned Zhongtong's plans for infiltrating the Border Region and its list of targets, identified several agents working the border belt, and copied out, over several trips, the Nationalist "Detailed Rules for Party-Network Activity." From Yang's intelligence Qin Ping compiled a report, "A Glimpse of the Zhongtong Shaanxi Office's Agents," which won the Border Security's approval, and Yang Hongchao was approved as a special Communist Party member.

Luochuan, 180 li south of Yan'an, was the very strongpoint from which the Nationalist zone faced the Border Region. The various Nationalist secret services all set up stations there; the head of Zhongtong's investigation office at Luochuan, Shan Buyi, was an old-hand agent. The leader of the Border Security's field group at Luochuan, Zhao Qufei, was by contrast a novice at intelligence.

On taking up the field-group post at Luochuan, Zhao Qufei set at once to finding intelligence men. The Ganquan primary-school headmaster Shi Zhiwen had been sent from Xi'an; a Nationalist Party member he was, but no agent, and an upright, progressive-minded man, one who could be developed. Zhao Qufei talked with Shi Zhiwen and, in a stroke, tore through the paper on the window, asking Shi to gather intelligence for the Communists. Shi did not yet have the nerve, and hesitated. Zhao Qufei goaded him: "We've talked this far—whether you'll do it or not, you'll do it!" And Shi, roused, answered: "If I do it, I'll do it like a man of the revolution; if not, I won't do it at all!" When the Communists drove out the Nationalist government of Ganquan County, Zhao Qufei told Shi Zhiwen to withdraw with them and take his chance. So it was that Shi Zhiwen entered Zhongtong's Shaanxi provincial office, in charge of the pre-trial interrogation of arrested Communists.

Zhang Jiping, a graduate of the second Qilipu class, came to work at Luochuan too, and hung out two open signboards—the "Eighteenth Group Army Liaison Station at Luochuan" and the "Eighth Route Army Office's Luochuan Motor Depot"—receiving passing vehicles on the surface while secretly gathering intelligence, relayed to Yan'an through the Fu County station. Zhang Jiping and Shi Zhiwen studied the personnel of Zhongtong's Luochuan sub-office and decided to break it up man by man. First they turned the deputy head of the office, Qi Kaizhang, obtaining Zhongtong's secret documents, then had him return in good time to go on lying low inside Zhongtong. Then, through Qi Kaizhang, they won over the head of the office, Nie Mingxi—and Nie Mingxi handed Zhang Jiping the whole of Luochuan Zhongtong's files and ciphers.

The Social Affairs Department had an inside line at Luochuan too. The Luochuan commissioner, Zhong Xiangyu, was from Hanchuan in Hubei, and rare was the fellow Hubei man he met in Shaanxi; so he kept Cheng Yonghe on as a reporter for the Luochuan Daily. Cheng Yonghe wrote a fine hand, often wrote pieces for his superiors, and soon became a well-known local newsman, and in due course secretly joined the Luochuan Zhongtong organization. Zhongtong made much of this university man: Cheng Yonghe's open post was education chief of the county government, but he was in fact Zhongtong's "full-time investigator," leader of the Shaanxi investigation office's central intelligence group for the Luochuan prefecture, and head of the special-education supervision corps—and whether it was an agent sent from Luochuan into the Border Region or an agent passing through Luochuan from Xi'an, all were reported by Cheng Yonghe to Zhao Qufei.

Cheng Yonghe got on well in Zhongtong, and when Xi'an tried several times to transfer him Zhao Qufei simply would not let him go. This handsome, dashing Nationalist official was Luochuan's gold-medal bachelor, forever pursued by a pretty woman. But the woman was a Nationalist Party member—how could Cheng Yonghe dare marry her? To keep refusing gave no reasonable excuse, and to stay single forever drew suspicion, so Cheng asked the organization to send someone as cover. A wife for Cheng Yonghe? Zhao Qufei had no such woman cadre on hand, and could only urge Cheng to find a progressive-minded woman and marry her himself. But Cheng did not dare. He had been arrested once before in underground work, and knew this was a thing to be told neither to one's parents above nor to one's wife below; a troublesome match would only make more trouble. And so Cheng Yonghe stayed a bachelor for ten years, holding out until the Liberation.

Besides controlling Zhongtong at Luochuan, the Border Security had a man inside the Juntong organization there too. Mao Peichun, a graduate of the first Qilipu class, penetrated Juntong's "Lanzhou training class," and was then posted to Luochuan as head of the Special Higher Section of the gendarmerie command, and concurrently head of the Yao County Special Higher Section. Deep in the enemy's camp, Mao Peichun trained his memory constantly, and from memory alone supplied the Border Security with the complete roster of the Lanzhou training class. This batch of agents had barely entered the Border Region before they were all in the Border Security's grasp.

Zhang Jiping's Luochuan intelligence net seeped, step by step, into every department of the Nationalist administration at Luochuan. Luochuan had no telegraph office, and the Nationalists conducted their liaison by telephone. The head of the county government's telephone switchboard, Ning Zhijie, was a Border Security agent, and the agent charged with tapping the calls was the Border Security's man too. In this way, by listening in, they could grasp the intelligence of every Nationalist system at Luochuan, and even the intelligence passing between Luochuan and Xi'an.

For the young Communist intelligence men, opening up outward intelligence work of course still had a process of learning to it. But these first moves already carried a fierce, vital energy.

### 5. Weaving the Net in Xi'an

The Chinese prize safety, and Shaanxi has two cities whose names carry the character *an*, "peace": Yan'an on the northern Shaanxi frontier, and Xi'an, the vital rear of Yan'an.

Xi'an encircled Yan'an. Hu Zongnan held unified command over the Party, government, army, police, gendarmerie, and secret services of the Northwest, and clamped a tight blockade around Yan'an.

Yan'an encircled Xi'an in return: the layered intelligence net Zhou Enlai had arranged now encircled and counter-watched Hu Zongnan.

The three-line deployment Zhou Enlai had arranged in 1937 had by now been woven into a network. The third line, buried deep—Wu Defeng's intelligence system—reached in everywhere. The intelligence out of Xi'an flowed steadily to Yan'an and guarded the Center's safety to good effect. The courier Tao Siyong, escorting agents to and fro between Yan'an and Xi'an, was once arrested by the Border Security by mistake, and still held to his secret; pursued by Nationalist agents, she pulled her coat over her head and rolled off a cliff.

The Xi'an intelligence station came under close watch by Nationalist agents, and Wu Defeng, Zeng San, Wang Zhong, Luo Qingchang, and others were recalled to Yan'an one after another, the work passing to Wang Shijian and Cheng Zhiping. On the Center's deployment, cover measures were tightened further and the political quality of the intelligence raised. The survey reports Xi'an sent the Center carried not only the enemy's ciphers but a systematic overview of the Shaanxi Party, government, and military organs, brief lives of notable men, even an analysis of Hu Zongnan's personal traits.

The idle chessmen Zhou Enlai had set out in Xi'an in years past had by now become a *tesuji*—a winning stroke in the game of go.

Xiong Xianghui won Hu Zongnan's deep trust and became his aide-de-camp. Hu had four locations in Xi'an, the documents of three of them managed by Xiong Xianghui. When Hu held a military conference or went down to inspect the troops, he took Xiong Xianghui along. Even when the crown prince, Chiang Ching-kuo, visited the Northwest, Xiong Xianghui accompanied him the whole way. With a Communist mole at the "King of the Northwest's" side, Hu Zongnan's secrets had long since gone to Yan'an.

Hu Zongnan was a man of large political ambition, and had a special taste for gathering able young men into a clique. Playing to this, Chen Zhongjing and Xu Huang swore a blood-oath of brotherhood with him; the deeply trusted Chen Zhongjing could see Hu Zongnan without prior notice. Chen Zhongjing rose high, to secretary of the Three People's Principles Youth League's Shaanxi provincial branch, and with Shen Jian as secretary of the Xijing city sub-branch, they had Hu Zongnan's little organization under their effective control.

Beyond these three, planted deep, still more intelligence men lay hidden around Hu Zongnan.

Yao Wenbin had been secretary of the Beibei special branch in Chongqing, his reach extending across several colleges and middle schools and up into the Nationalist upper crust; he had once brought Chen Lian, daughter of the Nationalist secretary-general Chen Bulei, into the Party. The Central Social Affairs Department, seeking out intelligence talent, brought Yao Wenbin from Chongqing to Yan'an, trained him, and posted him to Xi'an to serve inside Hu Zongnan's intelligence group.

The intelligence group's workplace was right beside Hu Zongnan's residence, and once Yao Wenbin saw Chiang Kai-shek and his wife strolling in the back garden, and heard Soong May-ling sigh: "Here at last I can feel at ease." Sharp-eyed, Yao Wenbin noticed that the one companion who set Soong May-ling at ease was none other than Xiong Xianghui.

In his work Yao Wenbin sometimes crossed paths with Chen Zhongjing and Xiong Xianghui, and knew that these two held many of Hu Zongnan's secrets, only he could not be sure whether they too had been sent from Yan'an.

Several of Xi'an's intelligence men were planted deep—deep into the secret services themselves. During the war of resistance the Communists adjusted their guideline for secret work, calling on underground members to make themselves "socialized, professionalized, partified"; and Xi'an went a step deeper still, achieving "agent-ification."

When Zhongtong got wind of Wang Shijian's intelligence radio, Chen Zhongjing stepped in to explain that it was a secret-service radio of his own Youth League. From then on Wang Shijian moved through the Xi'an secret-service world under the cover of a Nationalist agent.

In May 1943 the Comintern announced its dissolution, Chiang Kai-shek stirred up an anti-Communist high tide, and Hu Zongnan was ordered to make a surprise strike at the pouch-shaped Guanzhong sub-district. At the critical moment Xiong Xianghui got secret word to Yan'an in advance.

Yan'an had no troops to spare just then; Mao Zedong had sent the guard units off to Shanxi to fight the Japanese.

He played the Empty City Stratagem at full voice. The Party Center openly exposed Hu Zongnan's plan of attack and denounced the Nationalists for wrecking the united resistance. Under the pressure of national opinion Hu Zongnan had to call the surprise attack off.

But this put Xiong Xianghui in danger of exposure.

As a rule, when intelligence material is used, it is altered to protect the source. But this time the military situation was urgent, and the Party Center had no choice but to use the material outright. That let Hu Zongnan narrow the leak to a very small circle, and someone had already informed on Xiong Xianghui as being in league with the bandits.

Luckily, it came out at the same time that two Nationalist organs had openly called for the Communists to be dissolved, and Xiong Xianghui kept his composure, and so Hu Zongnan's attention was turned aside.

The Xi'an intelligence network was cleverly woven: not only a three-line deployment in layers, but different networks by category.

As the Communists' open organ in Xi'an, the Eighth Route Army office was already under tight Nationalist watch, and Lin Boqu had to keep a second card in hand: he set the office's transport chief, Wang Chaobei, to building a secret organization.

Wang Chaobei was a Shaanxi native, and in 1925 was secretary of the Communist Yan'an special branch. He had served as a divisional Party representative in the National Army, had many connections in the Northwest Army, and had secretly shipped radios and other supplies to the Red armies of northern Shaanxi and northern Sichuan. In Xi'an there stood the "Zhiyuan," once the mansion of General Yang Hucheng; after Yang went abroad, the Xi'an field-headquarters director Xiong Bin took over the front courtyard, while the rear courtyard was kept by Yang Hucheng's aide Bai Junsheng. Bai Junsheng was on good terms with Wang Chaobei's intelligence man Qin Zhi'an, and through this connection Wang Chaobei built a Communist secret radio right in the rear courtyard of the Nationalist field-headquarters director.

Wang Chaobei's line later grew into the "Xi'an Intelligence Office," answering directly to the Central Intelligence Department and reaching deep into the Xi'an Nationalist secret services for intelligence. This secret radio changed its address eight times in Xi'an, yet never lost its secret wireless link with Yan'an. Practice proved that this networked-in-parts deployment made the intelligence work more reliable: during the War of Liberation, when Wang Shijian's system was broken, Wang Chaobei's still ran on, keeping the Center's channel open.

Xi'an's network grew and grew. By 1943 the cadres of the Xi'an Intelligence Office had grown to more than 20, with intelligence ties spread through the core organs—the Party headquarters, the government, the army, the police, the secret services—and a grasp of the Nationalists' secret dispositions for besieging and infiltrating the Border Region. Hu Zongnan's every move in Xi'an lay within Yan'an's grasp. In a sense one could say that, to Yan'an, Xi'an had no secrets left.

### 6. Urgent Military Intelligence at the Front

In September 1941 the Central Intelligence Department was established. The Party Center and the Central Military Commission at once decided that intelligence organizations were to be set up in each strategic unit.

At this time the Eighth Route Army's forward general headquarters and its three divisions had not yet finished fitting out full-time intelligence organs.

The armies of the world have always had reconnaissance branches, generally as the second section of a headquarters—the first for operations, the second for intelligence—which shows how important military intelligence is to an army. The Eighth Route Army's senior organs were pared very lean, often having only a staff section and no established intelligence section. Now the Center and the Military Commission raised the standing of intelligence work greatly. So, rear or front, full-time intelligence branches were to be set up.

The Eighth Route Army's forward general headquarters was in southeastern Shanxi, and in October 1941 it established an intelligence section, with the Eighth Route Army chief of staff Zuo Quan holding the post of section chief concurrently (after Zuo Quan fell, Teng Daiyuan), and Xiang Benli as deputy; under it were four sub-sections—espionage, troop reconnaissance, technical reconnaissance, and demolition. The woman chief of the espionage sub-section, Lin Yi, had been secretary-general of the Central Social Affairs Department, and at the end of 1940 had brought a work team from Yan'an to the forward headquarters to found its intelligence organization. There was already the wireless technical reconnaissance run by Qian Jiang, and now this too was folded into the intelligence branch. The Eighth Route Army's 120th Division doubled as the Jin-Sui Military District, the 129th as the Taihang Military District, the 115th as the Shandong Military District, and each set up an intelligence section to match. By the division of labor, the forward headquarters' intelligence section handled strategic intelligence, and each military district's handled campaign and tactical intelligence.

The forward headquarters' intelligence section pushed forward actively: it soon put three classes of cadres through intensive training and sent them secretly into the cities and along the transport arteries of the occupied areas. The dispatch-section chief, Lin Yi, dressed as a man and slipped into Beiping, Tianjin, and other great cities, arranging secret placements on the spot; in Beiping, Kaifeng, and Zhengzhou she built 20 secret way-stations, sent in a hundred-odd people, and got 23 of them posted as officials in the puppet regime. Wang Yueshi served as a police-battalion commander in Beiping and held three armed units in hand besides.

The forward headquarters' placements showed real strategic vision: Xu Chuguang slipped from Shanxi to distant Nanjing and found a footing inside the Wang Jingwei regime.

The Jin-Sui Border Region held a position of great strategic weight: to the west it screened the Party Center's seat in Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia; to the east it joined the Jin-Cha-Ji Border Region; to the north lay the secret international corridor running to Outer Mongolia and the Soviet Union. The 120th Division in northwestern Shanxi formed an intelligence committee of the division commander He Long, the political commissar Guan Xiangying, the chief of staff Zhou Shidi, and the intelligence-section chief Zou Dapeng. Under the intelligence section were espionage, reconnaissance, courier, and research sub-sections. The intelligence branch here had one special task besides: it kept two dedicated book-and-press courier lines to relay newspapers and journals from the occupied areas to the Central Intelligence Department. Enemy and puppet publications from Beiping and Tianjin could reach Yan'an in a week at the fastest.

The Eighth Route Army's stations interlocked with the enemy's like the teeth of a saw, in constant danger of a surprise blow. The Japanese and puppet forces at Lan County massed 4,000 men to strike by surprise at the Jin-Sui Border Region headquarters at Xing County on February 4, 1942. The inside contact planted in the enemy camp, Ding Haoxin, got word a day early; the Jin-Sui headquarters moved in haste and broke out successfully one hour before the enemy could close the ring.

The Taihang Military District was the 129th Division. It had an advisory office in charge of united front work that had also handled intelligence, and later Shen Bochun became its intelligence-section chief.

The commander of the 386th Brigade and of the Taiyue Military District, Chen Geng, was an amphibious general: a Red Army commander who had graduated in the first Whampoa class, and once the intelligence-section chief of the Central Special Branch. As a military chief deeply versed in intelligence, Chen Geng took hold of intelligence placement from the start.

The Shanxi battlefield had two nations and four sides—the Japanese army, the Eighth Route Army, the central-government army, the Jin-Sui army—their fronts interwoven, all maneuvering against one another. The Japanese too dealt with the three Chinese armies by dividing them: they worked hardest at courting Yan Xishan's Jin-Sui army, and were most wary of the Communists' Eighth Route Army. Chen Geng, who had experience of getting men inside, planned to get men into the Japanese army—and to do it he would have to come at it roundabout, through the Jin-Sui army.

The 21-year-old Chen Huanzhang (Chen Tao) became an inside man, though for a time he did not know how to make use of it. He served as a staff officer in a Jin-Sui army guerrilla battalion, and unexpectedly his detachment commander, Liu Miao, led the unit over to the Japanese, so that Chen Huanzhang found himself trapped in a puppet force. Chen made secret contact with the organization, meaning to lead an uprising, but the military district's intelligence branch instructed him to go on lying low—for getting into the puppet forces fit Chen Geng's design for penetration exactly.

The cadres the Communists trained were honest and hard-working, and this one could write, so the Japanese soon promoted Chen Huanzhang to major-general commanding a puppet unit, the "Great Han Righteous Army." A major-general could hardly be without a household, and the great families of Linfen came pressing offers of marriage; Chen Huanzhang hurriedly asked the organization to send him a false wife, but the Taiyue intelligence station had no such special talent to spare. With no other course, Chen married a simple girl of fifteen.

As the puppet-army chief at Linfen, Chen Huanzhang was charged by the Japanese secret service with winning over Jin-Sui army generals. In this way the Eighth Route Army came to hold the roster of secret collaborators. Chen Geng valued Chen Huanzhang's post, and sent in more agents—Zhu Xiangli and others—to Linfen too, building the Linfen intelligence station around Chen Huanzhang.

The Japanese too made much of intelligence: every division had an intelligence squad, its chief always a Japanese, trusting neither Koreans nor Manchurians. In 1942, when the Japanese forces at Linfen were reshuffled, the newly arrived 69th Division was in a hurry to form an intelligence squad, and settled, of all people, on the Chinese Chen Huanzhang—because the man's intelligence gifts were extraordinary. On the urgent intelligence Chen Huanzhang supplied, the Japanese struck out overnight. In a small mountain village the quilt of the Eighth Route Army commander was still warm; they had missed taking Chen Geng by five minutes. Able to get the hardest-won Eighth Route Army intelligence, Chen Huanzhang thereby won the deep trust of the Japanese commander—who had no idea that all this intelligence had been designed by the Eighth Route Army commander Chen Geng, who meant to help Chen Huanzhang take over as intelligence-squad chief.

As Linfen's Japanese intelligence-squad chief, Chen Huanzhang held unheard-of power: he issued passes to Eighth Route Army couriers and slipped three comrades into the squad. In the autumn of 1943 the Japanese launched an "iron-roller" mopping-up against the Eighth Route Army in Shanxi, and the Linfen station got hold of their operational plan in advance, and learned besides the itinerary of an officers' observation party, and reported it to Chen Geng in good time.

Chen Geng arranged it himself, and Wang Jinshan directed the Hanlüe Village ambush, wiping out more than 180 Japanese officers at a stroke, among them Major-General Hattori and 6 colonels. Chen Geng's fighting gave too high a return on the cost, and Okamura Yasuji's iron-roller mopping-up came to nothing. The Yan'an Liberation Daily ran the battle dispatch on its front page, praising it as a shining model of the ambush behind enemy lines, and the Central Social Affairs Department and the Taiyue Military District cited the Linfen station by internal telegram.

Chen Huanzhang carried real weight in southern Shanxi, prized not only by the Japanese but coveted by the Nationalists too: Juntong secretly commissioned him as major-general commanding the Tongpu Advance Guerrilla Division of southern Shanxi, and Yan Xishan gave him the title of major-general adviser to the Jin-Sui army. And so the Communist intelligence man Chen Huanzhang came to hold three separate major-general's commissions.

The main force of the Eighth Route Army's 115th Division marched off to Shandong, the pivotal ground linking the New Fourth Army and the Eighth Route Army. Shandong looked across the sea to the Northeast, and could serve as a jumping-off place for retaking it; look farther still, and Shandong lay only a narrow strip of water from Korea and Japan, a future strategic jumping-off place for an Allied landing on Japan. Mao Zedong foresaw two possibilities after victory in the war: either the Nationalists would demand that the New Fourth and Eighth Route armies move to the Northeast as the price of continued cooperation, or the Nationalists would touch off civil war, first attacking Shandong in force to cut the New Fourth Army's road north. So the Party Center attached the greatest strategic importance to "holding Shandong."

The Japanese had garrisoned Qingdao and Jinan since the 1920s, their secret-service organs spread through the towns of Shandong, their claws reaching into every walk of society. The Eighth Route Army could scarcely find a foothold in Shandong; its base areas were tiny, "a single bullet could shoot clear through." The Nationalist die-hards squeezed the Eighth Route Army too, and in a surprise attack killed the Communist Lunan prefectural secretary, Zhao Bo.

The Shandong Military District and the various units of the 115th Division had branches that did enemy-army work and intelligence work together, operating in secret in the cracks between the enemy-and-puppet forces and the Nationalist army.

Looking to the long view, the Party Center specially set up a Jiaodong Liaison Department answering directly to the Central Intelligence Department, and transferred in the able intelligence man Zou Dapeng, once secretary of the Manchuria Youth League committee, as its head.

Of all China's resistance armies the New Fourth Army stood nearest the great cities behind enemy lines. Isolated deep in enemy country, its fighting environment was extraordinarily complex.

New Fourth Army cadres, long surviving as guerrillas behind the lines, were especially skilled at secret work. Its units thrust secret feelers into Shanghai, Nanjing, Hangzhou, and other cities; add the men the old Central Special Branch had left in Shanghai, such as Xu Qiang, plus Pan Hannian's network under the Central Intelligence Department and Liu Shaowen's under the Southern Bureau, and Communist intelligence work behind the lines formed a crisscrossing web, each part supporting the others.

The New Fourth Army's headquarters organ was pared to the bone: under the command headquarters was a staff section, whose Second Sub-section handled intelligence and reconnaissance, with espionage and troop-reconnaissance desks under it and, directly attached, an espionage squad, a reconnaissance detachment, and a radio platoon. The Second Sub-section chief, Ma Buying, was a veteran of the Northern Expedition, especially skilled at troop reconnaissance, and had once got advance word of Han Deqin's movements at the decisive battle of Huangqiao. The deputy chief, Wang Zhengming, was an educated youth who had joined up during the resistance, skilled at work in the cities behind the lines; while with the 6th Division he had slipped into the Wusong naval port at Shanghai.

The lean-staffed Second Sub-section ran four small classes to train espionage cadres, writing its own texts and finding its own instructors. Liu Panquan of the Shanghai Party's underground police committee had been a traffic policeman, and could introduce Shanghai's every trade and station, the callings of the underworld with their cant names—"gold," "han," "profit," "gang," "flying horse," "swallow-and-sparrow."

The headquarters lay in Huainan, next door to Nanjing, the radius of the base area no more than 150 li, forever a target for the enemy to surround and destroy. The Second Sub-section set up four intelligence stations with radios in the four directions of the base area, to watch the enemy's movements at all times. When the enemy struck by surprise at the Huanghuatang headquarters over the Spring Festival, the Second Sub-section reported the movement three days ahead, and the army chiefs brought up the 18th Brigade and drove the enemy off.

Across their wide territory the New Fourth Army's units fought each on its own, each division with an intelligence branch of its own, and some worked with great daring. The Tang regiment staged a false surrender, then pulled back out after entering enemy country, and dealt the enemy a hard, cruel blow.

### 7. Breaking Through the "National Defense Line"!

Work against Japan was the first-rank task of the war of resistance. When the Central Investigation and Study Bureau was established, it set up four branch bureaus in the strategic regions across the country, and the Jin-Cha-Ji Border Region ranked as the First Branch Bureau, whose main task was precisely to investigate Japan.

North China was the front line of China's war against Japan. Japan seized China's Northeast in 1931, and after six years of consolidating it marched into North China at the Marco Polo Bridge Incident of 1937, rousing China's all-out resistance. Making the Northeast the strategic rear of its invasion of China, Japan propped up a "Manchukuo," and set up a "National Defense Line" along the Great Wall, so that a Chinese crossing it needed a passport to leave the country.

The Eighth Route Army pushed behind the lines, its spearhead pointed straight at the Great Wall. Nie Rongzhen led a part of the 115th Division and opened the Jin-Cha-Ji base area at the foot of Wutai Mountain. The Central Social Affairs Department set store by Jin-Cha-Ji work, and specially sent the theater-department head Xu Jianguo with a 13-man work team to guide the work at the front. Nie Rongzhen was short of cadres just then, and kept Xu Jianguo on the spot to build the Jin-Cha-Ji Border Region social affairs department.

Across North China were scattered no small number of able intelligence men. Chen Geng and Wang Shiying had in turn led Central Special Branch men over to work in Tianjin and Beiping, and Nan Hanchen, Zou Dapeng, Chen Lei, Xie Fusheng, Zhu Jun, Liu Guanyi, and others had held on in North China throughout. At the very hour when the white areas across the country were suffering heavy losses, the North China Liaison Bureau actively opened up work among the Northwest Army, laying the groundwork for the Center's foothold in northern Shaanxi. North China was also the region to which the Central Social Affairs Department sent the most cadres. The first batch, in 1939, was Shi Guang, Su Yiran, Chen Shuliang, Zhang Youheng (Li Cai), Su Yumin, Sun Wendong, Zheng Dakun, Zhang Jiliang, Wang Jixiang, Yang Ning, Jiang Tao, Ren Yuan (Ma Yaowu), Wu Tong, and others, 13 in all; 1940 brought Li Zhenyuan, Zhou Meiying, Zhang Youheng, Zhou Shi, Wang Shuwei, Ye Chengzhi, Huo Yansheng, Ma Guangrong, Zhang Ziyu, Liu Shunfa, and others, 10 in all; and later came Wang Wen, He Changqian, Zhang Fa, Sun Yefu, Zhang Zhiming, Su Jianxiao (a Mongol), Wang Song, Shi Tuo, Lin Keming, Wang Dong, and others—more than 30 sent from Yan'an to North China in all.

Xu Jianguo was an Anyuan miner who had joined the Party in 1922, a two-gun general who could lead a charge; he had served as a divisional special commissioner in the Third Red Army Corps and as head of the security bureau of the First Red Army Corps, and in 1938 became head of the Central Security Department. Rich in security experience, Xu Jianguo actively opened up North China intelligence work and developed a fresh crop of intelligence strength.

The opening up of intelligence work aided the resistance fighting powerfully. Through Japanese-language translation Xie Fusheng grasped the Japanese army's operational dispositions, relayed them by radio to General Li Zongren, contributed to the victory at the battle of Tai'erzhuang, and won Li Zongren's commendation.

In the battle of Huangtuling the Jin-Cha-Ji Military District killed, at a stroke, the Japanese "flower of famous generals," Lieutenant-General Abe Norihide—and part of the credit belonged to the "Death-Hastener" (Cui Minggui of the Laiyuan station). The central Hebei plain was good ground for large-unit operations, and Okamura Yasuji launched the May First great mopping-up, seeking to encircle and destroy the resistance forces of central Hebei. The central Hebei social affairs chief Zhang Guojian won over the local Green Gang boss Zhang Huaisan, made contact with the puppet troops along the blockade line, and broke out cleverly.

The Jin-Cha-Ji Border Region social affairs department also built a string of intelligence way-stations. The Pingxi station was the earliest of the large stations, and there were bodies at Shijiazhuang, Taiyuan, central Hebei, and elsewhere. The eastern Hebei station, also called the Northeast Liaison Station, developed toward the Northeast along the Beining railway, and built intelligence ties at Shanhaiguan, Qinhuangdao, and Tangshan. The station chief, Ren Yuan, was gravely wounded and captured in battle, cleverly deceived the Japanese agents, and escaped through the organization's rescue.

By using the higher family backgrounds of some of its agents, the liaison group slipped successfully into the great cities. He Changqian's father-in-law was a great collaborator; Yang Ning's family were relatives of a Tongrentang branch—excellent conditions for cover. Chen Shuliang, arrested, met it with composure, and not only implicated no comrade but was released in the end.

The Beiping group of Wang Dingnan, in contact with Chen Lei and Xie Fusheng, made ties with such prominent public figures as the Yenching University professor Zhang Dongsun and the acting mayor of Beiping He Qigong, and Wang Dingnan even had direct dealings with foreign friends such as John Leighton Stuart and Michael Lindsay. Huang Hao was chairman of the Beiping YMCA, made a great many upper-level connections, and bought medicine besides for the Border Region.

Intelligence work not only seeped into the great cities of North China; it reached toward distant Nanjing and Shanghai.

Xu Jianguo sent in the Northeasterner Li Shiyu, who took his chance to enter the national congress Wang Jingwei convened, and went on to become chief of the military-law office in the Nanjing regime. Japan had nurtured three great collaborator blocs in China: Puyi's "Manchukuo" in the Northeast, Wang Kemin's "Autonomous Government" in North China, Wang Jingwei's "National Government" in Nanjing. Li Zhenyuan took the post of Shanghai liaison-station chief, developed secret organizations in the Nanjing-Shanghai area and elsewhere, and obtained the important intelligence that the three great collaborator blocs of Nanjing, Changchun, and Beiping were planning to merge.

The Center laid on North China one more important strategic task: find the Anti-Japanese United Army.

China's Northeast was, and remained, a piece of intelligence ground on which all eyes were fixed. Japan had seized the Northeast in 1931, and to mask its colonial deed had propped up a puppet regime. Rich in the experience of secret war, the Japanese secret service smuggled the last emperor of the Qing, Puyi, out of Tianjin to the Northeast and set up a "Manchukuo." This "state" was in fact no more than a puppet regime, directed from emperor to premier by Japanese advisers. Puyi had thrown in with Japan in order to restore the Qing, and was not content to be a Japanese lackey; the premier, Zheng Xiaoxu, was Puyi's loyal servant and did not wholly obey the Japanese advisers either. So Japan changed the premier for Zhang Jinghui. Zhang Zuolin, Zhang Zuoxiang, Zhang Jinghui—the "Three Zhangs of the Northeast"—held great power there, and the Japanese secret service meant to control the Chinese through great collaborators.

The Communists had long had an underground Party organization in the Northeast, the Manchuria Provincial Committee, and after the Mukden Incident had organized the Northeast Anti-Japanese Democratic United Army as well. When the Party Center moved from Shanghai to Jiangxi, its ties to the Northeast organization could only be relayed through Moscow. After 1938, when Wang Ming and Kang Sheng of the Communist delegation to the Comintern both returned home, the tie between the Northeast underground and the Center was cut entirely. The Japanese suppressed hard in the Northeast, the United Army suffered grievous losses, and after 1942 only a small part were left, forced to fall back into the Soviet Union. The Soviets kept these men in training in the Far East to be used one day against Japan, and would not report on them to Yan'an.

The Japanese imposed a tight control over the whole Northeast, and called the regional boundary between the Northeast and North China the "National Defense Line." The "National Defense Line" searched travelers leaving for the Northeast rigorously, so that the resistance's intelligence organizations inside the Wall could scarcely get into the Northeast at all.

Yan'an set the greatest store by Northeast work, and specially set up a "Northeast Work Committee," sending the former United Army cadre Zhong Ziyun to the Jin-Cha-Ji Border Region to find a way to open a secret line to the Northeast. The Japanese ran a tight fascist administration in the Northeast: the cities kept a strict household-registration system, newcomers had to register, and in the countryside they gathered the villages into fortified hamlets to strip the United Army of its popular base. Of the five picked cadres Zhong Ziyun sent out, some could find no underground contacts, some were sold out by turncoats, and some turned coat themselves.

And so "Manchukuo" became a solid sheet of iron, and the "National Defense Line" seemed impossible to break.

The "National Defense Line" could shut off traffic, but it could not bar ideas. To cultivate a pro-Japanese party among the Chinese, the Japanese authorities encouraged Northeastern youth to study in Japan, and the ministers of "Manchukuo" made it all the more the fashion to send their sons to Japan.

Against all expectation, an undercurrent of Marxism was stirring in Japan itself. National duty outweighed the lure of high office and rich pay: the commoner's son Hou Luo took the lead in organizing a "Social Science Reading Society." The secret Communist Zhang Weixian, studying in Japan, though he had lost his organizational tie for the time, stayed active, organizing Ding Yi, Chen Zhuoyi, and others to found in secret an "Anti-Imperialist Grand Alliance." In 1938 these two patriotic, progressive bodies merged into the "General Association of Northeastern Students in Japan for National Salvation," which used ties of native place, schooling, clan, kinship, and friendship to develop a crop of resistance men.

By 1940 the leading backbone of this organization had all returned home and set at once to finding the Communist organization, reaching Nan Hanchen at last through He Songting. Li Zhenyuan and his wife Zhou Meiying slipped into Shenyang and instructed the Northeast intelligence group to get inside the enemy and lie deep, its main task to obtain strategic intelligence, with no lateral ties between the different local organizations. Li Cai slipped into Shenyang, vetted the intelligence men one by one, and on returning to Jin-Cha-Ji made a formal report to the Center. From then on the Communists had a reliable intelligence network inside Manchukuo too.

The Communist Northeast intelligence group lay low for the long term and moved with care. When the Nationalists' secret organization in the Northeast was broken by the Japanese secret service and its five hundred-odd members all fell into the net, only two of the Communist agents were caught up in it, and the whole organization was preserved intact. This secret intelligence net grew to more than 70 members, centered on Shenyang, Changchun, and Harbin, and spread across a dozen districts of the Northeast.

The members of this intelligence group were mostly sons of high "Manchukuo" officials: among them the premier's son Zhang Mengshi; the son of the head of the Local Self-Government Guidance Board, Yu Jingchun; the son of the chief of Puyi's guard, Tong Zhibin; Kawashima Yoshiko's younger brother Xiandong; the son of the chief of the military-law office, Wang Cheng, and his nephew Wang Qian; the son of a brigade commander of the Harbin garrison district, Sun Wei. On their family ties they got smoothly into the military and civil organs and obtained a great deal of important intelligence.

The dispositions of Japan's Kwantung Army in the Northeast; the anti-war sentiment of the Japanese there; the strength of Manchukuo's army, navy, and air force; maps of the ports, airfields, and fortresses; maps of industrial targets; the economic state of the Northeast; the counter-espionage situation of the Japanese and their puppets—all of Japan's strategic secrets in the Northeast went to Yan'an. In the late stage of the war, when the Soviet army destroyed the Kwantung Army; in the War of Liberation, when the Communists seized the Northeast and launched the Liaoshen Campaign—both owed a great debt to this intelligence group.

Out of the collaborators' own citadel came loyal sons of the nation.

Mao Zedong was elated: "'Manchukuo' is not a solid sheet of iron; the 'National Defense Line' can be broken through!"

### 8. Challenging the Intelligence Powers

Japan was China's number-one enemy, an enemy not only militarily strong but a great intelligence power.

Could the young Chinese intelligence men, then, outfight the seasoned Japanese agent?

As for personal ties, the Nationalists' with Japan ran long and deep: from Sun Yat-sen to Chiang Kai-shek they had all studied in Japan. In the great Sino-Japanese war the leading generals on both sides—He Yingqin and Yan Xishan, and Okamura Yasuji and Itagaki Seishirō—turned out to be classmates from the Japanese military academies.

Chiang Kai-shek set great store by intelligence work against Japan. Zhongtong and Juntong, whose opponents had once been the Communists and the regional powers at home, now turned toward the foreign enemy too.

Both Zhongtong and Juntong had a Shanghai district, the largest local organization under headquarters. Japan's headquarters for the invasion of China was at Nanjing, where there stood not only the Wang Jingwei regime but the general headquarters of the Japan China Expeditionary Army; and yet Japan's espionage center was at Shanghai—the Ume Kikan and No. 76 both in the foreign concessions of Shanghai—so that this Shanghai was the "spy heaven" of China and indeed of the Far East.

Zhongtong had worked the Shanghai scene since 1928, and was especially good at arresting underground Party members. Adept in civil war, inept in foreign war: by the resistance years the key Zhongtong men Ding Mocun and Li Shiqun had both gone over to Japan and become the secret-service heads of the Wang Jingwei regime. With these turncoats who knew it inside out now running No. 76, Zhongtong's Shanghai district was squeezed until it had nowhere to hide.

This was where Juntong came in. Juntong's standing in Shanghai was more recent—its forerunner, the Blue Shirts' Special Services Department, was founded after the January 28 Incident of 1932—but, being soldiers, Juntong men fought outward more boldly. Dai Li decided to set the front line of work against Japan in Shanghai and establish a special "Shanghai district."

Juntong's field units numbered well over a hundred. Elsewhere they were called stations; a local body like the Beiping station or the Tianjin station generally had only twenty or thirty men. But Shanghai was called a "district," and the "Shanghai district" had a thousand men.

This was because the Shanghai district lay at the heart of the secret war against Japan, and its operational tasks were especially many. Given special weight, the Shanghai district had the best working conditions too. For a local body, it had nearly all the functions of Juntong's own headquarters—intelligence, action, subversion, counter-espionage, psychological warfare, political warfare, youth work, labor work, technical research, liaison, and the rest—all that Chongqing had, Shanghai had. In the foreign concessions the Shanghai district kept 3 secret command centers, 22 secret way-stations, 3 wireless stations, and a technical room for developing and storing demolition materiel; and the bureau's headquarters kept a "chief accountant" in Shanghai to release funds at any time and keep the district amply supplied.

Many though the Shanghai district's tasks were, they fell into two great classes: the pen and the sword.

The pen was the soft units—intelligence gathering, counter-espionage, subversion—a hundred-odd men in 5 groups, no more than a tenth of the district's strength.

The sword was the units devoted to action and sabotage: assassination, bombing, poisoning, kidnapping. This force was quite large, 8 detachments each with dozens of men.

For all the talk of a pen and a sword, the pen won little, while the sword made a stir across Shanghai. One collaborator agent after another, one Japanese soldier or policeman after another, dropped dead in the street, cut down by Juntong's action men.

Alas, the action men's opponents were action men too. The chief of No. 76, Li Shiqun, had in his early days been a member of the Communist Special Branch's dog-beating squad, and had trained under the Cheka in the Soviet Union. Li Shiqun had once been arrested in an operation and put to Juntong's severe torture, which planted a deep hatred. Chen Gongshu tried to kill Li Shiqun, Li Shiqun hunted Chen Gongshu, and Juntong and No. 76 fought to the death across Shanghai, making many a bloody case.

Chen Gongshu mounted more than two hundred operations of action and sabotage in Shanghai, yet these operations could not stop Wang Jingwei from going over to the enemy, could not cow Ding Mocun from his treason, and only brought down a crushing blow in return. To kill one had not warned a hundred; it only turned kin and comrades away. No sooner had he killed the French Concession police inspector Lu Haitao than the Juntong Shanghai district's action-group leader, Wan Lilang, secretly went over to the enemy.

Juntong, which prized action, could not obtain the Japanese army's operational dispositions, could not grasp Japan's strategic intentions, and was, on the contrary, lured and deceived by Japanese agents. In the great midnight round-up across Shanghai on October 29, 1941, Chen Gongshu was arrested in the French Concession, and Juntong's Shanghai district was all but wiped out.

The Communists' intelligence work against Japan took another road—deep into the tiger's den.

Pan Hannian, that former head of the Central Special Branch, went in person to reconnoiter behind the lines. The Party Center promoted Pan Hannian to deputy head of the Central Social Affairs Department, charged with intelligence work against Japan and its puppets. For an intelligence chief of Pan Hannian's rank to go deep onto the front line for the long term was, in the international intelligence world, all but unheard of.

To reconnoiter Japan one could not merely sit in the base area and gaze; one had to go deep into the enemy's array and reconnoiter on the spot. Pan Hannian's main ground of activity was Hong Kong and Shanghai. Hong Kong was a free port under British rule, where not only goods but people passed in and out untaxed and without visas, so that the international business world called it "the adventurer's paradise" and the international intelligence world called it "spy heaven." Communists, Nationalists, Soviets, Americans—the intelligence services of every side showed their paces there.

Hong Kong had an Eighth Route Army office too—the Eighth Route Army's Hong Kong office, run by Liao Chengzhi. The Northeast Anti-Japanese Democratic United Army also kept an office in Hong Kong, with Dong Lin'ge running an intelligence post. The Northeast United Army office and the Eighth Route Army office worked together, and the two exchanged their intelligence. The Soviet intelligence post in Hong Kong was run by the Chinese Zhu Ming, with members such as Jin Zhonghua and Shao Zonghan; this post cooperated laterally with the Eighth Route Army office's post and exchanged intelligence with it at set intervals. The president of the National Government's Executive Yuan, H.H. Kung, kept an intelligence post in Hong Kong too, run by Hu Egong, and though he had no system of exchange with the Communists there was frequent traffic all the same. Hu Egong had once been a Special Branch contact, often sent intelligence to Pan Hannian of his own accord, and even introduced H.H. Kung's wife, Soong Ai-ling, to Pan Hannian to discuss Nationalist-Communist cooperation.

Pan Hannian set up his points and wove his net in Hong Kong, with many connections. The Party Center decided to establish a South China Intelligence Bureau, with Pan Hannian in overall charge of the various intelligence teams. To run the bureau's inner office and its confidential work he needed a trusty steward, and Pan Hannian brought in a mysterious figure—"the Old Master." In the Central Special Branch Chen Yun's nickname was "Mister," Kang Sheng's was "the Boss," Pan Hannian was no more than "the Young Master"—and now this man was "the Old Master"?

The Old Master's real name was Zhang Weiyi. In the Special Branch he had managed the inner office and confidential work, and later served as head of the Central Secretariat. Arrested in February 1935, he was not released until the war of resistance broke out. Once in Hong Kong, Zhang Weiyi was given heavy responsibility again, and built a little team around himself: Chen Manyun for outside liaison, Mei Li for deciphering, Gao Zhi'ang for wireless. Chen Manyun had just married the film director Cai Chusheng, and Mei Li joined Zhang Weiyi in "living the safe house."

To "live the safe house" was for several underground members to live together posing as kin, using the identity of ordinary residents to cover a Communist underground station. Mei Li had once lived in Gong Yinbing's home, playing the "daughter," using Shanghainese to cover for a "father" with a Hunan accent in dealing with outsiders. The "Old Master's" work in Hong Kong called for a high polish, and Mei Li, who came from a great city, was just the person for it.

Old Lady Qin, a woman in her thirties, brought the eighteen-year-old Mei Li to rent a room on the fourth floor of a wooden building in a poor Hong Kong street, and they lived there under cover as mother and daughter. This humble household often received Pan Hannian, Liao Chengzhi, Zhang Weiyi, and other callers. A band of intelligence experts held forth on the great affairs of China and the world, and Mei Li listened at one side in admiration. Later she took over the deciphering work too, and only then made out the substance of the intelligence—materials of every kind on Japan, and the anti-war currents of international society. New callers came—the eldest aunt and the sixth aunt. The eldest aunt was Chen Manyun; the sixth aunt was a middle-school headmistress whose father was a famous Hong Kong industrialist. Two such splendid women, coming and going plainly dressed to Mei Li's shabby lodging, made Mei Li feel all the more the allure of the revolution.

One day, when Mama Qin had gone out to buy food and Mei Li was deciphering in the back room, six Hong Kong British police burst in and searched about without asking a reason. Mei Li kept her composure as best she could, would not so much as glance at the drawer that held the secret telegrams, and had already prepared her heart to die for the revolution.

Mei Li lived the safe house for a long time with Zhang Weiyi, twenty-nine years her senior; at first she looked on Zhang Weiyi as an elder, but feeling grew between them, and they became a true husband and wife.

Hong Kong was a fine place to obtain intelligence, but this "island paradise" lay far from the country's political center, and one could not reach directly into the enemy's inner workings. So Pan Hannian slipped back to Shanghai, his target—the Wang Jingwei regime.

For the titles of "the adventurer's paradise" and "spy heaven," Shanghai's claim was harder still than Hong Kong's. It had had foreign concessions from the day the port opened, and had always been a place where dragons and fish were jumbled together. Shanghai at this hour was the very foremost secret-service strongpoint of Japan and Wang Jingwei.

Japan's secret-service system was quite large: the cabinet, the foreign ministry, the army, the navy, the gendarmerie, the South Manchuria Railway Company each built a secret-service organization of its own. After seizing Shanghai, the Japanese resolved on unified leadership for greater efficiency, and on August 22, 1939, set up in Shanghai a secret-service organ governing the whole of Central China, the "Ume Kikan." The "Ume Kikan" answered directly to the Japanese cabinet and army ministry, and its first chief was Lieutenant-General Kagesa Sadaaki. It was this man who plotted Wang Jingwei's defection, who went in person to Hanoi in Vietnam to bring Wang Jingwei to Nanjing, and who then served as the supreme military adviser of the Wang Jingwei regime. Besides the Ume Kikan, the Japanese deputy consul-general in Shanghai, Iwai Eiichi, also ran a foreign-ministry secret-service organ—the "Iwai Kōkan."

The Wang Jingwei regime's "Secret Service General Headquarters" was set up in Shanghai too. The former Nationalist Juntong agent Ding Mocun was its director, and the former Communist Li Shiqun its deputy director. This general headquarters, at No. 76 Jessfield Road, made men in Shanghai pale at the mention of it, and went by the byname "No. 76."

Though the Japanese army had seized Shanghai, it still left the British, French, and other concessions standing. The relatively independent Shanghai International Settlement hid the intelligence organizations of Britain, France, America, the Soviet Union, the Comintern, and the Communists, and swarmed with spies of every shade—a battlefield of intelligence without the smoke of gunpowder.

At Yan'an the Central Intelligence Department studied the Shanghai situation in detail, and judged that the head of the Wang Jingwei Secret Service General Headquarters, Li Shiqun, was a man who could be won over.

Li Shiqun had studied in the Soviet Union, and later worked in the Communist Special Branch's dog-beating squad. Arrested in 1932, he confessed and then served as an agent of the Investigation Section's Shanghai district. The Special Branch head Pan Hannian went in person to warn him, and Li Shiqun promised to go on working for the Party. In May 1933 the dog-beating squad assassinated the Shanghai police inspector Chen Qing, and Li Shiqun was arrested on suspicion. Held for two years and put to every torture, Li Shiqun buried a deep hatred of Juntong. Before the fall of Shanghai, Juntong kept Li Shiqun on as head of an underground secret-service squad. Li Shiqun instead threw in with Japan, and became the head of the Wang Jingwei secret service.

Li Shiqun was an opportunist for whom whoever fed him was his mother; he had worked in turn for the secret services of the Communists, the Nationalists, Japan, and Wang Jingwei, wanting to keep a line of retreat open on every side. He still kept a secret tie with the Nationalist Zhongtong, and tried to make contact with the Communists too. In the autumn of 1939 Li Shiqun, through a connection, proposed placing Hu Xiufeng at his side as liaison with the Communists. When Li Shiqun had been arrested earlier, his pregnant wife Ye Jiqing had lived in Hu Xiufeng's home, and Li Shiqun and his wife looked on the Hu family as the saviors of their lives.

Hu Xiufeng and her husband were secret Communists too, but at that time were engaged in work among the Nationalist upper crust and could not be spared just then. So Pan Hannian thought of Hu Xiufeng's elder sister, Guan Lu.

Guan Lu's original name was Hu Shoumei; she lost her father at eight and her mother at fifteen, and the two sisters took shelter with relatives. Bright and gifted, Guan Lu published her first work at twenty-one. She took an active part in the resistance activities of the Shanghai League of Left-Wing Writers, joined the Communist Party in 1932, and served as deputy propaganda chief of the Shanghai Women's Anti-Japanese Anti-Imperialist Grand Alliance. She published a book of poems, Songs over the Pacific, and wrote the theme song "In the Spring" for the film Crossroads, which the whole of Shanghai, young and old, went about humming: "Spring comes and a hundred flowers are fragrant, lang-li-ge-lang li-ge-lang li-ge-lang…"

Ding Ling, Guan Lu, and Eileen Chang were together called "the three talented women of Chinese letters." Ding Ling went to Yan'an, to write and fight on the front line of resistance. Eileen Chang stayed in the Shanghai concessions, to fall in love and write novels. And Guan Lu was given the secret task of turning Li Shiqun.

For a single woman to turn herself into a spy—how great a pressure to bear. For a famous woman poet to be reviled by the cultural world as a "collaborator scribbler"—how great a pain to endure.

Pan Hannian had a saying: for an intelligence officer, to give up one's life is not the hard thing; harder is to destroy one's own good name.

Getting into No. 76 was not enough. The Japanese agents did not wholly trust the collaborators; the most secret intelligence stayed always in Japanese hands, unknown even to Li Shiqun. So Pan Hannian turned his gaze on the Japanese intelligence organs as well.

Japan had put much effort into intelligence work in China. As far back as 1905 it founded in Shanghai an "East Asia Common Culture Academy," to raise Japanese "China hands" in a Chinese-language setting—in fact a spy school.

Iwai Eiichi, a graduate of that academy, took the post of Japanese deputy consul-general in Shanghai. He often showed a "leftish" face and made friends with many progressive Chinese men of letters. In Shanghai Iwai built a foreign-ministry intelligence organization whose task, unlike the other military-intelligence organs, was not action but the specialized gathering of China's strategic intelligence.

One of Iwai's intelligence men, however, came to Pan Hannian of his own accord. The international intelligence world has always spoken of double agents, but this Yuan Shu was a marvel of many identities at once.

Yuan Shu was a left-wing man of letters, brought into the Special Branch by Pan Hannian. By the organization's arrangement he used a native-place tie to get into the "Gan She" of the Nationalist secret-service boss Wu Xingya, while at the same time drawing Iwai's intelligence stipend—one man in three roles. Later Yuan Shu transferred to work for the Comintern's Far Eastern intelligence bureau, was arrested when a turncoat sold him out, and gave up the Communist contacts Xia Yan and Wang Ying. Later still he was got out of prison through his Japanese connection, and when he sought the Communist organization again, he was not taken back.

When the resistance broke out and every side set up intelligence posts in Shanghai, Yuan Shu was more sought-after than ever: a central committeeman of the Wang Jingwei regime, deputy propaganda chief, education commissioner of Jiangsu Province, commander-in-chief of a column of the Loyal and Righteous National Salvation Army, head of Iwai Eiichi's "Asia Development and National Reconstruction Movement Committee," a Nationalist Juntong major-general… And yet, for all his moving among the many spy organs, Yuan Shu still wanted to return to the Communist ranks.

Pan Hannian made a bold decision, and through Yuan Shu made contact with Iwai Eiichi. In Hong Kong a magazine, the Twentieth Century, was founded. Every half-month the representative of this intelligence post, Chen Manyun, supplied one report; every month Iwai Eiichi's representative, Koizumi Seiichi, supplied two thousand yuan in funds. In this way false intelligence compiled by the Communist intelligence organs flowed steadily into the Iwai Kōkan, and the funds of the Japanese intelligence organ flowed steadily into the Communists' South China Intelligence Bureau.

This intelligence line drew the notice of Japan's supreme intelligence organ in China, the "Ume Kikan," too. Kagesa Sadaaki went out of his way to give a banquet for Pan Hannian—under the alias "Hu Yueming"—with Iwai and Yuan Shu keeping them company. At the Roku-san Garden in Shanghai, four top spies met face to face, all outward harmony, all inward daggers drawn.

Pan Hannian shuttled again and again between Hong Kong and Shanghai, laid out secret working networks in both, and even got Communist intelligence men into the Japanese secret-service organs.

Under cover of working for Iwai, Pan Hannian got Liu Renshou into the Iwai Kōkan, in charge of a radio on the top floor. When the New Fourth Army Incident broke, the Nationalists sealed off tight the news of their own slaughter of a resistance ally. Liu Renshou copied down from the radio the Central Military Commission's decision to rebuild the New Fourth Army headquarters, and put it out through the Japanese-run Shanghai press. One night, while Liu Renshou was working the radio on the top floor, a party of Japanese naval agents suddenly burst in and copied down the set's call sign and wavelength. It showed that the Communist intelligence man planted deep in that den of demons was himself under the other side's close watch.

Through the Iwai Kōkan connection Pan Hannian obtained internal intelligence from the Japanese foreign ministry. When the ministry decided to negotiate a non-aggression pact with the Soviet Union, Pan Hannian reported it to Yan'an in good time.

The intricacy of the spy war is hard for an ordinary person to imagine. The Japanese intelligence boss Iwai Eiichi tried by every means to develop intelligence ties among the Chinese, never knowing that at his own side there were a number of Japanese gathering intelligence for the Chinese.

The Japanese foreign ministry had founded the Common Culture Academy to raise for Japan spies deeply versed in Chinese; against all expectation, it raised a crop of Japanese who loved China. The Chinese professor Wang Xuewen was in fact a Special Branch member, and among the academy's students he developed a group of Youth League members; Anzai Kuraji, Nakanishi Kō, Nishizato Tatsuo, Tejima Hirotoshi, Shirai Yukiyuki, and others founded a "Japan-China Struggle League," with twenty or thirty members. On graduating, these men entered, one after another, the Japanese secret-service organs in China.

On reaching Shanghai, Pan Hannian promptly reorganized the Shanghai intelligence apparatus, with Wu Chengfang as group leader, to guide this Japanese intelligence cell in its deeper work.

Nakanishi Kō held a post in the South Manchuria Railway Company; Shirai Yukiyuki in the general headquarters of the Central China Expeditionary Army; Tejima Hirotoshi kept contact with the military-attaché office of the Japanese embassy in China. Nishizato Tatsuo served as chief of the press section of the Central China Expeditionary Army headquarters, and brought Wang Jinyuan, at Wang Jingwei's side, into the Communist Party. Wang Jinyuan later served as secretary and diplomatic commissioner of the Wang Jingwei mansion, and so could obtain the high top-secret intelligence of the Wang Jingwei regime and of Doihara's "Kōa-in."

Deep into the tiger's den, the Communist intelligence men had succeeded in reaching into the very depths of the Japanese secret service.

The secret war against Japan was a transnational war, and how to win hearts in a transnational contest was a test, too, of how a directing headquarters "used its spies."

The Art of War of Sun Tzu sets out three principles for using spies: "Of all in the army, none should be treated with closer intimacy than the spy; none more richly rewarded; no matter kept more secret." Trust, treatment, secrecy—on all three the Communists did quite well.

For its important intelligence workers the Communists always had a senior chief guide them in person. Yang Du, who had once supported Yuan Shikai's bid to make himself emperor and later inclined to the revolution, was brought into the Party by Zhou Enlai himself.

Sometimes Mao Zedong guided a man in person. The left-wing Nationalist Hua Kezhi had plotted to assassinate Chiang Kai-shek, and his comrade Sun Fengming had braved death to wound Wang Jingwei. When the Nationalists put out a nationwide warrant for the assassins, Hua Kezhi, with nowhere to shelter, fled to Yan'an. On May 4, 1937, Mao received Hua Kezhi in person and said the Communists did not approve of assassination. The strength of an individual or a small clique, Mao held, could not overthrow an evil system. What to do with Hua Kezhi gave Mao pause. Nationalist-Communist cooperation was under negotiation, and if he kept Hua Kezhi at Yan'an, then the moment the Nationalists found out and demanded the wanted man be handed over, the Communists would be badly placed. Mao advised Hua Kezhi to go to South China and serve as the Communists' "messenger" (liaison) with Li Jishen and others.

Once in South China, Hua Kezhi worked under Pan Hannian's direction. From then on he moved among Japan, Chiang Kai-shek, and Wang Jingwei, obtaining much important intelligence. Hua Kezhi loved the secret calling, had ties on every side, and was brought into play at every critical moment, always to marvelous effect.

A spy can often keep contact only with his leader, along a single line. That is to say, a spy will hand over both his bodily life and his political life to a single superior. The man who takes up that trust of a life must first be worthy of the spy's respect and trust. On the Nationalist side, Zhongtong's Xu Enzeng chased women and Juntong's Dai Li lived in wine and pleasure, and the two, scrambling for power within the Nationalist Party, set the world in an uproar besides. On the Communist side, intelligence work had not only a stern organizational discipline but personal magnetism enough as well.

"The Duke of Zhou spat out his food, and the empire turned its heart to him." No money down, no insurance, no signed bond was needed; a single word from Zhou Enlai, and Hua Kezhi—not yet even a Communist—would give up life and home. The woman writer Guan Lu, famed across China, on nothing but Pan Hannian's word of honor was willing to bear the shame of lying low in a collaborator organ.

An army is kept a thousand days to be used in a single hour. By 1941 the hour to test the Communist intelligence men had come.

On June 22, 1941, the German army struck the Soviet Union by surprise.

War opened on the eastern and western fronts at once, and won great victories on both at once. Such a strategy showed the madness of the German army; such results showed the strength of Germany. And while men dreaded Germany, they could not help dreading Germany's ally—Japan.

By the terms of the three-nation German-Italian-Japanese military pact, Japan was bound to support Germany. So then, since the German army was driving east against the Soviet Union, the Japanese army ought to drive north against it, the two forming a pincer.

The international intelligence world turned its aim from Germany to Japan: when would Japan strike north?

Germany's intentions, that foremost riddle of the world intelligence world, had already been solved by the Communist intelligence man Yan Baohang. Now the great world contest of intelligence entered its second round—

Whoever could seize the strategic intelligence of Japan's intentions would be the super-spy.

Li Yue, "The Intelligence Talent Wang Xinchao," public-security historical materials of the Lanzhou Public Security Bureau. This account reveals one corner of the Soviet Party's intelligence activity in China.

Yao Lun, "A Strange Encounter in My Intelligence Work," in *Moments of History 1*, Masses Press. Yao Lun, who never became a "Soviet agent," entered the Communist intelligence and security service from that time, and after 1949 headed the pre-trial bureau of the Ministry of Public Security, taking charge of the interrogation in many major cases.

Liu Yilun, daughter of Liu Hekong; interviewed February 6, 2012. Liu Hekong lay hidden in Shanghai; arrested, he held out and gave nothing, and the enemy could get no evidence. Liu Wangshu and Ke Xianchang, *A Pictorial Biography of Liu Hekong*, Central Party Literature Press. During the war of resistance the Chinese Red Army man Liu Hekong was ordered to take Soviet Red Army intelligence training, and lay hidden in Shanghai as a secret radio operator.

Yang Guoguang, *Merit and Tragedy: The Red Spy King Sorge*, China Youth Press. China, the Soviet Union, America, Germany, and Japan all have monographs on Sorge, but each has its own emphasis, and the foreign works say little of Sorge's time in China. This book newly synthesizes the various findings and covers the ground more fully.

Yan Mingfu, son of Yan Baohang; interviewed November 20, 2000. Wang Yantang, former deputy minister of the Central Investigation Department; interviewed January 28, 2003. The newly established Central Intelligence Department changed its organization rather often, and later settled down.

Cheng Yonghe, *Seeking the Light: The Memoirs of Cheng Yonghe*. Cheng Zhaolin and Cheng Zhaoming, daughters of Cheng Yonghe; interviewed December 13, 2013. Cheng Yonghe's revolutionary career was the stuff of legend: he joined the Party in May 1927, lost his organizational tie three times, and joined the Party four times in all.

Yao Wenbin, former cadre of the Central Investigation Department; interviewed May 11, 2010.
