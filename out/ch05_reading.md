## Chapter Five. The Hour of the Goat, Second Half (2 p.m.)

{v} When the wooden box was opened, on the left lay a soft-leather ink-bag, and on the right, set into the case, a short writing-brush with an inch-long tip and a roll of coarse-edged paper. The whole thing had been made for the long-distance trader: with the box for a desk, a man could write on it astride a camel or on horseback.

{d} The third year of Tianbao, the fourteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Goat, second half.

{d} Chang’an; the place, unknown.

Several open, two-shaft baggage carts drove for a second time into this out-of-the-way depot; this time they carried, not round wooden casks, but rank upon rank of green-yellow bamboo poles, nearly a thousand of them at the least, standing up like a forest of spears. Each pole was three-year Xiang bamboo, about as thick as a man’s arm and some three feet long. Both ends of every pole had been carefully sawn into a round, tongued mouth, plainly meant to be fitted into something. And in the up-curved sterns of the carts was heaped a fair quantity of wet river-mud.

Along with the carts came a dozen-odd steppe craftsmen. Every one of them had heavy bags under his eyes and a worn look to his face, and as they walked they steadied themselves on the cart-sides, their steps a little unsure. They had been laboring overtime for days on end and had scarcely closed their eyes.

The moment the convoy was inside, several Wolf Guards took up brooms and went out to sweep the wheel-ruts clean nearby, then shut the courtyard gate.

Cao Poyan sprang down from the lead cart and directed them to draw up slowly along the edge of the loading platform. The whole of Chang’an was keyed up in the excitement before the Lantern Festival, and since this little convoy carried nothing that looked dangerous, it drew no notice at all.

Long Bo came over, chewing a mint leaf. He walked once round the carts, drew out a few of the bamboo poles at random and looked them over, then cocked his head to signal that they could begin unloading. The great doors of the warehouse were shoved creaking open, and out of it drifted a foul, stinging smell, as though something were being boiled down over a fire. The steppe craftsmen knew that inside was the very soul of the Que-le Huo-duo, and they burst into excited shouts, some of them even prostrating themselves on the spot.

The last stage of the work was about to begin. The Que-le Huo-duo was about to be made whole, its two halves joined into one, and no one could stop the destruction of Chang’an.

“Enough. Get it inside and assembled, quickly,” Long Bo ordered.

Several hands came out of the warehouse, all with water-soaked hemp cloths pressed over nose and mouth. First they passed the same cloths to the steppe craftsmen, then set about it methodically, lifting the bamboo poles down from the carts and carrying them bundle by bundle into the storehouse.

Cao Poyan stood with his arms folded, watching the whole business in silence. Long Bo came up beside him and clapped him on the shoulder: “By order of the noble Right Shad, your last task is to keep good watch here. Understood?”

Long Bo laid a deliberate weight on the words “last task,” and Cao Poyan slowly nodded. His crown-lock had been taken from him; that marked him out to be given up as a sacrifice within the walls of Chang’an, and he had long since steeled himself to it.

Still, there was a small grievance in Cao Poyan’s heart: at so crucial a moment the noble Right Shad did not come in person, but had instead set a man of Kucha over them to order them about. The Right Shad had said he had a more important matter to see to; but what could there be more important than the Que-le Huo-duo?

Long Bo took up a hemp cloth to cover his face and went into the warehouse. Behind him the great doors swung creaking shut again. What went on inside, no outsider could know.

Cao Poyan sat down slowly in the warehouse doorway, his back against a gallery-pillar, and drew from his neck that necklace of colored stones to turn it over in his hands. They were smooth shoal-pebbles his daughter had gathered by the Onon River, strung with her own hands on a cord she had twisted of white horsehair, into which she had worked three strands of her own hair and a single breath of her own. So bound, it was said, however far the two of them might be parted, their souls could still speak to each other. Cao Poyan’s fingers slid nimbly over every stone, the way a monk of the Central Plains tells his prayer-beads. The stones’ faces were worn glass-smooth, rubbed past all counting, and each time it calmed his heart.

The Right Shad had already cut away Cao Poyan’s crown-lock. By what the steppe shamans held, should he ever harbor a treasonous heart, then even in death his soul would be tormented in the underworld. But Cao Poyan cared nothing for that. What he truly cared about was no trifling thing like his own life, but whether the mission would come off, and whether the khagan’s will would be carried through.

Only one more double-hour to endure, and it would all be over. Gripping the necklace, Cao Poyan smiled for the first time.

Before long a banging came at the courtyard gate, in a rhythm of three short and four long, repeated four times. Cao Poyan hung the necklace of colored stones back around his neck, but did not hurry to open up; instead he climbed a raised platform nearby and looked out over the gate.

He saw Ma Ge’er standing outside the gate with two others, and between them, held fast, a woman of the Central Plains. Despite himself, his brows knit.

They had gone to abduct Wang Zhongsi’s daughter; that much Cao Poyan knew. But she was supposed to be shut up in the safe-house in Xiuzheng Ward. How came she to be brought here? And eight Wolf Guards had gone in all; how was it that only three were left now?

He quickly opened the gate, let them in, and shut it again at once. Then Cao Poyan seized Ma Ge’er by the collar and demanded ferociously, in Turkic: “What in the world happened?”

A little shamefaced, Ma Ge’er explained that a Tang agent had ambushed them, and only because they had arranged a line of retreat beforehand had they managed to escape by luck. To prove he was telling no lie, he fished out a bronze tally and a satchel. On the tally were graven the four characters “Jing’an ce ping”; in the satchel were smoke pellets, an oxsinew binding-cord, and a hand-spanned pocket crossbow. All of it had been taken off that fierce agent.

Cao Poyan took stock of them, and his face grew grave. These articles were the exact match of the gear carried by the soldiers who had earlier raided the Bing-Six depot, which showed they were the same crowd. It was a most dangerous sign: it meant the Jing’an Bureau had already unearthed the link between that safe-house and the Wolf Guards.

Cao Poyan did not dare in the least to underrate this adversary. The other was like a spider crouched in the heart of Chang’an: at the slightest touch upon its web, death came running.

One safe-house was no great loss; what mattered was how the Tang had come to know of it. Might the other safe-houses be exposed the same way? For all he knew, the Jing’an Bureau’s host was already on the road here. The Right Shad’s foolish, meddlesome scheme had brought trouble after all, and might well imperil the rebirth of the Que-le Huo-duo.

Seeing Cao Poyan’s face darken, Ma Ge’er hastened to curry favor: “We got Wang Zhongsi’s daughter out; we didn’t let them take her from us.”

Cao Poyan asked, “As I recall, you seized two women. How did you judge which was she?” A touch smugly, Ma Ge’er answered: “We only made it out after we’d brought them back to the safe-house. The scent on her is the stronger of the two.” With that he grabbed Wen Ran’s jacket and wrenched it roughly open to either side, baring the pink under-shift; Wen Ran gave a shriek, but her arms were clamped fast, and a wave of fragrance rushed up into their faces.

Cao Poyan looked Wen Ran over, then made a sign and ordered her taken for the moment to the well-pavilion a little way off, and went up to the warehouse. He knocked. The door soon cracked open; first the stinging smell came out, and then Long Bo thrust his head out impatiently, pulling aside the hemp cloth at his mouth.

Cao Poyan said the place was likely no longer safe, and they had best pull out at once. But Long Bo flatly refused: “This is the crucial moment of the assembly; we can’t move now. Are you certain the Jing’an Bureau has already felt its way here?”

Cao Poyan replied, “The safe-house in Xiuzheng Ward was just now attacked by the Lüben Guards; fewer than half of Ma Ge’er’s men got out. So you had best think hard: in your recent doings, has there been any oversight, any slip?”

Long Bo was much displeased. He was the man who had saved the whole plan, and here was this Türk, who had not even done his own part properly, picking holes in him: “Hey now, the Right Shad and I are partners, nothing more; I’m no subordinate of you Wolf Guards. Don’t cross-question me so.”

Cao Poyan lifted his arm to bar the way and pressed on: “Your lodgings, the people you’ve dealt with, could any of them have a link with that mansion in Xiuzheng Ward?”

At these words Long Bo’s face changed. In an instant a possibility had occurred to him, one that could on no account be spoken aloud. He countered: “That mansion is near Qujiang; it was a fallback point for the withdrawal. What were your people doing running off there just now?” The question struck to the quick, and Cao Poyan could only hold his tongue.

Each of them had a secret he could not tell, and so they came to a deadlock. Long Bo scratched his head and said helplessly: “All right, all right. This depot I arranged on my own; even if they trace it to Xiuzheng Ward, it won’t lead them here. There now, does that set your mind at rest?”

Cao Poyan’s arm still barred the way.

Long Bo looked him in the eye and sighed: “Are the wolves of the steppe all as suspicious as you? Here’s a thought. Off at the west end of this depot’s outer edge, below the flag-tower, there’s an infirmary. Dozens of idle beggars gather there the year round. Hire a few to keep watch about the place, and then, should any suspicious sort come near, they can warn you in good time.”

“Beggars? They do that sort of thing too?”

“For money, they’ll do anything at all,” said Long Bo. Then he leaned in and murmured a few words to Cao Poyan, after which he slammed the warehouse door shut again. The business of the Que-le Huo-duo would wait for no man.

Cao Poyan did not like Long Bo, but he had to grant that the man’s suggestion was the best to be had just now, and it solved the trouble of too few hands for the watch. Turning about with a mind full of cares, he was still casting about for how to find the beggars’ headman when he raised his eyes, and his fury flared at once.

He saw Ma Ge’er in the well-pavilion, astride Wen Ran and tearing at her clothes in a fever. Already in Xiuzheng Ward Ma Ge’er had been burning with lust; and having just dragged Wen Ran with him the whole flight, skin brushing skin and her fragrance in his nose, he had long been unable to hold himself in. Wen Ran writhed and struggled with all her might, but could not fend off the brutal assault; she could only weep and cry out “Papa, Papa,” begging for a rescue that could not possibly come.

Cao Poyan hauled Ma Ge’er up off the woman and dealt him a ringing slap. What a time to be at such things! Had the man no sense of what was urgent and what could wait?

His eyes gone red, Ma Ge’er howled and reached to grab Cao Poyan by the shoulder. Cao Poyan swayed aside and drove a fist into his throat, so that the pain robbed him of speech. Then Ma Ge’er remembered: when he had joined the Wolf Guards, it was Cao Poyan who had taught them the arts of hand-to-hand combat.

“The depot’s short of hands. The three of you, get in there and work. We’re one step from the Que-le Huo-duo. Don’t you idle out here and make trouble for me!”

Sullenly Ma Ge’er pulled up his trousers and headed for the warehouse with his two men. Wen Ran lay on the ground, gasping and gasping, her breast heaving, her hair-knot torn to a shambles. Cao Poyan bent to drag her up, but she braced herself, snatched a broken stone from the ground, and struck violently at his forehead. Cao Poyan had not reckoned that in such a plight the woman would still try to fight back. He dodged aside, and his foot flashed up and struck her on the wrist. The stone flew to the well-mouth and dropped with a plop into the water.

This time Wen Ran truly despaired. The killing air about this fellow was far heavier than that of the Bear Fire Gang riff-raff, or of that swine a moment ago. Rubbing her fiercely aching wrist, she watched the man slowly reach a hand into his breast, and closed her eyes in defeat.

But what Cao Poyan drew out was not a knife; it was a portable box of boxwood.

When the wooden box was opened, on the left lay a soft-leather ink-bag, and on the right, set into the case, a short writing-brush with an inch-long tip and a roll of coarse-edged paper. The whole thing had been made for the long-distance trader: with the box for a desk, a man could write on it astride a camel or on horseback.

Without a word Cao Poyan spread out the paper, poured the ink from the ink-bag and thinned it with well-water, then held out the brush. Not knowing what medicine he was selling in that gourd of his, Wen Ran would not take it. Cao Poyan offered the brush again and said, in halting Chinese: “You’re going to die. Leave your father some last words, or he’ll grieve for certain.”

The words left Wen Ran wholly at sea. What did he mean by it?

Cao Poyan knew that she would soon fall into the hands of the Right Shad, and that her end would be wretched past bearing. Yet the sight of Wen Ran weeping and crying out “Papa” a moment ago seemed to have touched some part of his heart, not the heart of a Türk Wolf Guard, but the heart of a father.

The woman was the Right Shad’s quarry; however his own heart might rebel, Cao Poyan could not disobey orders and set her free. All he could do was let her leave a few last words.

All at once it came to Wen Ran: these foreigners had nothing to do with the Bear Fire Gang; they had plainly taken her for Wang Yunxiu, and they meant to kill her. She cried out in haste that she was not the one, she was not the one, her name was Wen Ran.

But Cao Poyan simply did not believe her; he took it that the girl was only casting about for excuses, unwilling to accept the cruel fact. He drew the dagger slowly from his belt and, with a soft thunk, drove it into the ink-box, as much as to say she should stop struggling to no purpose and honestly write out the last words of her life.

Wen Ran bit her lip and gripped the brush once more, though tears welled uncontrollably from her eyes. To be seized twice over within two double-hours had worn her out in body and spirit, and now, driven to such a pass, she could hold out no longer. Exhaustion, terror, and the fear of death came upon her all at once and broke down her defenses.

She remembered the terrible thing that had befallen the Wen household the year before, when she had been as panic-stricken as now. Had it not been for her benefactor’s shielding her with all his strength, she would surely have gone mad long ago. A bitter sense of wrong surged up in Wen Ran’s heart: What have I done? I only wanted to live an ordinary person’s life!

Suddenly Wen Ran flung the brush far away and drove her head at Cao Poyan. His body rocked, but he did not budge an inch. Then she snatched a scent-pouch from her waist and hurled it at him; it burst against his chest in a puff of smoke. Cao Poyan caught her arm in a single motion and forced her down against the well’s edge.

Wen Ran broke into loud weeping.

Cao Poyan did not grow angry; he took it for a good sign, that her resistance was crumbling, like the gazelles of the steppe, which, once they know there is no escaping the wolf-pack, kneel down on their forelegs and bleat out their lament.

And so, keeping his temper, he bent down, picked up the brush, and pressed it back into Wen Ran’s hand. Just then a heavy, rumbling crash came from within the depot, as though one of the great casks had rolled down onto the floor.

Cao Poyan’s attention was drawn away by the sound for no more than a few snaps of the fingers; but when he turned back again, the pavilion and its surroundings stood empty, and Wen Ran was nowhere to be seen.

A dozen-odd constables roughly lifted the round lids from a row of wide-mouthed jars and thrust in the wooden poles they carried to give them a stir. The poles had been split at the tip into several backward-facing notches, and when they were drawn up from the jars the clefts hung dripping with a wet brown murk of oil.

This was fresh-pressed flax oil, still carrying its fragrance. Sunlight came slanting down through the gaps above the work-shed; inside, the seven or eight oil-presses had all been stopped, and the bare-armed pressmen stood off to one side with their arms folded, staring blankly at the constables’ search, unable to make head or tail of it.

Not far off, several ledger-clerks held greasy account-books and were checking off the heaps of rapeseed cake, turnip-seed cake, and bushel-measures of sesame at their feet. In the storehouse of the rear court, another party was counting yet more jars and urns, and did not overlook so much as the stoves where the rendered oil was cooked.

The owner of the oil-works came hurrying out, and at the sight of all this disorder flew first into a rage, only to be called aside at once by an official who murmured a few words in his ear, at which his whole manner changed, and he fell to nodding and bowing and scraping.

The like was happening at the same moment in a dozen-odd oil-works, of animal and vegetable oils both, across Chang’an. Whether they supplied the palace or the common wards, every one without exception was searched from top to bottom and required to produce a full account of its dealings over the past month. Some proprietors, presuming on their backers, tried to resist, and were put down without ceremony.

All these figures of transactions and stores were gathered up into the great hall of the Jing’an Bureau. There Xu Bin led some scores of reckoning-clerks bent over their sums, cross-checking the numbers against the gate-wardens’ customs records for oil to see whether they tallied.

“I report to the Deputy Director: there is nothing.” Cradling a scroll whose ink was not yet dry, Xu Bin made his report gingerly to Li Bi, who stood before the sand-table.

“Nothing of what?” Li Bi’s tone was none too pleasant.

“Within the month, every transaction of oil, animal or vegetable, greater than five shi, palace consumption aside, has been traced back to actual stock on hand; there is nothing suspect. Here is the list.”

“And the depots outside the city?”

“Oil declarations have always been listed as a category of their own at the gate-wardens’ office, and inspected with special care; ai, ai... there’s nothing out of the ordinary there either.” When Xu Bin grew nervous he was apt to stammer his “ai, ai.”

Li Bi’s face fell, and he flung his fly-whisk down hard on the edge of the sand-table: “Nothing out of the ordinary! Nothing out of the ordinary! Hmph, wait until the fires are up, and let’s see what you say then!” Xu Bin bent and lowered his head, not daring to answer, nor needing to. He knew that his superior was not so much rebuking him as venting his own feelings.

In truth it was not the Deputy Director alone; everyone in the great hall of the Jing’an Bureau was a little on edge. An inkstone knocked over by a careless hand, a foot slipping on the floorboards, a few faint sighs half-heard, the click of a tea-lid against the rim of a book, a scroll fumbled to the ground: such small mishaps began to come thick and fast.

Xu Bin knew this was the mark of too much strain. Ever since the hour of the Snake, the bad news had come one piece after another, each time doubling their workload and each time allowing less time to finish. When these clerks had been reckoners in the various ministries, their work had been measured out by the day or the ten-day; not like the Jing’an Bureau, which reckoned by the very double-hour.

Now the whole Jing’an Bureau was like a thing crouched atop a burning stove, fretful and restless, never knowing when some great blunder would come.

But what could he, a mere recorder, do about it? Xu Bin turned to look at the corner of sky beyond the hall, and could only pin his hopes on his good friend sending back some good news soon, to let these clerks, all but drowning in their counting-rods, draw a breath.

Just then Li Bi’s voice rose again, harsh and impatient: “Keep searching for me! When you’ve done the oil, go and search the firewood! When you’ve done the firewood, search the coal! And the hemp-stalks, the fodder, the paper, the bamboo and wooden wares, the silk and taffeta! Everything that can be set alight, search the whole of it through for me!”

To this impractical demand Xu Bin made no protest, but answered respectfully, handed the scroll to Tanqi, and withdrew with a bow. No joke: the Deputy Director was in the heat of his temper, and to cross him to his face would be plain suicide; in a while he would come round of himself.

It was, after all, the weather of the first month, and though braziers had been lit in all four corners of the great hall, one’s hands still felt the cold. Xu Bin tucked his hands into his sleeves and passed between the rows of clerks laboring with bowed heads, his ears full of the rustle of scrolls and the clack of counting-rods. Looking at these worn-out petty clerks, Xu Bin found himself unconsciously straightening his back, moved by a certain feeling.

Xu Bin’s memory was famous throughout Chang’an. He could overturn a game of Go near its end and then set every stone back, one by one. A pity that his career had never come to much; he had always been a petty clerk of no standing. This summons to the Jing’an Bureau had shown Xu Bin the first faint glimmer of a chance to rise. His title just now was Acting Recorder of the Jing’an Bureau; and if he could win some great merit and strike off that “Acting,” why, that would be a proper official’s rank, the junior eighth grade, lower class!

And so the more troublesome the situation, the readier the chance to distinguish himself!

A rush of excitement rose in him, and as he caught up a handful of counting-rods, Li Bi’s near-brutal order sprang suddenly into his mind: “Everything that can be set alight, search the whole of it through for me!” He turned it over, and all at once his eyes went bright, as though he had caught hold of some inspiration.

Xu Bin halted, thinking to gather a few clerks and go through the files afresh. But the words came to his lips, and he swallowed them again. Everyone just now was busy to the death; to have them spend their strength on a spur-of-the-moment guess was a bit too great a risk.

No help for it; he would have to do it himself. Xu Bin sighed, caught hold of a passing message-clerk, reeled off a string of catalogue numbers for him to fetch the files, then went back to his own desk, rolled up his sleeves halfway, and took up a fine-tipped vermilion brush.

I cannot charge into the fray as Zhang Xiaojing does; if I would win merit, the files are my battlefield. At the thought, Xu Bin could not keep his eager gaze from straying toward the Deputy Director, not far off.

But Li Bi took no notice of Xu Bin’s doings, and would not have cared had he noticed. His eyes held nothing but the great sand-table of Chang’an, as though, if he only stared a while longer, he might discover how those Türk Wolf Guards had smuggled fuel-oil into Chang’an without gods or ghosts being the wiser.

The water-clock in the corner of the hall dripped on, neither hurried nor slow; less than three double-hours remained until the lantern fair, and yet the matter had made no real progress at all.

Zhang Xiaojing, given his charge in the hour of peril, had not failed the hopes set on him, and had by a marvel dug out a thread of a lead; but in the turn of an eye the advantage had been lost. Just now both lines of the inquiry had come to a dead stop, which vexed Li Bi to no end. He was by conviction a Daoist, holding to stillness and non-doing; but ever since he had taken up this post, his whole state of mind had risen and fallen in turmoil, running clean against the Daoist teaching.

The petty affairs of the mundane world would indeed ruin a man’s Way-heart, Li Bi thought, restless and out of temper, and yet there was nothing he could do.

At that moment a herald rushed into the hall, his footsteps ringing on the flagstones, and everyone’s movements checked a little. Another piece of news had come; good or bad, it would set the mood of the whole Jing’an Bureau for what came next.

This time, though, the herald made no loud announcement, but walked straight up to the Deputy Director and handed him a letter. That meant the matter touched on secrets, which could not be passed by the watchtowers but had to be sent as a sealed dispatch. Tanqi, standing nearest him, watched uneasily out of the corner of her eye, and saw the young master tear open the seal and his face change abruptly: first flushing red, then iron-blue, and then shrouded in a grey, dismal pall, with even a small clenching of his fist.

How bad could this news be? Tanqi was at once anxious and curious.

What Li Bi held pinched in his hand was a secret report sent by Cui Qi, bearing but a single plain line: It is confirmed that the Wolf Guards have carried off Wang Zhongsi’s daughter; her whereabouts unknown.

Those Wolf Guards who had slipped the Nine-Gate Drum out of Xiuzheng Ward had actually gone and abducted the military commissioner Wang’s daughter as well?

Wang Zhongsi was no ordinary officer of the court. He was, no less, General of the Left Jinwu Guard, Area Commander of Lingzhou, and Military Commissioner of Shuofang, the most renowned general of all Great Tang just now, and one in whom the Sage placed the deepest trust.

In this campaign of Great Tang against the Türk khagan, it was Wang Zhongsi who directed the whole from the center, using his renown to hold the tribes of the steppe in hand and press the attack. At so critical a juncture, were the Türks to carry off his kin openly within Chang’an, then, quite apart from the court’s utter loss of face, it might well throw off the war north of the desert. Should the Sage then fly into a rage and the court be thrown into turmoil, even Li Bi, deep as he stood in the imperial favor, could hardly be sure of keeping the head on his shoulders; and the heir apparent, Li Heng, would be dragged in the more.

At the thought, a chill ran despite himself down Li Bi’s spine.

It seemed the strategy against the Türk Wolf Guards had to be revised at once. Even if their hiding-place were found, there could be no rash assault, for fear of harming the life of Wang’s daughter. The Jing’an Bureau was already hemmed in on every side; to add now one more restraint was frost heaped upon snow. But Li Bi had no choice.

Only now did Li Bi grasp the care behind Li Heng’s wish to have He Zhizhang serve as Director of the Jing’an Bureau. The abduction of Wang’s daughter could not be kept quiet for long; soon pressures would come rushing in from every quarter. Only an old hand at the world like He Zhizhang could deftly reckon out the coming shifts at court and make ready for them in advance.

At taking men he himself might have his knack, but in dealing with those crafty and ill-meaning political foes he was still far too green.

Li Bi thought: Am I to go and beg back in person the Director He I sickened with anger?

“Fetch some ice!” Li Bi ordered aloud, driving the unwelcome thought from his mind.

Tanqi stood blank where she was, and only when Li Bi gave the order a second time did she come to herself, and found it something of a puzzle. It was still the first month; who would keep such a thing ready indoors? Tanqi cast about, and at last had someone draw from the water-channel in the rear court a bucket of water shot through with ice-slush, which, once strained, she used to soak a brocade kerchief and brought over.

Li Bi snatched up the kerchief roughly and, without waiting to wring it out, dashed it, ice-water and all, against his face. The sharp cold was like ten thousand fine needles, stinging his whole face raw, so that he could not help baring his teeth. But his mind, all in disorder before, was thereby restored to clarity.

The more such a moment, the more one must master it with stillness.

Li Bi looked over the secret report again and set it against the earlier watchtower dispatches. He found that the hiding-place of the Türk Wolf Guards who had abducted Wang’s daughter was the very one provided by Long Bo, who had stolen the ward-map; which was to say, these two affairs were the work of one and the same band.

But to burn Chang’an and to abduct Wang’s daughter were things different in kind: the one a demented work of destruction, the other a cool taking of a hostage for leverage; the two pulled in very different directions. A good archer does not aim at two hares at once; a competent planner ought not, by rights, to pursue two mutually interfering aims at the same time.

Li Bi, his calm restored, caught in this a faint scent of something out of joint.

Perhaps here was an opening. The more the aims, the greater the difficulty. Only keep up the pressure on the Türk Wolf Guards, and it might force them into more mistakes, made to show more chinks in their guard.

Li Bi wiped his face once more with the ice-cloth and turned his eyes to the sand-table, seeking out that one grey piece unlike any other. Just now there was but one man who could help him.

“Where is Zhang Xiaojing now? What is he doing?” Li Bi asked aloud.

Zhang Xiaojing was just inside the Qixia Gate, and he was walking a dog.

It was a long-muzzled sighthound of the Hedong breed, with pointed ears and a narrow face, grey all over with white patches, its great black nose twitching rhythmically. Its legs were long and lean, and it ran with a springing strength; Zhang Xiaojing had to grip the leash tight to keep up with its pace at all.

To “borrow” this dog had cost no small amount of trouble.

The Dog Kennel of the Xuanhui Court lay in Tongji Ward, at the very south end of the eastern city, and reared the lapdogs and park hounds for the palace. When Cui Qi came to arrange the loan, the kennel-master refused flat out; they belonged to the Palace Domestic Service, and cared nothing for the good graces of an outer-court agency like the Jing’an Bureau. Cui Qi had been a little afraid of offending the palace eunuchs, but Zhang Xiaojing said coldly that to work for the Jing’an Bureau was to set aside all other scruples, and so Cui Qi could only grit his teeth and press on.

Cui Qi tried both soft words and hard, but the man would not give an inch. In the end Zhang Xiaojing stepped forward impatiently, leveled a crossbow bolt at the kennel-master’s head, and simply carried off one of the park hounds by force. At such crude, high-handed conduct Cui Qi could only smile bitterly. That kennel-master was already threatening to charge the two of them with robbing palace property; once the Lantern Festival was past, the whole Jing’an Bureau would likely be in for grave trouble.

But then again, if the crisis at hand were not resolved in time, they might not last out even the day. To slake a present thirst, a man must pinch his nose and drink even poisoned wine.

The hound was brought swiftly to the Qixia Gate, the last point where the Türks were known to have passed. Zhang Xiaojing had it sniff the fragrance Wen Ran had left behind and gave a whistle; the hound pressed its nose to the ground and worked it a few times, then its two ears shot up all at once, and it wheeled and bolted off to the west.

Zhang Xiaojing followed hard behind, holding the leash, and Cui Qi, Yao Runeng, and a company of Lüben Guards soldiers came trailing after, making an odd sort of procession through the streets. Passers-by stopped to stare, taking it for some Lantern Festival stunt got up by one wine-house or another.

The hound ran on at full stretch, halting at every crossing to sniff and take its bearings. As time went on, the number of its hesitations began to grow. By afternoon the lantern-watchers were gathering thicker and thicker, and the smells grew ever more mingled. Roast meat behind the ward-walls, horse-dung on the road, the shoulder-to-shoulder press of the crowd, the rank reek of camels, the fragrance of wine drifting from the taverns: all of it threw the hound into great confusion.

Each time the hound faltered, Zhang Xiaojing would draw out a scent-pouch, brought on purpose from the Wen Incense Shop, to sharpen its sensitivity to the smell. But before long even this trick was near to failing; the trace Wen Ran had left had grown too faint for even the hound to tell apart. That one thread, so near to nothing, was quietly breaking off.

Zhang Xiaojing urged the hound on hard, hoping to gain one more step closer before the last shred of fragrance vanished. The hound managed to run another stretch, and at last stopped at a crossroads. It lifted its head to sniff, gave out a whining sound, then turned about in place in agitation, pawing at the earth with its forefeet, but would go no further forward for anything.

Zhang Xiaojing sighed; he knew the beast had reached its limit.

By now Cui Qi and Yao Runeng had come hurrying up as well. At the sight of the hound in this state their hearts sank. Cui Qi, in a fury, dealt the dog a savage kick that drew a piteous yelp from it. He would have kicked again, but Zhang Xiaojing stopped him.

“Don’t stop me. This lazy brute needs a beating, or it’s always slacking off!” Cui Qi barked, beside himself. But Zhang Xiaojing crouched down, put an arm around the hound’s neck, and did his best to soothe it: “A dog’s nature is the most honest of all: it neither shirks and dodges nor lies to claim a reward. It has done very well; why be so hard on it?” He stroked the hound’s head, and there was, of all things, a note of tenderness in his voice.

“Is there anything to eat?” Zhang Xiaojing asked Yao Runeng, who quickly dug a strip of pork jerky from his belt. Zhang Xiaojing tore it into slivers and fed them to the hound.

Yao Runeng, watching from the side, marveled inwardly. This man’s way with a dog was that of a bosom friend, one heart laid open to another; yet in his dealings with people there was a strong air of keeping his distance. It seemed that in his eyes human beings were nowhere near so worthy of trust as a dog.

The task Li Bi had first set Yao Runeng was only to watch whether Zhang Xiaojing showed any sign of turning traitor; but from all his watching until now, Yao Runeng had come to feel a curiosity about the man himself. What in the world had he been through? What had forged him into a man of this cast?

Cui Qi had no interest in any of this; he cared for one thing only: “Commander Zhang, what do we do next?” Zhang Xiaojing did not answer, but looked all about him, first making out where they were.

The hound had run all the way west from the Qixia Gate, cutting across the Vermilion Bird Way, and had led them into the jurisdiction of Chang’an County in the western city, coming to rest at last at Guangxing-Anle.

The wards of Chang’an were laid out like a chessboard: at every crossroads, each of the four corners abutted a ward, and each of a ward’s four corners lay next to a crossroads. The people of Chang’an were used to naming a crossing by the two diagonally opposite wards at its corners, east first and then west, so that every crossing had a name of its own that was not easily confused. This crossing, with Guangxing Ward at its northeast corner and Anle Ward at its southwest, was thus called Guangxing-Anle.

It lay at the southern end of the First Street West of the Vermilion Bird Gate Avenue; one ward further south and one reached the city wall. Though the hound could make out no more precise a direction, that it could guide them to the broad region of the southern city was enough to let Zhang Xiaojing read the Türks’ thinking.

Chang’an was dense in the north and sparse in the south: the further north, the thicker the dwellings, while the wards toward the south were often broad and desolate, thin of people and rank with weeds within their walls.

Cui Qi’s eyes lit up: “I’ll muster the men at once and search every ward hereabouts from top to bottom! I don’t believe we can’t lay hold of those few bastards!”

But Zhang Xiaojing shook his head: “This is only where the scent breaks off; it need not be where the Wolf Guards are hidden. The Türks have too many choices in these parts.” He put out his hand and traced a circle in the air, taking in nearly the whole southwest corner of Chang’an; the fifteen or sixteen wards here were all relatively desolate, and it would be no surprise to find the Türks hidden in any one of them.

“As things stand now, we can’t beat the grass and startle the snake.” Zhang Xiaojing’s pace of speech slowed suddenly, and Cui Qi caught his meaning. Ever since the Deputy Director had learned of the abduction of Wang Zhongsi’s daughter, he had sent express orders: a hard assault like the one on the Bing-Six depot in the West Market was no longer possible. Whatever action was taken, the safety of Wang’s daughter had to be secured; caution upon caution.

“If only my elder brother were still here...” Cui Qi lamented, then, feeling at once that it was ill-put, hastened to explain: “He grew up in the west from a boy and knew the whole of Chang’an well. I don’t mean anything against you, Commander Zhang.”

“Which is why the Türks sought him out to draw their map, no doubt?”

“Mm.” Cui Qi’s eyes reddened a little, and he clenched his fists. His brother’s death had thrown him into confusion and set him blundering again and again; more than anyone he burned to root out Cao Poyan.

Zhang Xiaojing suddenly knit his brows, feeling that something was amiss, but the feeling flickered up and was gone. He shook his head and looked ahead together with Cui Qi; the sun was leaning a little westward by now, and the grey-white ward-walls stretching off into the distance ran on past where the eye could follow. Cui Qi flung his helmet to the ground in vexation; for the first time he felt that Chang’an was simply, maddeningly big.

The hound, chewing its jerky, was so startled by this that it darted to hide behind Zhang Xiaojing’s legs.

Yao Runeng ventured a cautious suggestion: “Could we not gather up the men of the watchtowers, the street-posts, and the ward-guards hereabouts, and see whether any of them noticed anything out of the ordinary?”

Zhang Xiaojing and Cui Qi sighed at the same moment, neither yea nor nay. The southern city was thin of people and slack in its street-government; the garrison soldiers were few and of poor quality, and to hope for any discovery from them was likely harder than getting the monks of the Ci’en Temple to break their vegetarian fast.

But the thing could not be left undone either, and Cui Qi at once put fifty Lüben Guards soldiers into motion, two by two, without weapons or armor, carrying only smoke pellets and horns, to go into the nearby wards and search for any trace or clue.

As for Zhang Xiaojing, holding the dog’s leash in his left hand and brushing the dust from his eye-socket with his right, he looked toward the several watchtowers nearby. It had become a habit with him; with cause or without, he would glance at the watchtowers to see whether there was any fresh news. But his feelings were mixed, for ever since he had taken this affair in hand, nearly all he had received from the watchtowers was bad news.

“One hopes for a bit of good now and again...” Zhang Xiaojing sighed, and, his fingers stroking the thick fur of the hound’s neck, said in a low voice one strange remark. The hound understood nothing of human speech and only barked once by way of answer. It did not know that, had any other human overheard those words, they would surely have raised a great uproar.

Daning Ward lay on the fourth street east of the Vermilion Bird Avenue, bordered on the west by the Yanxi Gate of the imperial city and separated from the Daming Palace to the north by no more than a single ward. Those who lived here were therefore mostly officials. Curiously, though every resident was of exalted rank, the mansions were nowhere near so lavish as those in wards like Anren and Qinren, being mostly seven-bay, three-courtyard houses with blue-ridged tiles. There was no help for it: the place lay too close to the Daming Palace and the Xingqing Palace, and one had only to have the Son of Heaven climb the wall and look down to see whose house was plain and whose extravagant.

Today being the Lantern Festival, when the Son of Heaven took his pleasure together with the people, the officials could not fall behind either. So the ward too was hung everywhere with lanterns and streamers, every ten households raising a lantern-wheel frame, though the whole gave off an air of constraint, the display of lanterns no more than middling. The lantern-watchers, therefore, were few, and the streets not so crowded as those outside.

Feng Dalun rode toward his own mansion, now and again giving way to the carriages great and small that came speeding past. In the shadows he was the boss of the Bear Fire Gang, lording it over Wannian County; here he was only a lowly junior ninth-rank recorder of the Ministry of Works, charged with the affairs of the Forestry and Crafts Bureau, and the proprieties that ought to be kept, he had to keep.

Small as the rank of a recorder of the Forestry and Crafts Bureau might be, its charge was nothing less than the repairs, dredging, and upkeep of the whole of Chang’an: craftsmen to be chosen, materials to be bought, works to be overseen; a post oozing with fat. Though of humble birth, Feng Dalun had a far keener eye than most. Using his office to advantage, he had fostered the power of the Bear Fire Gang; whatever could not be moved in the open, he had them tamper with from the shadows. The two working in concert, open and hidden, had all but monopolized the construction of half of Wannian County, and the profit was rich indeed.

Had it not been for that case last year, Feng Dalun would likely long since have won promotion and be riding high on the spring breeze. But no matter; the thing was past, and those who had made him uncomfortable had nearly all been cleared away.

Today he had run into Wen Ran, and the old grudge stirred faintly up again; she was the one person in that case who had escaped the miseries of prison. So Feng Dalun had sent a few of his men, resolved to deal her a small chastisement. The manner of the punishment did not matter; what mattered was that everyone should know that anyone who crossed him would pay for it, however long past the matter might be.

By now that little slut Wen Ran should be weeping her heart out, no?

At the thought Feng Dalun’s brow eased a little, and a sinister satisfaction showed at the corner of his mouth. He rode up to his own gate and was about to dismount when suddenly a man sprang out from behind a tree beside him, goggling a pair of bulging toad-eyes, and, grabbing at the reins, shouted: “Recorder Feng! Recorder Feng!”

Recorder Feng looked down and knew him for the death-cell warder of the Chang’an County yamen, and his look changed utterly: “How come it’s you?” The warder had plainly been waiting a good while, and said urgently: “Zhang the Yama, he, he’s left the death cells!”

At the words Recorder Feng near fell from his horse. He hurriedly righted himself and asked with a darkening face: “How did he escape?”

The warder’s face was a picture of woe: “It wasn’t escaping at all; he was requisitioned away by someone.”

“Requisitioned?” Recorder Feng ran swiftly through his mind the offices with power to requisition a prisoner: the Court of Judicial Review? The Ministry of Justice? The Censorate?

“No, it was the Jing’an Bureau that took him, seals and documents all in order; your servant had no way to refuse.”

“The Jing’an Bureau...” At the name Feng Dalun felt a vague familiarity. He cast his mind back over the Tianbao gazettes of the past half-year, and his gaze suddenly froze into two sharp needles.

“When?”

“More than two double-hours ago; I’ve been waiting here for you half the day.”

“What did the Jing’an Bureau requisition him for?”

The warder shook his head: “The document said only ‘as the Bureau’s business requires.’ But the moment he was out of prison they struck off his cangue and fetters, and when he left they used no prison-cart; he and the Jing’an Bureau’s envoy rode side by side, a man and a horse each.”

Feng Dalun’s hands gave a sudden jerk, and he wheeled his horse’s head about and raised his whip to go. The warder dodged hastily aside and cried: “You... where are you off to?” But Feng Dalun paid him no heed and galloped away back down the road he had come.

The warder stayed where he was, and only then did it come to him that this great man of Chang’an’s shadow-side, when he had gripped the reins just now, had had fingers that were faintly trembling.

Feng Dalun spurred his horse into a headlong gallop, south all the way, making straight for Jinggong Ward.

Jinggong Ward lay at the easternmost edge of Chang’an, hard against the city wall. The ward was of no small fame in the capital, for within it was a mounted polo ground called the Oil-Sprinkled Ground, built in its day by Yang Shenjiao, consort of Princess Changning. Setting aside those within the palace, this was reckoned the largest polo ground in Chang’an, and princes and nobles were fond of coming here to play at horse-ball.

The moment he entered the polo grounds he heard bursts of cheering from off in the distance. Passing through a slope of deliberately trimmed shrubbery, he came in sight of a broad earthen polo field below it. The field, of an earthy yellow, was about a hundred and fifty paces wide and four hundred paces long, its railings all round wound with colored silk. Along the sides stood ten-odd thick felt curtains ranged among the willows, and the house-banners inscribed with family names were set out here and there, each banner standing for one of the great and illustrious houses of the capital.

At the very center of the field, a dozen-odd riders in futou caps were locked in a tight tangle on horseback. Figures crossed and recrossed, hooves churned in confusion, and the little ball flickered in and out of the dust, bouncing to and fro. Suddenly a brocade-robed rider burst free of the press, raised his crescent mallet high and swung it with all his force; the ball cut a streak of molten gold through the air, drove straight through the goal, and struck heavily against the sounding-board. From the curtains all around rose the cheers of the ladies, and the rider, spurring his horse and lifting his mallet, made one proud circuit of the field.

This was the ball-match held as custom required on the day of the Lantern Festival, called the Spring-Opening Match. Behind the goal a brocade cloud-board was set up, and the ball too was changed for a gold-embroidered fortune-ball. Whoever first rode and scored was the golden dragon mounting the clouds, a very great good omen, and that year would surely be smooth and auspicious.

Just then came the clang, clang of a gong sounded from the corner of the field: the first half was up. The riders reined in, saluted one another, and then went each back to the curtains at the field’s edge.

Chang’an polo had a taboo about it. In Emperor Zhongzong’s time, the present Sage had once ridden too hard and crashed headlong into a swallow-terrace at the field’s edge, so that his beloved horse’s neck was broken and several young kinsmen were injured besides. From that time on, no stands were set at the polo field’s edge, nor any rain-shelter raised; curtains were rigged up for the occasion instead, for the ladies to watch from and for the riders to change and rest.

The brocade-robed rider rode back to his own curtained enclosure and leapt down from his horse. A page-boy came up beside him and murmured a few words. The rider first clicked his tongue impatiently, then rolled up his eyelids and said his horse had just run itself into a lather and could not be kept waiting; let the man cool his heels!

Feng Dalun, knowing that this Highness loved his horses as his own life, dared not press him, and could only wait at the field’s edge with hands hanging respectfully. The rider undid his mount’s tail-binding, tightened its shoes, and washed down its back, doing the whole round of care with his own hands, and only then came sauntering over at a measured pace. Several Silla maidservants came to change him out of his riding-robe and take away his futou. Feng Dalun hastened to bow and greet him, styling him “Your Highness, Prince Yong”; for this rider was none other than the Son of Heaven’s sixteenth son, Prince Yong, Li Lin.

For a business as great as his, Feng Dalun naturally needed backers to lean on, and Prince Yong was among the stoutest of the thighs he clung to. That case last year had begun with this Sixteenth Prince, which was why Feng Dalun had come hurrying to ask his guidance.

Prince Yong lounged aslant on a broad couch, took up a snow-cordial and sipped it, and said lazily: “Out with it, quickly; I’ve the second half still to play.” He had a hidden ailment, something wrong with his neck, so that he always looked at a man with his face turned aside, which left the other unable to read him.

Feng Dalun glanced to left and right, then bent close and said in a low voice: “I report to Your Highness: Zhang the Yama, he’s out of prison...” At the name Prince Yong’s wrist gave a shudder, and he all but dropped his drink onto the yellow earth, his face turning ghastly, as though he were about to be sick. The maidservant beside him quickly kneaded him a good while before he managed to press the nausea down.

“What’s this? Wasn’t he thrown into the death cells?”

Feng Dalun told him of the Jing’an Bureau’s requisition. When Prince Yong had heard it out, he rubbed his temples with his fingers: “And this Jing’an Bureau, what’s the story there?”

Knowing that this Highness took little interest in the affairs of the court, Feng Dalun explained: “It’s a new agency, set up only a few months ago, charged with threat-prevention against crimes in the western capital. Its Director proper is He Zhizhang; its Deputy Director is the Hanlin academician-in-waiting Li Bi.” Then he passed over a folded memorandum, on which were written certain veiled hints, so that this Highness might understand the import behind these appointments.

Prince Yong ran his eyes over it a few times with his face turned aside, and a troubled look came over his bronzed features: “So the Jing’an Bureau has a pedigree like that... Trouble, real trouble!” Fretfully he flung the snow-cordial aside: “That petty business of the Wen family, dragging on from last year into this! And still no end to it! I ask you, this Zhang the Yama, why couldn’t he just die and have done with it, quick and clean? Why must he make fresh complications!”

At the mere mention of the name, Prince Yong’s stomach began to cramp again. He hated trouble above all things in life, and that these base creatures would not die, one after another, aggrieved him beyond measure. Feng Dalun smiled slightly and said: “In truth Your Highness need not fret over this. The Wen daughter is already in the hands of the Bear Fire Gang; I fancy Zhang the Yama will not dare act rashly.”

“Ah, yes, Wen Ran, now there’s a fine woman...” Prince Yong scraped a finger at the corner of his mouth, showing a lecherous smile, then knit his brows a little: “I swore a solemn oath before the bodhisattva not to pursue them further. To do this now, is it not to deceive the bodhisattva? It won’t do, it won’t do.” Said Feng Dalun: “Your Highness knew nothing of it; it was the Bear Fire Gang that acted, out of righteous indignation. That is no breaking of the oath.”

Prince Yong was won over by this reasoning, thinking to himself that the Bear Fire Gang were understanding fellows after all, and his face eased greatly. Judging the moment about right, Feng Dalun spoke up: “Still, to leave Zhang the Yama loose out there is a bane in the end. Your Highness had best arrange soon to have him put back behind bars, and then rest easy.”

To deal with Zhang Xiaojing, official means were needed, and Feng Dalun was but a ninth-rank recorder, his rank too low; he could not do without borrowing Prince Yong’s power.

Sure enough, Prince Yong’s eyelid gave a twitch; the words had struck home with him: “And how would you arrange it?”

“The Jing’an Bureau drew Zhang the Yama out by way of a requisition, not an acquittal, so he is still a man under sentence. Best have a few censors of our acquaintance impeach the Jing’an Bureau for its improper use of a prisoner, as unfitting to its dignity, and force them to drive Zhang the Yama out.”

Prince Yong shook his head hard: “That won’t do. Censors are of the breed of mad dogs. Go to them for help, and I fear they’ll fix on me first, and it’ll reach my imperial father’s ears... tsk, tsk; I’ll not go courting that ill luck.”

The censors of Great Tang bore the charge of oversight and could impeach on hearsay. With nothing better to do, they kept their eyes on the offices and guard-posts of Chang’an, great and small. Wherever there was a lapse, they would pounce on it at once and bite down hard, blowing the affair up as large as they could, knowing neither kith nor kin; officials and nobles alike found them a sore trial.

Feng Dalun hastened to add: “I have another scheme. We can have the Court of Judicial Review send down a document, demanding the prisoner on the ground that the adjudication is not yet complete. Even should the Jing’an Bureau put us off, we can still sound out the other side’s intentions.”

This scheme was a normal exchange of documents between offices, leaving no traces. Prince Yong thought it over and said: “That’s good. It happens I’ve an old acquaintance among the evaluators of the Court of Judicial Review; go and speak to him, and it’s done.”

An evaluator of the Court of Judicial Review was of the junior eighth grade, lower class, charged with deliberating on criminal cases and rectifying the statutes, which made him the very man for this. Feng Dalun hastened to ask his name; Prince Yong gazed at the sky and thought a good long while before he spoke: “Er... surname Yuan, I think, some connection to the Consort of the Prince of Cao; ah, yes, Yuan Zai; the courtesy name escapes me.”

Feng Dalun noted the name down inside his sleeve and hastily took his leave. Just then a gong sounded at the field’s edge, and the Silla maids quickly took up the riding-robe and futou to change Prince Yong back into them. But he waved them off impatiently and turned about in place several times in agitation, while the discomfort in his stomach grew only more marked. At last, unable to hold it back, he bolted to a slop-bucket and vomited in great heaves.

Just then, from off in the southwest, a faint sound of drumming came, its beats urgent, each one falling in the space between breaths, and peculiarly unsettling to the mind. Prince Yong wiped the corner of his mouth with his sleeve and gave a feeble wave of his hand:

“No more playing. Back to the mansion!”

Cao Poyan’s shock was no small thing.

He had only turned his head for an instant; how had the woman vanished? The well-pavilion stood dozens of paces from the walls on every side; not even a bird could have flown over them so fast.

Stunned for a couple of snaps of the fingers, he came at last to himself, crossed to the well in two or three hurried strides, and leaned over the well-rail to peer down. Sure enough, just as Cao Poyan had guessed, the woman had actually jumped into the well.

At the bottom of the well there was only a shallow skin of water; Wen Ran lay face-down in it, not stirring. Cao Poyan called out, and got no response.

Had the woman thrown herself down the well for fear of being defiled, or for fear of being used against her father? Cao Poyan did not care; what he cared about now was how to get her out. At such a distance he could not judge whether she had truly fallen to her death or was only feigning a faint.

On an ordinary day a well-rope would have settled it. But for Cao Poyan as he now was, it had become an all but insurmountable difficulty.

In the earlier raid by the Lüben Guards, Cao Poyan had taken a bolt from Cui Qi’s crossbow in the elbow. Bandaged, it was no longer serious, but he could put no strength into it. With one arm alone it was impossible to haul her up. And he could not, of all things, go into the depot to find someone to help; they were all busy with the affair of the Que-le Huo-duo, and not a snap of the fingers could be wasted.

A simple predicament had thus fairly stumped Cao Poyan.

Cao Poyan circled the well-mouth a few times, bent down, and studied the well-wall closely; there ran along it a string of shallow chiseled holes, spaced out in staggered order, plainly left by the well-diggers. Without some special skill, an ordinary person would find it hard to climb them bare-handed. Then a fresh thought struck Cao Poyan: why must he get her up at all?

If she was dead, that was the end of all things. And even were she not dead, she could never climb out by herself. He had only to cap the well-mouth and weight it fast with stones, and it made a cage as if fashioned by heaven and earth.

Should the Right Shad want her, he could come and fetch her at any time. Cao Poyan had proper business to see to, and could not waste his time on her.

Cao Poyan felt a slight regret. It was a rare thing for him to be moved to any pity for a woman of the Central Plains, and he had wished to let this daughter leave her father something. But the woman would rather throw herself down the well than write a letter; it seemed the women of the Central Plains were a good deal more stubborn than one imagined. Cao Poyan could not help thinking of Wang Zhongsi, that scourge of the steppe, pitiless and unyielding, cruel and cunning. Every time his banner appeared on the banks of the Orkhon River, it swept away more blood than the river’s own waters, and struck terror even into the cattle and sheep.

Like father, like daughter, indeed.

As a boy Cao Poyan had heard his forebears tell how splendid the Türk wolf-banner had once been, how it had pressed close to Chang’an time and again, until even the emperor of Great Tang trembled at it. And now they huddled in one corner of the steppe, holding out grimly under Tang’s martial might. One of his reasons for coming to Chang’an this time was to look upon this great city that had witnessed both the glory and the humiliation of his ancestors, and to destroy it with his own hands.

“How I should love to beat Chang’an once, fair and square.”

With a faint regret, Cao Poyan found a scrap of rag and dropped it down the well to cover Wen Ran’s body. The rag was close in color to the well-bottom, so that even were someone to peer down the well-mouth, they would not make out that anyone was inside. Then he weighted the well-mouth fast with a few stones and left the depot.

This ward was far more desolate than those to the north; there was scarcely a living soul nearby, only a few rows of long-abandoned, dilapidated houses and an earth-god shrine. Now and again crows flew past the banner-poles wound with tattered cloth, and even wild dogs slipped by, gone in a flash.

Watching warily to left and right, Cao Poyan strolled toward the outer street. He had passed some two crossroads before he came on a little market within the ward, where the peddlers dealt mostly in cheap food, broths and hu-bing flatcakes and vegetable soups, with here and there a sundry stall selling needles and thread. On an earthen rise not far off stood a small courtyard hung with a green gourd, its walls low, three big green vats set at the gate. Just now dozens of ragged beggars were scattered over the slope outside the courtyard, sprawled this way and that in an air of idle ease.

This, surely, was the infirmary Long Bo had spoken of, which was said to take in the beggars and the sick of Chang’an and to provide treatment and medicine besides. Cao Poyan simply could not understand it: had Great Tang really no other place to spend its money? The steppe had never fed such useless mouths.

Cao Poyan walked straight over, catching gusts of a sour stench. The beggars, like mountain monkeys, picked lice off one another and sunned themselves, and cared nothing for this one intruder. Brows slightly knit, he searched for the one wearing a flowered gauze futou. This was no hard task, for most of the beggars were bare-headed with their hair loose.

He soon found his mark: a man dozing against a pine tree, wrapped in a cloth robe with a bald old felt blanket beneath him, a flowered gauze futou set crookedly on his head, conspicuous indeed among the pack of ill-clad beggars.

“I need a few men.” Cao Poyan came up before him and went straight to the point.

The man yawned and looked him over lazily out of crust-rimmed, squinting eyes, saying nothing. Cao Poyan unfastened from his waist a small silver flask with a curved spout, each of its two sides chased with a lifelike galloping horse; it was the wine-flask he had carried at his side when he rode on the steppe.

“Do this for me, and it’s yours.”
