## Xuanwu Lake

The orderly brought freshly boiled water, and Mu Chuan took from the cabinet that round, bridge-knobbed pot of vermilion zhuni clay, sat down on the sofa, and dropped a little rock tea into it. He rinsed the cups with the first steeping, then poured fresh water and let it brew. Steam curled in the sunlight. He thought a moment, then picked up the telephone and rang You Tianxiao.

“Captain You is here—Director Mu's inside having his tea; do go in.” The orderly said it loudly at the door.

You Tianxiao knocked and, without waiting for Mu Chuan to speak, pushed the door open and came in, a stack of case files in his hand.

“Director Mu, I've brought you the interrogation records.”

Mu Chuan waved him to a seat. You Tianxiao set the files on the tea table, and as he sat down on the sofa a pair of dice fell from his trouser pocket; he hastily bent to pick them up.

Mu Chuan glanced at him, picked out a cup, and busied himself rinsing it and pouring, tipping the water back and forth.

“What fine tea is the Director drinking?”

Mu Chuan gave an affected yawn. “Adjutant Weng dragged me out drinking last night; I had a drop too much. Just now I felt like a mouthful or two of tea.”

“That Cantonese who's always about the Garrison Command in a Panama hat?”

“Nothing escapes the captain, I see.” Mu Chuan poured You Tianxiao a cup. “Try this Wuyi Da Hong Pao—I find it has a good flavor.”

“Fine tea.” You Tianxiao took a sip, though he himself preferred cold water.

Quite against his habit, Mu Chuan actually set to reading the files in earnest. He turned a page and said suddenly: “I know your detective squad has no end of tricks. But with money in hand, a man could find himself a woman, set up a household—”

He pointed at You Tianxiao's trouser pocket. “That sort of thing—play at it for the occasion and let it go at that.”

You Tianxiao made as if to speak, then thought better of it, and in the end said only: “Yes, Director.”

For a while there was no sound in the room but the turning of pages.

“Still hasn't opened his mouth,” he said softly, as though to himself, and lit himself a cigarette when he had done.

“You've been at it two days now, I take it?” Mu Chuan did not raise his head, and turned another page as he spoke.

“These people are of every trade under the sun, all sorts thrown together higgledy-piggledy—on that count alone one can be certain.”

“Communist cases may fall under emergency justice,” Mu Chuan said as he read, “but in this period of political tutelage the Judge Advocate's office can't have its own way as it once did; a verdict still wants evidence.”

“This Ling Wen—a writer, and a rich merchant's widow besides; a leisured-class woman if ever there was one.” Mu Chuan turned a few pages on. “A woman schoolteacher, a reporter, a bank clerk, the owner of a curio-and-scroll shop, and one who's been a soldier. Suspect on every side—no wonder you swept them all in together. That intelligence lead of yours, what exactly did it say?” Mu Chuan spoke in an easy tone. “This Yi Junnian—did you put him through a little something?”

“He's in the painting-and-scroll trade; he looked a bit frightened, so we gave him a knock or two.”

“His confession's all topsy-turvy—you must have beaten him hard.” Mu Chuan laughed.

“No beating. We ran a wire through him.”

“You used that German kit?” The year before, Nanjing had sent the Garrison Command a consignment of German-made equipment, an electric-torture apparatus among it.

“The bank clerk, Lin Shi—which bank?”

“The Rentai Bank. He took a bullet in the leg during the arrest, and the Command's medical officer patched him up. He's half-conscious; we've hardly questioned him.”

“Liang Shichao—out of the ranks, is he?”

“He claims to have served in the Nineteenth Route Army once, was badly wounded in the ‘January 28’ fighting at Shanghai, and has spent these last years recovering since his discharge.”

“Oh—” Mu Chuan looked the confession on this page over closely. “Have you wired their army headquarters to ask?”

“Officers and men are all at the ‘Communist-suppression’ front in Fujian. The Command says the name's on the muster roll, but they've been fighting all along, and the soldiers have turned over several times.”

“You take Yi Junnian for their group leader—why?”

You Tianxiao did not tell Mu Chuan that he had found a pair of dice on Yi Junnian, and that Yi Junnian insisted he had brought the dice himself. Now and then You Tianxiao felt his mind would split in two: of every record he had to make two watertight versions of differing content, one for the Judge Advocate's office and the other to be handed to the Special Operations Headquarters.

“Yi Junnian and that writer—” Mu Chuan turned a few pages forward. “—Ling Wen; old acquaintances, are they?”

“The neighbors say Yi Junnian went often to her house. Ask the two of them and both say it was over buying and selling paintings. Ling Wen's husband's family was surnamed Long, wealthy merchants of the two Guang provinces, it's said; one year he went out on some matter of business and was kidnapped and killed. These years she's lived by selling off curios and scrolls and by subletting rooms as a go-between landlady.”

“And a person like that turns Communist?” Mu Chuan said thoughtfully. “Why is there no record of Chen Qianyuan's?”

“He's still in the interrogation room. I've questioned him the better part of the night—”

“And got nothing out of him either?”

“Dong Huiwen, the woman schoolteacher, is his weak point; I mean to break through by way of her.”

“Oh? His darling?” Mu Chuan was much taken with the idea. He lit a cigarette and watched the smoke rise in wisps. “These interrogation records you've shown me—they don't seem to be numbered in the order of questioning?”

“I've but the one clerk there; only once a day's questioning is done does he have time to sort and file, and he may have muddled them.”

Mu Chuan smiled like an old fox. “The captain is deep, to be sure—you're afraid I'll see just what it is you're looking for, aren't you?”

“Director Mu—”

Mu Chuan waved a hand. “Don't take it to heart, Captain; you and I both serve the Party and the state.”

He stared at the photograph on the page of Chen Qianyuan's file, as if he could read something in those burning, furious eyes. You Tianxiao too was turning matters over in his mind, and his cigarette ash fell to the carefully waxed floor of the Director's office.

“Adjutant Weng stood you drinks last night, Director,” said You Tianxiao, a phrase at a time, as though laboring to find the words. “Or does Commander Cai have some word to pass on?”

“Commander Cai is a favorite at the Nanchang Field Headquarters; in the years since he led his troops out of Shanghai he's been hard-pressed with soldiering. Back when he was garrisoned in Shanghai, Commander Cai made no few friends.”

You Tianxiao straightened and shifted to the outer edge of the sofa. He stubbed out his cigarette, dropped his eyes, and went on listening.

After a pause Mu Chuan continued: “Adjutant Weng said a great deal; the most important of it was this—if these people are Communists, do it by the book, judge the case fairly. If they are not Communists, then he asks that you show them some leniency.”

The telephone rang. Mu Chuan rose to answer it. “It's for you, Captain.” And he laid the receiver on the desk.

“I'm questioning Chen Qianyuan; I told them they'd find me here with you,” You Tianxiao explained.

He picked up the telephone, listened a moment, and said loudly: “Passed out again? Then—take him back to his cell for now.”

“Has he owned to anything?” Mu Chuan leaned back against the sofa, stroking the armrest.

“Not a word.” You Tianxiao stood by the tea table. “I take the Director's meaning. I'll go back and see to it.”

Mu Chuan nodded. You Tianxiao was just leaving when Mu Chuan added: “That Tao woman—”

“Miss Tao is to be released today. When she was sent over that day, we talked the whole afternoon through. They say Mr. Song came forward in person to set the terms; she's learned her lesson.”

“These women—shut them up a while and they come to heel.” Mu Chuan flicked the cigarette ash from his trousers, then asked with sudden contempt: “Is she with child or not, in the end?”

“Shut up all these days—not that I can see.”

“Just as well she isn't; otherwise Mrs. Song would never let her in the door. Before she goes out, have another word with her—tell her to keep her mouth shut.”

Miss Tao came out of her cell wreathed in smiles. She had thought she would go straight out by the lockup's back door—that was how the car had come for her that morning to see Mr. Song—but the warder took her instead to You Tianxiao. Each time she set eyes on this man, a chill ran through her to the roots of her hair.

The sun outside the window was fine, yet You Tianxiao sat in shadow. She heard him say, grimly: “Sit down, Miss Tao. You won't make any more trouble once you're out, I trust?”

“No more, Captain You.”

“Very good—” You Tianxiao watched her a long while, then said abruptly: “Did they give you anything to carry out?”

Miss Tao said nothing.

You Tianxiao rose, came up to her, bent down until his face all but touched that pretty one, and, eyes narrowed, went on staring at her. Miss Tao felt those two pupils shrink to a needle of ice and pierce into her heart; the blood in her whole body seemed about to freeze. All at once You Tianxiao stood straight and moved around behind her. There was not a sound in the room; Miss Tao could almost hear her own heartbeat. Her legs went weak, she could not keep her seat, and she longed to curl into a ball and drop to the floor.

You Tianxiao's hand shot out; he seized the rattan case the warder had set by Miss Tao's feet, put it on the table, opened it, and tipped it upside down, spilling the whole of it out—cheongsams, clothes, lipstick, mirror, strewn across the table. He turned things over twice, and the neatly folded petticoats, silk stockings, garters, and drawers were at once in a heap; the paint-chipped table had turned into the counter of a department store's ladies' section.

“You'd think you were putting up at a grand hotel—” You Tianxiao said sharply. “I'll have you stripped and searched by and by, and if anything turns up, you can forget about walking out that door.”

Miss Tao broke into a sudden giggle, cast a coquettish glance at the captain, and reached out to touch the leather belt of his gray woolen uniform. The detective squad were issued uniforms too, but had never had much in the way of dress regulations; yet the moment You Tianxiao entered the Command, he kept strictly to military bearing and turnout.

Miss Tao seemed to rise, with great effort, half out of her seat, pointed at the table, and said: “There really is a letter.”

“Take it out.” You Tianxiao kept his back to her.

“Tucked under the lining of a cheongsam.”

“Which one?”

“The sapphire-blue one, the woolen stuff.”

You Tianxiao found the cheongsam among the pile of clothes and tore open the lining. To Miss Tao it was as if the cheongsam were still on her own body, and her heart lurched.

The letter was found.

Brother Fang, as though we met face to face: the circumstances of Old Yi and your sisters, I trust you already know in full. Having now entered the hospital, we are resolved to fight it out. Our hearts are quite at peace; the worst that can come is no more than a single death. The weather is bitterly cold; may you and the others take good care. Please also convey word to our honored father and mother, and may they keep themselves well. Your sister Ling and the rest.

You Tianxiao turned the sheet over and over, studying it, and asked Miss Tao again: “Where were you told to send the letter?”

“Once I got out I was to put it in an envelope and post it to the Xujiahui postal sub-office, to be called for at the counter—Box 137.” Miss Tao said, hesitating.

You Tianxiao nodded and said no more. He reached out and switched on the desk lamp, turned the sheet face-about to the light, then laid it down, took a bottle of chemical from a drawer, and let a few drops fall on the paper. In no time a line of writing came up:

All comrades are resolved. The matter of the dice is out; there is a traitor. Further: they are asking after Haohan's whereabouts.

You Tianxiao drained half a glass of cold boiled water at a draught and lit another cigarette. Seeing his look change, Miss Tao dared not make a sound for a long while. It was some time before You Tianxiao raised his head, his expression strange, as though he had only just noticed there was such a person as Miss Tao beside him. He lifted his chin, had her taken out by the lockup's back door, and set free.

The wooden shutters were turned down, and the light in the room was dim. You Tianxiao smoked two cigarettes one after another, then took the dice from his pocket, gave them a few feints in his closed fist, and threw them on the table. He looked at the points, picked up the telephone, and had the Garrison Command's woman operator put the call through to the Zhanyuan in Nanjing.

“Put me through to Deputy Director Ye at the Special Operations Headquarters.” You Tianxiao was all formality on the telephone; but like everyone else, in Ye Qinian's presence he addressed him simply as Director Ye.

Half an hour later the call to Nanjing came through.

“Teacher,” said You Tianxiao, standing, respectful into the telephone, “I must report to you in person.”

You Tianxiao had scarcely come out of Nanjing's Xiaguan Station when someone stopped him outside the door of the newly built oval hall.

“Captain You.” It was Secretary Ma, who pointed at a car parked a little way off. “Director Ye is waiting for you over there.”

It was not yet six o'clock; morning fog wrapped the south bank of the Yangtze. On Ye Qinian's arrangement, the afternoon before, You Tianxiao had drawn his ticket from the supervisory office of the Nanjing-Shanghai Railway Bureau and traveled through the night on the Blue Steel Express straight for Nanjing.

He saw Ye Qinian sitting at the wheel himself, and was just about to open the front passenger door—

“You sit in the back.”

Ye Qinian was You Tianxiao's teacher; in the old training-class days, Teacher Ye alone was a true master of secret-service work. This teacher was hard to draw near to; all those years, in Teacher Ye's presence he had always stood at a distance, and even meeting one to one, the space between their bodies had never once closed to within five meters—the front and back seats of a car counted for a rare nearness.

Yet whenever anything came up, he still put a telephone call straight through to Ye Qinian's desk. The Special Operations Headquarters was a subordinate unit of the Kuomintang's Central Organization Department, but within it something more like a family law held sway. Commit some fault and the punishment was severe—even execution was possible. At the Headquarters, You Tianxiao's immediate superior was not Ye Qinian; but Ye Qinian never objected to You Tianxiao's telephoning to report to him directly. They never commanded by the surface order of rank.

“Teacher, I've questioned them several days and can get nothing out of them.”

“Even an old hand like you can get nothing?”

“The Municipal Police let word leak, and we were forced to make the arrests early. Your student handled it badly. I ask to be disciplined.”

“Punishing you solves nothing.”

The car rounded a bend at the Xiaguan wharf and drove east through the morning fog. There were neither pedestrians nor other cars on the road, and the car slowed; Ye Qinian gazed out the window at the Ming city wall along the shore of Xuanwu Lake.

You Tianxiao looked at the silhouette in the dim front seat and made no sound.

“What is it you want to tell me this time?” As the car passed Jiming Temple, Ye Qinian asked abruptly.

“I'd like to release them for the time being.”

The car halted a while beside the old city wall; You Tianxiao gazed at the moss on the cracked wall-bricks and slowly set out his idea.

To persuade this teacher was no easy thing. In the training-class days, Teacher Ye had been supremely skilled at seeing through his students' every stratagem. He distrusted over-complicated plans, and always said that if you thought a thing too complicated, you would meet too many surprises in the doing of it. But ‘Xi Shi’ was his own proud stroke, and You Tianxiao pointedly stressed releasing them all first, so that ‘Xi Shi’ might be turned to greater effect.

“As it looks now, Yi Junnian doesn't much seem to be their central special commissioner,” he answered the teacher's question.

“Anyone might be. The whole duty of secret-service work is to suspect everything.” Ye Qinian mouthed the same hollow platitudes, now and then putting a question he had already put several times over. You Tianxiao knew that Teacher Ye was weighing things carefully.

“That Mu Chuan—has he heard of ‘Xi Shi’ too?”

“Yes. He's often up to Nanjing.” You Tianxiao thought, then added: “He'd likely just as soon not go on being Judge Advocate—he finds it does too much harm, and it tells against his prospects.”

“What talk is this! Harm? The Party and the state have far too many such cadres—the whole thing's like a sieve, leaking secrets everywhere.” Ye Qinian was greatly angered.

“What did you do with that letter?”

“Burned it.”

“Send it out.”

You Tianxiao sat there dumbfounded, and Ye Qinian went on: “Write a fresh one.”

Seeing that his student had not yet grasped his meaning, Ye Qinian added: “They're in single-line contact; the letter is written to the one surnamed Fang, and that man must be taken back, whatever it costs.”

He seemed to recall something. “I don't smoke—you go ahead.”

You Tianxiao shook his head. After a moment Ye Qinian said: “I approve your plan. Go back and send a telegram to the Headquarters; once they've handed the prisoners over, I'll give you my endorsement. Have them released on bail, and whoever comes to post the bond, you're to look into thoroughly. Every one of them who goes out is to be kept under close watch—double the men on it. I'll send you some new trainees from the Hangzhou training class. That detective squad of yours has become the Garrison Command's nursery—there's every kind of person in it.”

“Yes, Teacher.”

“Any more slips, and even I won't be able to save you.”

“Yes, Director.” You Tianxiao caught the change in Ye Qinian's tone.

The car drove back to the railway station, and You Tianxiao got out, meaning to take the next train back to Shanghai.

Ye Qinian moved back to the rear seat, and Secretary Ma drove off in the direction of the Zhanyuan.

“What did you say when you came to meet me this morning?” As the car passed the old city wall at Shence Gate in the early morning, a shadow rose suddenly in Ye Qinian's heart, and his mind wandered a long while.

Secretary Ma reported: “Director, two days before, the Headquarters sent men to Shanghai to arrest Haohan in secret, and some fellow spoiled the operation; we suspected for a good while whether there wasn't a traitor among the men the Headquarters had sent. Now they say someone has read the case file sent back to the Headquarters from Shanghai and found that the Communist Fang Yunping, the one who didn't go to the meeting, must be the man who opened fire on Pu'enjishi Road. Fang Yunping came up close to borrow a light, and our man fixed his face in memory.”

“Have them press the pursuit of Fang Yunping.” Ye Qinian ordered Secretary Ma. “Has ‘Xi Shi’ not learned of this?”

“He may not know of it.”

“Pass it to him and have him look into it. Fang Yunping doesn't go to the meeting, but runs to a bun shop to save someone. Had he had inside intelligence?”

“Director, I think not. It's very likely the operatives on the scene gave themselves away. In all likelihood Fang Yunping went to make contact with Haohan and found something amiss on the spot.”

“That too is possible.”

They both knew that men who had been long in the secret-service trade did indeed come to look a little unlike ordinary people.

Ye Qinian mused: “Fang Yunping meant both to go to the meeting and to make contact with Haohan—which is rather worth pondering.”

“Does the Director mean that this meeting has to do with Haohan?”

“The branch stations everywhere have been passing word these last days that the Communist Central may be planning something big—that there is a secret plan.”
