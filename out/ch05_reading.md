The surest and most effective way to obtain the enemy's intelligence and track his every move was to find some means of planting the Party's finest Communists inside his own machinery. Like the Monkey King boring into the belly of the ogress Raksasi, the Ox-Demon King's wife, to work his magic within, they wormed their way into the enemy's secret-service organs, his police and gendarmerie, the Concession police stations, and the vital nerve-centers of his party and government, and there they lay low for the long haul, gathering what they saw. Trusting to their whole-hearted devotion to the Party and to their own coolness, wit, and daring, they pushed deep into the enemy's heart and waged a struggle that would not bend.

As early as 31 December 1927, the Party Center, in Central Circular No. 25 issued at Shanghai, had laid down a rule: on a decision of the Party branch, one or two trusted comrades might be sent into a Kuomintang party office or some other reactionary body to do detective work and sabotage, but only where they could serve one of these two purposes. Once the Central Special Section's Second Branch was formed under Chen Geng, it acted on this directive from the Center and picked a body of loyal, daring Party members to plant inside the enemy's ranks and strike at the Kuomintang's detective organs from every side.

From 1929 the Second Branch planted Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di inside the Kuomintang's highest secret-service organ, the Investigation Section of the Organization Department of the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters, the forerunner of the CC Clique's Central Bureau. Through them the Party laid hold of the enemy's innermost secrets and gained a commanding view of the whole workings of the Kuomintang's nationwide secret service. Deep in the tiger's den, in this hidden war they played out its most heart-stopping scene. In little more than a year they gave the Party a great deal of vital intelligence, an outstanding service for which the Center commended them. In February 1962 Li Kenong died of illness in Beijing. His funeral eulogy, written on Zhou Enlai's own instruction, carried this passage: "After the defeat of the Great Revolution, under a savage White Terror, he waged a staunch and courageous struggle against the enemy. Together with Comrades Qian Zhuangfei and Hu Di, who gave their lives for the revolution, he rendered an outstanding service in safeguarding the leading organs of the Party Center." Earlier, in July 1949, Zhou Enlai had spoken to Xiong Xianghui of the intelligence success that Xiong, Chen Zhongjing, and Shen Jian had won inside Hu Zongnan's headquarters at Xi'an: "In our Party's intelligence work, Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di may be called the 'first three heroes,' and you three the 'second three heroes.' I say so because all of you served the defense of the Party Center. Chairman Mao once remarked that if medals were ever to be struck, you should have them too."

### Li Kenong: "I Am the Party Central's Bodyguard"

Li Kenong was an outstanding organizer and leader of intelligence and security work for the Chinese Communist Party and the people's army, and a distinguished representative of the Party on the hidden front. Conscientious and uncomplaining, he worked for the Party tirelessly even through grave illness. He liked to say, with a chuckle: "We are donkeys. We have grown used to our loads, and feel wrong without one. Whatever we can carry, we carry." Li Kenong gave his whole life to the Party's intelligence work; at the critical moment he supplied the Center, accurately and in time, with the intelligence its decisions turned on, and his was a signal service to the Chinese revolution and to the building of the country. He was a figure of legend and mystery, a "baleful star" the enemy held in dread. When Li Kenong died in Beijing in 1962, Dong Biwu wrote these lines in an elegy for him:

{p} Thirty years now since those days, so long gone by; I knew your gifts stood first in all of China.
{p} In counsel you were much the Vice-Director Fang; in running agents, the equal of Li Zuoche.
{p} Would Heaven not grieve to take from us this one old man? could he be ransomed, would a thousand homes not give their all?
{p} His mantle passes on, bright as iris and orchid; he raised the red flag high to hold back the falling dusk.

In this elegy for Comrade Li Kenong, Dong Biwu voiced a deep grief for a comrade-in-arms and set a high estimate on the major achievements of Li's leadership in intelligence and security work. The word puye in the poem is a Tang-dynasty office. "Vice-Director Fang" is Fang Xuanling, who served Emperor Taizong as Left Vice-Director of the Department of State Affairs; resourceful and decisive, he was leaned on by Taizong as heavily as were Wei Zheng and Du Ruhui. Li Zuoche was a strategist of the Qin-Han transition who, after going over to the Han general Han Xin, urged him to press his advantage and win over Yan and Qi, and so gained Han Xin the land of Yan. With these figures Dong Biwu honored Li Kenong's success in the revolution's two-faced strategy. Li Kenong himself put it modestly: "In my years in the revolution I have done two things for the Party: served as a 'bodyguard' to the Party Center, and served as a 'vanguard scout' of the united front."

Li Kenong (1899–1962), also known as Manzi, Jiaxuan, Zetian, Xiagong, Zhenzhong, and Zhonghe, was a native of Wuhu, Anhui. Democratic-revolutionary ideas reached him while he was still in middle school. In 1917 he left school for Beijing and took a job in the distribution office of the Popular Weekly, a paper that preached progressive ideas; when Zhang Xun's attempt in the capital to "restore the Xuantong Emperor" threw everything into turmoil and the paper was banned, he was forced back to Wuhu. In 1919 the storm of the May Fourth Movement swept the country, and Li Kenong threw himself into the New Culture Movement. After the May Thirtieth Movement of 1925, on the Party's instruction and in order to provide for students expelled from the missionary schools, he joined Zhang Qiuren, Gong Qiaoyan, Ah Ying (Qian Xingcun), and others in setting up a preparatory committee for a new school to take such students in, and opened two middle schools, Minsheng and Xinmin. Around the time of the Great Revolution, Minsheng Middle School "became an important base of the underground Party and trained many able people," and through Zhang Qiuren, Ke Qingshi, and Gao Yuhan it sent more than thirty students to Guangzhou. From the day Minsheng was founded Li Kenong carried the real running of the school, and he served as its second principal.

In 1926 Li Kenong joined the Chinese Communist Party at Wuhu. That autumn the Northern Expedition took the three towns of Wuhan at a stroke, and the revolutionary storm swept both banks of the Yangtze. The Chinese Communist Party and Communist Youth League bodies at Minsheng Middle School grew markedly, and the Party's outer organizations too used the school as a base for their secret work. That winter the Wuhu Party organization, joining with the Kuomintang left, dealt the right a firm rebuff and convened the second congress of the Wuhu municipal Kuomintang, reaffirming the Three Great Policies of "alliance with Russia, alliance with the Communists, and support for the peasants and workers." On 22 October the Wuhu municipal Kuomintang office was formally set up, and the Communists who had joined the Kuomintang as individuals—Li Kenong, Hu Haochuan, Qian Xingcun, and others—took seats on its executive committee. In early March 1927 the Wuhu county Kuomintang office was founded, and the organization named Li Kenong a committee member and head of propaganda.

During this time Li Kenong also organized a struggle against the Anhui warlord Chen Diaoyuan. Chen Diaoyuan had belonged to Feng Guozhang's Zhili faction of the Northern warlords; in 1925 he threw in with Sun Chuanfang and became commander-in-chief of the Anhui army. From the day his troops moved into Wuhu they seized people's houses and looted their shops, and the people came to call him a "ticket bandit." In three years his private plunder came to some twenty million yuan. In March 1927 this "ticket bandit" attached himself in turn to Chiang Kai-shek, who appointed him commander of the 37th Army of the National Revolutionary Army, concurrently general commander of the Northern Route Army and chairman of the Anhui provincial government affairs committee. On 6 April Li Kenong led a student corps into the streets, marching under banners that read "Down with the ticket bandit Chen Diaoyuan," and laid bare Chen's crimes.

After Chiang Kai-shek launched his counter-revolutionary coup of "April 12" in Shanghai, the Wuhu region too fell at once under a White Terror. Communists and revolutionary patriots were arrested and killed in numbers, the mass organizations and the municipal Kuomintang office were sealed and smashed, and Li Kenong was forced to move to Yuxikou township in northern Jiangsu and Fuge township in Chao county, where he carried on his work in hiding. On 18 April Chen Diaoyuan set a reward of 50,000 yuan on Li Kenong's head. In the face of this bitter struggle he held on with dauntless revolutionary spirit, on the one hand leading the masses against Chiang Kai-shek and Chen Diaoyuan, the old and new warlords, and their claws, the local bullies and landlords, and on the other planting men inside the secret societies of the underworld to build ties there and strike back.

In August 1927 Chiang Kai-shek stepped down and the situation eased; Li Kenong returned to Wuhu to gather up the pieces, worked to reopen the school, and served as chairman of Minsheng Middle School's board and as its principal. On the instruction of the CCP Anhui provincial committee, he set up a relief society within the school. On 17 January 1928 the Kuomintang public security bureau of Wuhu county arrested the relief society's head, Wang Shaoyu, and more than thirty other Communists and ordinary people, and the Wuhu county committee's liaison office was broken up. Early the next morning Minsheng Middle School was sealed and the enemy went on making arrests; Li Kenong, warned in time, slipped away to Nanjing and then hurried on to Shanghai.

Ah Ying, who had fought at Li Kenong's side in Wuhu the whole time, had himself moved to Shanghai a few days earlier under pursuit by Chen Diaoyuan. He later set down in writing an account of Li Kenong's brave struggle at Wuhu:

Toward the end of 1926, when the National Revolutionary Northern Expedition took Wuhan, I went home. After Comrade Kenong and Comrade Gong Qiaoyan joined the Party, we worked together under the Party's leadership, preparing the uprising and, later, the work of government. Around the "April 12" affair, when the Kuomintang began using the underworld secret societies to attack us, Comrade Kenong and the rest of us fought the local bullies and landlords on the one hand while planting ourselves inside the ruffians' gangs on the other, building ties and striking back—right up to the desperate days when Chiang Kai-shek was openly slaughtering workers and arresting Communists, when we all pulled out together. Through those tense and tangled struggles Comrade Kenong showed extraordinary courage and quickness of mind.

I cannot forget how, after Comrade Kenong and the rest of us had crossed the river to a village on the north bank and the enemy was hunting us again and again, old Uncle Li and Comrade Zhao Ying cared for us and loved us. Ahead of the enemy's pursuit boat, she braved the rain to cross by small craft, toiled a long way over dikes where the mud ran a foot deep, falling again and again until her face and body were caked with filth, all to come and warn us to leave at once. Uncle Li too once walked nearly two hundred li to his home in Chao county to look in on us. Fierce as the storms were, Comrade Kenong's spirit stayed hopeful and firm.

In the spring of 1928, after Li Kenong slipped secretly through to Shanghai, he reconnected with the organization through Ah Ying, who had transferred there ahead of him, and on the Party's arrangement took part in Party life in the Chunye Bookstore cell (the Chunye branch, that is, the Sun Society branch) on North Sichuan Road. The Sun Society was a revolutionary literary group founded in Shanghai in the winter of 1927, with Ah Ying, Jiang Guangci, Hong Lingfei, and Meng Chao among its initiators; its members were all Communists, and it put out the Sun Monthly to spread revolutionary literature. It also founded the Chunye Bookstore, which was in fact a secret organ of the Shanghai Party. This cell was led directly by Qu Qiubai. When Li Kenong first reached Shanghai he often went with Ah Ying and the others to paste up handbills in the streets and do propaganda work. Afterward, on the organization's decision, he ran small newspapers in Shanghai as their manager—first the Armored Train and then the Common People's Paper, each shut down not long after it appeared. That winter Li Kenong was transferred to the CCP's Central-Shanghai district committee as its propaganda commissioner.

Newly arrived in Shanghai and unable to find any open employment, with the Party's funds stretched desperately thin, Li Kenong went through a stretch of hardship and often went hungry. In those days Song Zhijia (Song Jiren), a former Minsheng Middle School student, came from Wuhu to share his lodging, and later spoke of the going-without-a-full-belly life he and Li Kenong lived through together:

…The organization decided to move me to the Chunye Bookstore branch in Shanghai. Arriving in Shanghai, blind to everything and without a soul to turn to, I had no foothold in that gaudy world. So I found the Jinglin Hall hostel where Li Kenong lodged. He too was a member of the Chunye branch, sharing a small room with Gong Qiaoyan. I squeezed in and set up a makeshift cot, made up at night and taken down by day, and the food that had fed the two of them was now shared three ways—a comradeship so deep it is hard to put into words.

Before long Gong Qiaoyan moved out of the little room to live with his younger brother. I began running messages and carrying intelligence for Li Kenong. He not only trained me to be a good courier but took my livelihood on his own shoulders. To fill our bellies he would often pawn his overcoat and other belongings. I could not bear to see my own leader and elder suffer such want on my account, and I longed to shoulder a little of the burden. One day I went to Zhapu Road Bridge and saw a few beggars pushing rickshaws up the slope for a small tip. I tried a few runs myself, but the ruffians set upon me all at once, beating me dizzy, my nose bloodied and my face swollen, and snatched the few coins from my pocket. The constable on duty made a show of it, asked no questions and paid no heed; I could only take my lumps and go back to our lodging. Li Kenong was aghast at the sight of me, thinking I had been beaten for some wrongdoing. To his stream of questions I made no answer at all, and at last he grew angry—the first and only time he ever truly lost his temper, saying that if I would not answer I could no longer do the work and must leave at once. Hearing him say so, I could only tell him what was in my mind and what had happened at the bridge. We both wept scalding tears. Afterward Li Kenong said that Shanghai was a heaven for foreigners and for the rich, and a hell for the poor. To push a rickshaw you had to pay the constable his "cut"—it was not so easy a thing. A world like this, he said, would one day be turned upside down by us. Soon I went to work as a typesetter at the New China Print Shop the underground Party ran. My livelihood secured, I still ran messages for Li Kenong at night.

One day in November 1929, at a film-company studio in Shanghai, Li Kenong ran into his old friend Hu Di. (Hu Di had moved to Shanghai together with Qian Zhuangfei and in 1928 had taken work as an actor at the Shanghai Film Company.) Through Hu Di, Li Kenong came to know Qian Zhuangfei, who told him that the Kuomintang was busy recruiting and expanding its secret-service organization, and hoped the organization might use their chance of being taken on as broadcast-news editors to get inside the Shanghai Wireless Administration Bureau. Li Kenong reported this at once, through the CCP Central-Shanghai district committee, to the Party Center, asking whether he might go and sit the examination. The Central Special Committee took the matter very seriously; Zhou Enlai judged it a fine chance to get inside the enemy and obtain intelligence, and approved. Qian Zhuangfei then arranged matters, and after careful preparation Li Kenong passed one hurdle after another with distinction and won a place in the Kuomintang's Shanghai Wireless Administration Bureau.

Li Kenong afterward spoke of how he sat that examination and of how he, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di came in turn into the wireless training class of Xu Enzeng's Shanghai Wireless Administration Bureau:

At that time secret-service work within the Kuomintang was directed by the party bosses Chen Guofu and Chen Lifu, and by another Central Committee member, Xu Enzeng; they hated the Communists to the bone, and were at odds with the Northern warlords too. To build up their own strength they had Xu Enzeng, in the name of the Shanghai Wireless Administration Bureau, run a wireless training class, hold entrance examinations, and gather in talent. In the spring of 1928 I fled to Shanghai from Wuhu, wanted by the enemy for my revolutionary activity, and ran a small paper with Pan Hannian; after it was closed I worked for the Central-Shanghai district committee. In 1929 I met Qian Zhuangfei and Hu Di one after another; both were members of the Beiping "Southern Society" and had come to Shanghai to escape the enemy's pursuit. None of us then had any employment, and when we saw in the paper that the wireless training class was enrolling, we reported to the organization to ask whether we might sit the examination. The Jiangsu provincial committee, the Special Section, and Gu Shunzhang all judged it a fine chance to get inside the enemy and obtain intelligence, and told us to go. Qian went first; his calligraphy placed first, and he was admitted with good marks. He then recommended me to the Bureau as a broadcast-news editor. But I too had to sit an examination. The subjects were the Three People's Principles, general science, and classical composition. I bought books on these, memorized some passages, and did very well. The classical essay's subject was: "Your mother has died, and your father too, and they are to be buried together." I wrote it there and then. I opened with "This is a matter of mourning, and also a matter for joy," then set out the principle of it in classical style, ending with the two lines: "The grave being thus at rest, a son's heart may declare itself without reproach." I passed that examination and was admitted. From then on we held Communist meetings right in the Kuomintang's offices. If someone came in, we were not afraid: "Look here, these are Communist documents we seized somewhere!"…

Once admitted, Li Kenong came through the enemy's close vetting and was made a broadcast-news editor of the Bureau. Gifted and quick-minded, with a fluent pen, he handled the investigation material so well that Xu Enzeng greatly admired everything that passed through his hands. The Party organization would often hand Li Kenong outdated documents to carry to Xu Enzeng's Investigation Section, so that he might win the trust of the Kuomintang's secret service. Xu Enzeng, reading these documents, praised him again and again. Feeding Xu a steady diet of intelligence and stale "internal Party documents," Li Kenong gained more and more of his confidence and was promoted to head of the telegraph-affairs subsection. The post was not a high one, yet it gave him charge of the wireless operators across the whole country—a superb position for obtaining intelligence—and with it the registration and examination of every wireless operator in the nation. At every examination he enrolled any name the Party organization sent him.

After Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di planted themselves inside the Kuomintang's highest secret-service organ, they supplied the Party with a great deal of vital intelligence.

### The Legendary Hero Qian Zhuangfei

Qian Zhuangfei was an outstanding intelligence worker of the Chinese Communist Party and a legendary hero of the hidden front. Because, planted inside the Kuomintang's Central Investigation Section, he intercepted the top-secret telegrams reporting Gu Shunzhang's arrest and defection and warned the Party organization in time, he shattered the enemy's deadly scheme to net the whole Party Center and its leaders at a stroke and averted a catastrophe on which the survival of the entire Party hung. Speaking of Qian Zhuangfei, Zhou Enlai more than once said with feeling: "But for Qian Zhuangfei, none of us would be here." At the time Qian Zhuangfei was so skilled at hiding himself deep inside the enemy's highest secret-service organ, and so trusted, that the Kuomintang's secret-service chief Xu Enzeng regarded him as a "bosom friend"; not until Gu Shunzhang's arrest and defection gave the game away did Xu Enzeng learn this "staggering piece of intelligence." Nearly thirty years later, telling of Qian Zhuangfei in his memoirs, Xu Enzeng was still aghast: "When I learned of Gu Shunzhang's capture I was overjoyed, and ordered him sent under escort to headquarters in Nanjing at once. … Gu Shunzhang's turning himself in gave me, at once, some staggering intelligence. Gu said that among my own men, one of the ablest, who kept our most secret documents, was in fact a Communist spy. He was my secretary; I had hired him in 1928 when I set up my first commercial wireless station, and he had worked under me for nearly three years. I had always thought him a diligent, loyal, and capable young man. He never talked much, never pried into matters outside his work, and carried out my orders expertly. I really doubted such a model clerk could be a Communist agent. The secret telegram reporting Gu Shunzhang's capture had been deciphered by him, and it was he who handed me the decoded message. I hoped Gu was lying, but the man I sent to look for him came back to report that my clerk had slipped quietly away the morning before, and only then did I believe what Gu Shunzhang had said" (Xu Enzeng, The Invisible Conflict, 1957). This blunder of Xu Enzeng's so infuriated his superior Chen Lifu that as late as 2 July 1980, in a letter to Cai Mengjian, Chen still wrote in a reproachful tone: "At the time, in the mutual penetration work of both sides, which left no crack unentered, Brother Kejun's [Xu Enzeng's] hiring of Qian Zhuangfei was a great oversight."

There is something comical in it. Cai Mengjian, the "special commissioner" Xu Enzeng had sent to Wuhan in those years, had himself arrested and interrogated Gu Shunzhang at Wuhan, personally escorted him to Nanjing, and had been the first to learn from his lips that Qian Zhuangfei was Xu Enzeng's secretary. Yet half a century later, in an article, "Two Stories That Might Have Rewritten Modern Chinese History," published in Taipei's Biographical Literature in November 1980, he still had not worked out who Qian Zhuangfei was. He wrote in it, with a perfectly straight face: "A representative of a certain security agency of ours, a Mr. Du, told me directly: there is solid material proving that after the mainland fell to the bandits, Li Kenong, the bandit regime's Minister of the Social Affairs Department, was in fact Qian Zhuangfei." Cai Mengjian gave his whole life to the service of the Chiang dynasty and won "merit" against the Communists; that a seasoned agent could talk such nonsense shows how muddle-headed and foolish these men were, and shows all the more the superb cover of our Party's intelligence heroes.

Qian Zhuangfei (1896–1935), whose original name was Zhuangqiu and who was also called Qian Chao, was a native of Wuxing county (present-day Huzhou) in Zhejiang. His father bought raw silk in their home district for a living and his mother kept house, and in his childhood the family was reasonably comfortable. His father died while he was studying at Zhejiang Provincial Third Middle School in Huzhou, and with a young son and a widowed mother the family's life grew harder by the day, kept going by pawning and selling off land. His mother had a matchmaker introduce him to the daughter of a cloth shop, Xu Shuangying, whom he married in 1914; the next year a daughter was born. After finishing middle school in 1915 he sat the examination for the Beijing Medical Specialist School. There he came to know a fellow student, Zhang Zhenhua, who came of a distinguished Anhui family of Tongcheng whose forebears had held high office, some as chief ministers, and who was fairly well-to-do. With her frequent help he was able to finish his studies, and after they graduated in 1919 the two set up house in Beijing. Later, when Qian Zhuangfei's mother, old Madam Fan, came up to Beijing to see him, she learned that he had taken a wife elsewhere and already had a son, Qian Jiang. The old lady was none too pleased with the match, and on returning home brought Xu Shuangying and her daughter up to live in Beijing as well. Fortunately Zhang and Xu treated each other with courtesy and lived in harmony, and the household stayed at peace throughout (Xu Shuangying later changed her name to Qian Shuangying).

After graduating from the Beijing Medical Specialist School in October 1919, Qian Zhuangfei hung out his shingle as a doctor on Changxing Street in Beijing, and later worked at the hospital attached to the Beijing-Suiyuan Railway. As his income was meager, by day he taught anatomy at an art school and by night edited a small newspaper to keep the household going. Zhang Zhenhua, for her part, worked as a doctor at the Temple of Heaven Infectious Diseases Hospital. In 1925 Zhang Zhenhua joined the Chinese Communist Party on the introduction of her younger brother Zhang Xianzhong; the next year Qian Zhuangfei too joined the Party on Zhang Xianzhong's introduction.

Qian Zhuangfei was a man of broad learning and wide interests. He not only was a fine doctor but had studied painting and calligraphy, and could write scripts and act as well. In 1926 the Qian couple moved to No. 13, Ping'an Lane, and helped fund and take part in Xu Guanghua's founding of the Guanghua Film Company. The couple, and Hu Di with them, played roles in films while using the work as cover for the Party. In the film Chivalrous Recluse of the Yan Hills, Qian Zhuangfei and his eleven-year-old daughter Lili (the actress Li Lili) both took parts (Li Lili became a famous progressive film star in the 1930s). To cover the Party meetings they held at home, they set up a "gambling den" there: to outward eyes it looked like gaming, but in fact they were meeting and working. Zhang Zhenhua recalled: "So many people came in those days, and everyone was called by his own peculiar mark—'the one with the fan,' 'Fatty,' 'the one with glasses,' and so on—so I never knew their real names. Sometimes they would send me to another house to find things out and report back to the Party. When someone asked to join the Party, they would sometimes send me to talk with him. …"

Using their profession as doctors to cover the Party's secret work, husband and wife were both active workers for the Party. On the pretext of "practicing medicine," they would carry the Party's secret documents, slogans, and handbills in a leather bag painted with a red cross, delivering and scattering them everywhere in the guise of "house calls." Sometimes under cover of night they slipped handbills through people's door cracks or pasted them up in the lanes, forever playing "hide-and-seek" with the reactionary police and agents. When a comrade was leaving and needed clothes, a suitcase, or other daily necessities, Zhang Zhenhua would buy them at once and send them over. While Qian Zhuangfei was a doctor at the Beijing-Suiyuan Railway hospital he would sometimes write a note for Zhang Zhenhua to carry out, and so she became a secret courier too. In those days the reactionaries' searches were very strict: to leave by the Xizhimen gate one was searched by the sentry (a woman searcher for a woman), one's whole body gone over. Bold as she was, she always managed with composure. She would sew the secret letters into the cotton wadding of her padded coat, where the enemy could not find them, and she always passed "the barrier" safely, never once making a slip. Sometimes the whole family, young and old, would dress up in their finest and go boating for pleasure at Beihai Park, seizing the chance to scatter handbills. …

After the defeat of the Great Revolution, the Party organizations across the North were battered again and again by the enemy, the White Terror was severe, and there was no living to be had there; on the organization's arrangement, at the end of 1927 he moved to Shanghai. Shanghai was safer, but work was hard to find. With difficulty he landed a temporary post copying rickshaw licenses at the Shanghai Public Utilities Bureau, and only then brought the whole family to Shanghai. Because his income was meager and the household hard pressed, in the summer of 1928 he and Zhang Zhenhua left Shanghai again for Kaifeng, to serve as army doctors in Feng Yuxiang's national army. Zhang Zhenhua recounted: "Zhuangfei and I went together. Feng Yuxiang was recruiting at Kaifeng and had promised eighty yuan a month plus some sum for a 'settling-in allowance.' On that, with old folk above and children below, we could get by. Unexpectedly they gave us no money, sometimes only twenty yuan, and would not let us leave. Later, because I fell ill, we slipped away and came back to Shanghai together." Afterward Zhang Zhenhua worked as a resident physician at the Kemei Maternity Hospital, run by foreigners behind the Sincere Company on Nanjing Road (later moved to Hart Road, today's Changde Road); Xu Shuangying and her mother-in-law, meanwhile, settled the children at No. 4, Xinxingshunli, on Route Sieyes in the French Concession (present-day Yongjia Road). By this time Qian Zhuangfei had picked up his Party ties again and been placed in the French Concession branch under the CCP's French-South district committee, whose secretary was Zhang Shenchuan (now a member of the National Committee of the CPPCC); sharing Party life in the same branch was Hu Di (Hu Beifeng, an Anhui man), who had joined the Party together with him in Beijing. Once, when the French-South committee secretary Li Fuchun set up a special propaganda team in the French Concession Party branch, charged with scattering handbills and pasting up slogans in support of the strike by the Chinese workers of the French tramway company, Qian Zhuangfei, to make the work of carrying and posting them easier, designed a miniature handbill only two fingers wide, easy to carry, which won the comrades' praise.

In the latter half of 1928 Qian Zhuangfei found a post at the Shanghai International Wireless Administration Office, painting advertisement pictures and drumming up business. This wireless office was a foreign-facing commercial body run by the National Government's Reconstruction Commission, handling the international telegrams of foreigners living in Shanghai. It was not a Kuomintang secret-service organ, but for our Party it was a most useful place—a spot in which to keep a Party cadre hidden while he mastered wireless-telegraph technique and gathered related intelligence. The Party branch therefore decided that Qian Zhuangfei should settle in for the long haul and not be asked to take part in other activity.

Later Chen Lifu sent Xu Enzeng to be director of the Shanghai International Wireless Administration Office. Seeing that Qian Zhuangfei was gifted, good with people, and moreover a fellow townsman from Huzhou, Xu Enzeng came to trust him deeply.

At the end of 1928, when Xu Enzeng was appointed by Chiang Kai-shek as director of the Shanghai Wireless Administration Bureau, Qian Zhuangfei was named the Bureau's secretary. In the spring of 1929, after Chen Lifu took on the concurrent post of chairman of the Kuomintang Reconstruction Commission, Xu Enzeng, promoted by this cousin of his, was for a time moved to be director of the Zhejiang provincial reconstruction department. Xu Enzeng took the Qian family to Hangzhou. That year the reconstruction department mounted the grand West Lake Exposition in Hangzhou, and Qian Zhuangfei helped Xu Enzeng plan its design and prepare its business, showing outstanding talent and organizing ability. To make a better impression on Xu Enzeng, Qian Zhuangfei deliberately "kept to the rules and worked conscientiously," making the exposition a special success that won high marks from society and from the Kuomintang officials; even the Kuomintang's "god of wealth," Kong Xiangxi, came to the opening. By this Qian Zhuangfei gained still more of Xu Enzeng's regard, and the moment the exposition closed he became Xu Enzeng's private secretary, which created favorable conditions for him, Li Kenong, and Hu Di to get inside the Kuomintang's highest secret-service organ later on.

### Hu Di, "the Youngest and the Most Gifted"

Hu Di (1905–1936), whose original name was Hu Baichang and who also went by Beifeng, Huma, Shangtian, Yiyu Hudi, and others, was a native of Shucheng county, Anhui. During the Great Revolution he was studying at China University in Beijing when, through a fellow student, Wu Luming, a Zhejiang townsman of Qian Zhuangfei's, he met Qian Zhuangfei at the Anhui Guild Hall. Like Qian Zhuangfei, he was a man of outstanding literary gifts, devoted to literature and art from his school days. The two were kindred spirits and grew close after they met, becoming intimate friends. Qian Zhuangfei was then living at Western City's Ping'an Lane, a Western-style house, No. 13, in Beijing, and Hu Di went often to his home; after graduating from China University he lived at the Qians'.

Hu Di was fairly young, nine years younger than Qian Zhuangfei, who had been born in 1896. After graduating from university he kept up his work in literature and art, had a solid literary grounding, wrote fluently, and had a beautiful hand; his talk was lively and engaging. Li Kenong, seven years his senior, later praised him: "Hu Di is the youngest, but in his pen and his gifts the highest." Hu Di's temperament was lively and open, he was accomplished at every kind of music, sociable and able to master himself, considerate of others and glad to help his friends. In many ways he was like Qian Zhuangfei.

In 1926 both Hu Di and Qian Zhuangfei joined the Chinese Communist Party, and they often carried on the Party's secret work together. Zhang Zhenhua later told people of their secret activity in Beijing in those years: "I remember one day when Zhuangfei, Hu Beifeng, Qian Shuangying, and I all put on very fine clothes and went boating at Beihai Park. Rowing to the middle of the lake, we brought out the handbills rolled into small rolls, and everyone went off to paste them up in different parts of the park. In those days we scattered handbills at night, slipping them quietly into people's door cracks. If the enemy tailed us, we would at once find a place to set down the handbills in our hands and slip quickly away."

After the "April 12" counter-revolutionary coup, the White Terror shrouded the whole country, and the Beijing reactionary authorities too were savage in hunting down and killing Communists; every day some comrade one knew "vanished." Hu Di's revolutionary activity was discovered by the enemy, and the Beijing reactionary authorities put out an open warrant for his arrest. On being warned by the organization he at once changed his name to Huma, styled Beifeng, while Qian Zhuangfei took the alias Nanfei ("Flying South"), from the old poem "The Tartar horse leans into the north wind, the crossing bird nests on southern boughs"; that winter he left Beijing together with Qian Zhuangfei and moved to Shanghai.

Newly in Shanghai, he lost touch with the Party organization. In the spring of 1928, to make a living, Hu Di was taken on as an actor at the Shanghai Film Company. He had come to Shanghai bearing the charge of being a "wanted" man, and was soon spotted by the Shanghai reactionary government, which sent soldiers and police to surround and seize him; alertly he leaped a wall and got away. Though Hu Di came of a well-off family, he had been tempered by the hard years of the revolution, and in the face of danger and difficulty he always pressed straight ahead. He now made his way to villages around Songjiang and along the coast, mixing in with the salt smugglers and uniting the masses in a struggle against the reactionary government's salt-inspection officers. In the summer of 1929, with the enemy hunting him hard, he went to Hangzhou to hide at Qian Zhuangfei's home. Afterward he returned to Shanghai with Qian Zhuangfei and took work at another film company. One day in November 1929 he ran into Li Kenong at a studio and introduced Qian Zhuangfei to him. Later, with the Party organization's approval, Hu Di followed Li Kenong and Qian Zhuangfei into Xu Enzeng's secret-service organ, and became one of the counter-espionage workers of the Central Special Section.
