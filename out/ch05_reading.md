## Chapter 5. Going Underground

*To the tune "The Immortal by the River": The Underground Great Wall*

*When the August First red flag was raised on high, / the foundation at Jinggang first laid down; / worker and peasant revolution joined in glad alliance. / The fallen in front, the followers behind— / wiping away blood and tears, we marched on.*

*The underground Great Wall, built through bitter toil, / heart bared to heart in crimson devotion; / laying our plans within the tent, we rescued the loyal and true. / The Long March sings of victory; / underground, we chronicle the secret soldiers.*

On 14 July 1927, Xiyan and I boarded the steamer running from Wuchang to Jiujiang. We had both disguised ourselves: Xiyan put on a long gown and mandarin jacket, looking like a gentleman or a merchant, while I did my hair up in a bun and got myself up as a housewife. Traveling on the same boat were several cadres of the instructional regiment, who also carried some documents. Before we boarded, the organization had specially warned us that if we met acquaintances on the boat, none of us was to greet the others; we were to pretend to be strangers who did not know one another.

On the boat, we found that some shady-looking people seemed to be paying us close attention. Xiyan's vigilance was very keen, and he said to me: "After we land, don't go straight to Nanchang; let's go 'play' on Mount Lu." So we secretly contrived to let our fellow travelers know they should not go on together with us after landing but hurry ahead to report at Nanchang. When the boat reached Jiujiang, someone came from Nanchang to meet us; we quietly told the man that we feared bad people might be tailing us and had to find a way to shake them off, and so we two went up Mount Lu first.

We stayed a few days at the Yunzhongtian Hotel on Mount Lu, and having watched and seen no suspicious persons, we hurried down the mountain and made for Nanchang, going as arranged beforehand to report at the Jiangxi Grand Hotel. The first person we saw was Comrade Chen Tanqiu. He said to us: "You have come at just the right time." He told us of the Party Central's decision to launch the Nanchang Uprising, though he did not mention the exact date. He also conveyed the Central's decision: Chen Tanqiu was to be secretary of the underground Jiangxi Provincial Committee, and Wan Xiyan a member of the Provincial Committee Standing Committee and head of its propaganda department. I and Chen Tanqiu's wife Xu Qianzhi were to be secretaries of the Provincial Committee: Xu was in charge of the inside work, responsible for personnel, documents and archives, and finances; I was in charge of the outside work, receiving the couriers who came down from the Central above and up from the localities below, and serving besides as the Provincial Committee's confidential courier. Because we were to engage in long-term concealed secret underground work, the organization instructed the four of us not to appear openly in Nanchang and to take no part in the preparations connected with the armed uprising. And so, in accordance with the Central's decision, we began the work of preparing to establish the underground Provincial Committee. From head of the women's department to secretary of the Provincial Committee, from a vigorous, open mass movement to underground work filled with white terror—my life underwent a historic, leaplike transformation.

We began the work of preparing to establish the underground Jiangxi Provincial Committee. In the city of Nanchang there was an old couple named Xu who ran a sauce-and-pickle shop; their sons and daughters were all our Party and League members, and so we set up the Provincial Committee organ inside this sauce shop. The front of the shop was the store; in the middle was the house where the old couple lived; and at the back was a courtyard for setting out the sauce vats to dry—all their own property, like a little Beijing courtyard house. Though the shopfront was not very large, it was of a middling scale. Tanqiu and Xiyan took the aliases Xu Guodong and Xu Guoliang and passed as nephews of the old couple; but because the two men differed greatly in age and appearance, they addressed each other as cousins, and both had the standing of partners with a share in the Xu family business—"shareholding proprietors." Chen Tanqiu grew a little mustache and carried a water pipe in his hand, looking every inch a small landlord who had fled from the countryside. Xu Qianzhi and I were both got up as housewives. I was pregnant by then, but we hired no servant; apart from Old Mrs. Xu helping us with the cooking, all the things a housewife must do—the washing of clothes, the cleaning, and the rest—we two did with our own hands. From the vigorous high tide of the great revolution to long-term concealed secret underground work, our whole way of working had changed, and it took a period of adjustment.

I did my hair up in a bun and dressed altogether as a housewife; I had never before shown my face in any public place in the city of Nanchang, and with a big belly besides, I was not likely to draw the enemy's notice or suspicion. One of my specific tasks was to keep up regular contact with the Jiangxi Provincial Committee of the Communist Youth League. The first League provincial secretary to make contact with me was called "Wang Mazi"—I do not remember his name; the League provincial secretary-general was Rao Shushi; and there was another comrade called Cao Ce. I arranged to meet them once a week in a park in Nanchang, and whenever the Party Central had documents to be conveyed or the localities had important matters to report to the Central, they were passed along at these meetings. Every document that came from the Central we had to copy out afresh, one copy to be kept at the Provincial Committee and one forwarded to the League Provincial Committee. It was then that I learned the ways and methods of copying secret documents. It is said that in Russia before the October Revolution, Lenin, communicating secretly with the outside from prison, wrote on paper with milk for ink, so that once it dried nothing could be seen at all, and the comrade who received the letter had only to hold the paper over a flame for the writing to appear. In China we mostly used rice water, writing with a fine-tipped brush on the back of the pages of a thread-bound book. The reader would smear it with tincture of iodine, and the starch and iodine, reacting chemically, made the writing appear. The rice water must not be too thick—if too thick, what was written on the back would seep through and leave a trace on the front. The brush too must not be too coarse—if too coarse, what was written on the back would show through on the front. When it was done, the book was rebound and pressed flat. Besides this, one had to decide flexibly how to write according to the length of the document and the disguised standing of the courier who was to carry it. I remember that once a document had to be sent to Shanghai; the courier's disguise was rather "foreign" in style, so we could not write the document secretly in a thread-bound book, which would not have suited his disguised standing and might easily have roused suspicion. So instead we wrote it in a Bible; but the Bible is very thick, and once taken apart it is hard to rebind and restore to its original look, so we could only use a fountain pen dipped in rice water to write in the gaps between the lines, making the characters smaller still. I had never worn glasses before, but under such conditions, from often bending over documents to copy them by lamplight at night, I became nearsighted—though fortunately not to any great degree.

For one doing courier work, the most important thing is that the memory must be very good: where someone lives, his surname and name, the distinguishing features of his build and looks, what the recognition signal is—all had to be firmly fixed in the mind. A courier had also to be extremely alert, paying attention at all times and in all places to the surrounding situation, and the moment anything unusual was discovered, contriving to respond flexibly. When I went to make contact, for instance, I had to watch what was before and behind me both going out and coming back: before going out I would first look around to see whether there were any shady characters outside; coming back, I would first buy something at a little shop nearby and see whether anyone was following or watching. If the place of contact was near, I went on foot; if far, I took a rickshaw, and on returning would not stop at the door of the organ but get down some distance away, and only after observing that no one was watching or following me would I return to the organ. The discipline of underground work laid it down that the Provincial Committee organ where the four of us lived was absolutely not to receive outsiders and not to be exposed. Anyone coming from outside to make contact could not come directly to the Provincial Committee organ. When I made contact with a courier from outside, it was always arranged beforehand to meet at some hotel. In these hotels there would always be a waiter or odd-job man who was a comrade of our underground Party. On arriving I would first find that comrade, match the signal in the fixed manner of contact, and only then meet and make the connection. At that time there were different fixed manners of contact for people coming from southwestern Jiangxi, from the Jinggang Mountains, and from northeastern Jiangxi. We also had an underground courier boat that plied the Gan River and made a fixed rendezvous once every half month. But the times of a boat are not as exact as those of a train or a plane, and for a while it could only be set beforehand to arrive within about three days. During those three days I had to go every day to loiter near the wharf by the river. On the surface I had to look as though I were idly gazing at the river view, but in fact I was very tense, searching the surface of the river at every moment for a sign of that courier boat. The courier's work was not only quite hard but quite dangerous; if anything unexpected happened, one had to improvise on the spot. I remember once I went to a designated contact station. This station was set up in an overhead room across a lane-mouth, and from the mouth of the lane one could see the station's window. By the earlier arrangement, a pot of flowers had to be set in the window; if there were no flowers there, it meant something had gone wrong. I went in the afternoon, while it was not yet dark, and I saw not only that there was no flowerpot in the window but that the electric light was on in the room. Feeling that this was bad, I dared not rashly barge in, and went into the neighboring lane to make inquiries. As luck would have it I met a nurse from the hospital, an acquaintance, and the moment she saw me she said: "Oh, we were scared to death yesterday—a lot of soldiers and police came and took away a man upstairs next door..." Pretending indifference, I chatted with her a few words, took a turn in the park, then rode a rickshaw home and at once reported to Comrade Chen Tanqiu. Comrade Tanqiu said I had done well, that one must be that alert to ensure safety and avoid danger.

When the Provincial Committee comrades discussed work in ordinary times, we secretaries kept no records, to prevent the leaking of secrets should the enemy come suddenly to search. Sometimes when an important comrade came from the Central to hold a meeting at the Provincial Committee, we would set mahjong tiles and counters out on the table and pretend to be playing mahjong. The old Xu couple kept watch out front in the shop, and if soldiers, police, or a stranger came in, they would give a few loud coughs. Hearing this, we would set to shuffling the mahjong tiles in earnest; even if only for show, we deliberately made a clatter, so as not to give ourselves away. The old Xu couple were very good to us, truly as close as one family. Sometimes when I had to copy documents until dawn, they always kept the kerosene lamp burning bright. So that I could work with a single mind, they would not sleep, but silently kept watch through the night and did the work of guarding us. To this day I still deeply cherish their memory; I only no longer remember their names. After the founding of New China, Comrade Wu Defeng told me that he had later seen the old Xu couple again, and that their sons and daughters were all working in southwestern Jiangxi and had all become senior cadres of the Party.

I remember very clearly that while Chen Tanqiu was directing the work of the Jiangxi Provincial Committee, the first document the Provincial Committee issued was a criticism of Chen Duxiu's Right opportunism; the second was the "Letter to All Party Members" and the other resolutions passed by the Party Central at the August Seventh Conference held in Hankou; the third was the notice by which the Party Central's Political Bureau, accepting the criticism of the Communist International, criticized the "Left" putschist error represented by Comrade Qu Qiubai. The latter two were both forwardings of documents issued by the Central. After the enlarged meeting of the provisional Central Political Bureau held in November 1927, the "Left" putschist error held a dominant position in the whole Party for a period of time, and the general strategy of carrying out armed insurrection throughout the country was fixed. During this period, in any region where the revolutionary forces were very weak and the enemy possessed a powerful armed force, the reckless, blind armed insurrections launched at all costs were for the most part quickly put down, so that the limited revolutionary forces preserved after the failure of the great revolution suffered further heavy losses. These documents analyzed how, after the failure of the great revolution, China's revolution was at a low ebb, fixed the general policy of agrarian revolution and armed resistance to the Kuomintang reactionaries, and at the same time required us to do the underground and trade-union work of the white areas in a solid, down-to-earth way.

Even before the Nanchang Uprising, our Party had reckoned that the city of Nanchang could not be held, and so only a few days after the uprising's victory the insurgent army, in accordance with the Central's preset plan, swiftly withdrew from Nanchang. After the troops moved out, the work of us who remained behind in the underground Jiangxi Provincial Committee at Nanchang was very tense and very dangerous. The enemy blockaded the city gates on all four sides and carried out a great hunt within the city for a whole week, seizing anyone who looked in the least suspicious. Comrade Chen Tanqiu was very calm and good at adapting; seeing that I was young and pregnant and fearing that if the enemy searched and questioned me I would flush and grow flustered and give myself away, he taught me just to keep my head down, not to look at the soldiers and police, and to feign a calm air of seeing without seeing, hearing without hearing, treating it as nothing. And with head lowered, doing what? I could wipe the dust from the kerosene lamp's shade, sweep the floor, or do sewing—buy some cloth and make little clothes and trousers or diapers for the child that was to be born. When the soldiers and police came, I had only to keep my head down over my work and make no sound, and let him deal with them alone. Chen Tanqiu, in his standing as a shareholding proprietor of the shop, could answer fluently whenever the enemy questioned him about the shop's accounts and capital, every inch the little shopkeeper. The searching soldiers and police would also enter the living quarters and look everywhere for any suspicious sign. They were, after all, regular soldiers and police, not like those defeated, routed soldiers who plundered everywhere and molested women, daring to do every kind of evil. But when they questioned people they were very much in earnest, and one had to be perfectly composed and answer calmly; otherwise, if one grew flustered or answered incoherently, it would rouse suspicion.

In leisure hours Comrade Tanqiu told us much of his revolutionary activities and experiences—his work experience, for example, when he was chairman of the Anyuan District Committee of the Communist Youth League, and the deeds of the Party Central leaders he knew well, especially the reverence he expressed for Comrade Zhou Enlai, his exposure and criticism of Chen Duxiu's Right-opportunist error, and many moving historical stories from the period of the first Kuomintang–Communist cooperation. Knowing that I liked to read *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*, he even made a joke of it: in *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*, to avoid rousing Cao Cao's suspicion, Liu Bei grew vegetables in his garden, hiding his light and biding his time. Now that we had gone into partnership with old Mr. Xu to keep a shop, using the disguise of small industrial-commercial proprietors to cover the truth of our underground revolutionary activities, were we not just like Liu Bei growing vegetables in those days? From such casual talk we learned a great deal from him. On 11 November 1927 I became a full member of the Chinese Communist Party, and Comrade Tanqiu even wrote a piece praising and encouraging me, saying that I put on no young-lady airs at all and had adapted very quickly to the new environment of underground work, my words and bearing giving no one cause to notice anything unsuitable. It may well be that just this appraisal was the origin of my being transferred to work at the Central a year later!

In January 1928, Xiyan received the Central's transfer order to go at once to the region of southwestern Jiangxi to serve concurrently as secretary of the Special Committee, leading the local peasants in agrarian revolution and armed struggle. Comrade Wu Defeng went with him. Xiyan was by nature a scholar, able only to wield a pen, and wrote very beautiful articles. Now he was to throw down the pen and take up arms, to do the work of leading peasants and an army; though he was entirely a layman at it, as a Communist he could only obey the Central's order unconditionally. I had just given birth then, our son Jie'er being only three days old, and naturally I could not go with him. As we parted, loath to part, in the Nanchang hospital, he charged me in a low voice: "If the work over there goes smoothly, when the time comes someone will be sent to bring you and the child to join me; if by any chance something should go wrong, then through your parents get word to my parents, and take the child back to our old home in Huangmei to be raised, so that when he grows up he can carry on the revolution. We are revolutionary companions; whatever the Party bids us do, we do—take good care of yourself, whatever happens." I could only bow my head and listen through my tears to his parting charge. I kept the words he charged me with firmly in my heart, stayed on to work with an easy mind in the Provincial Committee's underground organ, and hoped that we might be reunited soon.

Seven months later, sorrowful news came one piece on the heels of another. First Comrade Chen Tanqiu and his wife were transferred by order of the Central to work in the Central Organization Department in Shanghai; then, before long, Rao Shushi told me the grievous news that Xiyan had already died a heroic death. He said: "It was not quite right of Comrade Tanqiu to conceal the news that Xiyan had fallen..." Hearing such words all at once, I was simply stunned and could say nothing; holding back my grief, I went and asked Comrades Tanqiu and Qianzhi, and only then did they tell me the truth: in May 1928, while leading a peasant armed force in an assault on the town of Wan'an county, Xiyan had unhappily died a heroic death. Although they had learned of it long before, considering that I was sickly after childbirth and in poor health, and fearing that I could not bear such a heavy blow all at once, they had decided to conceal it for the time being, not letting me know, meaning to tell me at a suitable moment. Now that the Central had ordered the two of them to go and work in Shanghai, they were about to explain the truth to me before we parted. My grief may well be imagined. Although I had long understood the principle that to struggle is to have sacrifice, and had been prepared for it in my mind, when the thing suddenly befell me, how could I bear it? I grieved without measure for the fall of Xiyan, my revered good teacher, helpful friend, and close revolutionary companion, and at the same time could not bear the transfer of the Tanqiu couple, who had all along guided me warmly and cared for me as an elder brother and sister—it was truly a parting of the living and a parting by death, and I mourned without ceasing. But in the circumstances of underground work at that time, I could not set up a memorial tablet for Xiyan openly or weep and wail aloud; I could only weep silently and alone in the dead of night, my hot tears falling drop by drop on the orphan who did not yet know he had lost his father forever.

The Tanqiu couple, who knew of the charge Xiyan had given me at parting, comforted me, saying: "When we resolved to join the Party, we resolved to sacrifice everything for the Party. Comrade Xiyan has fulfilled his oath. The duty of us who die later is to wipe away the bloodstains and tears, stand up, and go on fighting. Though you were close revolutionary companions, you should now restrain your grief; you must not grieve too much and harm your own body. You must turn grief into strength, rouse your spirit, fulfill Xiyan's dying wish, and obey the organization's arrangements in everything. The child is not yet a year old; once he is weaned, slowly find a way for your parents to come and take him back and hand him on to the Wan family in Huangmei, so that under his grandparents' care the child may grow up safely and you too may go on working for the Party without anything to worry about behind you..."

I could only, through tears and stifled grief, part from Comrades Tanqiu and Qianzhi, loath to part. Though I had worked under their direct leadership and care only a year, they left in my heart a warm and deep impression I can never forget. Little did I imagine that, once parted at Nanchang, I would never see the two of them again.

Xiyan was just twenty-six when he fell. This was the heaviest blow I suffered in my life, and it left in the depths of my heart a wound that can never be fully healed. So young and vigorous a life had thus vanished, and his voice and face and his care and help for me in days past rose up in my heart again and again. I could only bear the pain and restrain my grief, and work and study all the harder, carrying on his unfinished cause through my dedication in revolutionary practice, so as to repay his kindness to me and console his spirit in heaven.

I went on staying at the Jiangxi Provincial Committee organ and holding to the work until, in December 1928, I received the Party Central's transfer order and left Nanchang for work in Shanghai. By this time the child could be weaned, and so, in accordance with Xiyan's dying charge, I wrote a letter to my parents at home, saying that I was going to Shanghai to go on with my studies, but that with a just-weaned child at my side it was not very convenient, and asking whether the family could send someone to take the child away and send him back to his father's old home to be raised by his grandparents. When my mother received the letter, she brought my second brother Huang Zhongyi (Zhangren) to meet me at Jiujiang as arranged (for I had already bought the boat ticket from Jiujiang to Shanghai). That day happened to be the bitter cold of year's end, wind and snow driving together, and three generations embraced and wept, loath to part. How could such a scene ever be forgotten! In that one year my milk, mingled with my tears, had nourished Jie'er; the little child did not know that his own father had already died for the revolution, or that his own mother, obeying the organization's arrangement, was about to leave him and set out on her road. But I knew that this parting would set us worlds apart, and my heart was of course grieved beyond measure. Yet to seek future happiness for all children is the revolutionary's duty. Through my tears I comforted my mother, believing there would surely come a day of the revolution's victory when mother and son would meet again. Mama too consoled me, saying the Huang and Wan families would surely treat Xiyan's own flesh and blood well, and telling me not to fret.

Just as we three—my mother, the child, and I—were loath to part, my second brother whispered in my ear that he had already joined the Communist Youth League, but that, fearing our parents would worry, he had not let them know. Hearing this, I could not help laughing through my tears, and warmly encouraged my second brother. My mother and second brother handed me a large kraft-paper envelope, on which was written: "To be delivered in person to Tang Mang, Bengbu garrison commander, from his sworn friend." Tang Mang was the eldest son of the martyr Tang Caichang, and he and his younger brother Tang Youren had both been my father's students; my father's students all respected him greatly. The content of the letter was very simple: "My daughter Shuyi (Zhangding) is going to Shanghai to study; please look after her in everything..." My mother told me: "Carry this letter with you against any mishap. If on the road you meet a soldiers'-and-police inspection, or run into any obstacle, you can take it out to prove that you are a person of no ordinary standing, and so pass through safely." My brother's cleverness and my father's love had planned it all thoroughly for me, never to be forgotten. Happily the journey was safe and the letter was never needed on the road; but it taught me that one could make use of progressive upper-class or social connections to cover our Party's underground work in the white areas. Later those of us who did underground courier work often used this method, putting into our own luggage a letter addressed to some senior Kuomintang official or general who had some connection, however slight, with our own family, so that when we met an inspection or fell under suspicion, taking it out could serve as a "pass" to get us clear.

From family letters I learned afterward that my parents and my brothers and sisters were all very fond of this child, and that even his name was given by my father: Wan Changjie. This was to commemorate his birth in Nanchang, the heroic city of the August First Uprising. The child stayed with the Huang family more than half a year, and then his grandfather Wan Yaofeng came in person to Wuhan to take his grandson back to their home village to raise. At parting my father even wrote out the child's "Eight Characters" of birth clearly on red paper and gave it to his grandfather, and blessed him to grow up safely and to be reunited with his mother soon.

In this way I faithfully carried out Xiyan's dying charge, entrusting the child to the old people of the two families for their loving care, so that he could grow up healthy in a safe environment; and I myself, with nothing more to worry about behind me, bore the pain of parting from my kin and resolutely set out on a new journey to meet still harder battles. Only after the nationwide victory of the revolution were flesh and blood reunited. Before long, because I was again placed under investigation and once more isolated for a long time, it was only after my rehabilitation, on the basis of a deepened understanding, that a new reunion was realized. Elder Sisters Zhang Yun and Yuan Puzhi, who had a deep revolutionary bond with me in the days of the great revolution, were both greatly glad, on seeing Changjie, that their old friend had a son, and said that we, having passed through every hardship and turn, with our blood tie unbroken, were a unity of Party spirit, human nature, and policy. I think this too must be the good fruit of my wondrous marriage and good cause!
