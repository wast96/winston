## Chapter Five. A Real Vault, a False Marriage

### 1. The Party Center's Most Secret Organ

On the morning of June 30, 2009, at around nine o'clock, a coach pulled up at the corner of Fuzhou Road and Yunnan Middle Road. The doors opened and out filed, one after another, Zhou Enlai's niece Zhou Bingde and nephew Zhou Bingjun; Liu Shaoqi's daughter Liu Aiqin and her husband Wo Baotian; Chen Yun's daughter Chen Weihua; Bo Gu (Qin Bangxian)'s son Qin Tie; Kai Feng (He Kequan)'s son He Ming; Pan Hannian's nephew Pan Guanru; and Xiong Changsu, daughter of Xiong Jinding and Zhu Duanshou — among others. The moment Xiong Changsu stepped down she went looking for the plaque on the wall. Her memory, she said, could not be wrong. Six years before, she had come to Shanghai, and had even had her photograph taken in front of that building, beside that marble plaque. She remembered clearly that the plaque was engraved with the words "Former Site of the Central Politburo Organ after the CCP's 'Sixth Congress.'" It carried, too, her father's name, and read: "After the 'Sixth Congress,' Xiong Jinding, who handled the Party Center's accounts in Shanghai, rented three rooms above the Shengli Hospital at No. 447 Yunnan Road in 1928, in the guise of a merchant, and there established the Central Politburo organ."

Xiong Changsu's words caught the attention of an old man who had just come back from his morning exercises in a nearby park. In Shanghai-accented Mandarin he told her: "That plaque you're talking about is in the little lane round the back. If you want to see it, I'll take you."

"In all these years," said Xiong Changsu, "this is the first time I've gone in by the back way, the first time I've walked into the lane behind that house. It twists and turns, and it's very, very narrow."

Xiong Changsu followed the old man who spoke Shanghai Mandarin and turned into the little lane behind the building. Close on their heels, not a step behind, came Zhou Bingde, Zhou Bingjun, Liu Aiqin, Wo Baotian, Chen Weihua, Qin Tie, He Ming, Pan Guanru, and the rest.

The old man asked her: "Who are you, all of you? Where have you come from? What are you here for?" Xiong Changsu said: "Our parents were all bound up with this house. I am the daughter of Xiong Jinding and Zhu Duanshou. Eighty years ago my parents lived in this very house." Astonishment crossed the old man's face; half believing, half doubting, he pressed her: "You're not making this up?" Xiong Changsu said: "Grandfather, do you know how many of our Party's central leaders once walked this lane, this narrow little lane? It was from here that our parents set out, breaking a trail through bramble and thorn, all the way to the final victory of the Chinese revolution." Zhou Bingde said: "Big sister, how well you put it." Xiong Changsu went on: "Without your uncle's introduction there would have been no marriage of my parents. Without their marriage, there would be no me. This is the place where my parents loved each other and fought shoulder to shoulder. I will never forget it. I would dearly love to write about the footprints in this lane, about the lamplight in this house."

Xiong Jinding — his original name was Jinting, his style-name Chuxiong — was born in 1886, a native of Changsha in Hunan. He joined the Party in 1927 and came to Shanghai in April 1928, where, under the direct leadership of Li Weihan of the CCP Politburo Standing Committee, he served as the Center's chief accountant; everyone teasingly called him "Boss Xiong."

Xiong Jinding's predecessor was Gong Yinbing. On February 28, 1956, Gong Yinbing wrote out a testimonial for Tang Liang. "In those days," he said, "I worked in the central organ; I had nothing to do with the courier lines, still less with distribution. In those days I handled only the finances. ... At the time I handled only the finances in the central organ, and because we were short of hands I might also see to whatever came up — sending someone to bring in a responsible comrade's family, sending someone off with money, and the like." On March 17, 2006, the Beijing Television program Lives in Science interviewed Gong Yinbing's son Gong Yuzhi — former deputy president of the CCP Central Party School, former deputy head of the Central Propaganda Department, and former deputy director of the Central Party Literature Research Office. Speaking of his father's early years, Gong Yuzhi said: "My father joined the Party in 1923 and, after the Great Revolution failed, began underground Party work. He was in touch with the Center and backed by the local organization, and had some contact with the upper levels too, but he was not the kind of underground member who did mass work down at the bottom. ... He did not speak of his own past; now and then he would let slip a line or two, but no more. That was the habit of the men who did underground work: none of them talked of such things. I had no sense of it at the time either, and never sat him down to ask, and he wrote no memoir. Later, when someone was compiling Biographies of Figures in CCP History and asked me to write one of my father, I found it very hard. I could not be at all specific, and his file, when I looked at it, was extremely thin. Drawing on my own memory, and on what others had said of my father in their writings, I gathered what I could, and so managed a piece of a bit over ten thousand characters, not quite twenty thousand, which went into Biographies of Figures in CCP History." (Gong Yuzhi, 2009) In volume 34 of Biographies of Figures in CCP History (1987) I found the "Gong Yinbing" that Gong Yuzhi wrote. On Gong Yinbing having "handled only the finances" at the Center, Gong Yuzhi wrote: "After the August 7 Conference, the CCP central organ withdrew to Shanghai, and Gong Yinbing too went to Shanghai to serve as the Center's chief accountant, working under Zhou Enlai and other comrades; and so began his more than twenty years of underground secret work. The post of chief accountant was charged with raising and managing the Party Center's funds, an arduous task in the conditions of the day. Later this work was taken over by Xiong Jinding." The most immediate reason, I think, that "later this work was taken over by Xiong Jinding" was that "in 1928 the Party's Sixth National Congress was convened in Moscow, and Gong Yinbing went there to attend it as a staff member."

During the Sixth Congress, Li Weihan and Ren Bishi stayed behind to hold Shanghai and run the work of the Center. With Gong Yinbing gone, Li Weihan asked the CCP Hunan Provincial Committee for a man, and had Xiong Jinding — then in Hankou — transferred to Shanghai to take over as the Center's chief accountant.

The organ on Yunnan Road (today Yunnan Middle Road) is said by almost everyone to have been the Central Politburo organ, set up by Xiong Jinding. First there was Xie Xiaonai, who wrote in A Devoted Heart (1986): "Xiong Jinding transferred to Shanghai, made contact with Li Weihan and the others, and thereafter worked in the Party's central organ as the Party Center's accountant. The Center also put him in charge of establishing the Politburo's secret working premises. Taking on the face of a 'merchant' for cover, he rented a two-story building behind the Tianchan Stage on Sima Road."

After that, Liu Menghua and Mo Xiuhua, in Xiong Jinding (1989), quoted A Devoted Heart directly, and added: "Xiong Jinding moved to Shanghai and found Li Weihan to re-establish his organizational ties. On the strength of his wealth of experience in managing money and his gift for making friends, the Party Center assigned him to serve as accountant of the central organ. His main task was to raise and manage funds, and to set up the secret premises where the Central Politburo met and worked, together with the addresses through which the Center kept in liaison with the localities. Once he had taken on the task, he searched everywhere for a house in the guise of a merchant, and at last found a two-story building at the corner of Sima Road and Yunnan Road (today the corner by People's Square on Fuzhou Road). Its number was No. 447 Sima Road (today Nos. 171–173 Sima Road); it stood behind the Tianchan Stage (today the Labor Theater), and to come and go one had to pass through an inconspicuous little alley."

Then came The Red "Boss" Xiong Jinding (Zhou Yan, 2007), which followed the account in Xiong Jinding and wrote: "On the strength of Xiong Jinding's wealth of experience in managing money and his gift for making friends, the Party Center assigned him to serve as accountant of the central organ, responsible for raising and managing funds for the Party, and further charged him with setting up the secret premises where the Central Politburo met and worked, along with its liaison points. Once he had taken on the task, Xiong Jinding searched everywhere for a house in the guise of a merchant, and at last settled on a two-story building behind the Tianchan Stage on Sima Road (at the Yunnan Road corner). The next day Xiong Jinding took Zhou Enlai — the Politburo Standing Committee member in charge of the Center's daily work — to see the house. Zhou Enlai was well satisfied, and had Xiong Jinding rent it in his own private name."

Last of all came the fullest account, in A Couple Who Braved Death Together: Xiong Jinding and His Wife, Zhou Enlai's Most Trusted (Dong Bingdi, 2007), which set the whole scene down in vivid detail: "Xiong Jinding, once settled in, opened up 'business' to the outside world in the guise of a Hunan cotton-mill owner and went out looking for a house. He tramped about for several days with nothing to show for it. This particular noon, black clouds gathered overhead, and before long it began to rain. Wandering the streets and lanes, without quite realizing it he came near the Racecourse on Sima Road, and turned into a little alley off one side behind the Tianchan Stage, where he saw a two-story building of brick and timber with a sign hanging at the door below: 'Shengli Hospital.' He stepped through the gate, yawning again and again — he had studied Chinese medicine and knew he had caught a chill — and so registered to be seen. As the physician took his pulse and asked after his condition, the two of them fell to talking of medicine and got on famously. Learning that his visitor wanted to rent, the physician introduced himself: 'My humble name is Zhou, Laisheng; I am the sublandlord of this building. The three rooms downstairs I use for the hospital; the three upstairs stand quite empty. If Boss Xiong does not mind how shabby the little alley is, do come and lodge in my poor house.' With that he took Xiong Jinding up to look at the second floor, where there were indeed three empty rooms and a kitchen besides, with doors front and back for easy coming and going — convenient to rent for offices and meetings, screened by the hospital below, and unlikely to draw notice: a fairly ideal spot. Xiong Jinding made up his mind, and, seeing that the rain outside had stopped, took his leave of Director Zhou, returned to his lodgings, and reported to the organization. The next day he went with Comrade Zhou Enlai to look the house over, and rented it in his private name."

In nothing he ever published did Xiong Jinding put it this way. Besides, when the Party went to ground in Shanghai and set up its premises, the finding of houses fell to the First Section (General Affairs) of the Central Special Branch, all handled together; even Zhou Enlai kept his hands off it, and Xiong Jinding had still less standing to meddle. How would the Center have let a man so newly arrived — before April 1928 Xiong Jinding had been only to Beijing and Wuhan, never to Shanghai — a man thick with a country accent, who could barely tell the Chinese city from the concessions, or inside the walls from out, go dashing all over town to find houses and set up premises?

Did Xie Xiaonai, Liu Menghua, Mo Xiuhua, Zhou Yan, and Dong Bingdi know that after He Jiaxing and He Zhihua sold out Luo Yinong and moved from the International Settlement to the French Concession, and wanted to rent a set of second-floor rooms on Rue Bourgeat (today Changle Road), the sublandlord, hearing them speak Sichuanese, shook his head hard? Did they know that in the Shanghai of those years what was scarce was not a suitable vacant room but "a solid shop to stand surety"? Hong Yangsheng, chief of the First Section of the Central Special Branch, was at his wits' end over just this; and Xiong Jinding, travel-worn, come from far away, a stranger to the place and its people, altogether in the dark — where were they supposing he would find a "solid shop" willing to "stand surety" for him? Not to mention that the "Shengli Hospital" specialized in venereal disease: had Xiong Jinding really caught a chill, really come down with a cold, he would hardly have gone looking for any "Director Zhou."

Xiong Jinding was the Center's chief accountant. His task was to mind the Center's purse. Setting up premises was not his proper work; it was not even, strictly, Zhu Duanshou's business. And the Center would never have muddled the two together and burdened him with both. When you are lying low, the worst thing you can do is try to grab everything in a single fistful. The division of labor, too, was better the finer and simpler it was kept. Zhou Enlai went so far as to draw up a list, found several women comrades, and let Xiong Jinding choose; Xiong Jinding settled on Zhu Duanshou. Zhou Enlai then had her transferred from Hankou to Shanghai and had them pose as husband and wife — and that only to satisfy the sublandlord, to make Xiong look more like a businessman of some standing, weighed down with a household, and not in order to cover his "setting up the secret premises where the Central Politburo met and worked, and the addresses through which the Center kept in liaison with the localities."

But the Party was underground, and its funds were limited. If even Luo Yinong, a member of the CCP Politburo Standing Committee, had to give over part of his own lodgings to double as the Central Organization Bureau's premises, and to put Qu Qiubai up there whenever he came to write his articles, then there was nothing so very startling in the fact that the "Fuxing" firm on Yunnan Road came, in the end, to double as the Central Politburo organ as well, where Xiang Zhongfa, Qu Qiubai, Li Lisan, Zhou Enlai, Xiang Ying, and the rest would now and then gather and talk.

First it was the "home" of Xiong Jinding and Zhu Duanshou; then the accounting section of the Central Secretariat; and after that the Central Politburo organ — and this was the second floor of No. 447 Yunnan Road (today Nos. 171–173 Yunnan Middle Road), from the autumn of 1928 to April 1931.

At seven on the evening of January 1, 1966, Zhou Enlai called in person to congratulate Xiong Jinding on his eightieth birthday. His gift was of an unusual kind: a statement in Zhou Enlai's own hand (Dong Bingdi, 2007), written directly onto an appeal that the Xiong Jinding–Zhu Duanshou couple had drawn up. Their appeal was addressed to Nan Hanchen (then chairman of the China Council for the Promotion of International Trade) and Wang Youlan (then deputy director of the Council's office), and its purpose was to make clear, on their daughter Xiong Changsu's behalf, the relationship between her and her half-brother Xiong Xiaosan.

The statement Zhou Enlai wrote ran as follows:

{v} The testimonial letter of Comrades Xiong Jinding and Zhu Duanshou is entirely factual, and I will further attest on their behalf: though Xiong Jinding and Xiong Xiaosan stand as father and son, from the failure of the Great Revolution (1927) onward the two were divided as enemies. During the civil-war years, the two comrades Xiong Jinding and Zhu Duanshou worked in the Party Center's most secret organ; they braved death, contributed greatly, and were most to be trusted. In the anti-Japanese years, in Chongqing, Xiong and Zhu also carried on united-front work upon Xiong Xiaosan, without much effect. When the War of Liberation began, the Center had Comrade Xiong Jinding write to Xiong Xiaosan urging him to come over; and though in the Huaihai Campaign Xiong Xiaosan received his father's letter, he remained obstinate and unrepentant, and fled when our troops attacked. Therefore not only has Xiong Changsu no relationship whatever with Xiong Xiaosan; the relationship of Comrades Xiong and Zhu themselves to Xiong Xiaosan is likewise perfectly clear. Let this matter be entered into Xiong Changsu's file, and let a copy of what we have written be sent to the Central Organization Department.

{v} Zhou Enlai

{v} January 1, 1966

For Xiong Jinding, Zhu Duanshou, Xiong Changsu, and the rest, Zhou Enlai's statement was, as Zhu Duanshou said, one where "every single [character] is a grain of gold." (Zhu Duanshou, 2007) Especially the words "during the civil-war years, the two comrades Xiong Jinding and Zhu Duanshou worked in the Party Center's most secret organ; they braved death, contributed greatly, and were most to be trusted"; and for a couple who had "found joy as fish in water for forty years, and trudged the long road raising the whip as one" (Xiong Jinding, 1987), they were more precious still.

### 2. A Weakness for Braised Lion's-Head Meatballs

In the summer of 1928, Zhu Duanshou arrived in Shanghai.

Xiong Changsu showed me her mother's autobiography, An Eighty-Six-Year Road Through Wind and Rain (2007), a piece she had come upon while sorting through Zhu Duanshou's effects after her death. Zhu Duanshou wrote:

{v} After I reached Shanghai I stayed at first in a hotel on Sima Road, and two days later moved into a tingzijian above a woodware shop on Sanma Road, which Jinding said was the premises where the Military Commission met. Only later did I learn that Wuhao (Zhou Enlai), head of the Organization Department, wanted to test whether I was fit to take charge of guarding an important organ. Before long I moved again, to the premises above the Shengli Hospital at Sima Road and Yunnan Road, where the Central Politburo Standing Committee worked and the Central Politburo met — three rooms in all, known to the outside world as the "Fuxing" firm.

Zhou Enlai's "testing" of Zhu Duanshou was thorough on every side.

At the outset, Xiong Jinding brought several people to the upper floor of the woodware shop on Sanma Road (today Hankou Road) for a meeting, and looked in on Zhu Duanshou while he was at it. She noticed one of them: a bowler hat, a long gown, a spirited face, sword-brows arched high. Very kindly he asked her where she was from, how many years she had been to school, what places she had seen. Hearing that she had been born in 1908 and was just twenty, he laughed a hearty laugh: "Well now, little sister — you're a full ten years younger than me." At his laugh, everyone laughed along. He seemed nothing like a leader, Zhu Duanshou thought; more like an easy, approachable elder brother. Only at lunch did she learn that the man was Zhou Enlai, and that the place was the Central Military Commission organ.

Zhu Duanshou's quickness and wit left a deep impression on Zhou Enlai.

Once, at a Military Commission meeting above the woodware shop on Sanma Road, Zhu Duanshou was keeping her usual watch on the stairs, on the lookout. Halfway through, Zhou Enlai reached out for the thermos on the table, and she caught the movement. She saw him lift it, give it a shake, pull the stopper, and cover the mouth with his palm — and at once she realized the thermos was empty. She went into the inner room, took it from his hand, went downstairs to fill it, and headed off to the laohuzao. Afterward Zhou Enlai used the incident as an example and singled her out for praise, saying she took the initiative, was quick to act, and observed things closely.

Before long Zhu Duanshou moved into the "Fuxing" firm, every inch the lady of the house, learning to play the "boss's wife."

Zhou Enlai made a point of talking with Zhu Duanshou, and charged her earnestly: "Little sister, don't make light of this place. This organ is very important; you must be bold and careful both, and work well together with Boss Xiong."

Zhu Duanshou's answer was not full of confidence: "I'm homegrown country stock; I've never seen anything of the wide world. I'm only afraid I'll slip up..."

Zhou Enlai laughed and said: "You needn't fret so, little sister. You're very sharp — I've noticed, and the comrades are pleased with you too. We know how you bore yourself in the days of the Great Revolution, or we would never have brought you to Shanghai to share this place with Boss Xiong. As for how to do the work better and truly keep this organ safe, I'll have the comrades concerned give you more lessons, and teach you hand over hand. You're young and clever; so long as you learn in earnest and work steadily, you won't fail the Party's hopes."

Within a few days people from the Central Special Branch came to Yunnan Road to find her. They taught Zhu Duanshou to give the warning signals and to shake off a tail. They taught her to bring up secret writing again with alum water. Those documents came up from the Central Soviet Area for the central leaders to read; ordinarily a full-time courier carried them, but now and then Xiong Jinding and Zhu Duanshou had to fetch them, bring them back, develop them, and copy them out. They also taught her how, when passing intelligence along, to handle a "frisking" by the concession patrolmen — a surprise search of the person in a public place.

Zhou Enlai knew Zhu Duanshou could not find her way, that the small streets and lanes and alleys, like so many spiderwebs, so many mazes, left her dizzy; so he found her a detailed street map and told her to study it whenever she had a moment. He looked after her down to the smallest thing: "When you go into a lane, you must first learn just how many ways in and out it has, and where each of them leads. Otherwise you'll blunder about blindly, and if trouble comes you'll never get clear."

Zhou Enlai also showed Zhu Duanshou the magazine Branch Life, edited by Deng Yingchao — an internal bulletin of the central organs' directly subordinate branch, printed in only seventy-five copies an issue. The directly subordinate branch was abbreviated the "Zhizhi," and went by the codename "Zhizhi" — the same two syllables, written with different characters. Deng Yingchao was then its secretary.

Zhu Duanshou took a great interest in the debate on "emptying the chamber pot" that Branch Life had opened. The one who first raised the "chamber pot" question was Hu Min, a staff member of the Central Secretariat. Hu Min pointed out that Shanghai's shikumen houses had no Western-style flush toilets, so that every household, first thing each morning, had to carry out its chamber pot to empty, and then scrub it. Who, then, ought to empty the pot, and who ought to scrub it, became a matter that called for theory. Why must the pot always be emptied by the woman comrade living in the premises? If men and women were equal, why could a false-married couple not take turns emptying one little pot? Take turns scrubbing it? Why must one be the dirty one — could they not all be dirty together? The smelly one — could they not all be smelly together? Zhou Enlai asked Zhu Duanshou what she thought, and she felt Hu Min had reason on her side and had it quite right: to insist the pot be emptied by the woman comrade did indeed express the feudal notion that man is high and woman low. Holding back a smile, Zhou Enlai pressed her on purpose: "Little sister, with a tongue as loose as that, aren't you afraid Boss Xiong will take you in hand?" Zhu Duanshou twisted her neck, her eyes wide: "Let him dare be feudal, and I'll rise in revolt against him!" Zhou Enlai could not help himself and burst out laughing. Baffled, Zhu Duanshou asked him back: "You're not laughing because I've said something wrong, are you?" Zhou Enlai said: "In theory you're both right, you and Hu Min alike. The trouble is this: in all of great Shanghai there must be, at the very least, several hundred thousand households. If everyone else has the woman empty the pot, and yours alone has the boss empty it, the boss wash it, the boss scrub it — or the boss and the boss's wife take turns emptying, washing, scrubbing — tell me, wouldn't it set people wondering? Mightn't the Kuomintang's agents fix their eyes on you and Boss Xiong on account of it?" "That—" Zhu Duanshou was struck dumb. She had not thought of this side of it. Seizing the moment, Zhou Enlai reasoned with her earnestly: "Perhaps insisting that the woman empty the pot is a disrespect to women, the leftover of feudal thinking at work. But secret work, the underground struggle, lying hidden in territory the enemy rules — the first thing is to be good at disguise, at keeping yourself concealed; the less notice you draw, the better. Since all over Shanghai it is the women who, at the crack of dawn, carry out the pots, empty them, and scrub them, we cannot step out of line, do something clever and different, and go our own way. Little sister, all you can do is follow the local custom and stoop to it."

Understanding dawned on Zhu Duanshou all at once, and she nodded and nodded.

From Xiong Jinding too Zhu Duanshou learned a great deal.

Xiong Changsu said: "Back home in Hunan, in the old days, my mother had only ever chopped firewood on the hills or gone to school to study; the cooking was all done by my grandmother, and of anything to do with the kitchen she hadn't the faintest idea. So when she reached Shanghai and was keeping the Yunnan Road premises with my father, he had to teach her everything. First he taught her how to buy food. For stewing beef, my father said, buy tendon; for stir-frying beef, buy the lean; for stir-frying pork, buy the loin — and my mother noted down each thing. He also taught her some home dishes. Trotters, he said, should be simmered plain, with no vegetable oil, and a little MSG added once they had cooked to tenderness — and this too she silently noted down."

Little by little Zhu Duanshou got the knack of it.

Learning that Zhou Enlai had a weakness for braised lion's-head meatballs, she taught herself to roll the meat in egg white, work in the seasonings and soy sauce and MSG, shape it into large meatballs to steam or deep-fry, and braise them with Chinese cabbage. Learning that Li Lisan loved ham-and-winter-melon soup, she taught herself to stew a good broth from a chicken, cook the winter melon in the broth, and, just before it was done, add slices of cooked ham.

Each month Zhu Duanshou also took living expenses to the family He Long had left behind in Shanghai. She wrote in her autobiography: "Their family lived at No. 17 Taichenli on Avenue Joffre in the French Concession; the rent being too dear, they moved in the winter of 1929 to Kangti Road, and later again to Ximen Road and to Shenchengli on Rue Ratard. In September 1932, when we came back to Shanghai from Honghu, they had moved to No. 162 Meishouli."

Upstairs at No. 447 Yunnan Road there were three rooms in all. Zhu Duanshou said in her autobiography: "The outer room was the office, the middle room the bedroom, the back room the kitchen. Xiong Jinding and I were husband and wife to the outside world and comrades within, and at night we slept apart. Each day, once I had eaten my supper and done the work I was to do, I would go into the inner room, bolt the door, and study on my own."

Zhu Duanshou loved to read the Tang poets, so Xiong Jinding bought her Three Hundred Tang Poems, and expounded to her Wang Bo's "Preface to the Pavilion of Prince Teng" and Bai Juyi's "Song of Everlasting Regret" and "Song of the Pipa," reading out for her, word by word, "The setting glow and a lone wild duck fly as one; the autumn waters and the boundless sky are of a single hue," and "Heaven is lasting and earth enduring, yet a time will come when both are gone; but this regret runs on and on, and will never reach an end." When she wanted to try writing something herself, Xiong Jinding, step by patient step, taught her the meters and tune-titles of the old verse: what was meant by five-character and seven-character lines, by regulated verse in five and in seven; what a "Southern Song," a "Fisherman's Song," a "Partridge Sky," a "Recalling the Maid of Qin" might be. Zhu Duanshou was heartily grateful for his careful guidance.

Zhu Duanshou and Xiong Jinding had in fact already known each other four years before. One was a student at the Changsha Women's Normal School, the other a trustee of it; they had set eyes on each other but never spoken. Four years on, Zhu Duanshou went from Changsha to Hankou and lodged with Xiong Jinding's nephew and the nephew's wife at the Hunan Guild Hall, and Xiong Jinding naturally came to see more of her, so that they understood each other better and better.

Liu Menghua and Mo Xiuhua wrote in Xiong Jinding (1989): "On the night of the Mid-Autumn Festival, 1928, after Zhou Enlai, Li Weihan, Deng Xiaoping, and other central leaders had held a meeting at the 'Fuxing' firm, Xiong Jinding and Zhu Duanshou laid on food and wine at a Sichuan restaurant on Sima Road and invited them to drink and admire the moon together. Meaning to further their marriage, Zhou Enlai proposed at the table: 'Comrade Jinding, you are our revolution's boss; and now the shop needs a clever boss's wife as well. I should think Comrade Duanshou very well suited to the part.' Li Weihan, Deng Xiaoping, and the others all warmly agreed. Zhu Duanshou said shyly: 'If the Party needs me to do it, I'll surely make a good boss's wife.' Zhou Enlai said happily: 'Then from now on we'll call you "little sister" no longer, but "boss's wife."' From that time the names 'boss' and 'boss's wife' passed into use inside the Party and out." In her autobiography Zhu Duanshou wrote with feeling: "Zhou Enlai did all he could to bring us together, and had Jinding stand host. On that very Mid-Autumn Festival day of 1928 we hosted a table of guests at the 'Taolechun' on Sima Road. Among the Politburo Standing Committee members present was Comrade Li Weihan. From then on Jinding and I went forward hand in hand and fought shoulder to shoulder for forty-five years."

Zhu Duanshou's account is plainly truer than the one given in Xiong Jinding. This is not because a person's own memory is more reliable than a later writer's reconstruction, but because in the Shanghai of 1928, in the thick of the White Terror, with the reek of blood and slaughter everywhere, how could our "central leaders" possibly, in a public place jumbled with every kind of person, have gone about with "revolution" on their lips and "comrade" at every turn?

### 3. Three Mentors and Friends

In April 1931, with a traitor risen within the Party, the "Fuxing" firm had to be given up, and Zhou Enlai left for the Central Soviet Area. Xiong Jinding went to West Hunan-Hubei to serve as head of culture and education and as secretary-general, and from Hankou he sent Zhu Duanshou two lines of verse: "Flesh and blood, parted so far; the root of feeling, cut too deep." Zhu Duanshou answered him with two lines from the Qing poet Yuan Mei: "On the banana leaves a scattering of rain, each drop shattering the ache of a parting heart."

In April 1932, Zhu Duanshou left Shanghai for Hubei, taking her elder daughter, Gengwu, to join Xiong Jinding. That autumn the West Hunan-Hubei Soviet fell altogether, and the whole family made their way back to Shanghai, to go on living underground and working for the Party — carrying documents, raising funds, keeping in touch with comrades and coming to their aid.

At around ten on the morning of April 8, 1933, Xiong Jinding, dressed as a doctor, went to take living expenses to He Long's family, walked into an enemy trap, and was seized and thrown into prison, where he was cruelly tortured for a confession; at the same time the concession patrolmen turned his home upside down.

Xiong Changsu pointed out to me a scar on her face and said: "This scar is from that raid on our home. I had only just been born then, and was in my mother's arms. When the patrolmen came to search, my mother resisted with all her strength and argued her case, and they shoved her to the ground, and she fell against the leg of the dining table. The table went over with her, and the glass sugar jar on it shattered to bits, and a shard of it cut a great gash in my face. The patrolmen also seized my sister by the hair and slammed her head against the cement floor, raising several big lumps on it — which is why my sister's memory was poor, her nerves were bad, and she lived only thirty-odd years."

Zhu Duanshou bore five children in all, and in the end only Xiong Changsu was left. In her autobiography Zhu Duanshou wrote in grief: "All five children were born while we were carrying on the underground struggle. Our living conditions, our means, our working conditions were all extremely hard; every day we worked under the strain of the White Terror, and so when the children came I had no milk to give them. My elder daughter Gengwu had only just turned one when the Yunnan Road premises were given up and Jinding and I were assigned, in turn, to work in the West Hunan-Hubei Soviet, and the poor child could only be left with fellow villagers to raise. Changsu I conceived in West Hunan-Hubei. She was frail from birth and ill-nourished after it, and was weaned before she was one. My third daughter we named Sangyu; she fed at a wet nurse's breast, took pneumonia at half a year, and died for want of medicine. The fourth child was eleven days old before we found a wet nurse, and died of the spoiled milk it was given. The fifth child, born in 1945, we named Liwu. In 1947 we went from northwest Shanxi to the Shanxi-Chahar-Hebei border region. In 1948 we went from there to Shijiazhuang, and before long Liwu took diphtheria and died. Gengwu, for her part, died of heart disease on December 9, 1961, having lived but thirty-one years in this world."

In mid-1946 Xiong Jinding and Zhu Duanshou returned to Shanghai, took lodgings on Zhu Baosan Road (today Xikou Road), and went to the Zhou residence on Rue Massenet (today Sinan Road) to visit Zhou Enlai. Zhou Enlai sent a car especially to take them on a tour of the old places, and to see the site of the "Fuxing" firm once more, wanting them to take a few photographs to keep. They went to the corner of Fuzhou Road and Yunnan Middle Road and found the Shengli Hospital gone, but the Xia Deji tailor's shop still there, and the old tailor named Xia still knew them. He told them: "You'd no sooner moved out than the police station's red van came. They said you were Communists, out to break the law on purpose. We all said you were good people — rich, but good. Boss Xiong was a proper man of business, doing big trade, easy with people, no airs about him, working his abacus and his accounts every day, honest and correct in everything." Old Xia's wife too said to Zhu Duanshou: "And you, Mrs. Xiong, were especially good to people — always taking my hand when you talked, warm and close, never once looking down on us. You were forever giving my children things to eat, and old clothes to wear. At every festival you'd buy them presents. There's no one under heaven better than the two of you."

In 1952, Mao Zedong presided over the drafting of the Constitution in Hangzhou, and wanted Deng Xiaoping and Li Weihan to take part together. Passing through Shanghai, the two went to look at the site of the "Fuxing" firm. They found a health station set up inside; the overall structure and the layout of the rooms were just as before, without great change.

In April 1980, Li Weihan came again to Shanghai and went once more to look, and found that "its structure had changed altogether, had become one unit in a row of ordinary dwellings, and even the way up from Yunnan Road was gone"; and so, with a sigh and much feeling, he wrote in his Recollections and Studies (1986): "From April 1928 until the new central leaders returned in September of that year, the meeting place was still the two upstairs rooms behind the Tianchan Stage on Sima Road, Shanghai. This secret organ had been set up in the winter of 1927 or the beginning of 1928 as the place for the Central Standing Committee to meet. The house was rented, and was kept by the couple Xiong Jinding and Zhu Duanshou (Xiong Jinding served as the Center's accountant). In those days the comrades going to a meeting climbed a staircase on Yunnan Road, on the west side of the Tianchan Stage, and could go straight up to the meeting room. Under the west-facing window of the room stood a small table, and at meetings Xiaoping did the minuting there. From the time it was set up until after the Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee in January 1931, this organ was never broken into. ... In those days, at nine each morning, Bishi and Xiaoping and I met to deal with daily business — not at this place, but at a shop upstairs not far off, a street away; also present were Xiong Jinding, the head of the internal courier line, and a few other responsible cadres — Li Fuchun, for instance, the caretaker head of the Jiangsu Provincial Committee, would sometimes come too."

On January 3, 1973, Xiong Jinding was taken gravely ill into hospital, and Zhou Enlai sent his own health physician and had Wu Jieping brought in to treat him besides. On the morning of January 15, Xiong Jinding lay at his last gasp, sunk in a coma. Zhou Enlai hurried to the hospital, and the moment he came in he asked Zhu Duanshou: "Has Old Xiong any word to leave?" Zhu Duanshou drew out a sheet of paper and handed it to him: "This is what Jinding left for you." Zhou Enlai opened it and saw two lines of verse written there: "I grieve that I have taken my leave of the land of joy; I pray you will long keep your body for the struggle." Xiong Changsu said: "At the time my father did not know that Zhou Enlai had already been found to have cancer, past all cure. He knew only that the 'Gang of Four' were tormenting Zhou Enlai cruelly. He was truly anxious for him. He was afraid Zhou Enlai would not be able to hold out. He was afraid China would be left without Zhou Enlai."

On January 24, 1973, Xiong Jinding let go of the world, at the age of eighty-seven.

On January 24, 1994, Zhu Duanshou departed this life, at the age of eighty-six.

Xiong Changsu said: "Two January 24ths, a full twenty-one years apart — I can only call it the will of Heaven. A loving husband and wife, and even in 'leaving' they 'chose' the very same day! ... My mother often said she had, in all her life, only three mentors and friends. The first was Mao Zedong. My mother knew him when she was young. Mao Zedong taught her the principles of revolution and guided her in revolutionary work. Mao Zedong was my mother's pathfinder. The second was Zhou Enlai. My mother worked under his leadership for sixteen years, learning from his word and his example, taking in by ear and eye, and gaining beyond measure. In the ten years of turmoil my parents were driven out of doors and cast adrift, and were even to be banished to Guangxi, thousands of li away. My mother was forced to write for help, asking Zhou Enlai to reach out a hand. Zhou Enlai at once wrote a note, found them a house, and had his staff instruct the neighborhood public-security station, over and over, to keep it strictly secret and on no account let out where my parents' new home was. The third was my father. They were husband and wife, and close comrades-in-arms as well. My mother was only twenty when she married my father, and understood nothing. My father cared for her in daily life, helped her in her work, and taught her in her studies. Together they fought through forty-five springs and autumns. While my father lived he wrote many poems for my mother. When he was gravely ill he charged her to give their joint photograph, taken on New Year's Day 1973, to Zhou Enlai and Deng Yingchao, as a keepsake of their forty-fifth wedding anniversary. In keeping with his wish, my mother wrote Zhou Enlai and Deng Yingchao a letter, and on the back of the photograph inscribed a poem: 'Sitting side by side to read, we grudged even that small distance between us; leaning each on each as we walked, we shared one shoulder. Fleeing in the Horse-Day terror, we tied the good bond; through mountains and seas our love grew only firmer with the years. Arm in arm over our books, our spirits soared; shoulder to shoulder on our walks, we delighted in the bright moon. Racing on for the revolution, we clean forgot the bitterness; serving the people, we found it all the sweeter. A hundred years, in the end, fell short of our vow to grow old together, and left a lasting regret behind us in the world.'"

The moment he received the photograph and the poem, Zhou Enlai had Deng Yingchao come at once to see the "boss" and the "boss's wife."

Gripping the "boss's wife's" hand, Deng Yingchao said only, softly: "Hold on, little sister!"

The hot tears welled up in Zhu Duanshou's eyes.

Zhu Duanshou knew that the ten-li foreign quarter, the "Fuxing" firm, was a knot of feeling in their hearts that would never fade.

Zhu Duanshou knew that from a "false marriage" to a true one, from the affection of teacher and pupil to the love of husband and wife — this, to put it in Xiong Jinding's own verse, was truly a case of "Strange indeed, white hair keeping company with youth; yet fish and water find their joy by nature. The common run, not knowing what true-hearted love is, laugh behind our backs at the muddle of our ages."
