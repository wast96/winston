## Gunsmoke

That night he saw ghosts.

It was the fifth hour of the night on the ninth of the fifth month, a clear night. Toshizō had come back from the fighting and was in his own room at the Goryōkaku main camp. Aware of a presence all at once, he got down from the bed. Straining his eyes, he looked at them. There were people before him. Not one, not two. They were in a crowd.

"A samurai leaves no vengeful ghost,"

so it has been said from of old. Toshizō too had believed it. Long ago, in the Mibu days, the abbot had once come running into the barracks to say that the ghost of a trooper who had cut open his belly was appearing in the graveyard of the Shintoku-ji.

Toshizō was not startled.

"That one can have had no samurai's spirit in him. If he is so rotten and clinging a wretch as to leave a vengeful ghost in this world, I will cut him down and send him off to the other world afresh,"

and Toshizō went to the graveyard, and stroking his sword, waited the whole night for the ghost to appear. In the end it did not come.

But now they were here in this room. The ghosts sat on chairs, or sat cross-legged on the floor, or lay sprawled at their ease.

All of them wore the dress of the Kyoto days, and had a carefree look.

Kondō Isami sat on a chair.

Okita Sōji lay resting his head on his elbow, looking this way. Beside him, Inoue Genzaburō, who had died of a ball at Fushimi, sat cross-legged and vacant, his farmer's face unchanged, watching Toshizō. Yamazaki Susumu was changing over a sword-guard in the corner of the room. How many other comrades were there besides?

(It seems I am tired.)

Toshizō thought so, sitting down on the edge of the bed. Since the fifth month came in he had led light troops out from Goryōkaku almost every day, striking again and again at the government forces as they pressed forward.

Sleepless nights had followed one on another. The phantoms in the room were owing to that, he supposed.

"What is the matter with you all?"

{j} Toshizō said to Kondō.

Kondō smiled without a word. Toshizō turned his eyes toward Okita.

"Sōji, your manners are as poor as ever,"

"It's because I'm tired,"

{j} said Okita, with his round, quick eyes.

"You too are tired?"

When Toshizō showed surprise, Okita fell silent. The lamplight did not reach him, but he seemed to be smiling. Every one of them is tired, Toshizō thought. Come to think of it, in the last days of the shogunate, while the eighty thousand hatamoto horse still lived their lives of idle ease, the "prestige" of that great edifice, the crumbling shogunate, had been held up by the hands of just this number of Shinsengumi troopers here present. What use it had been to history, Toshizō himself no longer well knew. But they had grown tired. Even become departed souls, the tiredness, it seemed, remained.

Toshizō was thinking such things vaguely.

"Toshi—tomorrow the town of Hakodate will fall,"

Kondō, opening his mouth for the first time, said it in a tone that was neither quite a prophecy nor quite a warning.

Toshizō ought to have been thunderstruck at this prophecy, but he no longer had the freshness left in him to be astonished at any turn of events. He was tired, his heart withered dry as a bone, it seemed.

"Will it fall?"

{j} he said, with a dull look. Kondō nodded,

"Behind the town of Hakodate there is what they call Mount Hakodate; that place seems thinly held. The government forces will secretly send surprise troops up it and attack the town at a stroke. The commander there, Nagai Genba-no-kami, is at bottom a clerk of sword and brush—a civil official—and will not be able to hold out,"

Strange, Toshizō thought. This was the very opinion he had again and again put to Enomoto Takeaki, urging that the mountain be made a fortress. But there had been neither the men nor the materiel.

──Then let me at least go myself.

{j} so he had said only that very morning. But Enomoto, uneasy at the thought of Toshizō being gone from Goryōkaku, would not allow it.

(Why—this is my own opinion.)

He turned over and rose up on the bed. He had been dozing, it seemed, still in his uniform and boots.

(A dream, then.──)

Toshizō got down from the bed and walked restlessly about the room. There, sure enough, was the chair on which Kondō had been sitting just now. And Toshizō crouched down at about the place on the floor where Okita had been lying sprawled.

He stroked the floor.

Oddly, the warmth of human skin lingered there.

(So Sōji, the rascal, did come after all?)

Toshizō lay down there and tried it, sprawling. Resting his head on his elbow, he tried mimicking Okita exactly.

Some half a koku after that, there came the sound of the door-knob turning, and Tachikawa Chikara came in. Tachikawa was a country samurai of Kai who had joined at the time of the Kōshū war; after the Restoration he took the name Takabayashi Kyokai, shaved his head, became a monk, and passed his life as abbot of the Jizō-in at Kasugai village in Higashi-Yamanashi district of Yamanashi prefecture. After Toshizō had become "Saishin'in-den Seizan Gihō Daikoji," it was this priest Kyokai who mourned for his soul all his life long.

"What has happened to you?"

{j} said Tachikawa Chikara in alarm, shaking Toshizō awake. Toshizō had fallen fast asleep again in the very posture of Okita just now.

"Sōji, the rascal, came. And Kondō, and Inoue, and Yamazaki, too...."

{j} said Toshizō, sitting up and crossing his legs, in a wonderfully cheerful voice.

Tachikawa Chikara thought, it seemed, that he had perhaps gone mad—for his look was utterly unlike the everyday Toshizō.

After this Toshizō gave order that the surviving troopers of the Shinsengumi be called.

They all came. Sawa Chūsuke the groom came too. All of them, though it was but twelve or thirteen men. Of these the most senior, from the Kyoto days, were the former Shinsengumi corporals Shimada Kai and Ozeki Masaichirō (Izumi), and two or three others; the rest were fellows enlisted at Fushimi, in Kōshū, at Nagareyama. Each was a commander of some grade in an infantry battalion.

"I thought we might have a drink,"

{j} and Toshizō had a cushion laid out for each on the floor, and held a drinking-party with dried squid for its only relish.

"What is the meaning of this feast?"

"A whim,"

Toshizō said nothing more. He was only in the highest good humor, and that, on the contrary, made them all uneasy.

The meaning of it came home to them all only the next morning. On the notice-board of the barracks the men who had gathered the night before had all been transferred at once. Every one of them was posted to the staff of the president, Enomoto Takeaki.

On this day, Hakodate fell.

Nagai Genba-no-kami and his beaten troops fled into Goryōkaku. Now the only strongpoints left were the Bentenzaki battery, the Chiyogatai battery, and the main camp at Goryōkaku.

"Hijikata-san, it was just as you had foretold. The enemy came from Mount Hakodate, they say,"

{j} said Enomoto, with a pale face. However Toshizō thought about it, it puzzled him. He had indeed foretold it, but he had not foretold the very day. Perhaps the dream of the night before had been no dream, and Kondō and the others had come on purpose to tell him of it.

"Tomorrow, let us go to Hakodate,"

{j} said Toshizō.

Enomoto put on a strange face. The town was already brimming over with government troops, was it not?

A council of war was opened.

Enomoto and Ōtori urged holding within the walls. Toshizō, as ever, was silent; but as the vice-president Matsudaira Tarō pressed him persistently for his opinion, he said, curtly,

"I shall go out and fight,"

{j} that alone. Ōtori Keisuke, the army commissioner, said, his face laying bare his ill feeling toward Toshizō,

"That, Hijikata-kun, is no opinion at all. This is the seat of a council of war. We are not asking what you will do; we are conferring on what we ought to do,"

Being a man who later became a diplomat, he was clear in his logic in any case whatever.

"You,"

{j} said Toshizō.

"hold to the argument for defense within the walls. A defense within the walls is a thing one does in order to wait for reinforcements. Where in all Japan have we an ally? In this case there is no room for a council of war—none, but to go out and fight.──"

he said, with irony in it. A defense within the walls, Toshizō suspected, was but a preliminary move to surrender.

Matsudaira Tarō and Hoshi Juntarō and others sided with Toshizō, and it was resolved to mount an operation to retake Hakodate at the following dawn.

By chance, the government staff headquarters too had fixed this same day for their general assault on Goryōkaku.

***

On the day itself, when Toshizō went out from the gate of Goryōkaku, heaven and earth were still dark. It was the eleventh of the fifth month of Meiji 2.

Toshizō was on horseback.

Those who followed him were but fifty men. He had drawn out only a single section each from the Gakuheitai of the former Sendai domain—said to be the strongest Western-drilled unit in Enomoto's army—and from the shogunate's Denshū officer corps.

At this recklessness Matsudaira and the others were, in truth, astonished. But Toshizō

{j} "With a few men I shall bore a hole in the government forces like an awl and drive into Hakodate. You, gentlemen, bring up all the strength and the ammunition-train you have, and widen that hole,"

{j} he said.

Toshizō had already made up his mind that on this day, at this battlefield's boundary, he would go to Kondō and Okita. Were he to live on carelessly a few days more, it was plain he would become, with Enomoto and Ōtori and the rest, one of the surrendered.

(Let them surrender. As for me—can I surrender to the Satsuma and Chōshū men who have been my enemies in this long brawl?)

so he thought. If he could, he wished, like the brawler he was, to plunge deep into the enemy's ranks and die with his corpse facing forward.

Toshizō advanced with three gun-carriages at the head. To put the guns in front was the common sense of that day, when their range was short.

On the way they passed through a wood. Someone sprang out suddenly from the dark shade of the trees and caught the horse's bridle-ring. It was Chūsuke the groom.

"Chūsuke—what are you at?"

"They have all come. They say they will die as Shinsengumi,"

Looking, he saw Shimada Kai and all the men with whom he had drunk the parting cup two nights before, every one of them there.

"Go back. Today's fight is more than you sword-fencers can handle,"

{j} and he urged his horse on. Shimada and the Shinsengumi broke into a run, ringing the horse's flanks.

The sun rose.

As though they had lain in wait, the government's mountain guns of four kin and their ship's guns began to fire, shaking the heavens with their roar.

From their own Goryōkaku, too, the fortress guns of twenty-four kin and the ship's guns began to spit fire. Behind Toshizō's corps came the corps of Matsudaira Tarō, Hoshi Juntarō and Nakajima Saburōsuke, and their towed mountain guns, rushing forward, began to fire.

In an instant heaven and earth were wrapped in gunsmoke.

Around Toshizō shells burst without pause and iron fragments flew, yet this man's corps only quickened its pace.

On the way there was a virgin forest.

When they had run through it, they met with the government vanguard, about a hundred men.

The enemy had begun to lay their gun on the road.

Toshizō kicked his horse's flank, ran like a gale, and from the saddle cut down the gunners.

There the Shinsengumi, the Gakuheitai and the Denshū officer corps rushed in, and as they fought, mixing gunfire and cold steel, the corps of Matsudaira, Hoshi and Nakajima rushed up and put the enemy to a rout at a single blow.

Toshizō pressed on farther. On the way he fell in with government troops who seemed to be Tsugaru men, mingled Japanese and Western dress; but with his three guns and volleys of Minié rifles he drove them off, and at last, at noon, came up short of the Ippongi barrier on the outskirts of Hakodate.

The government forces had gathered their main strength here, and, ranging their guns and drawing up their firing-lines, opened a furious fire.

The guns and rifle-companies of Matsudaira's corps and the rest came forward too and deployed, and

{j} ──that hard fight had no like in past or present──

{j} it became a battle fierce enough to be so spoken of.

Toshizō, his bared blade shouldered, directed the fight furiously from horseback, but the tide of battle was against them. The enemy were mainly the battle-hardened Satsuma and Chōshū, with the troops of the other domains held in reserve, and showed no sign of yielding a single step. And by the time they had come this far, the ship's-gun fire loosed from Hakodate harbor grew ever truer in its aim, and Matsudaira Tarō and the others were, rather, desperate merely to keep their own force from breaking.

Toshizō saw that there was now no course but a charge with cold steel. Seeing, luckily, that the fire from the enemy's left wing was sluggish, he turned back to his men.

"I am going to Hakodate. Most likely I shall not return to Goryōkaku again. Let only those who have grown weary of living in this world come after me,"

and at the words, as though drawn by that voice, troops came running up from the corps of Matsudaira, of Hoshi, and of Nakajima as well, until in a moment they were two hundred, and just so, without even forming ranks, they began a shouting charge against the enemy's left wing.

Toshizō advanced, leaping and leaping over the heads of the enemy, mowing them down to left and right with one-handed strokes.

There is no word for it but a demon.

There the government reserves came running up, and the moment the breaking of the left wing was barely held, it was the Goryōkaku army, on the contrary, that fell into rout.

Farther than this they could not advance.

But one horseman alone, Toshizō only, went on. Calmly, at his ease, he advanced through the powder-smoke.

The other corps tried to follow him, but, driven back by the wall of government troops, could not advance a single step.

All of them, dumbstruck, watched the mounted figure of Toshizō go. Not the Goryōkaku army only: the officers and men of the government forces, firing from where they lay flat on the ground, felt something overawing in the figure of this enemy general passing at his ease through the midst of their own army, so that no one came near, and they forgot even to turn their muzzles on him.

Toshizō goes on.

When at last he came to the Eikoku Bridge at the edge of the town of Hakodate, a Chōshū unit of reinforcements, come at the double from the direction of Jizō-machi, marked this general officer in the unfamiliar French-style uniform, and an officer came forward and

{j} "Where is it you are going?"

{j} asked.

"I go to the staff headquarters,"

said Toshizō, narrowing those double-lidded eyes that were said to hold a menace when he smiled. He meant, of course, to charge in single-handed.

"What is your name?"

The officer of the Chōshū unit thought he might perhaps be some newly appointed staff officer of Satsuma.

"My name?"

Toshizō thought a moment. But to name himself army commissioner of the Hakodate government—for some reason he did not wish to.

"Hijikata Toshizō, vice-commander of the Shinsengumi,"

and when he said it, the government men were as astounded as though they had seen a dragon go winding by in broad day.

Toshizō began to walk his horse forward.

The officer had his men scatter, made them ready to fire, and then asked again,

"What is your business at the staff headquarters? If you are an envoy of surrender, there is a proper form for it,"

"Surrender?"

Toshizō did not slacken his horse's pace.

"I have just told you. If the vice-commander of the Shinsengumi has business at the staff headquarters, it is only to go and cut his way in,"

With a cry the whole force took up the posture to fire.

Toshizō kicked his horse's flank and leapt over their heads.

But when the horse set its feet to the ground again, the body of Toshizō on the saddle had, with a fearful sound, tumbled to the earth.

Fearing him still, no one came near.

But when Toshizō's black woolen coat began to grow wet with blood, then for the first time the Chōshū men knew that this enemy general had become a corpse.

Toshizō was dead.

Six days after this, Goryōkaku surrendered and opened its gates. Of the eight ministers—the president, the vice-president, the army and navy commissioners and the rest—Toshizō was the only one killed in battle. Of the eight ministers, as many as four were later pardoned and served the new government: Enomoto Takeaki, Arai Ikunosuke, Ōtori Keisuke, Nagai Naomune (Genba-no-kami).

His body was buried at the Nōryō-ji within the town of Hakodate; but besides this, a monument was raised at the Shōmyō-ji of the Jōdo sect in the same town, by the hand of Yūjirō, the Kōnoike clerk.

The moving spirit was Yūjirō, but the money was given by the merchant houses of the whole town. The reason was one thing only: that Toshizō had left behind him in Hakodate a curious "good deed." In the last days of Goryōkaku, at Ōtori's proposal, they had meant to have the townsfolk of Hakodate contribute the costs of the war.

"Water on a burning stone,"

{j} Toshizō opposed it.

"Though Goryōkaku perish, this town will remain. If we levy so much as a single sen, the impression that ours was a government of tyranny will not fade even to later ages,"

At that one word, the matter was dropped.

The Buddhist name on the grave-marker is Kōchō'in Shaku Gisō, and the secular name Hijikata Toshizō Yoshinao—with one character mistaken. But on the one the townsfolk of Hakodate raised, the secular name is correctly Yoshitoyo, and the Buddhist name Saishin'in-den Seizan Gihō Daikoji.

At Aizu too there seems to have been one among the domain samurai who held services for Toshizō, and a Buddhist name, Yūtō'in-den Tesshin Nichigen Koji, remains.

The Hijikata family learned of his death in battle in the seventh month of Meiji 2, by the visit of Toshizō's page Ichimura Tetsunosuke. The next year, the third, the groom Sawa Chūsuke came calling and learned the Buddhist name, and taking the "Saishin'in-den..." one for the mortuary tablet, held services with it.

Ichimura Tetsunosuke's visit seems to have been a dramatic one.

In the rain, in the guise of a beggar, he stood before the gate of the Hijikata house at Ishida village on the outskirts of the Hino post-station in Bushū. There was a rumor at the time that the inquiry into the Hakodate rebel army was severe, and it was doubtless for that reason that he had come stealing in in such a shape.

"I should like to be allowed to pray before the household altar,"

{j} he said; and when they let him in,

{j} "Commander.──"

{j} was all he called out before he lay prostrate, weeping, for about an hour, they say.

The Hijikata and Satō families sheltered Tetsunosuke for some three years, and when the heat of the world's rumor had cooled, sent him home to Ōgaki in the company of a neighbor named Anzai Kichizaemon. That he later left his home country and died in battle in the Seinan War has already been told. It may be that Toshizō's madness had passed into this young man, so that the frenzy of the Boshin days never, in the end, subsided in him.

Oyuki.

She died at Yokohama.

Beyond that, nothing is known. About the time of the green leaves in Meiji 15, there was a small woman who left an offering for the services of Toshizō at the Shōmyō-ji in Hakodate and went away. When the temple monk asked her relation to the dead man, the woman put on a smile that seemed to soak through one.

But she said nothing.

It was Oyuki, no doubt.

In the early summer of that year sun-showers fell often at Hakodate. On that day too, perhaps, a bright rain was falling, one fancies, upon the flagstones of this temple.

(The End)
