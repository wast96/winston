### Mourning-Cloth Debts

Among the loans of the cut-throat usurers, besides "stamp-money," the "rubber ball," and the "jiao-a-night," there is yet another kind called the "mourning-cloth debt." Those who borrow on a mourning-cloth debt are all of the young-master sort: their fathers at home have money enough, but love it as they love their own lives and will not lightly let a son squander it—and so the sons cannot play the spendthrift, and not to play the spendthrift is to forfeit their standing as young gentlemen. Thus they are driven to plead with people, or by roundabout means to raise loan upon loan, and on the promissory note it is set down that the sum is to be repaid, with interest, only when the old man has "stretched out his legs" and the son has put on the hemp of mourning—which is why it is called a "mourning-cloth debt."

The cut-throat usury of the mourning-cloth lender is much like the stamp-money trade, only the method differs. Say you borrow five hundred dollars of him: at the handing-over, no more than three hundred and a little over comes into your hand, for the interest runs heavy and some years of it are docked in advance, and there are introduction fees and handling fees besides, all cut off on the spot. Yet when the day comes to repay, not a single copper may be short, for the note is written out clear as clear and plain as plain, and the borrower has signed it in his own hand—so that welsh on it though you might, there is no welshing now.

You whole tribe of fathers who hold money dearer than life and would die scraping it together—while your two legs are yet unstraightened, take a look at what your fine young sons are about, and you may as well wake up while there is time!
