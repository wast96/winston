### Flushing Birds

The keeping of caged birds is by rights a pastime of the leisured class, and at bottom a thing steeped in the creed of pleasure. Those who keep birds are mostly of a few sorts: opera actors, idle hoodlums with no employment, and the "young bosses" who live at ease off their fathers (that is, the junior masters of the shops). But once a man keeps a bird he must air it every day. No sooner has the sky grown light than he takes up the birdcage and carries it out beyond the bamboo paling of the Racecourse, holding it in his hand or hanging it on a branch—and this is what is called "flushing the birds." Then a hundred birds sing out together in a twitter of song, and the bird-keeper, all his mind bent upon it, listens rapt to their calling, with quite the air of a man who lets the whole world go and asks only what is sweet to the ear.

At the west end of Sixth Avenue there is a teahouse called the Longyuan, opened now these several decades, and it has long since become as good as a club for the bird-keepers. Within, inside and out, the birdcages hang thick; for after airing his birds a keeper must still repair to the Longyuan to drink a few mouthfuls of tea and pass a few words of chat and take his pleasure a while before turning for home. There are besides, in the City God Temple, two teahouses, the Leyi and the Shangle, which serve like the Longyuan as gathering-places for the bird-keepers: below they run a snack-shop, but above the cages hang in crowds, for the keepers who live in the Nanshi quarter all air their birds within the City God Temple. Other spots for flushing birds there may be, but they are always thin and scattered, and fall far short of these two, the Racecourse and the City God Temple.
