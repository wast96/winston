## Chapter 12. In Seclusion by West Lake

The distribution office for the publications of the World and China Society, organized by Chen Zhigao, Sun Xiaocun and others, was set up in the porter's lodge of the Yongye Bank on Jiangxi Road in Shanghai. This was because Chen Zhigao's cousin Chen Zhenjiu was a deputy manager at the Yongye, and another cousin, from the second branch of the family, served there as head of general affairs. The manuscript reader for World and China was Sun Xiaocun, the editor was Chen Gaoyong, and the proofreader was Chen Gaoyong's student Cheng Zhongwen; all of them came to the distribution office regularly. Only a few days after the old master of the Chen family passed away, Chen Gaoyong and Cheng Zhongwen were suddenly arrested at the distribution office on Jiangxi Road.

When the trouble broke, Sun Xiaocun, Li Nanxiang and a few others talked it over and decided that anyone connected with the World and China Society should find a way to lie low for a time. Chen Zhigao was notified at once. He was keeping vigil at home over his father's coffin and could not go out on any activity, but because the editorial office had been set up in his study, they had to guard against the police coming to search it. On hearing the news, the elder Madame Chen took her maid Qian Sao and packed a great many of the Marxist-Leninist books in Chen Zhigao's room—along with a French edition of *Das Kapital* that his closest schoolfriend had sent him from France—into two large trunks, carried them down to the ground floor, and stowed them under the bed where the old lady slept. The old master's coffin was still lying in the downstairs parlor at the time, and it was reckoned that even if the police came to search, they would not disturb the old lady. Chen Zhigao, for his part, burned all the letters between himself and his schoolmates that might arouse suspicion. The old lady did not know my true identity, but she knew that I had dealings with the people of the World and China Society, so she told me to go and hide for a while at the home of one of her younger sisters, Aunt Wang. I slipped quietly back to Shenjiang Hospital to report the situation to Director Liu, who told me not to come to the hospital any more, because it was very close to both the Chen and the Wang households: if anyone were tailing me and this hospital were exposed, it would bring down a great calamity. When I returned, I said that my own former lodgings and Aunt Wang's house were probably not safe either. Sun Xiaocun and Li Nanxiang then suggested that I go to Hangzhou to lie low for a time, since Sun Xiaocun was preparing to go to his father-in-law's home in Hangzhou and try from there to rescue Chen Gaoyong and Cheng Zhongwen. So once again a report and request for instructions were sent up to the higher Party organization through Director Liu, and with the organization's approval I was permitted to go to Hangzhou to lie low until the tense situation eased, and then return to Shanghai. Fortunately there was a telephone at Chen Zhigao's law office, so we could stay in touch when anything came up. And so, in the spring of 1932, I moved to Hangzhou and went into hiding for a time. When the mourning period of a hundred days for the old master was complete, on the day of the funeral procession I slipped secretly back to Shanghai, rode in the old lady's motorcar, and joined the cortege, discharging my duty as an adopted daughter. I recall that the Haining native-place association in Shanghai bestowed on the old master a private posthumous title, "Mr. Suhui," praising him for administering the law with fairness and rigor and for his benevolence toward the people. That day I went again to Shenjiang Hospital, and Director Liu said that the situation in Shanghai was still very tense and that I should stay hidden in Hangzhou for the time being; as to when I could return to Shanghai, the organization would notify me in due course. So after seeing the old master's coffin to its burial, I went back to Hangzhou.

In Hangzhou I first stayed at the West Lake Rest-and-Recuperation Society, introduced by Sun Xiaocun, and later moved to the Yu Mansion beside Solitary Hill. The Yu Mansion was a three-story building that Zeng Guofan had paid to have built for Yu Yue (styled Quyuan), the celebrated Qing-dynasty master of classical learning; it had small gardens front and back and stood within the scenic zone of West Lake, with beautiful surroundings. Next door was a Buddhist monastery, and beyond that the Taihu Hotel and the famous Louwailou restaurant. At that time the Yu Mansion was the villa of Yu Yue's descendant, the Hanlin academician Yu Biyun. Yu Biyun was the father of the famous "Redologist" Yu Pingbo; his style name was Jieqing, and he had passed the metropolitan examination, placed third (tanhua) in the palace examination, and been appointed to the Hanlin Academy—a very well-known poet of the late Qing. Yu Biyun did not often live in the Yu Mansion, and the vacant rooms were opened to the public, offered to certain well-known members of society to rest and stay in for a time, with meals provided by the Taihu Hotel next door. I was given a room on the third floor, very quiet and secluded.

When I moved into the Yu Mansion, the Hanlin academician Yu Biyun had just returned from sweeping the ancestral graves at his old home in Huzhou. After I had lodged in the Yu Mansion for a while, Chen Zhigao introduced me and I paid my respects to him. In the course of our conversation I mentioned that my own father, the venerable Yingchu, had been a fellow student and close friend of Tan Sitong; the Hanlin academician thought highly of my coming from a scholarly family, and gladly took me on as a pupil, teaching me the rules of classical poetry and lyric verse. Although I had loved to recite poetry since childhood, and had once copied out for my father his own work, the *Cilin Yunzhun* (*Prosodic Standard for the Forest of Lyrics*), my father had never taught me the fundamentals of poetry, nor had I ever studied or pondered them on my own; only now did I formally become Yu's disciple and learn the tonal patterns and rhyme schemes of poetry. I remember that four of us became his pupils at the same time: besides me, there was Mr. Shen Shijia, the famous Shanghai traditional-ink painter, who passed away in the summer of 2001 (I studied painting under him at the same time); and a Mr. Yang and a Miss Sun, with whom I later lost touch. The daughter of the maid who had come with Yu's wife in her dowry was named Jiang Xueying; she later became Yu Pingbo's goddaughter and pupil, and she called me "shishu"—junior aunt in learning. For all the earnest instruction and patient guidance of these good teachers and friends, my progress in poetry and painting was very slow, and a year later I returned once more to my post in the underground struggle, with even less heart for composing poems or painting. Not until the victory of the War of Resistance did I, together with Chen Zhigao's sister Baoqi and Pan Liying, the wife of my brother-in-law Tingxiang, again take up the study of poetry as pupils of the noted Jiangnan poet Mao Heting; I left few manuscripts, under the pen name "Master of the Studio of Double Cultivation." During my later investigation, whenever I was moved by feeling I poured it out in poems and lyrics, collected under the title Anthology of the Hidden Orchid, which to this day has never been published. Only in the 1980s, after the review of my history was concluded and I was completely rehabilitated, did I, in my old age, take up the brush again and learn to write poems and fill in lyrics—but that is a story for later.

Here I need to explain the several times I changed my name. My childhood school name was Huang Zhangding, style name Shuyi. In 1926, when I joined the revolution, I changed my name to Huang Mulan, which is why the old comrades of the underground Party all called me Mulan. But when I entrusted the rescue of arrested comrades to Chen Zhigao and his father, and when I became the Hanlin academician Yu's pupil, I used the name Huang Shuyi. In 1932 I changed my name again, to Huang Dinghui, and I have used that name ever since.

Why did I take this name? Because, as my mother told me, my father, in his dying days, still worried greatly about me. My father, who had made a considerable study of Buddhism, had once said: "Ding'er"—meaning me—"was born with an innate wisdom from a former life, but her karmic burden from that life is also heavy. It would be best if she could take refuge in the Buddhist faith, so that the Buddha's protection might let her cultivate both blessing and wisdom. The reason I did not teach her to write poetry is that, since ancient times, talented women have all had thin fortunes. You"—meaning my mother—"had better let her take refuge in the Buddha, and pray that she cultivate both blessing and wisdom!" As it happened, that very day I had gone with Chen Zhigao and Li Nanxiang to drink tea at Hupao Temple (also called Dinghui Temple) and to discuss how to rescue Chen Gaoyong. As we talked, I showed them the letter from home my mother had written, which mentioned my father's dying wish that I take refuge in the Buddhist faith. They said: "Since we are drinking tea at Dinghui Temple today, why not simply change your name to Dinghui!" At that time, to honor my late father, I had also taken advantage of the rare leisure of lying low in Hangzhou to visit the ancient temples and famous monasteries around West Lake, and I felt that the virtue of those cultivated eminent monks—single-mindedly devoted to the Buddha, delivering all living beings from suffering—had something in common with the spirit in which we now devoted ourselves to the revolutionary cause, willing to struggle and sacrifice to win liberation for the countless suffering people; at the same time, taking a name of strong Buddhist coloring was of considerable use in covering the rescue work I was engaged in. After some thought, I gladly adopted their suggestion, and from then on changed my name to Huang Dinghui. So, apart from my own family and the people of Chen Zhigao's household, who knew my real name was Huang Zhangding, style Shuyi, everyone who came to know me after 1932 knew me only as Huang Dinghui; while the old underground-Party comrades from the time of the Great Revolution down to the early 1930s all called me only Huang Mulan. Few of these old comrades are still living now, but when they write their memoirs, or articles commemorating the martyrs Wan Xiyan and He Chang, they still refer to me by the name Huang Mulan. It was only in 1993, in her book *My Father Deng Xiaoping*, that Maomao publicly revealed that the Party's underground worker Huang Mulan was Huang Dinghui.

I lived in hiding in Hangzhou for close to a year. No one knew my true identity; they knew only that I was the Hanlin academician Yu's pupil, and took me for the daughter of a wealthy family, purely at leisure there to enjoy the hills and waters. And indeed, I did take that rare opportunity to enjoy, one by one, the scenery of West Lake through all four seasons: in spring, strolling the Bai and Su causeways among the alternating willows and peach trees in their bewitching splendor; in summer, rowing out at dawn with Jiang Xueying in the Yu family's own little pleasure-boat among the lotus blossoms, delighting in the fresh fragrance of the dew-moistened lotuses; in autumn, going to Manjuelong to enjoy the sweet osmanthus and eat chestnuts; in winter, climbing Solitary Hill to gather plum blossoms. The beauty of West Lake in its four seasons left a deep impression on me. In ordinary times I studied poetry with Teacher Yu and painting with my senior fellow-pupil Shen Shijia. I especially remember that the first long poem Teacher Yu taught me to read was Wu Meicun's "Song of Chen Yuanyuan," which contains the two lines: "Bitter grief that the whole army donned white mourning; / in a wrathful surge that lifted his cap, all for a lovely woman." Thinking that since ancient times lovely women have often had thin fortunes, and that my own first love, Wan Xiyan, had already fallen unhappily as a martyr; that my second husband, a member of the CCP Central Committee, had gone to the Soviet area more than half a year before—it had been agreed that someone would come to fetch me, yet no one ever came; that my father had died, and that my two children were scattered far apart, their whereabouts unknown—I do not know how many tears I shed, and I grew more haggard by the day, in the pain of longing for my kin and the pain of my own circumstances. People knew only that I wept from longing for my family; they did not know that in my heart I also concealed the sorrow of parting and mourning. On the surface I lived in seclusion at the Yu Mansion, seemingly steeped in a pampered life of composing poems and painting; but in reality, drawing on the Chen family's official-gentry standing and the literary elegance of the Yu household, this not only served objectively as protection but also helped me further my associations with upper-class people of justice, which was naturally of benefit to the rescue work.

During the time I was lying low in Hangzhou, men from the French Concession police did come to search the Chen house, but his younger brother Chen Yingu handled them very cleverly. Because the family had heard the wind in advance and prepared, the police could find no "contraband" Marxist-Leninist books or periodicals in Chen Zhigao's study; and the interpreters, who were on very good terms with Cao Bingsheng and knew that Cao and Chen Zhigao were close schoolmates, all spoke up for Chen Zhigao during the questioning. So, apart from Chen Gaoyong and his student Cheng Zhongwen, who had been arrested at the distribution department, no one else was implicated. Although the World and China Society was forced to close and its publications were banned, the friendship among Chen Zhigao, Sun Xiaocun and the others was never broken.

Chen Gaoyong, who had been arrested, was a professor at Jinan University. Chen Zhigao, Sun Xiaocun and Li Nanxiang had all been classmates at Aurora University, where they had another close friend among their fellow students, Li Xinyang, who had just returned from studying in France and was working as an assistant lawyer in the office of the famous barrister Zheng Yuxiu. Zheng Yuxiu was Shanghai's first woman lawyer, qualified to appear as defense counsel in the Mixed Court. When she was studying in France, someone had even written a novel with her as the heroine, A Record of Overseas Splendors. At that time she had many suitors, and because her standing in the legal profession was so high, she would generally not take on minor cases. After talking it over, Chen Zhigao and the others entrusted Li Xinyang with asking Zheng Yuxiu to come forward and defend Chen Gaoyong. Because the old master of the Chen family had once done Zheng Yuxiu a favor when she was first taking up practice in the Mixed Court, she not only agreed to take the case but waived her fee, willing to defend Chen Gaoyong without charge. Relying on her prestige and influence in Shanghai's political and legal circles, the defense and hearing of the case cost little effort, and after Chen Gaoyong and the others had been held for a few months, they were released unconditionally. Although it was a release without charge, a guarantor was still required, and that guarantor was the deputy manager of the Yongye Bank, Chen Zhigao's cousin Chen Zhenjiu. In those days, if a bank or a prominent barrister stood surety, only one guarantor was needed; otherwise two were required. After the case was settled, Chen Zhigao, Sun Xiaocun, Li Xinyang and the others came to Hangzhou for an outing, and in front of the Yanxia Cave they saw two boulders. One was shaped very like an elephant, the "trunk" especially so, and amusingly there was a smaller stone within the great stone elephant, like a baby elephant. Chen Zhigao inscribed it "Xiangxiang," with three meanings in mind: first, that the stone resembled an elephant; second, that there were two elephants, one large and one small; and third, that on the day they had walked to the Yanxia Cave their appetites had swelled so that they swept the temple's food clean, mocking themselves that, scholars though they were, they ate like elephants. The other great stone resembled both a tiger and a lion, and since it was just after the Songhu War, when patriotic feeling ran high and people were crying out that the sleeping lion of China had awakened, they inscribed it "Shishi," meaning "to take the lion as one's teacher." Li Xinyang wrote out the characters, and they gave the temple's abbot a hundred dollars to have them carved into the stone, inscribed: "Composed by Chen Zhigao, written by Li Xinyang." Decades have passed, and "Shishi" is no longer to be seen; but "Xiangxiang," carved very large and deep, has to this day become a commemorative sight at the Yanxia Cave in Hangzhou, and a witness to the friendship of Chen Zhigao and the others in their rescue work of those years.

I lived in the Yu Mansion by West Lake for close to a year, using the Yu family's literary elegance to cover my movements. During that time I developed yet another sphere of social connections. Lodging in the Yu Mansion together with me was an overseas-Chinese young lady who happened to share my name, Li Mulan; she was studying at the National Art College by West Lake. At that time she was being courted by Hu Weike, an instructor at the National Government's air-force aviation school. Every Saturday Hu would come to the Yu Mansion to invite Li Mulan out. Li took me as her sworn elder sister, so sometimes she would invite me along to enjoy the hills and waters. Hu Weike was also an overseas Chinese, and when the two of them married, they asked me to be their witness, which gave me the chance to have some contact and connection with the teachers and cadets of the Jianqiao Aviation School in Hangzhou. They held their wedding at the West Lake Hotel, and more than two hundred instructors and cadets from the Jianqiao school came to attend, among them the air hero Gao Zhihang, who in the "August Fourteenth" air battle of 1937 would shoot down several Japanese planes in a single engagement. The company all urged me to offer the congratulatory address to the newlyweds. Since it was just after the "September Eighteenth" Incident, and the National Government's air force had only recently been established, I said in my address: "Now, with the nation in peril and the aviation school newly founded, once the enemy invades our country's skies, I offer my advance congratulations that every one of you here today will become an airborne warrior defending the nation and slaying the foe—a national hero!" The aviation-school instructors present all knew me through Hu Weike, and no sooner had I finished my address than instructors and cadets alike cried out with one voice: "We are willing to be airborne warriors and national heroes, fighting to defend the motherland!"—and they pressed forward one after another to drink to me. My apt and fitting words, and the heroic spirit with which I drank with composure, left a deep impression on the school's teachers and students. This chance also let me form many social connections within the National Government's air force.

Chen Zhigao and I were each the most cherished child of our parents, and we had lost our fathers at almost the same time; imperceptibly, a kind of fellow-feeling in shared misfortune seemed to grow between us. Chen Zhigao was smitten with me at first sight, but he was no frivolous playboy. He had heard me say that my husband Wan Xiyan had fallen a few years earlier (I had not told him of my union with He Chang), and that an orphaned child survived; and since we were both in the mourning period for our fathers, he naturally could not soon declare his love. I had openly acknowledged his parents as my adoptive father and mother, and I was older than he, so we associated openly, addressing each other as elder sister and younger brother. What steadily drew our feelings closer was that, under my influence, Chen Zhigao made full use of his standing as a lawyer and his social connections to work hard at rescuing underground CCP members and patriotic democrats.

During my time in Hangzhou, although I could not openly come forward to engage in rescue work, I always followed Comrade Zhou Enlai's original instructions and, through my connection with Chen Zhigao, took part behind the scenes in planning rescue activities of all kinds. Chen Zhigao had a cousin named Chen Dexin (style name Mingzai), who was then chairman of the Zhejiang Provincial Bar Association; we entrusted him with the cases of quite a few arrested comrades. The two of them, of one heart and one mind, served the rescue of arrested revolutionaries and patriots, and later even formally established the Baoquan Law Office. Much of the rescue work I helped plan in Hangzhou was handled through this law office.

Out of a desire for long-term cooperation and to keep the rescue work safe and effective, Zhigao, in accepting the rescue tasks I entrusted to him, was also extremely careful and prudent, trying as far as possible not to appear in public himself, and mostly acting through the connections of other trusted people he knew well to come forward and rescue our arrested comrades. For example, the rescues of Comrade Chen Geng and Comrade Liao Chengzhi were both planned by him and me behind the scenes and entrusted to Wu Kaisheng to carry out. As for asking such notables as Song Qingling, He Xiangning, Shen Junru, Liu Yazi, Lin Gengbai, Yang Zhihao and Zhou Xiao'an to come forward, that was all the more legitimate and aboveboard. Of the comrades handed over to us for rescue in those days, some I knew, but more I did not; I was given the alias each had used on entering prison, and by that name I would call them. What their original names were, what they were called afterward, what their surnames and given names are now—of all that I truly knew nothing. Fortunately, several of them lived long lives, such as Comrade Chen Congying, wife of Ren Bishi, who was implicated and arrested when Xiang Zhongfa's betrayal led to the destruction of the Red Flag printing house; He Shishan and He Shisi, the two daughters of the martyr He Shuheng, who took part in the First Congress of the CCP; and Comrades Xiong Jinding and Zhu Duanshou—all still living into the 1980s. Reunited after the catastrophe, they can serve as witnesses to my rescue work. As my younger sister wrote in a letter to me: this is Heaven protecting the good, and good people living long!

In cooperating with me on the rescue work, Chen Zhigao actively supported my activities, very warmly coming forward to ask favors of people, to host banquets, and to run about on every side, and he never accepted any payment from our Party. What is very interesting is that only after we married did he say to me: "I long ago knew that you had never really left the Communist Party—for if you had truly left it, how could there still be so many cases of rescuing political prisoners for you to entrust to me?"

During my time in Hangzhou, the Party organization did not relax its work of winning over Chen Zhigao; on the contrary, it strengthened it. Comrade Wu Defeng selected a Comrade Zhang Yilin, who had joined the Party in 1926, to work specifically at Chen Zhigao's side. This Zhang Yilin was a native of Taihe County in Jiangxi, eight years older than I; he had a very good hand with the writing brush and excelled at small regular script. Through the social connections of Pan Zhenya and Luo Jiaheng, who were also Jiangxi men, Wu Defeng had him introduced to serve as secretary of Chen Zhigao's law office, in charge of keeping the case files of the rescue work. That law office was run jointly by Chen Zhigao and Zhou Xiao'an, and it had a private office for the secretary, Zhang Yilin; Zhang was unmarried, and both ate and lodged there. Wu Defeng very cleverly made use of this law office as a reception and liaison station for people coming from the Soviet area, and as a liaison point for transferring comrades released on bail from prison and sending them off to various places. At the time Zhigao did not know Zhang's true identity, and I too only learned of this top-secret arrangement from Zhao Wanhua after I returned to Shanghai from Hangzhou. Knowing that Zhang Yilin was honest and faithful to his duty, I could set my mind at ease and let him take my place in visiting prisons and in receiving and transferring released prisoners. By the winter of 1935, after we had succeeded in mediating the Tongyi Company affair, Chen Zhigao's law office moved into the Tongyi Building, and Zhang Yilin naturally moved along with it.

Through the rescue work, I came to know and stay in touch with more and more prominent figures of the upper levels of society, and I steadily widened my broad connections with people of every stratum, beginning to make my contribution in the field of the united front—expanding our Party's influence and winning over more collaborators. To combine rescue work with united-front work, so that the two promote each other—developing united-front work through rescue work, and completing rescue tasks through united-front channels—this was a profound lesson I learned in practice.

In 1933 I returned from Hangzhou to Shanghai. By then Pan Hannian had been transferred to work in the Central Soviet area, and his former post had been taken over by Comrade Liu Bochui. Not long after I returned to Shanghai, I received a telegram from home saying "Mother gravely ill, hope you return at once," signed jointly by my second sister and third brother. I applied to the organization for leave to go home and visit my family. Liu Bochui had Zhao Wanhua tell me that, considering I had not been able to return for my father's funeral when he died, and now that my mother was gravely ill and my second brother Huang Zhangren had also died of illness not long before, the organization agreed to grant me leave to go home and visit my family, and assigned Zhao Wanhua to accompany and escort me the whole way. When Chen Zhigao heard that I was going home to visit my family, he feared I might run into trouble on the road, so he placed in my suitcase a letter that Li Nanxiang's younger brother Li Huanzhi had written to Chen Zhigao's sister Chen Baozhang, and said: "This letter should guarantee your safety the whole way." What was the reason for this? It turned out that after Li Huanzhi graduated from Daxia University in Shanghai in 1931, he had gone north to resist Japan together with his classmate Wu Zhendong (also a member of the World and China Society); at first he joined Feng Yuxiang's forces and won Feng's high regard. Once, going with Feng Yuxiang to Nanchang, he caught the eye of Chen Lifu, who insisted that Feng Yuxiang hand him over to be Chen's secretary. Knowing that Chen Lifu was firmly pro-Chiang and anti-Communist, Li Huanzhi wrote to his elder brother Li Nanxiang asking what he should do. When I heard Li Nanxiang speak of this, I thought: if Li Huanzhi worked in a core department of the Nationalist central authorities, he could learn more of their secrets—would that not be all the more helpful to our work? So I did my utmost to encourage Li Nanxiang to give his consent. Li Huanzhi thus became Chen Lifu's secretary. At that time Li Huanzhi was ardently courting Chen Zhigao's sister Chen Baozhang, and often wrote to her in very intimate terms. Chen Zhigao, knowing of this connection, took one of Li Huanzhi's letters and gave it to me; it was in a special envelope of Chiang Kai-shek's Nanchang Field Headquarters, meant to serve as my safe-conduct. When Liu Bochui and Zhao Wanhua learned of this, Liu Bochui greatly admired Chen Zhigao's cleverness, considering it a masterly move. After I reached Wuhan, Zhao Wanhua specially arranged a gathering of the sisters from the Women's Association of those years—Wang Wenqiu, Liang Jianhua, Zhang Duhe and others—and I even composed a lyric to the tune "Langtaosha" as a memento of the meeting.

When I hurried home, I learned that my mother was not seriously ill at all; rather, my second sister and third brother had "an urgent matter to discuss." In January 1932, my second brother Huang Zhangren, a member of the Communist Youth League, had been struck and injured by a police car that had come to suppress and arrest people while he was distributing leaflets; he had suffered from lung disease to begin with, and with the serious injuries added, he soon coughed up blood and died. My third brother Zhangxin had been expelled from his school for taking part in a class boycott launched by the student association, just at the crucial moment when, having finished senior middle school, he was about to go on to university; without a graduation diploma, he could not sit the university examinations, and he was frantic with worry. Because after our father's death our mother had scrimped and saved to put her children through school, and disciplined her sons and daughters very strictly, my third brother dared not tell her he had been expelled, yet he could not conceal it forever. My second sister Zhangrong was at that time in love with Guo Changlu, a top student in the Department of Politics and Diplomacy at Wuhan University; Guo's family was poor, while our mother was preparing to accept betrothal gifts from a family in Liuyang on my second sister's behalf. My second sister of course did not want to become another victim of "the parents' command and the matchmaker's word," but she dared not tell our mother that she was already in a free-choice love match and had someone she cared for. So the sister and brother secretly agreed to send, under both their names, an urgent summons for me to return, on the pretext that our mother was gravely ill, tricking me home in the hope that I could devise a way out of the impasse—which I, of course, was duty-bound not to refuse.

My third brother was very diligent in his studies, his examination results ranking among the best, and he had the support of progressive teachers and fellow students. I had my third brother find a way to use the name of our late second brother Huang Zhangren to make up his coursework and sit the university entrance examinations. I also brought my mother from Liuyang to Wuhan and accompanied her to the Canton–Hankou Railway Bureau, where we found my father's old superior and requested that, under the compensation regulations for railway employees, my mother receive a pension as a widow. This request was granted, and the railway bureau paid my mother a pension of thirty dollars a month, for a term of five years. This money was just enough to support my third brother's studies at Wuhan University. My mother then stopped blaming my third brother for being expelled. Taking on my second brother's name, Huang Zhangren, and using his graduation diploma, my third brother entered Wuhan University and later graduated with distinction. Thus my third brother's wish was fulfilled. My second brother, though dead, lived on, and both the dead and the living were comforted. Not only does my third brother still remember this episode vividly to this day, but his classmate Comrade Li Rui—who once served as secretary to Chairman Mao and deputy head of the Central Organization Department, and was a member of the Central Advisory Commission—also knew of the matter.

For the sake of my second sister's marriage, I arranged to meet Guo Changlu at Luojia Hill in Wuchang. He said to me: "A state that has no rival states or outside threats will always perish"—the nation's peril was grave, and we must press on with equipping and training our army, navy and air force to resist the Japanese invaders. Through our conversation I saw that he was indeed very talented and had ample patriotic conviction. He also told me that his father had died, his mother was bedridden, and his family was poor. So I went to his home to see, and found that the one chair they had was already broken, so that when I sat on it I nearly fell. But I told my mother none of this, saying only: "Mother, of course the Guo family is not as wealthy as the family you picked for my second sister. But think back—when you first married into the Huang family, wasn't Father just a poor scholar too? Our family's circumstances only gradually improved later on. In choosing a partner, as long as the man has good character and talent, and the two of them truly have feeling for each other, nothing else matters—let them marry for love!" My mother was also a very open-minded person; she probably reflected that arranging my marriage in those years had brought me much suffering, and that she ought not to repeat it now with my second sister, so she consented to the match between my second sister and Guo Changlu. Hearing that the Guo family was poor, my mother even gave my second sister the one thousand dollars she had saved at the bottom of her chest as a dowry. The wedding was very simple and plain, with only two tables of guests: besides the relatives of both families, there were only a few close friends from the Canton–Hankou Railway and a few of my own good friends from my Wuhan days, such as Zhao Wanhua and Zhang Duhe. My second sister married in 1933, and later had a son and a daughter, both of whom achieved success in their studies.

After I had smoothly helped my younger brother and sister persuade our mother and resolve their difficulties, I returned again to Shanghai. Chen Zhigao had just come back from Qingdao, where he had attended the Fifth Congress of the National Bar Association, and he told me that ever since Lin Gengbai's motion at the National Emergency Conference calling for the reduction of sentences and the release of political prisoners had passed, many of those arrested could now be released on bail; and that he and Shen Junru and others had now put forward at the Bar Association a further motion for a movement to compensate for wrongful imprisonment, which would be all the more favorable to the rescue work (of which more later). Comrade Liu Bochui especially admired Chen Zhigao, praising him as a man of justice with both courage and insight.

In 1933, Zhigao formally declared his love to me. He explained to me, insistently and repeatedly: that his father, while alive, had praised me as a young lady of a scholarly family and hoped I could become the Chen family's daughter-in-law; that his mother too had taken a liking to me; and that I got on very well with his younger brothers and sisters and indeed the whole family, all of whom welcomed our union and supported the match. He also said that his family had earlier arranged a betrothal for him, to a Miss Shen who was six years older than he, but that she had died of lung disease before the marriage could take place; and that to be able now to meet a soulmate like me was surely a bond ordained by fate!

At that time, because I still had no news of He Chang, I never ceased longing for and awaiting the Central Committee's sending someone to fetch me to the Central Soviet area, hoping to be reunited with He Chang. So I said to Zhigao: "My station in life does not match your family's; with my present status I am not suited to be your lifelong companion." To show his faithful love for me, he actually bit his finger till it bled and wrote his love on a white handkerchief. For the moment I was at a loss, not knowing how to answer him, and could only say that the matter required careful thought and that I would reply to him later. I reported the matter to the Party organization at once. Liu Bochui and Zhao Wanhua understood my feelings. So the matter dragged on for a time, and I put off giving Chen Zhigao any clear answer.

In 1934, Chen's mother came forward and asked Zhao Wanhua to go to my mother to propose the marriage. My mother had also felt the match rather unsuitable—the Chen family's position in Shanghai was too high, and she feared our family could not reach up to it; moreover I was three years older than Chen Zhigao, and she did not know whether the relationship would be harmonious and secure after marriage. So she said it was best to let me consider and decide for myself; whether the match came off was entirely for me to decide, and she had no objection. After repeated thought and discussion, Liu Bochui and Zhao Wanhua concluded that I was carrying out rescue work through Chen Zhigao in accordance with Comrade Enlai's instructions. Chen seemed already to have sensed that I had an extremely close relationship with the Communist Party; and yet, over these several years, whatever I entrusted him to do, he would spare no risk and go all out to do it, cleverly combining ordinary open rescue with secret special rescue work, helping our Party rescue many arrested comrades from prison in its most difficult times—truly making a great contribution to our Party's cause. Especially since I got on so well with his family, such an important working position must on no account be lightly given up. So Comrades Liu Bochui and Zhao Wanhua did their utmost to bring this special marriage to fruition. Liu Bochui said to me: "Your working post is in Shanghai, and Chen Zhigao is the principal work-target the Central Committee assigned you. If you now abandon this extremely important post on your own initiative for the sake of a personal marriage, there would be no way to account for it to the Party organization. Once you and Chen Zhigao unite and form a family, it will be all the more favorable for covering your own identity, and there is every likelihood of opening up a new situation—this is absolutely in keeping with the needs of the work. I will certainly certify to the Central Committee that this is not your own unauthorized, undisciplined act, but wholly in obedience to the needs of the work. We hear that Earth Leopard"—He Chang—"has done very well in the Central Soviet area in Jiangxi; he firmly upholds Comrade Mao Zedong's correct leadership and has become deputy director of the General Political Department of the Red Army. The Central Committee has still not sent anyone to fetch you to the Soviet area, and from this you should understand the importance of your present special working post in the Shanghai white area, and the Party's trust in you and expectations of you. As for Comrade He Chang, we will all explain the reasons to him in the future. He Chang is a very open-minded man, and he certainly will not blame you." These words of Comrade Liu Bochui's were of course by no means merely his personal opinion, but a guarantee and decision made on behalf of the Party organization. Things having come to this point, although I still could not let go of my feelings for He Chang, I had no choice but to agree to this special marriage.

As for how this match was viewed, opinions naturally differed widely: the enemy slanderously called it "the Communist Party's beauty trap," and some said I was coveting a life of ease and had "gone off to be a rich man's wife." But to this day my heart is still at ease, without resentment or regret, and I do not think it was a choice made entirely against my will. Though I constantly missed Comrade He Chang and could not forget the love between us as husband and wife, Comrade Liu Bochui's earnest, heartfelt persuasion was the organization's view, and I had to consider it carefully. What is more, human beings are not grass or trees—who can be without feeling? Over these several years Chen Zhigao and I had shared bitter and sweet amid the fierce winds and evil waves of the severe White Terror, shared hardship and danger, been utterly open with each other, and fought side by side, so that we had naturally developed considerable feeling for each other. He was upright and warm-hearted, sought progress, had a strong sense of justice, was decent in his conduct, respected women greatly, and had none of the dissolute airs of a great family's young master; and in my heart I too grew steadily fonder of him. So I finally consented to his proposal, and in 1938 obtained the approval and sanction of Liu Shaowen, on behalf of Comrade Zhou Enlai.

Before finally settling the match, Zhao Wanhua laid down three conditions to Chen Zhigao: "First, you must continue to do your utmost to support our work of rescuing arrested comrades. This also accords with your duty as a lawyer to represent the 'light of justice'! Second, after the marriage, neither of you is to interfere in the other's personal actions—that is, once Mulan marries you, she cannot merely be the Chen family's daughter-in-law, but must also go out to take part in certain social activities and work. Third, you must allow her in the future to acknowledge and be reunited with the orphaned child left by her former husband." Chen Zhigao unconditionally accepted the three conditions that Elder Sister Zhao Wanhua put forward on my behalf, and the facts afterward proved that he did indeed fully observe and fulfill all three.

As for He Chang's fate, I only heard it conveyed to me later. After the Central Red Army began the Long March, he stayed behind with Comrades Xiang Ying, Chen Yi and others to carry on guerrilla warfare in southern Jiangxi, serving as a member of the CCP Central Soviet Area Sub-bureau and as director of the political department of the Central Military Region. Once, while leading his troops in a transfer toward the northern Guangdong border region, he fell into an enemy ambush at the Huichang River in Jiangxi; surrounded for seven days and seven nights, out of food and ammunition, wounded in the leg, he died a hero's death at the last. Comrade Chen Yi wrote a poem mourning the martyrdom of He Chang and Ruan Xiaoxian, "Weeping for Comrades Ruan Xiaoxian and He Chang":

Looking round among our comrades, Ruan and He may truly be called worthy; Ruan's renown was borne through the Southern Ranges, He's name spread across the northern lands of You and Yan.

In auditing they toiled with their heart's blood; in governing they showed a stern majesty. Alas that, breaking out of the encirclement together, I alone came through with my life.

Comrade Chen Pixian published an article of reminiscence and mourning for Comrade He Chang in the Red Flag Fluttering series, and many other comrades' memoirs mention their admiration and remembrance of He Chang. In his home region of Liulin in Shanxi, a memorial hall has been built for him. The Central Committee too has given a correct appraisal of his life, calling him an immortal proletarian revolutionary. To this day I still do not regret my union with Chen Zhigao; I know well that Comrade He Chang's spirit in heaven can understand the situation and the hardships I faced at the time. At our parting he had specially charged me: "You rescue a few more comrades, and I will win a few more victories in the Soviet area—I'll be waiting for your reports of triumph to come thick and fast!" My decision was made precisely so that he and the Party might see more of my reports of triumph.

In May 1935, Chen Zhigao and I held a grand wedding at the Chinese Society for Arts and Sciences in Shanghai. *Shen Bao* and other papers reported it, especially noting that Chen Zhigao was an executive committee member of the Shanghai Bar Association, the drafter of the Lawyers Law, and a leading figure in the movement to compensate for wrongful imprisonment. The presiding elders at the wedding were the mothers of both sides; as witnesses we invited Mr. Cai Yuanpei and Mr. Shen Junru; the introducers should properly have been Sun Xiaocun and Zhao Wanhua, but at that time Sun was in Nanjing and had not come to Shanghai, and Zhao Wanhua could not appear in public, so the groom's side invited Mr. Liu Yazi, and for the bride's side my mother came forward to invite Mr. Tang Youren, the husband of the younger sister of my father's student Ouyang Yuqian. Among those who posed for the wedding photograph, besides the relatives of the two families, were Mr. and Mrs. Wu Gengmei, Li Nanxiang, Chen Gaoyong, the lawyer Yang Zhihao, and other relatives and close friends.

Before the wedding, I accompanied my mother and fifth brother, who had come from our home region to Shanghai, to burn incense and worship the Buddha at the Lingyin Temple by West Lake in Hangzhou. Before the bodhisattva's image my mother silently made a vow, saying: "My daughter was born to a hard fate. I pray the bodhisattva bless her so that from now on her life may be a little more settled, and I pray still more that one day in the future she may have a reunion of flesh and blood with the son born to her former husband. If that day truly comes, I will surely come to gild the Buddha's image in fulfillment of my vow." My mother by then already knew of the three conditions Zhao Wanhua had settled with Chen Zhigao on my behalf, and knew too that I was still working for the Party; but to protect the Party organization and my own safety, I could not reveal my identity to Chen Zhigao and his family. Now that I had the cover of a household of such high social standing as the Chen family, my circumstances hereafter would be somewhat better. She understood all this, and so was fairly at ease. When I accompanied my mother to the City God Temple in Shanghai, she grew carsick and vomited as soon as she got into the motorcar; fortunately there were still horse-carriages in Shanghai then, so I accompanied her by carriage to see the sights all over the city. Because my eldest brother was then working on the Longhai Railway in Zhengzhou, on the third day after our wedding my mother left for Zhengzhou. Zhigao and I, attending upon his mother, the Elder Madame Pan, went together to West Tianmu in Zhejiang for our honeymoon. Beside a waterfall on West Tianmu Mountain, we paid to have five hundred plum trees planted. I never went there again, and do not know whether the plum grove is still there today.
