## Chapter 12. What Day Shall I Go Home to Wash the Traveler’s Robe?

“Benefactor, this fortune hides a deep design within it. In these coming days you will meet with a great joy, and with a great grief. It can wash away twenty years of drifting dust and ill fortune; flesh and blood shall be reunited, and the family estate revived.” The old nun, her face all scarred, spoke with an air of utter piety.

“And what marvellous meeting is this to be?” A-Chu smiled, committing himself neither way. “Might the reverend mother tell me where the deep design lies?”

“Writing cannot exhaust words, and words cannot exhaust meaning. This poor nun dares not presume to guess. The gentleman is of gifted mind; he should read out its sense for himself.”

“My mind holds no store of ink; I can hardly thread it together.” A-Chu answered with great respect. “And yet . . .”

“And yet what?” the old nun asked.

“And yet, in the small hours when I wake from dreams, I often hear a sound of digging, of spades, terrible to hear. In some obscure way I always feel it has to do with my own origins. Once in particular, when I went to a friend’s birthday party, the moment I stepped into her house it was as though everything there were somehow known to me, every step familiar.”

“Why have I never once heard you speak of this?” Fourth Madam’s face was all astonishment.

“I did not wish to worry you.”

“Then why do you pour out this hidden trouble today, of all days?” the old nun asked mildly.

“Because you and I, reverend mother . . .” A-Chu paused a moment, then said: “You and I too seem somehow known to each other.”

“Amitabha. If the benefactor can see clearly into the past, he will surely come to understand the future.” The old nun smiled. “Let me give the benefactor eight words. ‘Fortune and calamity lean each on the other; when ill fortune reaches its end, good fortune comes.’ Amitabha—it is well, it is well.”

When A-Chu and Fourth Madam had ended their brief communion within the gates of the Buddha, they came back out of that vision of emptiness into the teeming, troubled dust of the world.

All along the way A-Chu’s mind rose and fell without rest; those four lines on the scrap of paper left him baffled and uneasy. “A whole life’s fortunes drift like duckweed; glory and wealth lie wrapped in cloud and mist. By error he takes the strange land for home—what day shall he go home to wash the traveller’s robe?”

By instinct he felt there must be some mysterious bond between himself and the old nun; the uncommon glances exchanged between Fourth Madam and the old nun told him the same thing—that between Fourth Madam, the old nun and himself there seemed to hang an invisible net. And what, in the end, was that net?

Fourth Madam had once admitted with her own lips that she was his own kin. Then might that old nun be Fourth Madam’s kin as well?

Could his future, his very fate, really be swayed, be settled, by nothing more than a scrap of paper? A-Chu began to doubt it; the thread of skepticism gained the upper hand.

What lay within reach of his hand was, most likely, not the “destined” truth at all, but perhaps a smokescreen of superstition. All this talk of flesh and blood reunited, of a family estate revived—perhaps it was only the empty flattery of a fortune-teller angling for a lucky word. The old nun, quite possibly, faced countless people on any given day and said the same words to them, countless times over. Reasoning his way idly to this point, A-Chu could not help a rueful laugh.

For him to weigh every word of “reading a fortune, casting the sticks” was, in itself, an absurdity.

What A-Chu did not know was that, all unknowing and unfeeling, he had already been drawn by the whirlpool of fate to the very muzzle of the gun, the very edge of the knife . . .

Yang Yubo sat quietly in the consulting room of Doctor Chu at the Tongji Hospital. He had gone to the trouble of booking a specialist’s appointment; he had come expressly to call on this man he had never met, and who of late had struck such dread into his heart. More than twenty years of tormented suffering had aged his mind and worn it old; he had long since been exhausted past bearing.

Did he exist? He ought to exist. Twenty years ago the body had never been found.

The image of the child Yang Muchu was everywhere, pressing at every moment upon his extremely sensitive, extremely fragile nerves. He would rather believe that Yingzi was inventing a thing out of nothing than face the slaughter a second time.

But in the instant he saw A-Chu come into the consulting room, all wreathed in spring smiles, he shivered as though struck with cold.

He marvelled at the Creator’s uncanny craftsmanship: the “radiant smile” he had once destroyed with his own hands was now displayed before him afresh, and the innocent look of that child, which he had pictured in exhaustive detail a thousand times over in his mind, was in this one instant confirmed. The nightmares of his twenty years all sprang, without exception, from this familiar face.

“How do you do, Doctor Chu. I made an appointment with you—my humble name is Yang Yubo.” Yang Yubo rose to show his respect for the doctor; his face was hung all over with the marks of a smile, though in his heart there was no longer the faintest trace of one.

“Your fame has long reached me,” said A-Chu. “Please sit, Mr. Yang. Where does it trouble you?”

“Of late, what with the weather so changeable, and business not going too smoothly, I have been vexed in spirit, and my heartbeat is none too steady. I fear it may be some ill omen before a great illness.”

This speech, mild in tone and strange in its words, roused no suspicion in A-Chu.

“Let me take a look at you.” A-Chu examined Yang Yubo by the usual procedure. “Your tongue is dry, and the skin has lost its elasticity. You have suffered a long time from a bad case of rhinitis, so your breathing feels obstructed; the habit of breathing through the mouth has left the fluids of your mouth depleted. How is your sleep?”

“None too good; I am forever beset by nightmares.”

“Which is why you smoke? Heavy smoking will harm your health. To speak precisely, you should take care of your lungs.” Having made his preliminary diagnosis, A-Chu prescribed several Western medicines for Yang Yubo.

“Forgive my presumption in asking—is your married life a harmonious one?”

“What has that to do with my health?” Yang Yubo asked.

“A great deal. The repression of feeling ends, in the end, in the violence of reason.” At this both men laughed, as if by common accord. “It is a hackneyed thing to say, but it is worth your consideration—if only for the sake of your wife’s health,” said A-Chu.

“My wife is not in good health, so we—you understand—and we are getting on in years . . . we cannot revel and give free rein to desire as the young do.”

“To give free rein to desire is, of course, no good thing; but for the body, abstinence too is a kind of injury.”

“A doctor come home from abroad indeed—open-minded, and amusing with it. In truth I am blind and ignorant of such common knowledge as medicine and the arts of health, but one thing I do know: a doctor of the old Chinese tradition would never counsel a patient in such terms.”

A-Chu laughed. “That is because you do not understand the tradition.”

“Perhaps so.”

“Come back next week for a follow-up.” In an easy, cheerful atmosphere A-Chu brought the talk with his patient to a close.

“This one meeting has taught me much. Thank you, Doctor Chu; until we meet again.” Having watched every movement of A-Chu’s, quietly, to its end, Yang Yubo—a grim killing air spreading now through his whole body—took his leave with great courtesy.

As Yang Yubo strode out through the gate of the Tongji Hospital, he quickened his pace towards “hell.” Twenty years now; one more innocent life taken or spared made little difference—and besides, this man was not necessarily “innocent.”

He must die.

For once the “crisis” came upon them, Yang Yubo might no longer be able to control the situation at will.

In that instant, Yang Yubo made up his mind.

Yang Yubo had scarcely left A-Chu’s consulting room when someone knocked at the door.

“May we come in, Doctor Chu?” Ronghua led the disguised Lao Yu into A-Chu’s consulting room. A-Chu was greatly surprised; he rose at once and warmly welcomed his two rare visitors, and, alert as ever, hung up on the door the sign reading “Emergency Examination—Do Not Disturb,” then reached back and locked the consulting-room door.

“What brings you here?” A-Chu asked.

“This friend of mine insisted on coming to thank you in person for saving his life.” Ronghua set down a leather case.

“You are leaving, second young lady?” A-Chu asked.

“It is not I who am leaving; it is this friend of mine.”

“I am about to go north, to leave Shanghai. I have come specially to take my leave of my benefactor.” Lao Yu smiled and cupped his hands in salute.

“You mustn’t, you mustn’t. It was the work of a moment, a trifling effort. If you would thank someone, sir, it is our second young lady you should thank. Without the second young lady giving her blood to keep your life going, I might have had powers to move heaven and yet been helpless to save you.”

“Just so, just so. Meeting with disaster, at the very brink of death, I was fortunate to have the aid of you two, and so was born anew. Without the joined efforts of you both, I would not now be speaking of going north—I should long since have ‘gone west.’” With that Lao Yu drew from his breast a cheque for no small sum and said: “I have been some years in trade and have laid by a little; the gift is light but the meaning is weighty—I beg Mr. Chu to accept it with a smile. In the days to come we may yet have occasion to trouble you, to lean on your help.”

A-Chu looked at Lao Yu, then at Ronghua—Lao Yu’s face all sincerity, Ronghua’s holding a look of encouragement—and could not help a gentle smile. He said: “To cure the sick and save lives is the doctor’s bounden duty. There is nothing in it to boast of, or to claim reward for.”

“That is not my meaning . . .” Lao Yu made to explain.

“Sir, he who offers a bribe robs a man of his integrity; he who does a kindness enjoys alone the ‘riches’ of the spirit. If you will but leave A-Chu his integrity, sir, you leave him his ‘riches’ as well. Henceforth neither owes the other anything—what could please you more, sir?”

When Lao Yu had heard A-Chu out, he was moved past measure. “To meet, in this muddy world, with a man so plain and unadorned, so untouched by a speck of dust as Mr. Chu—that is a rare thing indeed. Mr. Chu is not only open and upright in his dealings with men, but grand and unfettered in the doing of things. He stirs in me a fresh measure of admiration and respect.” Lao Yu put the cheque away. In that instant his liking for A-Chu grew by no small degree—no longer because he so resembled A-Ci, but because A-Chu was, in truth, a most admirable young man. Just then Lao Yu suddenly recalled that a moment before, in the hospital corridor, he had seen the retreating back of A-Ci’s father, Yang Yubo, hurrying away; a doubt sprang up in him, and so he questioned A-Chu about it.

“Forgive my presumption in asking. Just now I saw the great financier Mr. Yang Yubo leaving here—did he too come to be treated?”

“He comes to me; if not to be treated, then for what?” A-Chu said, with a touch of humour.

“To look at you.”

“And what is there to look at in me? A single eye and three legs?” A-Chu laughed heartily.

“Did he not tell you that you closely resemble a certain person?”

“No.”

Lao Yu was much surprised.

“Whom do I resemble?” A-Chu’s own curiosity was piqued.

“A friend of mine.” Lao Yu did not find it convenient to say more.

“We ought to be going.” Ronghua reminded Lao Yu that he must not linger too long over pleasantries.

“You go straight to the station, then?” A-Chu asked.

“Yes, the one-o’clock train this afternoon,” Lao Yu answered.

“Let me see you there. A-Fu has gone to the countryside today, so I drove myself to work,” said A-Chu.

This offer took Ronghua quite by surprise. And Lao Yu’s response, next, surprised her still more.

“Very good—one guest need not trouble two hosts; I’ll ride in your car.” Lao Yu agreed with brisk decision.

While A-Chu went off to bring the car round, Ronghua and Lao Yu made a brief farewell.

“This man is no ordinary fellow. I have a presentiment that one day he may do things to astonish the age, and become a new power calling up the winds and rains of Shanghai.” Lao Yu gave his brief verdict on A-Chu. “I am leaving, but ‘Timely Rain’ cannot leave,” Lao Yu went on, turning to Ronghua. “From this day forward, you are ‘Timely Rain’; ‘Floating Dust’ has passed away on the wind.”

“And my task?”

“Await the return of ‘Drifting Wind,’” said Lao Yu.

Ronghua and Lao Yu parted at the gate of the Tongji Hospital; Lao Yu got into A-Chu’s car, and they drove straight for the railway station. On the way, Lao Yu and A-Chu talked over the political situation at home and abroad. Lao Yu found A-Chu a man mild and gentle on the surface, but proud to the very bone underneath. His temperament, too, greatly resembled Yang Muci’s—could it truly be mere “coincidence”?

Halfway there, the car ran into a “spot check” by the police of the British Concession. This “spot check” was part of a joint operation, mounted together with the Shanghai Garrison Command, to net the fish that had slipped through in the roundup of the CCP Special Branch. The questioning was rigorous, the scale wide, a dragnet search; among the crowd submitting to inspection, one after another of the so-called “Communist” suspects was detained, questioned, examined, and some arrested on the spot.

Disguised though Lao Yu was, the bullet-scar on his neck was still plainly to be seen; and to wrap one’s neck tightly in a scarf in the weather of April was, in itself, a confession before the whip.

“What’s to be done?” Lao Yu was thinking.

In the very instant Lao Yu was thinking it, A-Chu had already swung the wheel back around and turned the car about.

“There’s no telling when a storm may break in a clear sky—let us take the back lanes,” said A-Chu.

“Will it serve?”

“Let us try.”

“Do you know, you are very brave. You are a man not thrown off by the unexpected. In the space of a moment you can lean on your own wits to save a man on the very brink of death—without so much as asking who he is.”

“I have no wish to know too much. Old sir, you find everything about me good; that is only because we have but a single meeting’s acquaintance, a few sentences’ friendship between us—the far is prized and the near despised. Before our young master I am worth nothing at all; wherever I turn, I am in the wrong.”

“Mr. Chu, there is no need to prize others and despise yourself. In days to come your road shall soar like the roc’s; the prospect before you . . .”

Even as they spoke, two police motor-tricycles came roaring up behind. Plainly they had a clear object; the man at their head all but rose to his feet on his machine to shout them down.

A-Chu stamped hard on the accelerator; Lao Yu’s hand tightened, in secret, upon his pistol.

At the very hair’s breadth of the crisis A-Chu suddenly saw something; he slowed the car and said to Lao Yu, “A misunderstanding—it’s a friend of mine.” Lao Yu’s nerves did not slacken, and he gave no sign at all; but he trusted A-Chu.

The car was pressed to a stop, flanked close on either side by the two police tricycles. A-Chu got his blow in first. He wound down the window and said, in a scolding tone: “Han, you fellow—do you not want to live!”

Han Yu leaped down from his tricycle with a great roar of laughter. “There, I knew it—my eyes are never wrong. A-Chu! Ha-ha, and how do things stand with you and A-Hui? Have you drunk the wedding wine yet? When did you get back from abroad?”

The newcomer was none other than Han Yu, whom A-Chu had known during his student days in England. Han Yu had studied forensic medicine; he was an old family friend of Cong Feng and Xia Yuechun, and in the days of A-Chu’s courtship with Cong Hui they had all gathered together more than once.

“Me? A solitary lord of no realm; as for wedding wine, there’s no hoping for it any time soon,” said A-Chu. “Hey, what’s all this? You, a man who studied forensic medicine—how have you turned into a police officer?”

“It’s a long story, a long story.” With one hand Han Yu tugged the brim of his cap low and with the other he hooked onto the car window; his whole face wore a look of helplessness, and that very air of the man who cannot help himself, hung upon his features, made him seem exceedingly comical.

“When did you get yourself ‘set right and made proper’ in the police bureau?” A-Chu said, teasing him.

“A father’s command is hard to disobey, hard to disobey; my old man kept at me, forced me to come home and toe the line. He held that a forensic doctor has no future—dealing with corpses all day long is unlucky—and pressed me into this trade. Nothing to be done; a son carries on his father’s calling. It’s the Chinese tradition, you know.”

“Then hasn’t the profession you trained in gone to waste?”

“Nowadays, so long as a man can earn money, can cut a fine figure, who cares whether it’s his trade or not? And it’s not only wilted fields and withered lotus like us that go to waste. Look at all of great Shanghai—sold off where it’s sold, leased out where it’s leased—didn’t it all go to waste just the same in the hands of the great Qing empire? Ah—do you know about Cong Feng?”

“What of Cong Feng? Has he come home?” With Cong Feng’s name on his lips, A-Chu’s heart turned again to Cong Hui.

Han Yu said, mysteriously: “Come home? Could he come home if he tried? He’s gone to the Soviet Union—and joined the Third Communist International, no less.”

“Then isn’t that the Communist Party?” said A-Chu.

“None other. He says he means to graft the sparks of revolution onto the barren spiritual soil of the East, and save the Chinese nation. Just listen to him—that tone, for all the world like the martyr who was first to storm the Bastille in the French Revolution and light the fires of vengeance. He’s always longed to be a hero.”

“He will surely become a hero; of that I have no doubt at all.”

“You seem to worship the man rather. Have a care.” Han Yu said. “All over Shanghai just now they’re hunting Reds; every station has its fixed quota, and when they can’t make up the number, they fill it with the likes of you—no backing, and under suspicion.”

“Get away with you.” A-Chu used his arm to push aside Han Yu’s hand where it rested on the window. Han Yu laughed, raised a hand and gave Lao Yu a half-comic salute of apology. “Forgive me—I was joking with him; I’ve been rude to you, old sir.”

Lao Yu nodded with a smile, and let it stand for a return of the courtesy.

“Have you any news of A-Hui?” A-Chu asked, feeling his way.

“I don’t know; go and ask Xia Yuechun—he may know.”

“Mr. Xia has come home too?”

“Last week, back from London. His father died, and he came back to inherit the family business. It’s his family, the Xias, that runs the Conghuimin Hospital—in the French Concession, I think it is.”

“Let us get together one of these days. Today I’ve business still.” A-Chu signalled that he must see the old gentleman off.

“And where are the two of you bound?”

“I’m seeing the gentleman to the railway station,” said A-Chu.

“The station? North, or south?”

“What of it if it’s north? What of it if it’s south?” Lao Yu put in.

“South is easy to speak of—a fair wind the whole way; but north, well, the inspection formalities are more of a nuisance. Just now it’s not only the police bureau drawing off men to search; even the Garrison Command has come down hard on this stretch.”

“What is it they’re searching for, in the end?” A-Chu asked.

“Communists.”

“Have they caught any?”

“Caught them, oh yes—seven or eight a day, come what may; true or false I couldn’t tell you, but they say the number shot alone is more than that.” Han Yu held up four fingers.

“How is that any different from putting an innocent man to the block?”

“How indeed. Just now it’s the peak hour for inspections at the station; if you go over, the queuing alone will cost you two or three hours. Look—the road’s jammed solid now, not a gap to be had, and the sun’s fierce; you’ll be baked half to death. Better let me open the way for you in the police car and take you straight into the station.”

“Splendid—ah, now here’s my saviour indeed.” A-Chu laughed.

“You’re standing me a dinner tonight.” Han Yu sprang onto his tricycle and called out: “Off we go.” And he sped ahead like the wind to clear the road. A-Chu reversed and followed close behind. Lao Yu’s gun went back to its place.

The whole way ran smooth and easy, safe and free of care.

That evening A-Chu played host at the Myriad Lamplights, and among his guests were Han Yu with his police brothers, and Xia Yuechun with several of his medical friends. Round the table they hailed one friend and called another, forging new ties and reviving old, and the merriment knew no bounds.

From Xia Yuechun’s lips A-Chu learned that Cong Hui had gone to Paris, in France. Xia Yuechun gave A-Chu a postcard Cong Hui had sent from Paris, with her address upon it.

For A-Chu this postcard was, beyond doubt, a tonic to wake the senses and clear the sight. The undertow of feeling silently coloured every cell of his body; passion took possession of his thoughts.

He would write to her, beg her forgiveness, and hope that Cong Hui might give him one more chance, and take up their bond of love anew. Paris was not so far away; “happiness” lay right before his eyes.

At some point Fourth Madam had conceived an enormous interest in pingtan ballad-singing; she even came expressly to the Moju Studio to seek the eldest young master’s guidance on the techniques of pingtan chanting and the art of the plucked strings.

And so “teaching pingtan” became a new pleasure that Rong Sheng had found amid a life that was ordinary and trivial, still as dead water, dry and dull. Fourth Madam had a quick gift for it; after a few pointers her pipa piece already had a shape and manner to it. The dead-still Moju Studio, having gained these refined airs and fragrant tunes, was now steeped in an atmosphere of longing and nostalgia. Only A-Chu and Hong’er were made to suffer for it—neither of them had ever cared for this endless, heart-rending, bittersweet strain of feeling, and yet the two of them were forced, however unwilling, to wait upon their elders at either hand and to be so very honoured as their audience, “appreciating” their musical “genius.”

“Pear blossoms fall, apricot blossoms open, my dreams wind round the twelve avenues of Chang’an. Deep in the night I stand on the green moss in the dew; and when evening comes I toss and turn outside the study. Paper, brush,

“ink and inkstone—each thing, every one, has my lord in it; my tears wet the empty study, and all it comes to is that I gaze till my eyes break through the autumn waters, and not one letter comes.”

Somehow, whenever A-Chu heard Fourth Madam reach this part of the song, a “chill” would run through him, cold to the very marrow.

“What is it Fourth Madam is singing?” Hong’er asked, crouching on the steps.

“Ghost-talk,” said A-Chu.

“Eh?” Hong’er’s clever, dainty little frame shrank another half a span.

“You slave, too dull to be waked! Wagging that tongue again!” Lishui had darted out from who knew where, and gave A-Chu’s forehead a hard rap. A-Chu cried “Ow,” and said Lishui was out of her mind.

“I can’t make out Fourth Madam’s refined tunes, but surely they aren’t ‘ghost-talk’?” said Lishui.

“How are they not ghost-talk? Is Jiao Guiying not a ghost? If ‘The Probing of Love’ is not ghost-talk, then what is it?” It vexed A-Chu no end that Lishui was forever putting on her airs as “mistress.”

“Jiao Guiying is a ghost—does that make Fourth Madam a ghost as well? I’ll go and tell Fourth Madam, and see if she doesn’t tear that mouth of yours to shreds.” Lishui made to seize the moment and go into the room, but A-Chu caught her and pulled her back. “That’ll do, you—off on your madness again,” said A-Chu. “You haven’t been crossed in love once more, have you? Or how would you have the leisure to come sparring with me?”

Lishui stared straight at A-Chu, and, catching him off his guard, gave his mouth a savage pinch. Hong’er cried out: “Miss, what are you doing?”

“Ah!” This time it really hurt; A-Chu flung Lishui off with all his strength, and Lishui laughed aloud. “Serves you right! Who told you to keep such a poisonous tongue in your head! It’s this crow’s mouth of yours that as good as cursed my match away!”

Hong’er hurried to rub the spot for A-Chu, but A-Chu would not let her.

“Fascist!” A-Chu swore at Lishui. “If you don’t mend that temper, what man would dare marry you?”

“I want none of them.” Lishui tossed A-Chu a little box wrapped up very smart and foreign. “A gift for you.”

It was a necktie, and no cheap one.

“What’s this for?” A-Chu asked.

“The match is off.”

“Why? Was he not satisfied with you?”

“He was satisfied enough—but his wife wouldn’t have it!”

“He—he has a wife?” A-Chu truly thought Lishui hard done by. “You didn’t know he had a wife?”

“What are you shouting for? Afraid the whole world won’t hear?” All at once Lishui broke into weeping, sore and grieved, so that it was A-Chu now who was rather at a loss.

“Let it go. You broke the traffic rules yourself, dashing across the road. That you weren’t run down and killed by a car is something to smile over in secret—why weep? Next time you cross, look sharp before you step out.” A-Chu spoke in veiled terms. Even as he comforted Lishui, he waved Hong’er away.

“I’m past thirty already, and still can’t get myself married off—am I to sponge on the Rong house all my life, then?”

“It’s those men who don’t know good goods when they see them.”

“They say your cousin has broken it off with Miss He?”

“That’s so. And dragged me into an undeserved calamity along with it.”

“Whose fault but your own, not knowing what’s good for you—daring to snatch at the young master’s wife-to-be.”

“A wrong to cry to high heaven . . .”

In the quiet of the night Fourth Madam’s and the eldest young master’s refined pleasures, keeping company with A-Chu and Lishui’s idle banter, made the moonlight seem uncommonly serene.

The sweltering summer came without a sound. Rong Sheng’s and Fourth Madam’s ardour for the art of pingtan rose, along with the mercury, higher and higher. One day Rong Sheng meant to go to the storytelling house to hear a performance, and asked A-Chu to come along; A-Chu begged off, saying he had a medical conference to attend and could not keep him company. Rong Sheng did not press him, and went off, easy and unhurried, by himself.

While A-Chu was at his work in the hospital, a nurse said someone was asking for him at the front gate—word was that Fourth Madam had fallen ill. A-Chu’s heart leaped in alarm; he rushed out in a fluster, only to catch sight of Fourth Madam, dressed in splendour, getting into the same rickshaw as a young man of a little over twenty. A-Chu called out several times, and, seeing that Fourth Madam did not turn her head, felt there was something amiss in it; so he hailed a rickshaw and followed after them.

From a good way off A-Chu saw Fourth Madam and the young man get down at the Dongfang Hotel; and so he too called “stop,” paid his fare, looked up at the Metropole’s signboard, hesitated a moment, and then made up his mind to go in after them.

Two bellboys at the door of the Dongfang Hotel bowed A-Chu a greeting and led him inside. The moment he entered the lobby, two lifts faced him, both already risen with their passengers; A-Chu, not knowing which to follow, stood blankly in the middle of the hall.

Had Fourth Madam come here to lodge? Or to meet someone? Or for something else?

“Might I be of help, sir?” A bellboy, seeing him somewhat at a loss, came forward of his own accord to assist.

“Besides the lifts, is there any other way in or out?” A-Chu asked.

“There is—to the right of the lobby is a reception room, and behind the lifts is the storytelling house.”

“The Dongfang storytelling house?”

“Just so. Have you come to hear the storytelling, sir?”

“Yes . . . yes! Thank you.” A-Chu thought: There’s some devilry today. When I was asked to come and hear the storytelling I wouldn’t; and now here I am come running all this way of my own accord. There’s a trick in it!

“A-Chu, weren’t you engaged today and unable to come?”

A-Chu turned to look: Rong Sheng was standing behind him, regarding him strangely.

“The medical conference was cancelled at short notice, so I came over,” said A-Chu.

Rong Sheng said, very well then, let’s go in. He walked straight ahead, and A-Chu followed him into the storytelling house. The Dongfang storytelling house was very spacious, with two or three hundred seats; at this moment there were still ten minutes to the start, and the audience was drifting in little by little—before long a good half the seats were filled.

Rong Sheng took a seat in the guests-of-honour row, and a waiter came bustling over to ask what tea they would have. A-Chu said: “Longjing.” In a moment the tea-server brought the tea up with great respect. Rong Sheng drew out his cigarette case and opened it—only a single cigarette left inside. Rong Sheng took the cigarette in his hand, and at the same moment A-Chu struck his lighter and lit it for him.

“Go out and buy me another packet,” Rong Sheng said to A-Chu.

“Young master, couldn’t you smoke a little less—”

“I tell you to go, so you go; are you really going to manage me now?”

“All right, I’ll go, I’ll go—” A-Chu edged out along the row of seats; he had just reached the turning when the bell of the storytelling house rang out loud. There was a certain disorder of sound within the house too; at that very moment the curtain was lifted aside, and out came a handsome young man and a lovely young woman, at whom the whole house brightened—and A-Chu was struck with amazement. That man was none other than the very youth on whose account A-Chu had come following after Fourth Madam. The woman wore a close-fitting jacket of thin pink silk gauze, wide-legged trousers of moon-white silk, and on the pink uppers of her shoes were embroidered lotus flowers; the man wore a clear-water-blue gown, and pinned at his breast was a golden lotus—and that lotus design was the very one Fourth Madam so often embroidered on her own collar. The golden lad and the jade maiden began to tune their strings, and the clamour in the house gradually died away; A-Chu suddenly remembered he was to buy cigarettes for the young master—the man wasn’t going to run off anyway, and whatever questions there were could wait until the show was over. A-Chu turned to go, and had barely done so when there came a clear woman’s voice, ringing sharp enough to pierce the clouds and split the stone. “A whole life’s fortunes drift like duckweed . . .” A-Chu wheeled sharply round. “Glory and wealth lie wrapped in cloud and mist. By error he takes the strange land for home . . .” The man pointed a finger down at the audience: “A-Chu, ah—what day shall you go home to wash the traveller’s robe?”

The moment that opening scene-setting verse was out, it fixed A-Chu where he stood, rooted and staring.

The woman asked: “A-Chu? Who is this A-Chu?”

The man said: “A-Chu is the hero of this tale of ours . . . our leading man!”

The woman said: “Ah—so A-Chu is our leading . . . man!”

“This way, sir; sit here.” A quick-witted waiter promptly led A-Chu to a seat.

The woman sang: “In the first year of Xuantong, in the city of Jinling, orioles sang and swallows danced in the spring of the third month. The Yangs of the Jiangnan gentry, a house of note, merchants for generations, their estate flourishing. The master’s name was called . . .”

The man asked: “Called what?”

The woman sang: “The master’s name was called Yang Yubo; he took to wife a lady of the Jin, and deep was their love. Beneath their knees one son, named A-Chu, cherished by his parents as a pearl in the palm. And there was, besides, a little concubine . . . Xu Yuzhen.”

The man sang: “A beauty of matchless grace and heaven-given loveliness. Ai-ya, a matchless beauty.”

The woman sang: “Just then the Yangs’ second master came home from his studies in Japan, his learning complete. Fragrance filled the beaded curtains, wine filled the goblets; the whole house gathered in joy—and calamity came to the door.”

The man said: “A day of singing orioles and dancing swallows, an hour of the whole house gathered in joy—how do you say calamity came to the door?”

The woman said: “Because the Yangs’ second master, Yang Yuhua, was a romantic scholar; sleeping alone, solitary, the nights came desolate upon him. And by ill chance he met with a fair young sister-in-law, Xu Yuzhen . . .” She sang on: “She was lovely as summer’s flowers, fair as jade, glad to watch the Herd-Boy and the Weaving-Maid stars. Yang Yubo’s springtime had already blown away on the wind—how could he match Yang Yuhua, so young and so full of passion? I do not want fine jade and pure gold; I do not want a top-graduate husband; I wish only to share pillow and quilt with the one who knows my heart . . .”

The man sang: “As the saying has it: the man has a heart, the woman has a heart. And there, beneath the moon and before the flowers, they spoke their words of love.”

The woman sang: “Spoke their words of love. She smoothed her collar, all smiles, a thousand charms, a myriad graces. Ai-ya, brother-in-law, ah . . .”

“Sister-in-law!”

“The fair night is short, the flowing water heartless. Who will keep me company—smelling the fragrance beneath the flowers, playing the reed-pipe under the moon, whispering by the pillow, melting the soul upon the mat?”

The man said: “Honoured patrons, you must know that a deed of vile and shameless treachery turned, in the end, into a wrong deep as a sea of blood. To know what came after, listen to the next telling.”

A pluck and a sweep of the little three-stringed sanxian, and the whole house rang with applause.

A-Chu stood up. He needed to know the whole truth. And at this moment the “truth” was right behind him.

Fourth Madam rose from the very last row of the audience, her gaze cold as ice and frost.

The blazing bonfire that would offer up the spirits of the dead had already been lit.
