## The Rōshigumi

Toshizō thoroughly disliked Yamanami Keisuke. The news Yamanami had picked up from another hall—that the shogunate was sponsoring the founding of a "corps of masterless men"—was, to Toshizō, who at bottom was forever thinking (I want to be a proper warrior), the kind of tidings to make a man leap for joy; and yet (wait a moment), he thought. It was the bearer of the news, Yamanami, that he could not stomach.

"Let us make sure of it once more,"

{j} he urged upon Kondō.

Yamanami Keisuke gave the shogunate's aim as,

{j} "for the sake of expelling the barbarian."

{j} This was the notion of the strategist Kiyokawa Hachirō. But was it truly so? To Toshizō it was a question.

Toshizō went with Kondō to a residence at Nigōhanzaka in Ushigome, to call on Matsudaira Kazusa-no-suke, the sponsor of this levy. They had, of course, a proper letter of introduction.

Matsudaira Kazusa-no-suke received them affably. This too was the times. Consider it: the house of Kazusa-no-suke was of the blood of Tadanaga, younger brother of the third shogun Iemitsu; a mere throwaway stipend of three hundred koku, yet in standing a collateral branch of the Tokugawa main house, of rank to take the lowest seat among the related-house daimyo within Chiyoda Castle. In another age he was not a man to meet a masterless swordsman so easily.

"Ah, that business,"

{j} said this exalted person.

"The duty is the guarding of the shogun's house."

By what Kazusa-no-suke said, the shogun would shortly go up to Kyoto.

Kyoto was a den of extremist rōnin. Every day, with bloody blades, they were cutting down the politicians of the opposing party. What danger might beset the shogun's person none could tell, and so men honored in the martial arts were being levied.

"Is it,"

{j} Kondō was overcome,

{j} "is it true?"

How deep Kondō's emotion was at that moment we of today cannot even imagine. The shogun was a being all but divine, for two hundred years and more the fountainhead of all value and authority under heaven. The rōnin Kondō Isami Masayoshi, his forehead pressed to the matting, could not still his trembling for a long while. When Toshizō stole a sidelong glance, Kondō was letting fall tears. In truth, to Kondō's mind, one life laid down, or two, would have been no matter for regret.

Such, at times, is a man.

Kondō's one beloved book was Rai San'yō's Nihon Gaishi. The Nihon Gaishi is a kind of literary work, painting in grand romance the rise and fall of power; and of all the figures in that romance, the man Kondō loved best was Kusunoki Masashige.

Kusunoki Masashige is a dashing spirit who appears out of nowhere at a certain moment in the history of the Northern and Southern Courts. Until then he had been a nameless local baron of Mount Kongō in Kawachi (not so much as listed in Kamakura's roll of vassals); but because the fugitive Southern emperor (Go-Daigo) tapped him on the shoulder with "aid me," on that emotion alone he threw his whole clan into desperate battle for the flagging Southern Court, and at last met a suicidal soldier's death at Minatogawa. In his book, Rai San'yō treats him as the greatest gallant of all Japanese history.

England has its like.

It is legend, but in the time of the famous Richard the Lionheart, while King Richard was away on crusade, the king's brother sought to usurp the throne. The one who rose to defend the royal right was Robin Hood, a local baron of Sherwood Forest, and the matchlessly dashing tale of this forest hero is loved by the English still. But this is a digression.

Some days after, Toshizō went to Satō Hikogorō, the village headman of Hino, told him the whole of the corps enlistment, and,

{j} "And so, brother, I have a favor to ask,"

{j} he said.

"Is it a thing I can do? You're to become a warrior, Toshi. If it's a thing I can grant, I'll grant anything. What is it?"

"A sword."

"How careless of me. I should have given you one unasked, without your having to press me,"

{j} and the scatterbrained Hikogorō led him to the Buddha-room, gave a light slap to a great sword-chest of oak fitted with iron, and,

{j} "There's thirty blades here. Take any that pleases you,"

{j} he said, with a bottomlessly good-natured smile.

Seeing his brother-in-law's smile, Toshizō was at a loss.

Such common blades he would not have wanted by the bundle. He wanted a famous sword. And in the matter of the signature, he had an outrageous ambition. He thought a while, then,

{j} "Is my sister at home?"

"Onobu? She's gone out, but should be back soon. Have you business with her too?"

"With the pair of you together, I'd like to beg something."

"So, so."

Presently Onobu came home from tending the last master's grave, and heard from Toshizō's own lips the matter of the corps.

"So."

{j} A big-bellied woman, she said nothing.

Onobu was the fourth of the six Hijikata children, and had doted terribly on this youngest brother, the trouble of the whole house. Toshizō too loved this sister greatly, and from childhood had spent more time at the Satō house, her married home, than at the house of his birth.

"This favor—what is it?"

{j} said Onobu.

"I mean to buy a sword. I'd like to beg the money for it."

"How much?"

"Having once opened my mouth, I'd not have you refuse me; so first, say you agree."

"Very well,"

{j} Hikogorō showed his big-bellied side.

{j} "How much?"

"A hundred ryō."

At this both husband and wife fell silent. Sell several good rice-fields of that country and you would not raise so much. It was an age when a menial kept in the household earned three ryō a year in wages.

Hikogorō's voice turned rough despite himself.

{j} "What manner of sword do you mean to buy?"

"A famous blade, such as a shogun or a great lord might carry,"

{j} said Toshizō, unruffled.

"Presumptuous. ..."

"So my brother thinks it?"

{j} Toshizō's eyes were set.

"But the sum is too great."

"In Kyoto the western domains and lawless rōnin swagger through the streets as if they owned them. It is the shogun I am to guard from those mad blades. The sword that guards him needs a fitting dignity and a fitting edge."

"———"

"Master Kondō, they say, is hunting for a Kotetsu."

"A Kotetsu?"

That too was a great lord's blade.

"Isami is? A Kotetsu?"

"Just so. A sword-dealer at Hikagechō below Atago is scouring for one this very moment. In Kyoto the work turns on arm and blade for life or death. I too would carry a masterpiece to rank with a Kotetsu."

"W-well, there's something in that,"

{j} Hikogorō looked at his wife Onobu with something near fright in his eyes. Onobu was calm. The truth was that when she came as a bride from the Hijikata house, her own father had put fifty ryō into her mirror-stand.

"Toshi, take fifty ryō from your brother."

"Will fifty do?"

Onobu then added her own fifty, made up four packets of twenty-five ryō, and handed them to Toshizō.

"I am in your debt,"

{j} and this man, so arrogant and overbearing with strangers, took them with a childlike smile that made Onobu long, in spite of herself, to stroke his cheek.

From the next day this man went running about the sword-dealers' quarter below Atago and every sword-shop in Edo, and,

{j} "Have you an Izumi-no-kami Kanesada?"

{j} he would ask.

A famous great-wazamono.

It cuts. A fine one will sever even Southern-barbarian iron. Now, the ranking of the great-wazamono among blades is a settled thing. There were twenty-one smiths—Horikawa Kunihiro, Tōshirō Sukesada, Tsuda Sukehiro (famous under the by-name Soboro Sukehiro), and the rest—and of them all the Izumi-no-kami Kanesada stood at the head, its edge such that the steel was said to hold a demon in it.

"A Kanesada? You, sir?"

{j} every dealer was startled. It was not a thing a mere rōnin ought to carry.

"A first- or a third-generation Kanesada I might have,"

{j} some would say. Even of the same Izumi-no-kami Kanesada, the first and the third generations were common smiths, and cheap—a fit blade for a rōnin. But Toshizō,

{j} "The Nosada,"

{j} he said. The second generation. What is called the great-wazamono Kanesada goes by the by-name Nosada. The reason is that this smith habitually cut the 定 of his signature in an old variant form; taking that character apart into its elements, 之 and 定, one reads it no-sada—hence "Nosada."

In the old days the Warring-States lords Hosokawa Yūsai and his son Tadaoki favored it; and besides them, Mori, Lord of Musashi—the fierce general of Toyotomi Hideyoshi called the "Demon Musashi"—was fond of this Kanesada's cruciform-bladed spear, on which he himself had graven the eerie words,

{j} —Ningen Mukotsu: man without bones—

{j} and would skewer his enemies on it like so many roots.

Toshizō knew the tale of that "Ningen Mukotsu" from hearsay. Where the great-wazamono Kanesada danced, a man became, as it were, a thing without bones.

"Have you an Izumi-no-kami Kanesada?"

{j} he walked the streets asking, day after day.

"I have,"

{j} said—of all places—a secondhand-goods dealer of Asakusa, an old man blind, the whites of both eyes gone milky.

"You are sure?"

"If you doubt it, you need not buy."

"No—I ask whether an appraisal is sure, with those eyes."

"Where swords are concerned,"

{j} the old man laughed in a dry voice,

{j} "the sighted are the more in danger. I went blind ten years ago, at seventy; and ever since, take a sword in hand and my mind is clear of all clutter. Even the sword-smiths below Atago, with a hard piece, come out here to Asakusa and put it in my hands."

"Show me."

The old man brought out from within a single blade in a plain wooden scabbard, so aged it was foul to the touch.

"Look you."

He drew it.

Red rust. Toshizō felt an anger he could feel draining the color from his own face. But with an air of unconcern,

{j} "The price—how much?"

"Five ryō."

Toshizō was silent. For a while he glared at the dried-up old blind man, then,

{j} "Why so cheap?"

{j} he said.

"This,"

{j} in the mouth that laughed there were no teeth,

{j} "well, it is a wonder to hear cheapness complained of. Would a hundred ryō, say, content you?"

"Do you mock me?"

{j} he said in a low voice; but the old man was not shaken.

"A sword too has its allotted life, its fortune of heaven. This blade—whatever it was in the Eishō years of its birth (the close of the Ashikaga age)—never once thereafter came into the hands of a great lord or a warrior of rank. Long it slept in the storehouse of some deep-country magnate of Dewa, and after several hundred years a thief stole it, and only so did it come out into the dark world at last. That thief brought it here to us—such is the tale that hangs on it."

The old man was disclosing no light matter. Reach the ears of the authorities, and it was a fact to put a rope on his hands. What could be his true meaning in disclosing it?

"I took a fancy to you,"

{j} the old man said carelessly, and went on. By a blind man's intuition, he had thought this rōnin who came expressly hunting an Izumi-no-kami Kanesada was no ordinary man.

"For several hundred years, this sword has been longing to meet you, I daresay. Somehow I know such things. Five ryō—and if that displeases you, I'll give it you for nothing. Do you laugh? Fifty years in the secondhand trade, and a man wants to indulge a whim like this."

There was something of the rough-tongued flash about him. No mere secondhand dealer, this old fellow; behind it all he might be doing things the magistrate's office would take no pleasure in.

"I'll lay five ryō on this,"

{j} said Toshizō.

He had it ground at once, below Atago.

It was finished in the First Month of the third year of Bunkyū, the departure for Kyoto now near.

The mounting was iron, single-mindedly for use, the scabbard black lacquer of a waxen sheen—all to Toshizō's own order.

A splendid grinding; to any eye it was, past mistaking, an Izumi-no-kami Kanesada.

In the temper-line lay little scatterings, fine as adzuki beans; the ground-metal was blue as if to drink in the pupil of the eye, and the straight grain stood up fierce as gooseflesh.

(It cuts.—)

{j} The hand that held the sword seemed like to tremble.

From that night on, this man's conduct grew so strange that Okita Sōji wondered at it.

For one thing, at night he did not come home to the hall.

Coming back toward dawn, he would sleep sound until noon, and at dusk go out again.

"Hijikata,"

{j} Okita cocked his head prettily.

"So it is a real thing, after all."

"What is?"

"Fox-possession, I mean. Even your face has come to look the part. I've a mountain-ascetic among my acquaintance—shall I take you along to have the fox driven out?"

"Fool."

{j} Toshizō was dusting the sword with polishing-powder.

The sun had begun to set, and with the paper screen at his back, Okita's face was too dark to see well.

"Which quarter is it tonight?"

"———?"

"No use hiding it."

The young man had noticed.

Of late, cut-and-run killings on the streets had come into fashion. Mostly it was not robbery; with the world grown murderous in the expel-the-barbarian fever, rōnin swordsmen, on the plea of honing their arms against the coming of the foreigner, would stand out in the town by night.

Almost every night a man was cut down.

Most of the victims were samurai. For this, samurai grew fewer who would go about the streets at night.

Such affairs were many in this Koishikawa neighborhood too.

That year's end, when night after night a comet flew toward the eastern sky, there were, on a single night, one such killing at Shimizudani in Kohinata, one at Kubomachi in Ōtsuka, and one in the paddies at Tozakichō—in each a samurai in a lord's service struck down.

Once, after the new year had come in, when a footman from a hatamoto household was cut down before the stonemason's by the hall at Yanagichō, a constable of the magistrate's office fixed his eye on Kondō's hall and came asking, again and again.

"An ill sport, that. You'd do better to leave off, I think, myself.—"

{j} said Okita, with a face that made no great matter of it.

But that night, too, Toshizō went out.

Cut-and-run killing was not his object.

It was to meet a man of that kind that Toshizō walked out each night, picking his way from one rumored spot to another.

At last he met one.

The lower half of the Hour of the Dog.

As Toshizō passed before the torii of the Kanasugi Inari and came to the corner of a hatamoto residence, one Kubota's, of a sudden a sword-stroke was dealt him from behind.

Barely springing to the wall and whirling round, he had already drawn the Izumi-no-kami Kanesada and set it in his peculiar low guard.

(………?)

Toshizō kept his sullen silence. There was a moon. Under it, his opponent's shadow was shifting quietly to the left.

(This one can fight,)

{j} he thought—for the man had sheathed his sword again and, right hand hanging, begun to circle Toshizō without a footfall. By that foot, that hip, he was a fine hand at iai.

Toshizō strained his eyes.

Night sight is a thing that must never fix the shadow head-on. Fix a little above the shape, and the shape rises clear at the rim of the field of view. It is the lore of night fighting.

"Ho,"

{j} said the man.

{j} "I'll ask you first. Of what domain are you? And if you'll give your name into the bargain, I'll see you get your funeral rites."

"Pfah,"

{j} Toshizō spat. After that he was silent.

The man seemed to wait for Toshizō to make the first move. The distance was but six shaku. Let either misstep, and one of the two would be a corpse.

Toshizō too waited for the other to move.

To strike the very rising of the foe's attack, once he committed—that was the trick of iai.

(How to make him draw?)

For iai there was no other counter but that.

Toshizō softly bent his knees.

He shot them straight in one thrust.

At that instant he sprang a full ken sideways along the wall, and in the same motion drew his short-sword with a speed like a released spring, and, drawn, leapt into the killing-ground of the distance between them as though gone mad.

He threw the short-sword.

Quicker than the throw the man drove in, dropped his hips, and, whining his bare blade through the air, cut straight down at Toshizō's crown.

The steel spat fire.

When had he taken it up?—Toshizō, gripping an iron war-fan reversed in his left fist, met the enemy's bare blade with it.

By then the Izumi-no-kami Kanesada, held light in Toshizō's right hand, had already whirled like a wind and been sucked into the right side of the man's face, split the bone, laid it open above the right eye-socket so that the eyeball started out and the jaw sank; and just so, the man pitched forward and dashed his face against the ground. Dead on the instant.

(It cuts.)

That night was the thirtieth of the First Month.

Some days later, on the eighth of the Second Month, Toshizō and the three hundred newly levied rōshi gathered at the Denzū-in in Koishikawa and set out from Edo; and it was on the evening of the twenty-third of the Second Month of the third year of Bunkyū, having tramped the sixty-eight stages of the Nakasendō, one hundred and thirty ri, that they came up to Kyoto.

Toshizō entered the lodgings at Mibu.

On his sleeve the blood of Edo was seeping still.
