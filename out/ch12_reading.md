## Chapter Twelve. The Hour of the Pig, First Half (9 p.m.)

Far off, at the end of the street, there appeared first six horsemen in golden armor, then eight attendants bearing vermilion-lacquered round fans and peacock screen-fans, and close behind them a stately four-windowed carriage came rolling up, drawn by four chestnut steeds, with ten-odd guards in brocade riding to left and right.

The third year of Tianbao, the fourteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Pig, first half.

Chang'an; Wannian County; Pingkang Ward.

The Shouzhuolang had split into ten-odd parties and, like quicksilver spilled across the ground, seeped into the cobweb of narrow, twisting lanes, searching back and forth. Each party numbered at least two men, for the fighting power of their quarry was truly astonishing.

Only moments before, they had plainly driven that reckless devil into the lanes—how could he have vanished in the blink of an eye? The Shouzhuolang squad leader, his face black as thunder, barked at his men to seal off every way in and out and hold them fast; he did not believe a wounded man had wings to fly away.

The day had gone badly enough already. With the firemaster dead, their trade in Chang'an would suffer grievously; and if the killer still could not be caught, then he, as squad leader, was finished.

"Chief, the constables are still over there . . ." one of the Shouzhuolang reminded him.

The squad leader followed his pointing finger and saw the same five constables as before, trailing close behind but keeping their distance. He spat in contempt. "Those good-for-nothings. Pay them no mind."

"I saw them strike the brass gong just now."

The squad leader frowned. For a post-soldier to strike the brass gong was a signal of alarm to the constable-posts round about; before long every constable in Pingkang Ward would be roused. The Shouzhuolang were, after all, no arm of the government, and to seal off several lanes so openly was to court trouble they did not need.

"Send the lads into the houses to search! Any who object, stop their mouths with money! And be quick about it!" the squad leader ordered through gritted teeth. Since the man was neither in the lanes nor gone from the district, he must have broken into one of the dwellings.

The little lanes and byways hereabouts were home to ordinary folk, their courtyards no more than two rooms deep. Most of the residents were out watching the lanterns just now, so the Shouzhuolang burst into empty houses. Now and then some commoner who had stayed home would see his door kicked in and tremble with fright; the Shouzhuolang would usually toss down a few strings of cash and warn him to say nothing of what he had seen. In no time the whole quarter was in an uproar of squawking hens and yelping dogs, as though brutal clerks had come down to the villages to wring out the rents.

Two of the Shouzhuolang, searching their way along, suddenly caught sight of a house at a corner ahead: no lamp burned within, yet its courtyard gate stood half open. The two exchanged a glance and drew near.

They did not rush in, but bent with their lanterns to examine the threshold, and found on it several drops of blood, not yet congealed. The two could not contain their delight; first they signaled their comrades round about to close in quickly, then drew their weapons and stepped into the courtyard . . .

All at once a shrill, ghastly scream tore across the night sky.

Every searching Shouzhuolang started at the sound, recognizing it as one of their own, and hurried to converge on the quarter it had come from. The squad leader arrived at the gate of the house with fury on his face and marked the blood on the threshold too. But he did not rush inside; instead he ordered his men to ring the whole house about, and only then, taking a few of his fiercest, charged into the little courtyard.

Just inside the gate they saw first a small vegetable plot, with one Shouzhuolang sprawled face-down on the earthen ridge, his face a mask of blood, whether living or dead none could tell. The squad leader and the others went at once on their guard and, weapons in hand, advanced step by careful step. Soon they saw, on the steps before the house, another Shouzhuolang lying likewise drenched in blood. Most horrible of all, a sharp-pointed spindle had been driven into his left eye, and beside him a spinning wheel lay overturned on the ground.

At so grim a sight the men drew breath as one: this fellow's hand was savage beyond all measure.

The squad leader ordered the two wounded men carried out with all speed, then led the way himself and kicked open the main room. But when they had gone round it—under the couch and behind the bed, atop the beams and inside the chests—and searched with care, they came up with nothing. The Shouzhuolang went on to the side chambers and the rear court, and there too found not a trace.

The Shouzhuolang posted outside reported one after another that they had seen no one climb the wall to get away—they had even checked the dog-hole at the foot of it.

The squad leader stood in the middle of the courtyard, pinching his chin in thought for a moment, when suddenly his eyes lit up—there was one place they had passed over! In two strides he rushed into the kitchen of the left side chamber. A large family must have lived here, for they had built a great vaulted stove. At a glance the squad leader saw fresh drops of blood among the dry kindling before the stove-mouth. He called loudly for the others to come at once, then took up an iron hook for raking out the fire-chamber and jabbed it hard inside.

Sure enough, halfway in the squad leader felt the hook strike something soft, like flesh. He drew it back a little and jabbed hard again. So he did, over and over, until he was certain the other had no power left to resist; only then did he have his men drag him out through the stove-mouth.

The Shouzhuolang, all hands at once, soon hauled a man out of the stove. The squad leader stepped up meaning to land a kick first, to vent his spleen—then he looked down, and the triumph on his face froze in an instant.

This was not Zhang Xiaojing, but one of the very Shouzhuolang who had gone in through the gate before!

In a flash the squad leader understood what had happened.

Zhang Xiaojing had struck down the two Shouzhuolang who came through the gate. The first he had left with his face all over blood, cast down by the doorway, so that whoever entered would fall into a fixed way of seeing; then he had disguised himself as the second man, and had deliberately used the spindle to hide his left eye—while the real second man was the one stuffed into the stove.

The courtyard was pitch dark, and even by lantern-light, in the heat of the moment, no one would look closely enough to tell one from another. While the squad leader was still hunting through the house, Zhang Xiaojing had already been carried out of the lanes by the Shouzhuolang themselves.

"After him, quick!" the squad leader roared.

They hurried back to the mouth of the lane, but it was already too late. Several Shouzhuolang lay fallen on the ground, and on the stretcher was only a single blood-faced casualty; the killer had long since vanished into the dark. With a crash, the great hammer in the squad leader's hand smashed against the earthen wall beside him.

Yet Zhang Xiaojing's peril, for all that, was far from over.

Out on the streets, party after party of constables went running past, busy throwing up defenses at every key thoroughfare. Farther off, more soldiers still were spreading into a cordon amid much shouting and clamor. Several of the main street-mouths had been barred. These men might lack the Shouzhuolang's stomach for a fight, but they won by numbers, and they wore the government's colors besides—which made them more troublesome yet.

Zhang Xiaojing had no idea why he was being hunted, who had given the order, or what the charge against him was. Just now his mind held only one thing—run!

Once clear of the lanes, trusting to his knowledge of the ground, he moved swiftly toward the gate of Pingkang Ward. But he soon found the road ahead sealed and impassable, and had to take cover behind the base of a banner-pole, his back to a wall. Zhang Xiaojing felt at his lower belly; the wound there, the deepest of the knife-cuts he had taken, was still seeping blood.

Zhang Xiaojing felt himself all but crushed by exhaustion. Gasping for breath, he tilted his head back without thinking, and saw that a watchtower in the distance was signaling toward him with a purple lamp.

The signal came from the great watchtower, and its content was simple, just two words:

Do not retreat.

Zhang Xiaojing guessed at once who had sent it. Who but that green fool Yao Runeng would put a thing that way?

But what good was there in not retreating?

Zhang Xiaojing gave a bitter smile. Yao Runeng's signal of "Do not retreat" declared his stand, true enough; but it also hinted that he was under enormous pressure—which meant the Jing'an Bureau's whole posture had violently changed, and that something must have befallen Li Bi.

At the thought his single eye dimmed a little. With no Jing'an Bureau to back him, how much farther could the investigation go? The Que-le Huo-duo was on the very point of destroying Chang'an, and yet the one man who still cared about it had become the enemy of the whole city—what a bitter irony that was.

The purple lamp of the distant watchtower still flickered, and Zhang Xiaojing knew it was the one thing in all Chang'an that still stood at his side. Yet just now he could not even answer it.

At that moment a broad-bodied open carriage went speeding past up the street. It was exquisitely adorned, and must belong to some grandee. A ravishing singing-girl stood in the very middle of it, turning and turning in a whirling dance, five-colored ribbons of satin flying out one after another from her sleeves, while five or six people sat round her cheering.

This was the new fashion of the hour. As she danced, the dancer would fling out the cut lengths of fine brocade one by one with a deft turn of strength; done well, the ribbons would trace all manner of patterns in mid-air, matched to the movements of the dance, like rosy clouds swirling in flight—and so it was called the Rosy-Cloud Fling. But a single dance cost two or three bolts of silk, an indulgence beyond the reach of ordinary folk.

Zhang Xiaojing watched the carriage roll on toward the barricade, scattering brocade the length of its way. Something stirred in him; while the constables at the street-mouth were stopping the carriage, he darted out, stooped to snatch up a handful, and hurried back.

From among them Zhang Xiaojing picked out two or three purple strips, wound them about a lantern he had lifted in passing from before someone's door, and, forcing down the fierce pain all through his body, climbed onto a wall-top and began to wave it toward the watchtower.

Soon the watchtower's signal flashed three times, to show it was received. The link was restored.

Even by the watchtower, Zhang Xiaojing dared not speak too plainly. He sent a reply back to the great watchtower, saying only two words: "Received."

Then he gave orders to the watchtowers of Pingkang Ward, requiring them to observe the state of every sealed-off stretch of road and to report continuously.

"Report continuously" meant this: without Zhang Xiaojing's having to ask, the moment a watchtower saw any change in the cordon it would send out a signal of its own accord. So Zhang Xiaojing had only to raise his eyes to know at any moment how the situation was shifting, without having to risk exposing himself by waving a lantern again.

When Li Bi first designed this system, he had meant to shut out interference from without as far as possible, and had laid down that the watchtowers were to take orders only from the great watchtower or from one bearing the tally of delegated authority, and to heed nothing else. So the constables of the watchtowers knew nothing of the changes outside; still less did they know that the man now sending them orders had been made the object of a city-wide hue and cry.

And so, in Pingkang Ward that night, a strange scene took shape. The soldiers of the constable-posts strove with all their might to seize the wanted criminal Zhang Xiaojing; and at the same time, the eyes of all Chang'an went on feeding intelligence to Commander Zhang. Two systems of security ran side by side without collision, both toward a single object, yet each for its own contrary purpose, and both turning like mad.

Under the watchtowers' guidance, the dispositions across Pingkang Ward had nowhere to hide. Zhang Xiaojing passed successfully through three cordons and was on the point of reaching the gate. But the ward guards there had by now received their orders and had raised a wall of thorns, checking every passing traveler and cart.

Zhang Xiaojing's single eye swept the scene, and he saw a post-soldier leave the gate and come round to a corner on this side to make water. He crept up softly, then all at once got him about the neck from behind.

The man let out a few strangled croaks, unable to make a sound. Zhang Xiaojing loosened his arm a little and said in a low voice: "Old Zhao. It's me."

"Ch-Chief Zhang? So it really is you!" The old post-soldier started, and even gave up his struggle. "When I heard the warrant read, I thought it must be someone else of the same name."

"I need a favor of you, to get out of Pingkang Ward," said Zhang Xiaojing. The old post-soldier hesitated a moment, then threw back his head. "Back when we were hunting Li the Swallow, if Chief Zhang hadn't stood in front of me, I'd have given up my life long ago. Only right that I pay it back this once."

"I'm not asking for your life, only that you play along."

He had the old post-soldier fetch a soldier's tabard and put it on himself. Old Zhao went and came back, and sure enough had roused no one. Once the two were dressed the part, they walked toward the gate, one behind the other. At the gate, Old Zhao's fellows were busy checking the passing carts and horses. Seeing there was one man more than before, they asked what was going on. Old Zhao said the man was a new recruit who had just spotted the wanted criminal and come to blows with him, and was on his way out to report.

His fellow blinked. "You saw his face? Was it that Zhang the Yama?"

Zhang Xiaojing bowed his head and gave a slight nod. His left eye was bound up with a strip of white cloth, as though he had taken a grave wound. His fellow clicked his tongue in sympathy. "Zhang the Yama, right enough—savage with his hands. Say, Old Zhao, as I recall you served under him a while, didn't you?"

"Ah, that was years ago now." Old Zhao coughed a few times to cover himself and shoved Zhang Xiaojing forward. "Off you go, quick; report and come straight back."

"Hold on." His fellow suddenly stopped Zhang Xiaojing.

Old Zhao and Zhang Xiaojing both tensed inwardly. The man looked him up and down, then suddenly laughed. "A raw recruit sure enough—you've even got your coat on backwards."

A post-soldier's tabard was a sleeveless smock of gray ochre, open at the front and closed behind. Zhang Xiaojing was too badly hurt, and Old Zhao too on edge, for either of them to have noticed the flaw.

A fierce light flashed in Zhang Xiaojing's single eye; he clenched his fist, ready to burst into action at any moment. Old Zhao hastily smoothed it over: "These tabards of ours are like a woman's—a new lad never can tell the front from the back." At the coarse joke the whole company roared with laughter. The fellow pressed the matter no further, and waved them through.

Old Zhao led Zhang Xiaojing past the wall of thorns, and at the sight of the sea of people on the great street outside the ward, the tension went out of him. Old Zhao made a small bow with both hands. "This is as far as I can see you. Take care of yourself." Then, after a moment's thought, he drew out half a string of copper cash and held it out to him.

Zhang Xiaojing did not take the money, but said mildly: "Get back quick. Next time you see me, arrest me all the same—no need to make it hard on yourself." Old Zhao rubbed his head. "It'd never come to that, never. Chief for a day, chief for life; I'll count you my chief as long as I live."

Zhang Xiaojing said no more, but turned and walked off out of the ward.

By the watchtower's latest report, this was the last cordon; once past it he would be more or less safe. He was just stepping forward when he suddenly saw a man ahead staring hard at him. Zhang Xiaojing did not know the fellow, but his dress and the flat-tined fork in his hand betrayed who he was.

A Shouzhuolang?

The watchtowers could keep watch over the constable-posts, but they could not see a Shouzhuolang acting alone. So the enemy had posted themselves at the gate early, waiting for Zhang Xiaojing to appear.

"You're Zhang Xiaojing!" The Shouzhuolang stepped forward and shouted at the top of his voice.

His voice was loud, loud enough that every ward guard and post-soldier stationed at the gate heard it. At the name they all turned their heads as one. Quick as thought, Zhang Xiaojing seized Old Zhao and fell rapidly back into the ward.

Old Zhao knew well enough that this was Chief Zhang clearing him of suspicion, and he played along, crying out, Don't kill me, don't kill me. Zhang Xiaojing drew back inside the gate, shoved Old Zhao hard into the knot of ward guards, then wheeled and ran. Right in his path stood a wall of thorns; Zhang Xiaojing had no time even to go around it, and simply crashed straight through, his robe tearing with a rip as the thorns dragged a bloody strip from it.

At this the post-soldiers were all roused, and went pouring after him. The Shouzhuolang, too, gave a whistle to summon his comrades nearby to muster at once.

Now it was truly a net stretched across heaven and earth. On the great streets were massed post-soldiers hunting him down; in the little lanes were parties of Shouzhuolang everywhere. With scarcely anywhere left to go, Zhang Xiaojing could only grit his teeth and run on.

Trusting to his knowledge of the ground and his hard-won experience of a fight, several times he escaped by a hair, breaking free of pursuit at the last possible instant. But Pingkang Ward was only so large, and each time the enemy pressed him harder than the last. Now it was post-soldiers, now Shouzhuolang, and every time his plight was graver than before.

Zhang Xiaojing gritted his teeth and gasped for breath, every wound on his body aching, his tattered robe seeping livid streaks of red. He did not know how much longer he could hold out.

But he could not stop, for behind him he could hear his pursuers' footfalls without cease, and could only force himself into a headlong run. He knew not how long he had run when a blackness began to close over his eyes—not the black of the night, but the black of a deep well. Even that one star of hope on the distant watchtower he could no longer see.

He could not tell whether it was for want of light along the road, or that his body was nearing its limit. Zhang Xiaojing lunged forward another ten-odd paces, and then a powerful sense of helplessness came down upon him.

No—it was less helplessness than despair, the despair of a man who, struggle as he might, could see no end to it.

That despair made his steps stagger in an instant, and he pitched forward.

And at that moment, a jet-black hand reached out of the jet-black night and caught Zhang Xiaojing beneath the crook of his arm.

Wang Yunxiu was, at this moment, both terrified and furious.

Terrified, because several vicious ruffians had suddenly appeared in the woodshed—men she knew, the very ones who had abducted her. They pulled a cloth sack over her head. The sack had once held old rice, and reeked so of mildew it nearly made her faint. Then they hauled her onto a mule-cart to carry her off, she knew not where.

Furious, because that man named Yuan Zai had broken his word and grown fat on it. He had sworn again and again that he would get her out, yet to this very moment there had been not a stir from him. Now that she was being dragged onto the cart, likely to be killed, still he did not appear. True, the man was a stranger to her; but a gentleman's single promise is worth a thousand in gold—ought he not to make good on what he had said? That was how it always went in the operas.

The more Wang Yunxiu thought on it the angrier she grew, but soon anger gave way to despair. If Yuan Zai did not come, was not her last shred of hope gone as well?

She lay slumped in the mule-cart, all before her eyes pitch black. The cart was driven none too steadily, swaying and lurching so that her back struck the wall of the box again and again. The little strength Wang Yunxiu had painfully gathered ebbed slowly away once more. Her spirit worn to its last, she heard the faint sound of singing and cheering drift in from outside, and two lines of aggrieved, clear tears rolled slowly down.

Today is the Lantern Festival; I ought by rights to be driving my Xi cart out to view the lanterns under ten thousand admiring eyes, not curled up in a corner of a wretched cart like this, like a beast being led to the slaughter. Father, save me, oh save me . . .

Just as Wang Yunxiu, drowsy and faint, was about to drift into sleep, the mule-cart suddenly jolted to a halt. Her body pitched forward and she nearly fell to the floor. Unable to see with either eye, she heard only shouts of challenge and the sounds of a struggle.

The fighting did not last long; then the cart shuddered, as though someone had stepped up onto it. In the next instant a hand pulled away the sack, and a warm light fell on Wang Yunxiu's face. She opened her eyes in a daze and saw a man holding a festival lantern up beside his ear, gazing at her; in the candlelight the strange, broad-browed face looked wonderfully kind.

"Mistress Wang, forgive me for coming late." Yuan Zai spoke gently, and reached out a hand to her.

Wang Yunxiu burst into loud weeping. Even as she wept she kicked and beat at Yuan Zai, reproaching him for not coming sooner. Yuan Zai said nothing, but gripped her hand firmly and helped her down from the cart. Having been bound so long, Wang Yunxiu could not keep her feet the moment they touched the ground; she swayed and was about to fall when Yuan Zai caught her by the waist.

Wang Yunxiu's cheeks flushed crimson all at once—was the man not being far too forward? But her body was limp and she had no way to struggle. Happily Yuan Zai let go the instant he had touched her, and turned to fetch a brocade fur, which he draped over her. "The night is too cold; put this on." Wang Yunxiu noticed that Yuan Zai's chest bore a gash, as though from the cut of a blade.

Yuan Zai seemed to sense Wang Yunxiu's gaze, and smiled. "Did I not say as much before? Having met me, Yuan Zai, today, you will come to no more harm." She looked about her, and sure enough, on the ground lay several corpses—all of them the men who had abducted her—while round about ten-odd mailed soldiers stood on patrol.

Wang Yunxiu asked what on earth had happened. Yuan Zai said: "It is a long tale. In short, a scoundrel named Zhang Xiaojing abducted you under the name of a Commander of the Jing'an Bureau, and I chanced to discover it. I mustered a body of men to search everywhere, and at last I have found you."

Whether or not it was her own fancy, Wang Yunxiu could not tell, but the words with which Yuan Zai said "at last I have found you" came out burning hot, holding within them a concern he could not suppress. She quickly bowed her head, afraid he might see her face.

Yuan Zai stretched out a hand, and from a distance a Xi cart came rolling up—not Wang Yunxiu's own, but the same model, differing only a little in its ornament. She was astonished; she had not thought he would have looked into the matter so thoroughly. Yuan Zai explained: "I went to survey the scene of the abduction, and so I thought that you might like to ride in a cart of this kind."

Wang Yunxiu's eyes shone, and for a moment she did not know what to say. When the Xi cart had stopped, Yuan Zai crooked his arm, and she meekly put out her hand, took the crook of it, and stepped up into the cart. Then Yuan Zai sprang up after her and bade the driver start.

The Xi cart set off, the mailed soldiers falling in at a run to left and right, their boots striking the ground in unison, a grand and stately array—but its course was not toward Anren Ward. Meeting Wang Yunxiu's puzzlement, Yuan Zai bowed over his clasped hands. "My apologies, Mistress Wang. You cannot return to your house just yet; you must first come along with me a while."

"I have suffered a great deal already, and my mother will be very anxious," Wang Yunxiu complained resentfully.

"Mistress Wang, this abduction of yours touches on grave matters, and must be handled with the greatest care—do you understand?" There was in Yuan Zai's words a decision that brooked no argument.

This time Wang Yunxiu did not lose her temper, but asked in a small voice where they were going. Yuan Zai said with a smile: "Rest easy. It is the safest place in the whole capital, save only the palace city itself—the Jing'an Bureau . . . ah, to be exact, the new Jing'an Bureau."

Their Xi cart went first along the southern city, where the press of people was relatively thin, then pressed on to the northwest, and soon reached Guangde Ward.

The blaze in the great hall of the Jing'an Bureau still raged, but those who could be saved had been saved, and the places that had to be cut off had been cut off; all that remained was to wait for it to burn itself out—perhaps by the third watch, perhaps by daybreak, no one could say for certain. The Jing'an Bureau had removed provisionally to the offices of the Jingzhao Prefecture next door, and had hastily conscripted a batch of new clerks from here and there; everywhere was in an uproar, and when it might truly recover its function and take up the hunt for the Pifu again, none could tell.

At this moment Ji Wen stood before the main hall, watching a long file of officials trudge dejectedly past. Every one of them was high of nose and deep of eye, plainly of foreign blood.

The leader of the attack had seemed to be a foreigner with a Kucha accent. So Ji Wen had issued an order that all the surviving foreign officials be driven out, one and all, and forbidden to go on with the work of the Jing'an Bureau.

The foreigners made up a third of the Jing'an Bureau's survivors, so this one order cut its precious body of experienced men by another three or four parts in ten. Several of the recorders objected strongly, but Ji Wen retorted with an air of righteous conviction: "One who is not of our kind must be of a different heart. Do your own hearts, then, incline to the barbarians?"

At these words no one dared speak another syllable. Their cowed silence pleased Ji Wen well: it meant he held absolute command over the Jing'an Bureau, and the feeling was truly splendid.

And so the foreigners, having no recourse, could only depart—under the helpless gaze of their colleagues—from the place to which they had given their loyalty. They could not even go home, for they had still to undergo a rigorous investigation, the very thing the Censorate did best.

As for that devil of a name the recorders kept muttering over—"Que-le Huo-duo," or was it "Que-te-le-duo," or some such—Ji Wen did not much care. Even if disaster came, the blame would fall on his predecessor; why should he fret? What he had to do now was pour every resource into "the hunt for the Pifu"—no, into "the hunt for Zhang Xiaojing, chief of the Pifu bandits."

This was the surest way to show results; to catch one man was always easier than to catch a whole band, and it would slap the face of the heir apparent's faction into the bargain.

Ji Wen signed off yet another scroll of documents, pressing the offices everywhere to intensify the search. Suddenly harness-bells jingled; he laid down his brush, looked up, and saw Yuan Zai stepping down from a sumptuous carriage that carried a young woman as well, and could not help a frown.

When Yuan Zai came before the hall, Ji Wen grumbled in displeasure: "Gongfu, with so much to do here, where have you been off amusing yourself?" But Yuan Zai bowed over his clasped hands, his face all delight. "My congratulations, Deputy Director Ji: the new Bureau is scarcely established, and already a great merit is won."

"Hm?" Ji Wen was at a loss. Had he brought off some feat?

Yuan Zai pointed to the Xi cart and said in a low voice: "The woman in the cart is Wang Zhongsi's daughter, Wang Yunxiu." Ji Wen said doubtfully: "Are you certain it is she?" For he had heard that the Jing'an Bureau had blundered before, and brought back some unrelated woman.

Yuan Zai said: "There is no mistake; I have already had a matron of the Wang household come to identify her."

Ji Wen, both startled and delighted, said to Yuan Zai: "How did you find her?" Yuan Zai answered with a broad smile: "Why, thanks to Deputy Director Ji's shrewd direction and skillful deployment. We intercepted the woman in a cart that was about to leave the city, and brought her here at once; the abductors have all been put to death."

To Ji Wen these few sentences were like a draught of warm broth, soothing him through and through. In every word, spoken and unspoken, Yuan Zai was making him a present of an enormous credit.

To speak truly, Ji Wen's coming to take over the Jing'an Bureau amounted to a seizure of power, and it had left him a little uneasy at heart. But now all was well: scarcely had he taken office when at once he had cracked a case his predecessor could not solve and rescued the daughter of a great minister of the court—enough to stop the mouths of every doubter.

Ji Wen drew his back straighter still, his beard quivering with pleasure. He clapped Yuan Zai on the shoulder, not knowing what to say. Yuan Zai lowered his voice again: "There is one small matter more. The means by which I found Wang Yunxiu were—heh heh—not quite fit for the light of day. Should the people of the Wang household ask, there must be some account that will pass officially; the Deputy Director need only remember to smooth it over for me."

Hearing this, Ji Wen waved a careless hand. "A trifle. Write me out a statement, Gongfu, and I will sign and seal it for you." He did not ask what the means had been; that was of no importance. What mattered was the result.

Yuan Zai made a deep bow of thanks, and let out a long breath of relief within.

He walked out of the main hall and helped Wang Yunxiu down from the cart; and as he steadied her he suddenly saw Feng Dalun standing in the crowd outside. Something shifted in his eyes. He had Wang Yunxiu go in first, then went out himself. The two did not fall to talking at once, but walked, one behind the other, into a small side-lane.

Feng Dalun asked urgently: "Did they believe it?" Yuan Zai lifted his chin in triumph: "I am glad to say I did not fail the charge." Feng Dalun's shoulders sagged, as a burden lifted.

Ever since he had learned that he had by mistake abducted Wang Zhongsi's daughter, he had felt as though he bore a stone of a thousand jun on his back. Happily, this Yuan Zai had come up with a plan beyond all imagining.

Yuan Zai had had Feng Dalun send out the very idle young toughs who had abducted Wang Yunxiu, load her into a cart and carry her off, telling him their route in advance. Yuan Zai had then detached a body of Lüben Guards to fall upon them midway and cut them all down. In this way, every one of the young toughs who had seen Wang Yunxiu's face was silenced.

Better still, precisely because the dead could bear no witness, the abduction could be laid neatly at Zhang Xiaojing's door, as the one who had ordered it. He already bore the charge of colluding with a foreign foe to attack the Jing'an Bureau; one more would make no difference.

Thus at a single stroke Feng Dalun was freed of his abduction troubles and Zhang Xiaojing was made harder yet to redeem—two birds with one arrow.

In the whole scheme there was but one flaw. Wang Yunxiu had earlier seen Yuan Zai in the woodshed; if the man behind it all was Zhang Xiaojing, then why had Yuan Zai appeared there?

Ji Wen might well not detect this gap, and Wang Yunxiu would surely never think of it; but as the details of the affair came out little by little, sooner or later some sharp mind would raise the question. Yuan Zai would not have his design fail at so small a point, and that was why, just now, he had taken care to have a word with Ji Wen.

The account he had prepared ran thus: the Censorate had long begun to suspect Zhang Xiaojing; Ji Wen, the Palace Censor, had charged Yuan Zai with a deep inquiry into the Pifu, and he had found the robbers' den where Zhang Xiaojing had gone to ground. Yuan Zai, at his own peril, had penetrated it, chanced upon Wang Yunxiu, and organized her rescue in time.

Censor Ji would be only too glad to own it, for it proved his own foresight.

When Feng Dalun had heard it all out, his astonishment and admiration knew no bounds. What manner of divinity was this Evaluator of the Court of Judicial Review? Several vexing matters, with the lightest of turns from his hand, had been made to prop one another up and become his own ladder to advancement—and everyone was happy, everyone felt he had gained. A man with such deft hands: what would become of him in officialdom hereafter?

"I must cultivate his acquaintance well," Feng Dalun thought, and hastily made a bow right to the ground. As Yuan Zai reached out to raise him up, Feng Dalun seized the chance to slip a few small ingots of gold into the other's sleeve.

Yuan Zai did not stand on ceremony, but with a flick of his sleeve took them straight in. Feng Dalun thought a moment, then asked again: "The matter of Zhang Xiaojing—there is no trouble there?"

Zhang Xiaojing had left too deep an impression on him; until the man was truly dead and done, he could not be easy. But Yuan Zai was wholly unconcerned. "Rest easy. Censor Ji has already sent out a city-wide warrant; he cannot get away."

"The Evaluator must not take it lightly . . . that man can always come at you from a quarter you never expect."

Yuan Zai gave Feng Dalun a contemptuous glance. This very night he was about to bring off a flawless miracle in the annals of a career, and here was this man still worrying at a trifling matter all but nailed to the board.

"Pray tell Recorder Feng to report to Prince Yong, and to set his mind at ease. Within three shichen this itch of a nuisance will surely be netted. I have other business; I will take my leave for now."

Yuan Zai left Feng Dalun standing where he was and turned back toward the Jingzhao Prefecture. He had Wang Yunxiu to attend to; and she was the greatest prize of this night's work.

Zhang Xiaojing came slowly back to himself, and found that he was lying on a soft brocade pad, dressed now in a clean round-collared jacket, with a blanket drawn over him. His wounds had all been carefully washed and dressed with a medicinal oil, so that the pain had eased a great deal.

All around was pitch black, yet he could feel his body swaying gently. From outside came the creak and knock of a cart's hubs and the sound of hooves, and the clamor of many voices.

It seemed he was in an ox-cart.

With difficulty Zhang Xiaojing turned his neck, trying to make out what all this was about. Just then, from the rear of the cart, a rueful voice came out of the darkness, though he could see no one:

"Marshal Zhang. This is the second time we've met today."

Zhang Xiaojing understood now why he could make out no shape. "Old Ge?"

Across from him was none other than Old Ge, once a Kunlun slave and now the boss of the Pingkang Quarter. Old Ge chuckled. "This old fellow has little sway in Chang'an, but nothing that stirs in Pingkang Ward can be kept from me for long—and you have truly stirred up a good many people."

"Where are they?"

Old Ge said: "The post-soldiers are easy enough to deal with; the Shouzhuolang are more of a bother. These northwestern men are foul of temper and hard, and it cost us a bit of hand and foot."

Zhang Xiaojing knew that where Old Ge said it had "cost a bit of hand and foot," the truer phrase was likely that it had "cost them a few hands and feet." He was about to speak when Old Ge cut him off: "You need not thank me. It was no kindness of mine; I only did not wish to see those men too pleased with themselves."

Old Ge headed a local gang, and the Shouzhuolang were mercenaries from outside; two such powers sharing the same Pingkang Ward could not but look on one another with an ill eye.

Zhang Xiaojing propped himself half up with an effort and rested a while, breathing hard. Old Ge said there was a lotus-seed and jujube broth close at hand, the very thing to restore his vital breath. Zhang Xiaojing took it up and tasted it; the broth was still hot, and he drank it slowly, turning the bowl by its rim. The warm stream flooded into his belly and seemed to give back a little of the vigor he had lost.

Old Ge said: "Marshal Zhang is Marshal Zhang, no mistake—even the crimes you commit shake heaven and earth. Do you know it? You are now wanted through the whole city; the streets are full of men looking for you."

"So then—Old Ge means to hand me over to the authorities and claim the reward?" He set down the bowl.

Old Ge laughed aloud. "What the government offers in reward would not buy me a comb to scrape my tongue. Rest easy: this ox-cart is to see you out of the city. There is no staying in Chang'an for you now; best to leave, and early."

Zhang Xiaojing was baffled. He and Old Ge had been enemies more often than allies, and more than once had nearly had each other's lives. Only a few shichen before, Old Ge had forced Zhang Xiaojing to kill one of his own planted informers, merely to buy a chance at an interrogation.

Yet now he had first saved his life, then tended his wounds, and was even so thoughtful as to arrange a cart out of the city—this old fox who never rose early save for gain: why this sudden overflow of goodwill?

Sure enough, Old Ge's grim voice soon came again: "Do not be too quick to thank me. This old fellow is no living Bodhisattva; this arrangement is not free of charge."

The cart fell into a stretch of silence, in which only the two men's breathing could be heard—one steady, one quick. Zhang Xiaojing wondered what price Old Ge would name this time. A longer list of planted informers? The disposition of forces in Wannian County? The secret scandals of the great and noble?

Any such intelligence would be of great worth—yet set against the risk Old Ge had run to save him, it seemed too cheap by far. But Zhang Xiaojing truly could not think what else of value he had about him.

The ox-cart crept along neither fast nor slow, the box swaying in rhythm. Old Ge leaned in close, his tone turning subtle: "This afternoon there were several explosions near the West Market; you had a hand in that, did you not?" Zhang Xiaojing narrowed his single eye. "Old Ge wishes to know what business I am mixed up in?"

"No, I do not wish to know; I have no interest in it. I want only one word from you: what manner of thing is it, that has such power?"

Those explosions had startled not only the government, but also the people of Chang'an's underworld. They had discovered, to their shock, that the source of the blasts had been no more than a few wooden barrels. The people of the underworld had a natural interest in dangerous things of great power, and had begun to make inquiries everywhere into the truth of it.

Even if Old Ge himself had no thought of touching the stuff, merely to sell its name would be enough to fetch an astonishing profit.

In the dark Zhang Xiaojing could not see Old Ge's expression. But he could imagine that, were he to refuse, this ox-cart might drive straight to the Wannian County yamen.

"When last we met, I urged you to leave Chang'an, and you would not believe me, but must serve the court instead—and to what end have you come now? You cherish the Great Tang; does the Great Tang cherish you?" Old Ge's voice was earnest, and full of temptation.

Zhang Xiaojing kept his silence. All that Old Ge said was the plain truth, and there was really nothing to gainsay.

"Now you have one last chance: speak the name of that thing, then leave the city, and all that follows is no concern of yours. What is there to hold you back?"

After a long silence, Zhang Xiaojing spoke at last: "Very well. I can tell you the name of the thing."

Old Ge patted the wall of the cart, well pleased. At this Zhang Xiaojing raised a hand again: "But . . . as the price of the exchange, I do not want to leave the city."

"Oh? Then what is it you want?"

"I want you to arrange me a meeting with the Shouzhuolang."

Within the Jingzhao Prefecture Yuan Zai had set aside a private room for Wang Yunxiu, furnished with a bronze mirror, powder-boxes, and cosmetics of every kind, and attended by a docile maidservant. It was not so opulent as the Wang mansion, but it met her needs well enough.

Wang Yunxiu had no wish to return home so grimy and disheveled, and the arrangement could only be called most thoughtful.

Wang Yunxiu washed her face clean and dressed her hair afresh into a double-looped coil, only she had not yet touched on the rouge and the flower-medallion. Seeing Yuan Zai come in behind her in the bronze mirror, she turned about and asked him which flower-medallion would look best.

Yuan Zai bowed respectfully over his hands. "The mistress has the beauty of an immortal; how should I presume to offer an opinion?" Before Wang Yunxiu could answer, he spoke again: "I have come expressly to take my leave."

Wang Yunxiu was startled. "Take your leave?"

"Now that the mistress is safe and sound, I ought to go on with the pursuit of the villain; after all, Zhang Xiaojing is not yet netted."

At the name Wang Yunxiu gave a cold snort. "That treacherous scoundrel—once he is caught, he must not be let off with a mere death!" Yuan Zai said: "Naturally. Only, the man is cunning and fierce, and exceedingly hard to subdue; so I have come first to take my leave of the mistress, lest I be left with the regret of a discourtesy."

He said no more, only let a smile show on his face. At first Wang Yunxiu was puzzled, and then at last she understood: Yuan Zai feared that he might fall in the course of the hunt and never see her again, and so had come to say his farewell in advance. She thought of the knife-scar on his chest, and something trembled in her; without meaning to she put out a hand to keep him. "You would leave, just like that? I . . . mm, my family has not yet thanked you properly."

"To right wrongs and set the world in order is but my duty; what thanks are owed?" Yuan Zai stepped back a pace and made a grave and formal bow.

Wang Yunxiu said, displeased: "Why do I feel that you are avoiding me?"

"I am of humble birth, a mere Evaluator of the Court of Judicial Review; how am I fit to stand before so great a house?"

Wang Yunxiu understood that Yuan Zai was ashamed of his lowly origins, and said coldly, despite herself: "Whoever dares wag his tongue about it, I will have my father cut the tongue out of his head!"

At these words Yuan Zai's face stayed calm, but within, his mind was at last set firm. With that one sentence, he was five parts in ten sure of Wang Yunxiu's heart. What he had to do next was to keep as far off, and as cold, as he could; the more so, the harder Wang Yunxiu would pursue. When the time was ripe, the thing would come of itself, like water reaching its channel, and he would have his ladder to advancement. This was what Lao Dan meant: "If you would be rid of a thing, you must first raise it up; if you would take a thing, you must first give it away."

Set against the other gains of this night, this was the greatest and the most far-reaching benefit of all.

Yuan Zai was about to say a few words more when suddenly a messenger outside the door announced that he had urgent business to report. This messenger had served in the Jing'an Bureau's old great hall, and had likewise survived the fire. His voice was none too soft, and he seemed to show his new superior scant courtesy. Yuan Zai gave a slight frown and said to Wang Yunxiu: "A matter of urgent military intelligence; permit me to leave first. Word has already been sent to the Wang mansion, and presently a carriage will come to bear the mistress home."

Seeing that there was truly no keeping him, Wang Yunxiu had Yuan Zai leave a calling-card, and only then watched him go with lingering reluctance.

Once out of the private room, Yuan Zai asked the messenger what was so urgent. In a hoarse voice the man said that, while clearing the rear garden of the Jing'an Bureau, they had found a recorder lying senseless, by the name of Xu Bin.

"Oh? And what is remarkable about him?"

The messenger said gruffly: "Recorder Xu has a matchless memory, and is the master of the great art of the archives. And what is more . . . ah, it was he who recommended Commander Zhang."

"Oh? Let us go and see."

At this Yuan Zai's interest was roused at once.

They came to a service hall behind the Jingzhao Prefecture, which had been the mess hall but was now turned provisionally into a place for treating the wounded. The moment they entered they heard groans rising one after another, and a foul stench hung in the air. A crowd of physicians, hastily drafted in, were bustling about at their work.

Xu Bin, being of higher station, had a corner of the hall to himself. He lay on a stretcher, a great bruise dark across his brow. Yuan Zai went over and asked how he did; the physician explained that Xu Bin had been found in a clump of grass in the rear garden—no burns, no wound of blade or crossbow, only a very grave mark of a blow to the head; likely he had struck his head on the floor-brick in a fall and been knocked senseless.

Yuan Zai's eyes rolled in thought. "A recorder—why should he be in the rear garden? Why, when all the others died, should he alone come through unharmed?"

No one round about dared take up the question, and all kept silent.

"Zhang Xiaojing was his man—clearly he too is a traitor within! It must have been he who let the Pifu in through the rear garden." Yuan Zai found the reasoning without a flaw. Today he was fortunate indeed: every matter, every man, was delivered to him just as he would have wished.

His face set hard, Yuan Zai said to those about him: "Post more guards, and watch this spy for me with care." Then he turned to the physician: "Is he awake now?" The physician said Recorder Xu responded to voices and could hold a simple conversation, though his senses had not fully cleared. Yuan Zai went over, bent down, and called: "Recorder Xu? Recorder Xu?"

"Ah . . . ah . . ." Xu Bin let out a feeble sound, his eyelids struggling up a few times, but in the end they did not open.

"Do you know where Zhang Xiaojing is?"

"The Persian Temple."

"Do you know where Wen Ran is?"

"The Jing'an Bureau."

Xu Bin was a genius of memory, right enough: even half-conscious, he could answer clearly. But Yuan Zai was disappointed, for both these answers were out of date and of no use at all. In truth Xu Bin could not be blamed; he had fainted before the attack, and did not so much as know that the great hall had been assaulted.

Yuan Zai thought a moment, then put a third question: "Has the Jing'an Bureau any hidden place unknown to outsiders—the sort where a person might be concealed?"

Xu Bin was silent a while, and Yuan Zai could feel that he knew something but wavered whether to tell it. Yuan Zai leaned down to his ear and put on a gentle, most tender tone: "This touches on the safety of Deputy Director Li and Commander Zhang."

At last Xu Bin spoke: "The thatched hut beside the Cibei Temple—there is a wooden ladder by which one may climb the wall to reach it."

At this Yuan Zai was taken aback, then all at once he understood: he had fallen into a blind spot. Who said that to break into the Jing'an Bureau one must then stay in the Jing'an Bureau? That man and Wen Ran must have climbed the wall again and gone to ground in the Cibei Temple.

He did not quite grasp why the Jing'an Bureau should have set up a post at the thatched hut by the Cibei Temple, but that did not hinder him from acting at once. Yuan Zai ordered Xu Bin closely watched, stressing that he was an important accomplice, then left the service hall and mustered a body of guards to go to the thatched hut by the Cibei Temple.

Halfway there, Yuan Zai suddenly stopped in his tracks, looked up at the great watchtower, and with a darkening face detached half his guards, ordering them to climb the tower quickly and bring Yao Runeng down.

Wen Ran's earlier escape must have been because this wretched boy had passed some signal. And even if not, the man had no business in so vital an installation as the great watchtower. Yuan Zai realized of a sudden that he was still too soft-hearted: everyone connected with Zhang Xiaojing ought to be swept away without mercy, guilty or wronged alike.

They knocked open the already-shut gates of the Cibei Temple, roused a guest-receiving monk, and made straight for the thatched hut. Another small party moved along the wall between the Jing'an Bureau and the Cibei Temple, to cut off any line of retreat.

Word soon came back from ahead that there was indeed someone moving about in the hut. This time Yuan Zai did not act rashly; he waited patiently for all his men to take their places, ringing the hut so that not a gap was left—even the release-pond before it was watched close—and only then gave the order to storm it.

Three burly, thick-waisted soldiers, great shields in hand, rushed to the door of the hut and at one blow smashed in the flimsy wooden door. From within came a woman's scream and a man's angry shout of rebuke, then a confusion of footsteps and the sounds of a struggle.

The seizure was over in a moment. Yuan Zai saw with satisfaction that Cen Shen and Wen Ran, each with his arms wrenched behind him by two soldiers, were marched out of the hut. He walked over and studied the young woman with curiosity.

She had a small, delicate face, but with large eyes and lips that curled slightly upward, giving her a look of stubbornness—a born beauty; small wonder Prince Yong had been taken with her. Yet her look was very haggard; she had no doubt been sorely knocked about these many hours.

Come to think of it, this girl was his benefactor. Had Feng Dalun not taken it into his head to abduct Wen Ran, how should there have been the whole chain of events that followed, by which he, Yuan Zai, climbed step by step straight into the blue?

A cruel whim rose up in Yuan Zai all at once. He came before Wen Ran. "Mistress Wen, I have been charged by another to send you back."

Wen Ran raised her head, a flicker of hope in her eyes. "Is it my benefactor?"

Yuan Zai laughed aloud. "Just so. He is dead already; and on his deathbed he entrusted you to Prince Yong."

He watched with keen interest as the color in Wen Ran's face drained from rosy to pale, and from pale to the gray of ashes; her whole body seemed to have had its bones drawn out of it, so that the soldiers lost their grip on her arms and she slid limply straight down onto the floor.

"So this is how a person reacts when hope is utterly lost." Yuan Zai clicked his tongue in wonder. Before a second thought could show on his face, Wen Ran suddenly sprang up and rammed her head into his lower belly, like an enraged young doe.

Caught off his guard, Yuan Zai toppled backward and fell with a great splash into the release-pond, and Wen Ran went in after him with the motion.

It was early spring, and the pond was not deep, its surface covered only by a thin skin of ice, which the two of them shattered to bits as they fell. At first Yuan Zai flailed his limbs in the icy water in panic, but soon his feet touched bottom and his heart eased a little. Just then Wen Ran pressed swiftly in, snatched up a jagged shard of ice, and laid it across his throat.

The scene fell at once into an uproar; the soldiers moved hurriedly to go in and save him, but seeing Wen Ran's threat, none dared come near.

This time it was Yuan Zai's turn to go pale. The keen, cold ice pressed hard against his skin, and made death terribly clear. His lips trembled beyond his control. How could this be? How could this be? Everything today had been so perfect—how could he die over so small a slip as this?

Half-steeped in the icy water, Wen Ran cried out sharply to those around: "All of you, get back!" Yuan Zai hurriedly cried too: "Quick, quick, do as she says."

The soldiers had no choice but to fall back. Then Wen Ran, holding Yuan Zai fast with the ice shard, walked out of the release-pond and made them let Cen Shen go as well. At Yuan Zai's urging, the soldiers could only do as they were told.

Cen Shen came forward, gave Yuan Zai a long, deep look, and shook his head. "Had you not gone toying with people's hearts, you would already have won." Yuan Zai kept his silence.

Holding Yuan Zai hostage, Wen Ran moved step by step toward the gate of the Cibei Temple. The soldiers followed close, at their wits' end. Yuan Zai said: "Outside it is all our men; you cannot get away. If you will lay down the blade, mistress, I can help clear your name and your benefactor's of their wrongs."

"Be silent!"

Wen Ran paid him no heed, but suddenly turned her head to Cen Shen: "Master Cen, go now; none of this ever had anything to do with you." Cen Shen was startled. "And leave you here alone? How can that be?"

"You have done all that kindness and honor could ask, Master. You are one who will hold office one day; do not let me drag you down." Wen Ran gripped the ice shard tight, her face desolate and resolved.

Cen Shen would have pressed his point, when suddenly he noticed that the palm in which Wen Ran gripped the ice was quietly dripping water. All at once he understood: her shard of ice would not last long before it melted of itself, and when it did, then likely neither of them would get away.

Cen Shen set his teeth. "Whatever else you would charge me with, I, Cen Shen, will surely see it done." Wen Ran gave a bitter smile. "Take down for me the signboard of the Wen's Incense Shop, and burn it together with my benefactor's spirit-tablet within—that will be enough. I only hope what the monks say is true, and that after death there is truly that Land of Bliss to which the good may go."

Hearing it, Cen Shen was torn by a hundred feelings at once, and a string of exquisite lines, steeped in grief and ardor, all but leapt from his lips. But he could not utter a single word, and could only make a grave salute with clasped hands, then turn and go.

The soldiers would have stopped him, but Yuan Zai was still in her hands, and they dared not stir. Wen Ran waited until Cen Shen's figure had vanished through the gate of the Cibei Temple, and only then, with one long sigh, cast away the ice-blade, melted down now to a last small piece, and sank down limply to the ground.

Yuan Zai, escaped from the jaws of death, ran quickly off a ten-odd paces, then ordered the soldiers to seize Wen Ran and hold her fast. Only now did he find the small of his back soaked through with cold sweat, so that at a breath of wind it turned an icy sheet.

In a flustered rage Yuan Zai lifted the front of his robe, wiped the water clean from his face, and a fierce light showed in his eyes.

For a man like Yuan Zai, to come to the brink of death was an experience of the keenest torment. That Cen Shen mattered nothing; but this Wen Ran had nearly left an irreparable blemish on a perfect night, and that could by no means be borne.

They marched Wen Ran out of the Cibei Temple and toward the Jingzhao Prefecture. This time Wen Ran had no chance whatever to flee: four soldiers held her fast between them, and another four outside them stood ready to draw their blades at any moment. Yuan Zai kept well away, to avoid falling into the same trap twice.

This column, wary as though facing a great foe, soon reached the gate of the Jingzhao Prefecture, just as a tall and splendid carriage was about to set out from it. The carriage brushed past the column, and suddenly a face full of glad surprise leaned out from within.

"Evaluator Yuan."

Yuan Zai saw that it was Wang Yunxiu; the Wang mansion's carriage had come to bear her home. He let a smile show, but before he could speak, Wang Yunxiu cried out again in joyful surprise: "Wen Ran? You are alive too?"

Wen Ran, under escort, jerked her head up, and at last broke into loud weeping:

"Sister Wang!"

The smile froze on Yuan Zai's face in an instant.

Tanqi stood beneath the fire-trees before the Xingqing Palace, gazing calmly toward the end of the street.

This was the liveliest quarter of all Chang'an. Here were not only the largest and most splendid banks of lantern-frames in the whole city and the most gifted performers, but here too, once the fourth watch was past, the Son of Heaven would in his own person climb the Qinzheng Wuben Tower to take his joy together with the people, and choose the final victor from among the several dozen lantern-float teams. It wanted less than two shichen now, and the common folk were gathering from every side, packing the place so tight that not a drop could have trickled through.

Yet all this clamor round about had nothing to do with her.

Far off, at the end of the street, there appeared first six horsemen in golden armor, then eight attendants bearing vermilion-lacquered round fans and peacock screen-fans, and close behind them a stately four-windowed carriage came rolling up, drawn by four chestnut steeds, with ten-odd guards in brocade riding to left and right.

This retinue had been pared to the barest possible, yet set against the boundless tide of people it still seemed swollen and unwieldy. The whole procession was forced to slow to a crawl, nudging the commoners ahead of it aside little by little as it made toward the Xingqing Palace.

Seizing her chance, Tanqi darted at furious speed into the retinue and, heedless of the guards drawing their swords all around, clung with both hands to the rail-board of the four-windowed carriage, and cried at the top of her voice till it cracked:

"Your Highness the Heir Apparent! The Jing'an is in peril!"

In Pingkang Ward there stood a ruined and deserted temple, called the Guan Zhong shrine, none knew in what year built nor in what year abandoned. Guan Zhong, it was said, was the patron sage of the courtesan trade, so that his shrine standing here was no strange thing. Next door to this derelict shrine was the Shouzhuolang's bookshop.

A score and more of Shouzhuolang stood about a broken incense-burner before the shrine, every face fierce, every hand holding a weapon. In their midst was the squad leader. They had raised no torch, but stood so, silent, in the dark. Before long, from a small path in the distance, came a creaking and squeaking as cart-wheels rolled and ground over the broken earth of the road. Many a Shouzhuolang lifted his weapon without thinking, but the squad leader gave no sign.

The ox-cart drew slowly up before the shrine, and the driver gathered his reins to hold it steady. Old Ge and Zhang Xiaojing came down from it, the one aged and frail, the other his wounds not yet mended—the old man and the injured man together standing in the sharpest contrast to the murderous air of the men before them.

The squad leader glanced about; it seemed no one followed behind the ox-cart. "Old Ge," he said, "what do you want with me?"

Old Ge shook his head. "I have nothing to say to you; it is this friend here who wants you." Then he slipped aside, and Zhang Xiaojing sprang down from behind the cart. His face was still pale, his step a little unsteady from the gravity of his wounds.

The moment he appeared, a stir went up among the men. Many a Shouzhuolang brandished his weapon, itching to spring forward at once and set to. The squad leader barked at them to be still, then glared across:

"Zhang the Yama? You still dare show your face?"

That the squad leader named his nickname straight off showed plainly he too had looked into the man's history. Zhang Xiaojing took a step forward, not in the least afraid: "The one who killed the firemaster was another man." The squad leader gave a cold snort, not believing a word of it. Zhang Xiaojing said: "If you doubt it, you may ask the men at the smithy next door whether, before me, another man did not go in as well—and never came out again."

Seeing how flatly and firmly he spoke, the squad leader called a few men over and questioned them in a low voice, then looked up. "What you say is true enough, but that does not mean you did not do the killing."

"I had no reason to kill the firemaster. I am a Commander of the Jing'an Bureau, and I have come here for one thing only: to learn who it was that commissioned the Shouzhuolang to assassinate an elder at the Persian Temple."

The squad leader laughed in derision. "A Commander of the Jing'an Bureau? The warrant for you has gone through the whole city; even if we Shouzhuolang do not touch you, there is nowhere left for you to go."

"That is no concern of yours. Who commissioned the Shouzhuolang to assassinate an elder at the Persian Temple?"

"And why should I tell you?"

"Because it bears upon the safety of the whole of Chang'an! The elder Puzhe of the Persian Temple is implicated in a great plot to destroy the city. If you refuse to cooperate, you are the tiger's henchmen, and enemies of the court." Zhang Xiaojing narrowed his single eye, and his tone turned dangerous.

"You, a fugitive—what right have you to peddle such alarms?!"

In a great rage the squad leader reached out and seized Zhang Xiaojing all at once. Zhang Xiaojing did not dodge, and was pressed down beside the incense-burner, his face grinding against its rough, raised bronze figuring, in a burst of raw pain.

Old Ge stood unmoved. He had promised only to bring Zhang Xiaojing to meet the Shouzhuolang; he had not promised to warrant his life.

The squad leader gripped Zhang Xiaojing by the hair and knocked his head against the burner a few clanging times, until the blood ran freely from his temple. Zhang Xiaojing did not resist; and when the squad leader's hand stopped, he went on in a tone so calm it was frightening: "The explosions at the West Market this afternoon—do you know of them?"

The squad leader started, and his hand loosened despite himself. He had not witnessed those explosions, but he had sent men to inquire; only, the cordon had been too tight, and they had learned nothing of what lay behind them.

Zhang Xiaojing straightened up and leaned against the incense-burner, baring his teeth in a grin. "There are several dozen more such explosions brewing in Chang'an even now, and the one thread that leads to them is the elder Puzhe. You assassinated the elder—so the blame is yours to carry."

One half of his face was stamped all over with the marks of the incense-burner, the other half streaming with blood, so that he looked like a fiend crawled up out of hell, ghastly and terrible to see.

The squad leader knitted his brows tight. This man's words were without proof, yet he could not lightly dismiss them. That the Shouzhuolang had survived to this day rested not on force and ferocity but on caution.

Zhang Xiaojing said: "I had already persuaded the assassin Liu Shiqi to bring us to you, but the convoy was intercepted on the road, and Liu Shiqi died on the spot. This shows that the other side means to cut the thread and make the Shouzhuolang the end of the line. When the authorities trace it back, they can trace it no further than to you."

Of this the squad leader too had heard. The crossroads where it happened was not far from Pingkang Ward, and besides Liu Shiqi a few officers had been caught up in it as well.

"So let me ask you once more: who commissioned the Shouzhuolang to assassinate an elder at the Persian Temple?"

The squad leader answered stiffly: "I do not know. The client dealt only with the firemaster, along a single line; only the firemaster knew what the commissioner looked like."

"Is there no other record?"

A long silence; then, grudgingly, the squad leader answered: "The firemaster kept a secret account-book, against any mishap. But where it is stored, only he and I know."

Small wonder he had hesitated so long before speaking. Were the clients to learn that the Shouzhuolang secretly kept records on them, they would surely trust them no more.

Zhang Xiaojing said: "I want to see this account-book."

"On what grounds?" said the squad leader, displeased.

Zhang Xiaojing pointed to Old Ge. "I had a fine chance to leave Chang'an and be well clear of your pursuit, and yet I turned back to find you instead—do you know why? Because this matter is so great, so vast, that I cannot spare a thought for my own gain or loss."

Old Ge nodded, to show that what he said was no lie, then curled his lip, to show his disdain for the choice.

"And so it is for you as well. The matter is too great; it has passed beyond your so-called grudges and rules." Zhang Xiaojing said: "Give me the book or not, as you please. Only be prepared to answer for your choice."

The squad leader conferred in a low voice with a few of the men around him, then spoke: "You may see the book, but it must be under our control, and you may see only the part we appoint."

Zhang Xiaojing agreed without hesitation.

The squad leader called two men to truss Zhang Xiaojing hand and foot, and led him off toward the bookshop. Old Ge and the greater part of the Shouzhuolang waited at the mouth of the lane, forbidden to come near. At the door of the bookshop the squad leader signed to Zhang Xiaojing to wait outside, and went in himself. Before long he came out holding a scroll bound in ochre leather.

The scroll was plain to look at, small in size, and little apt to draw the eye—a good hiding-place indeed for a secret account-book.

Holding the scroll, the squad leader was just about to undo the silk cord bound round it when he suddenly felt the air stir overhead. He looked up; a black shadow dropped abruptly out of the sky, and in a flash of lightning the scroll had changed hands.

At the same instant Zhang Xiaojing gave a great shout, burst free of the ropes on him, and sprang at the shadow. The ropes, it turned out, had been but loosely knotted, undone at the lightest tug. The shadow had not reckoned on this, and shot back—only to have its retreat cut off by the rammed-earth wall of the bookshop.

Quick-witted in the crisis, the shadow gripped the scroll in one hand and set a foot against the rough face of the rammed-earth wall; using that row of little pits, it just barely dodged Zhang Xiaojing's lunge, and seemed on the very point of leaping to the top of the wall.

Just then came several shouts, and three or four fishing-nets were flung up high from either side. Swift as the shadow's movements were, they could not escape this all-enveloping cover; it sprang upward once, taking the nets with it, then was hauled back to the ground by the Shouzhuolang and dashed down heavily on the earth.

Zhang Xiaojing came before the shadow and kicked the scroll from its hand. At the kick it fell open and scattered: the paper within was one blank sheet, not a single word written on it.

"The Shouzhuolang put good faith above all—how should they secretly keep petty accounts on their clients? Had you a shred of trust in them, you would never have fallen into this trap," Zhang Xiaojing said mockingly.

The whole of it, it turned out, had been a snare they had laid.

This shadow had killed the firemaster first, and then Liu Shiqi; his charge must be to cut, for his organization, every thread that could possibly lead back. But the fellow was too swift to be run down; there was nothing for it but to wait for him to throw himself into the net.

And so, through Old Ge's mediation, the half-doubting squad leader and Zhang Xiaojing had played out a scene together, reckoning that the shadow would surely be lurking nearby, waiting his chance to strike.

They had feigned that there was such a secret account-book, hiding within it the thread to the commissioner. This forced the shadow to snatch it away before Zhang Xiaojing could get it. Cunning as he was, he had not foreseen that the Shouzhuolang and Zhang Xiaojing, sworn enemies to begin with, would join hands to lay a great trap and await his coming.

Lanterns kindled round about and lit up the shadow. The man wore still that same aspect of an old fellow, and his close-fitting hemp clothes could not hide a well-knit, powerful frame. He lay in the net like a fish long since landed, without a stir.

The squad leader came over, iron hammer in hand, a keen light in both his eyes. "This is the killer who slew the firemaster?"

"That is right."

The squad leader gave him a kick, and the shadow made no response. He put more weight into it, kicking again and again. Zhang Xiaojing said mildly: "Do not beat him to death; I have things yet to ask him." The squad leader raised the great hammer high. "To ask questions, one mouth left is enough, is it not?" and brought it down hard toward the shadow's knee. But the shadow gave a sudden heave within the net, shifting his whole body over a little, and dodged the blow just in time.

"Struggling in your death-throes," the squad leader sneered, and turned the hammer once more, making ready for a second blow.

But just then the Shouzhuolang from beyond the mouth of the lane came running in, in alarm, shouting that a great force of constables was massing on them.

"Eh? How is it they have come? Who informed the authorities?" The squad leader frowned and looked to Old Ge; Old Ge spread his hands, to show his innocence. Zhang Xiaojing's gaze swept toward the net; he knew now who had done it.

This killer had never been a mere killer; he turned every circumstance to his own use. The moment Zhang Xiaojing had reached the bookshop, the fellow had, by a chain of clever devices, set the Shouzhuolang and Zhang Xiaojing at cross-purposes, and escaped in the confusion.

Now he had played the same old trick again, informing the authorities beforehand that Zhang Xiaojing was hiding in the bookshop, and only then making his move. So, whether he succeeded or not, the constables swarming in could throw the whole situation into disorder.

It was not Zhang Xiaojing alone who plotted before he acted.

Sullenly the squad leader put away his hammer and ordered his men to draw the net tight. "This man we must take with us." Zhang Xiaojing's face darkened. "Did we not agree? Once I have asked what I want of him, you may do with him as you please."

The squad leader pointed to the mouth of the lane. "First you settle the matter out there. The Shouzhuolang will give no shelter to a wanted man." Zhang Xiaojing sneered: "So much for 'every kindness repaid, every debt settled'—it seems one may count on the latter half only." The squad leader's face turned faintly awkward, but in the end he merely waved a hand. "If you can shake off the pursuit, it will not be too late to come find us again."

The Shouzhuolang's enemy must be dealt with by the Shouzhuolang; it was a matter of face. But they had no wish to provoke the authorities.

Afraid Zhang Xiaojing would press him again, he forced his own body in front of the man and hurried his subordinates to carry the assassin off. Seeing this, Zhang Xiaojing said urgently: "Break both his legs first!"

But he spoke too late; several Shouzhuolang had already thrown back the net and were bending to pin the shadow's four limbs. By their reckoning, four men, one to each limb, made the thing foolproof. Yet in the very instant the net was lifted, a gush of oily green liquid shot suddenly from the shadow's cuff.

Caught off guard, the four were splashed with the liquid and screamed as one, their movements checked. Seizing the moment, the shadow leapt up where he stood and, springing for the top of the wall, flung the green fluid about him as he went.

Zhang Xiaojing reacted quickly and reached to seize the man's trouser-leg; where the green fluid touched his skin there came a scorching, burning pain. Checked by the grip, the shadow paused a moment, and the squad leader's swinging hammer was already crashing down. The shadow neither dodged nor gave way, but met it with his left arm. The hammer struck the arm, and with a crack the bone snapped at once; but at the price of that one arm the shadow bought himself a chance, and with a few sharp flicks of his left hand sent the green fluid flying straight into the squad leader's eyes.

The squad leader let out a wild howl of pain, cast the hammer aside, and rubbed frantically at his eyes. Using that instant's gap, the shadow shot up from the ground and gained the top of the wall once more.

This whole chain of turns takes long in the telling, but passed in the blink of an eye. The shadow was savage indeed: to win the first move, he had thrown away a whole arm. Once atop the wall he looked back at Zhang Xiaojing, and a hoarse voice, like wind blowing over broken tiles, came down: "Zhang Xiaojing—I, Yuchang, will surely have your life."

With that he swayed and was gone into the night.

Paying no heed to the squad leader writhing on the ground, Zhang Xiaojing raised the green fluid on his sleeve to his nose and sniffed, and made it out to be green-vitriol oil, a stuff of the Daoists' alchemy. It had the temper of a tiger: touch it to paper, wood, or flesh, and it ate them away in an instant. Many an assassin would hide a pouch in his cuff, filled with green-vitriol oil, that in a crisis could work wonders.

"This fellow who calls himself Yuchang—what in the world is his history . . ." Zhang Xiaojing was inwardly shaken, and the shadow of dread on his face deepened past all bearing.

He had done all in his power, straining in so hard a pass to catch at one thread of hope, and in the end had still let Yuchang slip away. Yuchang would not be fooled again; the last thread was cut off here.

The moment hope was cut off, an endless pressure came surging in from every side. Firm-willed as Zhang Xiaojing was, at last even he was worn out in heart and strength. He began to doubt: perhaps this was Heaven's will, just as it had been in that slaughter the year before—what use to do all in one's power? A lone fighter could not, in the end, turn the whole tide back, nor save his comrades' lives. One man, when all was said, had no way to stand against an organization.

The more so now, caught as he was between two vast leviathans, the court of the Great Tang before and the Que-le Huo-duo behind.

All his striving had been labor lost from the very moment it was spent. Old Ge's words circled round and round in his mind, like a demon of the heart—You cherish the Great Tang; does the Great Tang cherish you?

Zhang Xiaojing forced his single eye open, but his sight had already begun to blur. The constables came rushing into the lane, swinging chains and iron rulers, meaning to take him like a turtle in a jar. The Shouzhuolang, supporting their wounded squad leader, had all withdrawn, and Old Ge too had quietly gone. Not one of them would lift a hand to save him.

A dead end, truly and utterly—a dead end within and without.

"Runeng—I am sorry. I cannot keep my promise not to retreat." Zhang Xiaojing let his shoulders slump in dejection and leaned his back against the earthen wall, aged in an instant by many years.

Suddenly his ear twitched and he jerked his head up: a black shadow was once more diving straight down from the eaves not far to one side, hurtling toward him. Zhang Xiaojing had not looked for the fellow to come back, and out of habit drove an elbow back at him. But the shadow had no guard up at all, took the elbow full on the nose, and with a yelp lay flat on the ground.

Hearing that the cry was wrong, Zhang Xiaojing looked hard—and it was Yisi, lost sight of so long ago. The fellow had never appeared again since they were parted on the Vermilion Bird Avenue, and Zhang Xiaojing had supposed him shaken off; he had never thought to see him turn up here. That pair of Persian-cat eyes brimmed over with stinging tears.

"How did you—"

"No more words—follow where I step!" Without stopping to explain, Yisi turned and began to climb the wall again.

Zhang Xiaojing found that the pits, the rafter-ends, the tile-edges, and the cracks along the wall and under the eaves, seemingly all at random, became under Yisi's feet a kind of invisible staircase. Follow only the set order and rhythm, and one could climb up with ease. He did the same, and sure enough gained the top of the wall without much effort.

Yisi led Zhang Xiaojing now over a beam, now across the eaves, working his deft footwork among the many houses, running the eaves and walking the walls as though he trod level ground. In a little while they had left the pursuers far behind, and dropped down into a deserted, secluded courtyard.

Before Zhang Xiaojing could even ask, Yisi went babbling on of his own accord.

It turned out that on the Vermilion Bird Avenue he had not been parted from Zhang Xiaojing by accident; rather, a wish to outdo him had risen in him, and he had meant to win the credit a step ahead. So Yisi had put his art of cave-running to use and climbed over first into the Pingkang Quarter. But able as he was, he had no sense of direction, and in his muddle blundered into a brothel by mistake, losing a good deal of time. By the time he had freed himself of the entanglement and got back to the street, he was just in time to witness Yuchang's attack on the cart in which Liu Shiqi was held.

Yisi went white with shock, and hurriedly fell in quietly behind him. Relying on his cave-running skill, he had never once lost the trail nor been discovered, and so had come, on Yuchang's heels, to the bookshop at the end of the little lane.

The rapid turns that followed left Yisi at a loss to keep up. He saw Yuchang flee and would have given chase, but then saw that Zhang Xiaojing was about to be taken by the constables; of the two he had to choose one, and in the end, setting his teeth, Yisi chose to save Zhang Xiaojing first.

"Such a pity! Such a pity!" Yisi scratched his head in regret.

Zhang Xiaojing wasted no words, but asked straight out: "You followed him so long—did his identity ever let slip any clue?—Speak plainly!"

"Ah . . . the fellow is surely a man of the Western Regions, or has at least spent some time there; that cave-running skill of his and my own are about a match, one for the other." Yisi said it very modestly.

"And his movements? Has he a hiding-place?"

"None; he kept roving over the rooftops of Pingkang Ward the whole time, nimble as a cat. But I did spy out . . ." Yisi fumbled and fumbled in his breast, and drew out a small thing.

It was half a sliver of bamboo, about the size of a fingernail, shaped like an eight-cornered star.

Yisi said that Yuchang, the better to spring and leap, wore a specially made pair of fish-bone shoes, their soles ridged with many spines like the bones of a fish. This half-sliver of bamboo had lodged just between the ridges. Yisi was sharp-eyed, and while trailing him had seen something fall from Yuchang's sole as he took off from one rooftop, and had picked it up in passing.

"I told you before: in Chang'an there is nothing that can hide from these eyes of mine."

Zhang Xiaojing took up the bamboo and studied it carefully, but could make nothing of it. To think that a thread of hope had just kindled within him—it was another phantom after all. He shook his head and said to Yisi in dejection: "Thank you. But there is no longer any way for us to stop the Que-le Huo-duo; you had best get back to the temple quickly and tell the monks to leave the city and seek safety."

Yisi was aghast. "But is this not a clue?"

"A scrap of bamboo of the commonest kind—what can it prove?" Zhang Xiaojing answered listlessly.

Yisi leaned his face in close, none too pleased. "The commonest kind? Do you doubt my eye? Would I take the trouble to pick up a common scrap of bamboo? Look here: this eight-cornered shape must have been carefully cut, and there is a groove running through the middle of it besides. That is not to be found everywhere in Chang'an . . ."

As he listened to Yisi, Zhang Xiaojing's dejected look seemed to have a trace of life breathed back into it.

He was right: the way this piece of bamboo had been cut was too rare—not that it could not be cut so, but that it was uneconomical. The knife-work was too fine; no one would spend such pains on a worthless little scrap of bamboo, unless it belonged to some larger part.

Zhang Xiaojing's eyes grew slowly grave, and all at once he remembered something.

After the explosion in Changming Ward, the Jing'an Bureau had gathered up a great quantity of fragments at the scene and carried them back to study. He had once gone over them carefully and recovered Cao Poyan's necklace. Recalling it now, there had seemed to be a good many broken bits of bamboo among the fragments, and Xu Bin had even complained that they pricked his hands.

But at the time he had given them only a hasty glance and could not remember the particulars, nor whether those broken bits of bamboo had anything to do with the sliver in his hand. Zhang Xiaojing thought: if he wished to be sure, he would have to go back to the Jing'an Bureau—but surely all that evidence had by now gone up in flames?

At the thought a weariness of disappointment came over him once more. Just then Yisi suddenly took Zhang Xiaojing's hand and pressed into it the cross from his own breast, saying earnestly: "Commander Zhang, let the heart in its faith be firm; it is too soon to give up. Look—even I have not lost heart."

Those jewel-like eyes seemed to hold a kind of innocent power. Zhang Xiaojing could not help a small laugh, and his spirit rallied a little. "This was never any affair of yours. Why do you take it so to heart?"

Yisi said gravely: "Whether the Persian Temple can have its name rightly set as the Luminous faith lies wholly in the Commander's hands; naturally I must bend every effort to it."

Zhang Xiaojing smiled bitterly. "I can scarcely save myself just now; I fear you will be disappointed." But Yisi said: "My religion teaches that with prayer unceasing, with hope in gladness, with suffering borne in patience, one must surely gain his end. You, Commander Zhang, are of a temper quite unlike other men; you can pour yourself wholly into a single course, and are bound for great things—you must be a patron sent to my church."

Zhang Xiaojing said, wondering: "Were it for wealth or fame, well and good. But a mere name—is it truly worth so great a risk to you?"

"It is. If the name is not right, then speech does not accord with it." Yisi answered with the utmost seriousness, as though there were nothing under heaven of greater weight. Seeing Zhang Xiaojing still not much convinced, he pointed to his own two eyes. "Does the Commander know the origin of these fine eyes of mine?"

"Persia?"

"Only the true Persian royal house has eyes of so clear a sapphire-glass blue." Yisi's tone held no little pride, then at once he sighed. "But alas, in the days of Taizong and Gaozong, hard pressed by the Arab lands, Persia came at last to the ruin of its kingdom. The late king Peroz led his whole clan in migration and cast himself upon the Great Tang, where he was appointed General of the Right Awesome Guard; and the royal house's descendants were scattered among the cities of the Western Regions. From my very birth I have been a subject of a fallen realm, sorely scorned; had I not met my Lord, I fear my bones would long since have lain lost beneath the desert sands."

Zhang Xiaojing gave a grunt. No wonder the man sometimes called himself a Persian prince; he had taken it for a jest, and never thought it true.

Yisi suddenly raised his head and made a cross over his breast. "My own life has borne witness to the impermanence of the world, the turning wheel of rise and fall. Power and wealth, none of them can endure; only the service of God is the eternal way. To set right its name is the very charge of my whole life; through fire and boiling water I would go, and never flinch from it!"

His two eyes shone and shone, and Zhang Xiaojing found there was simply no refusing him, and could only say helplessly:

"Very well, very well. I will find some way back to the Jing'an Bureau and see what this scrap of bamboo is really about—there is no harm in doctoring a dead horse as though it were still alive."

The words were scarcely out of his mouth when the watchtowers on every side, far and near, all began at once to flash, on and on without cease. Zhang Xiaojing frowned and looked up, and saw that this was the most urgent state of communication, which broadcasts the same message over and over until the next order comes in. He soon deciphered it: it came from the great watchtower, only four words, repeating without end:

"Do not come back. Do not come back. Do not come back."
