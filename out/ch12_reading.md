## Chapter 2. Unfathomable Hearts, Hidden Designs

### 1. Not Yet Aware That Wang Had Entered into Secret Communication with the Enemy State

After Mr. Dai had gone back to Chongqing, the working comrades who had reached Hanoi were but three: Fang Bingxi, Wang Luqiao, and the writer. This working unit of ours had no formal designation and no fixed establishment; it could be reckoned nothing more than a special, temporarily dispatched combination. As for those appellations set down in our internal papers—"the Work Group" and "Work-Group Leader Chen So-and-so"—they were very likely a convenient fiction for the sake of internal business; in truth, I myself never once knew that this working unit, drawn together for the moment from posts all over, was called "the Work Group." We really had no name of the sort of "the Fourth Detachment" or "the 636 Special-Attack Group"; had there been one, it would have passed well enough for the "Hanoi Work Unit." For instance, those who took part in the Hanoi work numbered, first and last, eighteen men, and were called "the Eighteen Arhats"—which seems to flatter ourselves; and were we to be called "the Eighteen Little Vajra-guardians," then, save that I was unworthy of it, the rest could bear the title without a blush.

Before Mr. Dai left, the working task he charged us with, face to face, was only these two points: "to keep strict watch on Wang's movements, and by every means to reconnoiter the doings of the Wang faction." Though these two tasks looked simple enough, when it came to the doing of them they were by no means easy.

Take "watching," for example: under ordinary conditions it still needed manpower, technique, and the fit combination of several kinds of apparatus; to rely on one or two men standing and gazing from afar was of no use whatever. And take "reconnoitering": its whole worth lies in being able to go deep, truly to grasp the other side's doings; to work only at the surface was of course not enough. But to penetrate the other side's inner circle, one must have a reliable working line; and the winning of such a line, its cultivation and its keeping in hand, that depended upon luck and upon skill. At this moment we were but three men, and Mr. Bingxi was busy with his affairs and his business—finding a site and setting up the wireless, hastening the exchange of telegrams with Chongqing; arranging the various formalities for the many comrades soon to arrive; renting houses, buying things, keeping the accounts of income and outlay; and at times, too, having to still his mind and translate the cipher telegrams one character at a time. The matters he handled were too many; naturally he had no leisure to share besides in the work of "watching" and "reconnoitering." Yet apart from him, Luqiao and I, the two of us, suffered in a place where we knew neither ground nor face, and, the tongue being strange to us as well, we could truly make nothing of it—which is as much as to say we were helpless.

Luqiao and I could not, however, idle away the time and wait for nothing. Was it not mentioned before? We had a little two-door Ford motorcar, and with nothing else to do we would run it here and there about the streets of Hanoi, and so grow a little familiar with the ways—that too was worth doing. Though we spoke no Annamese and knew no French either, yet in this world nothing is hard: when a difficulty arose, a wave of the hand and a bit of expression on the face would settle it all the same. Only in one thing was there no shift—there had to be money in the pocket!

This is a glimpse of the earliest state of things among us few after we reached Hanoi. Happily it lasted but a short while, and changed by degrees as the working comrades came one after another and the working ties were opened out in their turn.

Here let me introduce once more the "vanguard" of the "Hanoi work," Mr. Fang Bingxi.

Something of Mr. Fang Bingxi's career was told already in the first section; I had supposed he was still living, but, to my grief, he passed away two or three years ago. Mr. Bingxi was square of face and large of ear, solid and thickset—at a glance a man of honest cast; in bearing and address courteous and refined, in his dealings with others sincere and earnest, and, set beside the writer, a good deal more steady besides. He had studied abroad in Belgium, reading politics and diplomacy, and not long after his return home was gathered in by Mr. Dai. Sent to Hanoi and assigned to duties purely of business and management, he was in truth a great talent put to petty use. He did his work in earnest and with never a slip, and should be reckoned a man of the gentleman's type.

Writing to this point, and calling to mind the days gone by, I have of a sudden an inkling: could it be that Mr. Fang Bingxi bore the secret charge of an "inspector"? The more I turn it over, the more it seems so. If it were indeed the case, then he was the ideal man, exactly fitted to the part. For he held to what was upright and would not bend; he said what there was to say, and what a thing was, that he let it be; he was in no wise a man to make right and wrong, or to turn black into white.

At the time, however, I gave him no little trouble; he was the keeper of the money, and I was the spender of it, and yet he was not the one who earned it. His money had its one source in the remittances allotted from above, and every payment had its fixed heading; whereas I drew and spent without measure, sparing neither the urgent nor the slow. All of it was for the work, true—but I owed much, even so, to his shifting of the funds.

Before long there came to Hanoi, by way of the Yunnan–Vietnam railway, two more comrades: my senior schoolfellow Cen Jiazhuo and Dr. Yu Lexing.

Cen Jiazhuo, an old elder brother of the third class of the Military Academy, a Guangdong man, was silent, steady, and had a gift for command. When the writer was still under training in the first class of the "Special-Service Police Training Class" at Nanjing, he was already deputy head of that class's education section—the head of the education section being Mr. Zheng Jiemin. My senior Cen Jiazhuo had been transferred down from an important post; as to his duties, no clear instruction had come from above, but weighing the sense of the thing, it was very likely that he was to assist in all the work. Only, being at the time young and unsettled and shallow in the ways of the world, I could not fathom the fine use of it; and though I took counsel of this old elder brother in everything, that was not the practical way—I ought rightly to have divided off to him a part of the authority and duty; but at the time I did not yet know to do so. Which shows that, whatever one does, one must have a certain measure to it.

Mr. Yu Lexing, a Hunan man, studied abroad in France, a Doctor of Chemistry. His standing in the Juntong was very high; his post at the time was deputy director of the "Linli Training Class," the director of the class being none other than Mr. Dai Yunong. By the system of the Juntong, Mr. Yu was the man who in fact bore the charge of this training class.

Before Mr. Yu Lexing was posted to "Linli," he had served as head of the Technical Office (or the Technical Research Office) of the Military Affairs Commission (or the Ministry of Military Administration), with special charge of the research and development of chemical munitions.

On Mr. Yu Lexing's coming, Mr. Dai had sent a separate wire of instruction, appointing him to counsel in matters confidential and to give technical guidance; put in a phrase easy to grasp, he was to be the "chief of staff" of this temporarily dispatched working unit of ours, and its "technical adviser" as well.

Mr. Yu was tall and thin, his two eyes like lamps; not great in years, yet his hair had gone a good deal white. A cigarette was often in his hand; he was a man who liked to use his head. Only, because he pondered overmuch, his breast seemed not broad enough; but for that devotion of his to his calling, few men indeed could match him.

The reviser of this book, Mr. Liu Yuanshen, was trained at the "Linli Training Class," and reports: "Mr. Yu, in the training class, made himself the model; his private life was most severe, and before others or behind their backs he would absolutely not smoke. Yet once, in his own office, I saw with my own eyes that he was smoking, and marveled at it then as a strange thing. His fellow students seldom saw him smile in the ordinary way, and now and then, when his mule's temper broke out, it was no small matter. His learning was deep; in teaching and in management alike he was most in earnest, so that the students revered him and the officers of the ranks stood in awe of him. At the end of the twenty-sixth year, when Mr. Dai came to the class to preside, on the Leader's behalf, over the graduation ceremony, he criticized him on the spot for the failure of his training; and Yu stood wooden and still, without a trace of expression. Yet afterward I heard Mr. Dai say, more than once: 'The training at Linli was very successful.'" In truth failure and success have no fixed boundary between them, and the exhortation and spurring-on of work are often meted according to the man; by the writer's own observation of Mr. Yu, he ought, in fairness, to be counted a man of considerable achievement.

After the two comrades Cen and Yu had arrived, they did not lodge together with me; Brother Bingxi found them separate quarters elsewhere, which should be reckoned the second base of the "Hanoi work."

My senior Jiazhuo and Mr. Lexing had brought with them several pieces of reference material, which may be called a course of "political instruction" from our superiors; two of them bore upon the "Yan Telegram." One was the third statement of the Japanese premier Konoe Fumimaro; the other was an address by the Generalissimo Chiang answering Konoe's third statement. Here let us first look at the original text of the Konoe statement, with the writer's explanation appended after it. During his term of office, the Japanese premier Konoe Fumimaro, on the settling of the Sino-Japanese war, published in all three statements—

At the beginning of the twenty-seventh year, the enemy army in Central China had already fallen upon our capital, Nanjing, and next upon Hangzhou; in North China it had seized Jinan in Shandong and occupied Taiyuan in Shanxi; and it was busily disposing its forces with a mind to fall upon Wuhan, the center of our military and civil government. Thereupon Konoe published his first statement, declaring: "It will not take the National Government for its counterpart." That is to say, our government had lost its footing of equality with the Japanese government—had lost even the standing so much as to "parley for peace." Such a posture, in Japan, was overweening beyond measure; in our country it was humiliation to the utmost.

After the Japanese premier had published his first statement, he ceaselessly set up puppet regimes in the fallen regions, yet could not settle the "China question." On the sixth of April in the twenty-seventh year, our army won a great victory at Tai'erzhuang in Shandong, routing the enemy's Fifth and Tenth Divisions and annihilating thirty thousand of the enemy (the Japanese army admitting 2,367 dead and 9,615 wounded), dealing the Japanese army a heavy blow, and, at the same time, greatly cutting down that arrogant spirit of the Japanese.

Though in the several months thereafter the Japanese army went on occupying a number of great cities, yet by reason of the widening of the war's ground and the lengthening of its front, the Japanese army was sunk in a hard case and could not attain the swift decision it had looked for; and so it could not but go on playing at the arts of suing for peace.

On the third of November in the twenty-seventh year, the Japanese premier Konoe Fumimaro published a second statement, saying in brief: "The aim of Japan's present war is the building of a 'New Order in East Asia'… By the joining of the three states of Japan, 'Manchukuo,' and 'China,' a mutually interlinked relation shall be set up in the political, economic, and cultural spheres… Should the National Government change its policy, alter its personnel, and take part in the building of the 'New Order,' Japan does not refuse it."

The earlier first statement had said, "It will not take the National Government for its counterpart"; this time, on the conditions of "changing policy," "altering personnel," and "taking part in the building of the 'New Order,'" the Japanese government might still deal with the National Government. We must point out in particular that at this moment the secret collusion of Wang Jingwei with the Japanese government had already taken shape. When Mei Siping, who represented Wang in his secret contact with Japan, had flown to Chongqing on the twenty-second of October in the twenty-seventh year, he reported to Wang Jingwei the other side's intent—"Japan hopes Wang will break away from Chongqing, form another government, and negotiate peace terms"—and Wang took counsel of Zhou Fohai, Chen Gongbo, and the rest, and they could not decide; only Wang's wife, Chen Bijun, strongly urged cooperation with Japan. This reply was then conveyed, by roundabout ways, to Japan on the second of November, and the premier Konoe forthwith, on the third of November, published his second statement to home and abroad. Seen thus, we may understand that the "changing of policy" spoken of in the "statement" meant "not resisting Japan"; that "altering personnel" of course meant "the Generalissimo Chiang to step down and Wang Jingwei to come up"; and as for "taking part in the building of the 'New Order,'" there lay much matter in it—including the acknowledgment of "Manchukuo" and "economic cooperation" and the like—which was but to sink China into a vassal-body of Japan, only put a little more prettily in the naming of it.

After Konoe's second statement was published, Wang openly declared: "We ought, upon the basis of the Japanese government's statement, to begin peace negotiations with the Japanese side."

Writing to this point, the writer must put in an aside: at that time our government authorities had not yet perceived that Wang Jingwei had entered into secret communication with Japan and taken up activities of treason against the state. It was by no means, as is commonly conjectured, that they had shown him any indulgence!

Wang's departure from Chongqing passed, too, through many twists and turns; but this is much the same as the accounts openly reported, and I shall not go over it again here. Suffice it to say that after Wang had flown to Kunming, Long Yun, the chairman of Yunnan province, who held armed strength in his hand, avoided seeing him and urged him to depart at once, lest there be inconvenience. This may have been a thing beyond Wang's reckoning; and as to whether Wang had reached a tacit understanding with Long Yun beforehand—though there is such a saying, no forceful evidence for it is to be gathered.

On the twentieth of December, Wang reached Hanoi; and when Tokyo had gotten word of it, Konoe, in accord with the compact of the "Record of the Sino-Japanese Agreement," published his statement on the twenty-second—that is, Konoe's third statement, whose original text is as follows:

"The Imperial Government, holding fast throughout to the policy of its repeated statements this year, shall utterly destroy the anti-Japanese National Government and join hands with the newborn regime, to build a 'New Order in East Asia.' The foundation of China's 'New Government' grows ever firmer; the movement in support of the 'New Government' throughout the several regions of 'Shina' surges without cease; the season is ripe for the building of a 'New China.' The Imperial Government therefore takes this occasion to make plain to home and abroad the root policy for the adjustment of relations between our government and a 'reborn China,' that the true intent of the Imperial Government may be thoroughly known. The three states of Japan, 'Manchukuo,' and 'Shina' should stand upon a footing of equality and labor together at the building of the 'New Order in East Asia,' carrying out joint defense against communism and economic cooperation. The 'New China' should liquidate all its mistaken policies of the past and take 'Manchukuo' by the hand. To speak plainly, what Japan hopes is frankly that China shall go forward to acknowledge 'Manchukuo' and open relations with it. East Asia will suffer no presence of a third international power; China should recognize this point, and, after the spirit of the anti-Comintern agreement concluded by Japan, Germany, and Italy, join with Japan in common defense, and conclude a Japan–'Shina' anti-Comintern agreement. Before this aim is attained, Japan, to obtain full assurance, shall station a suitable number of troops in the regions of North China and Inner Mongolia. Yet this act is not that Japan would thereby monopolize, or exclude and restrict, the rights and interests of the men of third countries in China; it is that without this the building of the 'New Order in East Asia' cannot be realized. The aim of our stationed troops is no other than to seek the wholly flawless cooperation of the two states of Japan and 'Shina.' China should understand the true intent of the Imperial Government, and grant to the Imperial subjects, within its territory, the freedom to reside and to do business, and especially, in the regions of North China and Inner Mongolia, grant to the Japanese subjects particular conveniences for development, so as to further the historical and economic relations of Japan and 'Shina' and complete at an early day the fusion of the two peoples. The carrying-out of the several points above is, in truth, what Japan most hopes of the 'New China.' These demands are, in truth, Japan's minimum demands upon China. Japan's aim is by no means to seek the territory of 'Shina' or the indemnity of war costs. The above demands are the several points that Japan cannot do without in the building of order in China. Where it is needful for helping 'Shina' become a wholly independent state, Japan will not grudge, on a later day, to abolish even its extraterritoriality in China."

After we had looked over the third statement of the Japanese premier Konoe, our first feeling was this: the fundamental attitude of this statement, just as it says of itself—"holding fast throughout to the policy of its repeated statements this year"—kept up its frenzy and its contempt; it was in no wise the "adjusting of relations" between the two countries that Wang Jingwei, against his conscience, called it. Only think: where under heaven is there such a thing as, for the sake of "friendship and amity between states," to bawl out openly and shamelessly the "utter destruction" of the other side's government?

That set of things bawled out in Konoe's three statements—no one can well believe that a man of Wang Jingwei's learning and cultivation could "fail to grasp the meaning within them" and yet swallow them whole and raw, and relish the taste; that is the great marvel. Though he said, over and over, that it was "for the state's sake," in our seeing this was, plainly and truly, "colluding with the enemy state."

### 2. The Enemy State's Plot Laid Bare to the Very Bottom

On the twenty-second of December in the twenty-seventh year, the Japanese premier Konoe Fumimaro published his third statement.

On the twenty-sixth of December in the same year, the Director-General Chiang attended the Party Leader's Memorial Week held by the Central Party Headquarters of the Nationalist Party. There the Director-General Chiang, under the head "Exposing the Enemy State's Plot and Setting Forth the National Policy of Resistance," delivered an address to the high comrades of the several party organs.

The matter of the address was aimed at the successive statements of the Japanese premier Konoe and at the many echoes raised throughout Japan, and should be among the most weighty and precious documents in history.

At this juncture Wang Jingwei had already turned his back on the state and reached Hanoi. In the Director-General Chiang's address the Wang affair was not so much as mentioned.

The original text runs to more than nine thousand characters; it saw through the ambition of Japan too clearly, so that not a single character can be abridged. Here it is respectfully set down according to the original:

Our war of resistance has entered a new stage. I have of late pointed out, again and again, that the past eighteen months may be named the first period of resistance, that is, the earlier phase of resistance; from now on comes the second period of resistance, that is, the latter phase of resistance. On every battlefield now, north and south, the flourishing morale and fighting spirit of our front is in truth a fair aspect such as we have not had since the war began. Officers and men in general understand that in this Sino-Japanese war the enemy would wholly destroy China, and we would save China from the root; and so their will is exceeding firm, their spirit yet more resolute. And the people at large can all recognize that the enemy will not cease until he has carried through his venomous scheme of aggression, will not stay his hand until he has destroyed China, and that we, unless we seek life out of death, have no way to survive. Therefore, however bitter the circumstances, the will of the soldiers and people everywhere grows the firmer; and if only front and rear alike recognize the crisis of the state, and the myriad be of one heart, and, toward the goal of final victory, toil bitterly, sacrifice and struggle, neither slacken nor submit, then I deeply believe that we can surely attain the aim of resistance.

On the enemy's side, because he saw the firmness of our resistance and the united will of the whole nation, he has, beside his military action, brought out all manner of methods of threat and enticement. Ever since the enemy government published its declaration on the third of November, his premier and his army, navy, and foreign-affairs authorities have published one after another a host of strange and monstrous fallacies. Motley and self-contradictory a hundredfold, their aim is to deceive their own people within and the friendly nations of the world without, and, further, wildly to work upon the Chinese people their venomous scheme of confusion, of drugging, and of intimidation. On the one hand their public and private organs of opinion, by turns soft and hard, shout at the side to lend them strength; and now, most lately, on the twenty-second of December, there is that statement of their premier Konoe Fumimaro, of the so-called adjusting of relations with a "reborn China"—which may be counted the grand summing-up of the enemy's juggling with mysteries, letting us understand the whole face of his plot.

This statement of Konoe's is nothing but stale, worn stuff; while we were bent single-mindedly on resistance, there was not only no need to refute it, but no worth even in heeding it. Yet gather up the enemy's words and deeds of these few months, analyze them a little, and one knows that on the surface he is empty and disjointed, while in the marrow there lies hid a keen blade of machinery. I may say a word: this is the enemy's grand confession of all his wild, plotting ambition—to swallow China whole, to lord it alone over East Asia, and thereafter to seek the conquest of the world; it is, too, the grand laying-bare of all the content of his schemes to destroy our state and wipe out our people. Now the juggling of words, the cunning sophistries, the sending-up of smoke-screens to poison the men of the world—these are ever the enemy state's practiced arts. See how, after he had published his talk, there came besides the so-called talk of the Japanese government's spokesman on the twenty-fourth, which flatly says that these are the conditions that must be laid before China, yet gives itself out for the view of the moderate party. What a venomous device it is, and what a farcical posture! I deeply fear there may yet be some small part of the world's men who do not understand the mischief hidden in this smoke-screen of his, and still think what he lays down not so very harsh; and so I purposely lay bare the whole of the enemy Japan's intent, that the people may know to be on their guard, and that the friendly nations of the world may understand Japan's ambition and plot, and to what pass, at its furthest reach, it shall stir up the world and bring calamity upon mankind!

What I would rouse you all to mark is the ferocity of the Japanese militarists, the frenzy of the Japanese militarists, the self-deceiving and men-deceiving of the Japanese militarists, and the blind ignorance of the Japanese militarists; and, most pressing of all, that you all should recognize that Japan is now resolved to swallow China whole. Now, taking Konoe's statement of the twenty-second of November for the center, let me trace again the various plots and slogans that Japanese opinion has been loudly advancing these few months, and that Japan has in fact been carrying out, and, by the method of analysis, offer a comprehensive understanding of them.

For convenience of exposition, I must first ask you all to mark the following four points:

(1) The building of a New Order in East Asia—this is the slogan and the practice the Japanese most plume themselves upon. By the exposition of their foreign minister Arita on the nineteenth of December, "By a New Order in East Asia is meant the close linkage and mutual aid, in the political, economic, and cultural spheres, of the 'three states of Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina,' to stem the Red peril, to uphold Eastern civilization, to do away with economic barriers, and to lift China out of her semi-colonial state, in the hope of the stability of East Asia." And Konoe, in his talk of the fourteenth, likewise said: "The final aim of the China affair lies not in military victory alone, but in the rebirth of China and the establishing of a New Order in East Asia; and this New Order rests upon the cooperation of the three sides, Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina, after China's rebirth." You should mark that his so-called reborn China is to destroy an independent China and produce instead a slave China, subject to him from age to age; and this New Order is founded upon the close linkage, after China has become a slave state, of China with Japan and with the "Manchukuo bogus state" that Japan has made. To what end? Under the name of stemming the Red peril, to control China's military; under the name of upholding Eastern civilization, to destroy China's national culture; under the name of doing away with economic barriers, to shut out the powers of Europe and America and lord it alone over the Pacific. And then, by the tool of the Japan-Manchukuo-Shina "economic unit" or "economic bloc," to throttle the very life-vein of China's economy. Only consider what mischief is hidden beneath these seven words, "the building of a New Order in East Asia." In one plain phrase: it is the general name for overturning the international order of East Asia, making a slave China, and so carrying through his design to lord it alone over the Pacific and carve up the world.

(2) The so-called "East Asian Cooperative Body," and the "inseparability of Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina," and "the mutually interlinked relation of mutual aid among Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina, to make an East Asian Cooperative Body"—this again is a slogan the enemy state, high and low, has by every means trumpeted these past months. This slogan of his is broader, more general, and a step further on than any of the former "economic unit" or "economic bloc" talk. He would take his doctrine of the "inseparability of Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina" for his ground, and hold that in the political, economic, and cultural spheres, China and the Northeast should wholly be swallowed and merged into a single body; and his magazines openly declare that under the East Asian Cooperative Body, Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina should stand in a vertical relation, not a level one. They say, too, it should be a patriarchal system, Japan the patriarch and "Manchukuo and Shina" the children. In other words, the former is ruler and master, the latter the ruled and the slave. Think, all of you—if this is not annexation, what is it? If this is not the whole destruction of China, what is it? And Konoe, but last month, scattered abroad a preposterous handbill, in the midst of which is a phrase most startling to the eye: "to set up the mutually interlinked relation of mutual aid among Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina in politics, economy, and culture." What is this interlinked relation? Have you not all seen the chains upon the cangue and the fetters? This one interlinked relation is precisely, like a chain, to drag our whole people down into the eighteenth level of hell, never to loose themselves forever.

(3) The so-called "economic unit" and "economic bloc"—this Japan has advocated for many years, and of late the doctrine still prevails, and is pushed forward with all his might. This is the chief link in the middle of the East Asian Cooperative Body. They change the slogan from time to time: now they call it economic cooperation, now economic collaboration; and their government's declaration of the third of November calls it "economic linkage." An enemy newspaper at the end of November carried: "Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina shall set up an economic unit, and henceforth share weal and woe." Then, in Arita's talk of the nineteenth of December, there is this sentence: "Japan has determined to open an economic conference, to seek the close conjoining of the economies of Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina, and to strengthen the 'economic unit.' Such an economic relation the men of the world call an 'economic bloc.'" In fact, the two companies he has made his tools of economic annexation, the "North China Development" and the "Central China Promotion," were long since set up; the Japan-Manchukuo-Shina economic conferences have been held more than once; and his so-called Planning Board, on the very next day after Konoe published his statement, drew up a "Draft Plan for the Expansion of the Productive Power of Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina." This so-called economic bloc of his is not only to manipulate the tariffs and finance of our China and to monopolize the production and trade of our whole nation and the hegemony of East Asia; carried step by step to its end, it must come at last to restraining the food, clothing, dwelling, and movement of every last one of us Chinese people, so that we may have not a shred of freedom, and life and death, taking and giving, are his to do as he wills; wholly making the Chinese people slaves and beasts of burden, under the whip and the sucking-dry wholly destroying the very living of our people!

(4) The setting up of the so-called "Kōain"—this comes on from the organ for Chinese affairs the enemy state has long been fussing over. In the past he once planned to set up a "Tai-Shi Board"; of late he has changed it to the Kōain. "Tai-Shi Board" was already an insulting and fearful enough name; changed to the Kōain, it is simply a grave insult to all the men of Asia. This practice of his is to break the whole of China into fragments—not only to destroy China, but to imperil the whole of Asia. This Kōain was formally set up on the fifteenth of this month; the day before, Konoe published a talk, saying: "We would organize a new administrative organ to build the New Order in East Asia; this organ, keeping a bond with the several organs abroad and with China, shall become the pivot for carrying out policy toward China, to realize Japan's final aim in the China affair." As to what this organ is, you should now have a clear understanding: it is the grand organ for carrying out every plan to destroy China; it may be called a grand secret-service organ that gathers into one the sundry secret-service organs by which Japan used to make mischief all over China. Only, in the past, holding that the time had not yet come, he did it by stealth; now he has flung aside the mask, and, brazen and heedless, brought the whole out into the open and formally set it up. By the setting up of the Kōain, you should all the more understand: as what does Japan regard our China? What is it he wants? What is his so-called final aim in the Sino-Japanese affair? We say "long-term resistance"; he says "long-term construction." What is it he would construct? Let it be said plainly: it is his long-term carrying-out of the plan to destroy China, never to stop until the aim is attained. Now his method is ready, his organ is ready; this too may be called the dagger showing at the map's end, nothing hidden at all!

Having understood the several notions above, look again at what is in Konoe's statement of the twenty-second of December, and one may get a true understanding of it, not to be blinded by the fog upon the surface of the words. Let me now set forth once more its points that deserve mark:

First, the backbone of this statement of his is still that same set of the "three of Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina" joining as one to labor at "the building of a New Order in East Asia." He says it is to make plain to home and abroad the true intent of his government; his aim, of course, is to appeal to Europe and America and to the world, and so he has ordered his phrases with especial care, seeming to say that what he asks of China is neither territory nor indemnity for war costs, that it is not for the private good of his one state but for the great cause of East Asia; and he says besides that he would have China become a wholly independent state, and so shows further that he considers doing away with extraterritoriality and giving back the concessions, and the like—seeming as though he would not only take nothing from China, but give something to her besides. This reckoning of his is as though the men of the world had none of them understood the true meaning of his so-called "New Order in East Asia," and might be lightly beguiled by it. In truth his so-called "Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina" joining as one, his so-called "New Order in East Asia"—the ambition is manifest, as I have set forth above. He has laid hold of this main axle of destroying China and lording it alone over East Asia; in his mind, territory of course means the territory he governs, resources the resources in his own bag. Having swept them off already, what more should he ask of trifling cessions of land and indemnities? What he asks being the whole of the land and the people, the great desire before him, he is naturally glad to gull the world with this talk of wanting no territory and no indemnity. In fact, from the standpoint of our China, to speak of indemnity for war costs and the rest, one must first make clear where the responsibility for the war lies; and this time it is plainly he who set his forces in motion to come and make war within our territory—the responsibility of aggression is clear to the eyes and ears of all. This saying of his is of course not worth a glance. As for extraterritoriality: if he grasps the whole sovereignty of China, that is a skin upon the skin. His so-called giving back of the concessions is likewise but a thing lodged in an outer treasury; not only as to other countries' concessions—his opinion-makers have already trumpeted his taking them in charge—but he would make China's concessions into concessions belonging to Japan alone. And in fact, if China acknowledged his "New Order in East Asia" and the "Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina" joint relation, it would be to turn the whole territory of China into one great concession belonging to Japan. So, then, China, if not turned into his slave-vassal state, would sink to a protectorate, and in fact be merged into Japan. He says he would make China a wholly independent state—is this not just the Korea of the Treaty of Shimonoseki? I may affirm: before this statement was published, there may have been in the world men who hoped Japan might repent; but since this statement of his was published, there is no longer a single Chinese who knows the great right and sees the times but has cast off all thought of peace and compromise!

Second, the chief points in his statement, beside the joint labor of Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina, are economic cooperation and joint defense against communism. The content of economic cooperation I set forth fully above in speaking of the economic bloc, and need not repeat. As for the so-called joint defense against communism, it is that China should conclude with him an anti-Comintern agreement, that troops should be stationed in North China and Inner Mongolia marked off as an anti-Comintern special zone. Whatever the meaning of his so-called joint defense against communism, in our China, which carries out the Three Principles of the People with one accord, to speak again of joint defense against communism is wholly to loose an arrow at no target. We may say he is but, under the name of joint defense against communism, first to control our country's military, and then to control our country's politics and culture and even our diplomacy. This very point was a chief cause of the grudge he bore in the years before the resistance of the Double Seventh, when his yearly demands went unmet. Because we would not enter his snare, we chose rather to bear a thousand hardships, and at the last extremity chose rather to make the whole nation's sacrifice and resist. Had this demand of joint defense against communism been one we could grant, need we have waited till today? Common opinion in the world may hold that Japan's so-called defense against communism is truly aimed at defense against Soviet Russia; in fact Japan's so-called concluding an agreement for joint defense against communism aims not at defense against communism, nor at defense against Russia, but truly at using this name to destroy China; and even were there some flavor of Russia in it, that is but a small part of it, the great part being the destruction of China. Else, if it were for national defense or truly a matter of Russia, then why, in July and August this year, at the military clash of Zhanggufeng, did his ambassador to the Soviet Union, Shigemitsu Mamoru, so cravenly flinch before the Soviet foreign minister and at the last submit? By this one may see that his so-called joint defense against communism, laid now before our country, is but to deceive world opinion without and his own people within, a mere pretext for demanding the stationing of troops in North China and Inner Mongolia. To speak plainly, if the stationing of troops in North China could be allowed, if Inner Mongolia could be marked off as a special zone, we should not have had the resistance of the Double Seventh; if China, out of fear of Japan, had let his forces dispose of North China, then in the seventeenth year, when Tanaka sent troops to Jinan, our National Revolutionary Army would not have pressed on regardless to Beiping, but might early have been stopped by him, and Inner Mongolia and North China been handed over long ago to his carving. Only because China was in the midst of revolution, and once the revolutionary force is set in motion, once the Three Principles of the People are unfolded, then, come what may, it must clear away every difficulty and rush toward its goal, by no power to be barred—only for this does he lay this demand, in truth understanding modern China all too little; knowing neither himself nor, still less, the other; still less understanding what age the present age is, and what manner of China the present China is. At the same time he declares openly in his statement that without the stationing of troops in North China and Inner Mongolia the "New Order in East Asia" cannot be established. Then what is this so-called "New Order in East Asia"? Can the friendly nations of the world and the men of our country not now see it all the more plainly?

Third, the latter part of the statement asks for particular conveniences of development in North China and Inner Mongolia—this is the confession of his design, under the name of joint defense against communism, to monopolize China's economy and to throttle the very heart of our economy. Besides, he further proposes that China should grant to the Japanese subjects the freedom to reside and do business in the interior. This point looks, at a glance, very commonplace; but he has failed, alas, to know what a deep and universal impression the crimes Japan wrought in China in the past have left upon the Chinese. To speak plainly: the common people of China, at the mere mention of Japan, will call to mind his secret-service organs and his lawless rōnin; will call to mind the trafficking in opium, the selling of morphine, the making of "white powder," the vending of heroin, the keeping of gambling-dens and brothels, the smuggling of arms to supply the bandits, the fostering of ruffians, the making of traitors—all the plots that disorder our order, corrupt our people's morals, and poison and banditize the land. And so, as for the opening of the interior to freedom of residence and business: in China, after her law-sovereignty is wholly independent hereafter, as to other countries it is not a thing that may not be discussed; but as to Japan, unless we are willing to suffer his poison and disorder, unless our country is willing to give up the right of keeping the peace, unless we are willing to have our good and wholesome usages corrupted by him and the very marrow of our economy sucked dry by him, there is no man who would grant it. The Japanese should not be so forgetful: this so-called freedom to reside and do business in the interior—is it not of the same nature as the commercial-lease right in the Northeast that he asked for in years past? In the eighteenth year, his present foreign minister Arita, in the standing of director of the East Asia Bureau, came to Nanjing to negotiate on the orders of his cabinet premier Tanaka, and we then strove to refuse it, and would not grant this demand of a commercial-lease right; we chose rather to let him take our Northeast by military force than conclude with him such an unequal treaty that ruins the state and loses its sovereignty. Why? Because the impression Japan gave us was too fearful: wherever his residence reached, the police power and the economic power would be lost along with it; if the Japanese had freedom to reside and do business, then the Chinese of the same place would have no freedom, would not even be able to stand their ground. Yet the commercial-lease question of that time was but a partial matter of one corner of the Northeast, and even so we could not grant it; now he widens it further, to reach our whole territory, and under the slogan of the so-called "New Order in East Asia" besides—I ask, can our people have any room left even to consider it?

Fourth, beside the several concrete demands set forth above in their order, I would rouse you all the more to mark two phrases of extreme venom in Konoe's statement. These are: (1) "the completing of the fusion of the two peoples." By our ideal, equality and friendship between people and people, reaching a harmonious coexistence—this of course is the right road. But what Japan asks is fusion; and set this against his so-called "inseparability of Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina," "the close conjoining of Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina," "setting up a mutually interlinked relation of mutual aid," and one may know what he seeks. Prove it further by his words that the "East Asian Cooperative Body" is a "vertical relation," and then his so-called "fusion" is plainly to have our Chinese people "dissolved" or "melted down" into the Japanese people, and "merged into one body" with them—is this not to destroy forever the independent existence of our people? (2) "wholly flawless cooperation." What meaning his asked-for cooperation and collaboration carry, one may already understand from my exposition above; but he asks besides for "wholly flawless cooperation," which is to say that cooperation not whole will not do. And what is wholly flawless? Liken it to swallowing: he must swallow the whole down, skin and bone together, before his desire is satisfied; that is to say, he would have the Chinese wholly in the place of slaves, offering up all they have, down to the labor of their very bodies, to be his drudges a lifetime long.

The above are the chief points within his statement. Venomous as it is, Konoe yet sums it up with a phrase: "These demands are, in truth, Japan's minimum demands upon China." To call this the minimum demand—I ask, what more can there be beyond this minimum? Set beside Hirota's Three Principles of former days, it is broader and more venomous by I know not how many times; and the enemy still wildly hopes to lure China into accepting it. I ask: before the war began, we could not even accept Hirota's Three Principles; can he today still wildly hope that China will accept such terms of national ruin? To throttle another's life-vein and be content only with the life of a neighbor state's whole nation and people—the enemy's venomous plot is wholly disclosed in this one sheet of a statement. And not this alone: the content of the so-called "Meiji legacy-policy" and the "Tanaka Memorial," which the enemy had ever concealed by a hundred devices, is now all proven out. Tanaka said: "To conquer the world, one must first conquer Shina!" Konoe, in his report to the Privy Council on the first of December, said, "We are resolved to fix the term for concluding the affair upon the state of the work of construction in China." His so-called state of the work of construction in China means the stage his destruction and swallowing have reached. Our people can now understand it fully. Until China is destroyed, Japan's work of aggression will not stop. The friendly nations of the world can now understand it too! Japan's policy has now widened from his continental policy to a maritime policy; from his northward-advance policy pushed on to a southward-advance policy. In brief, Japan's aggressive policy is now driving forward on land and sea at once, plying both hands together. In his design to annex China, he would at the same time overturn the international order, lord it alone over East Asia, and drive out the powers of Europe and America; and once this step is planted firm, what he will go on to do is, indeed, as plain as if his liver and lungs were held up bare! In sum, Japan has now laid out whole the set of ambitious madness and schemes he kept secret and unspoken for decades. The words we spoke in the past to rouse a little wariness of Japan's ambition, some of you may still have thought alarmist talk, holding that Japan would not go so far; but from now on, from the state to the world, there is no one who does not see Japan's ambition clear as fire.

Surveying this statement of Konoe's, we may affirm that what Japan truly desires is the whole annexation of our state and the root destruction of our people, and by no means the form of so-called Sino-Japanese cooperation or economic cooperation and the like; as for cession of land and indemnity, before this great desire they are of course the less regarded by the aggressor. To lay it bare: his so-called economic bloc is to bring the whole financial power and resources of China under Japanese control in place of his so-called wanting no indemnity; his demand for the stationing of troops in Inner Mongolia and North China, and for free residence and business throughout the land of China, is to bring the whole territory of China under his control and disposal, and the whole people of China under his oppression and enslavement, in place of his so-called wanting no cession of land. We remember that before Korea was annexed, the Japanese often used such talk as "Japan and Korea one body," "Japan and Korea inseparable," to drug and dazzle the people of Korea; and today he again loudly trumpets the "East Asian Cooperative Body" of the "inseparability of Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina." Let us lay it bare plainly for him: what is the "East Asian Cooperative Body"? It is flatly the "merging of China and Japan," it is the merging of the whole of China into Japan, it is the completing of a "Japanese Continental Empire." And he has besides a set of theory, "the building of a New Order in East Asia," to be the smoke-screen that hides the plot, holding all the world's men fools and himself alone wise, thinking to cover the ears and eyes of all under heaven with one hand. This is truly the new invention, the new method, of the Japanese imperialists for destroying men's states and wiping out men's peoples!

Now his plan and his tools for the destruction of China are all made ready; his means and his mind for aggression and annexation are laid wholly bare; what is wanting is only that China should suffer his deceit, suffer his threat, and submit and enter his snare. When the state of things is disclosed plainly to this pass, if we would still, under the tiger's jaw, seek a bare survival, and by peace and compromise seek an independent and equal existence, that is no other than a fool's talk of dreams: once the spirit submits, it sinks to ten thousand ruins; once the chains are put on, a hundred generations cannot loose them. I may say a further word: though Japan's plot and madness are only today wholly laid bare, this venomous scheme and deep design of the enemy militarists has been handed down from master to disciple; it is no matter of a morning or an evening. In these ten years and more, Japan's grave ministers and men of old repute have passed away one after another, and there is not a single statesman who understands the great principle of survival and ruin; they sit and let the soldiery break the law and confound the rule and dispose of all. The more the crisis grows, the wilder the ambition; long ago he laid out the whole net, that China might not loose herself. Happily our whole nation of countrymen rose up in resistance last July, and would not let his slow plot to subdue us without a war have its way, but laid bare his plot step by step, so that now he cannot but hold up whole his fierce liver and lungs before the world. Had we not risen in resistance last year, and let him nibble us step by step, then in the world he would indeed have worked his deceit, and in our China all the more, like a slow chronic sickness, hidden and unbreaking, our bodily strength would waste away by degrees and our nerves grow numb, and in no more than three or five years we must have sunk to ruin. See how he then used the means of destroying Korea: on the one hand with the fair name of friendly cooperation and support of independence; on the other with threat and enticement, the venomous plot of drugging and dividing, and at the last took her into his bag before she was aware. But we have, in this year and a half of resistance, raised the people's hatred of the enemy and their wariness; and by the death and wounding and sacrifice of more than a million officers and men at the front and several million people in the rear, their firm struggle and their unbroken constancy, we have forced the enemy to lay bare whole his hideous face. So it is that not only has China no danger of being annexed and destroyed, but the world too has been roused in time to what pass this ambitious and ungovernable state, left to its frenzy, shall imperil the peace of the world. Great as our sacrifice is, this one war of ours has not only saved the state from the crisis of destruction, but has dissolved a dire calamity and catastrophe for the world. Our cruel sacrifice has, in truth, a grave meaning; our spirit, never bent and never broken, has built a firm safeguard for the survival of our state and people. Comrades must recognize this point clearly, and go a step further to do our whole duty.

By our general reckoning of Japan's plot, we have found the ferocity of the enemy, and found too the frenzy of the enemy; we truly do not understand how the enemy has lost his reason to such a pass. Is there anywhere in the world a people of seventy million that can destroy a great state of five thousand years' history, four hundred and fifty million people, and more than twelve million square kilometers of land? Is there a revolutionary government that holds a principle, and can be lightly threatened by men into giving up its mission of revolution and saving the state? The enemy would, under the name of joint defense against communism, control our military; by the tool of the economic bloc, destroy our resources; and by the tool of the "East Asian Cooperative Body," control our politics and culture and destroy the very living of our people—his reckoning is exceeding fine. The enemy declares again and again that the three states of "Japan, Manchukuo, and Shina" would set up an inseparable relation of politics, economy, and culture, and even a mutually interlinked relation of mutual aid; in other words, to destroy, in the political, economic, and cultural spheres, the independent existence of China's nationhood, and, in the political, economic, and cultural spheres, to dominate East Asia—his design too is exceeding thorough. But to speak truly: of the culture of East Asia, what independent culture is there besides the culture of China? East Asia has China for its center of gravity; if China lose her independent existence, what economy of East Asia is left to speak of? Even in the politics of East Asia: in five thousand years, only China's political thought of loving her kin and being good to her neighbors, of loyalty and filial love and benevolence, of peace and good faith, has been able to be the pillar of East Asia. And now it is our Party Leader's Three Principles of the People that are the principle of equality, freedom, independence, and coexistence, and the safeguard of a lasting peace; what politics has Japan to speak of? I ask, what manner of politics is Japan's politics of today? Japan knows not to reflect upon herself, but holds the world's men to be gullible, and makes the destruction of China her means of building East Asia and reviving East Asia—this is flatly to turn against reason and betray righteousness, to run counter and do amiss! I ask, without China, what East Asia? and what Japan? The enemy militarists' action of destroying China must end in hastening Japan to her own destruction. In our China, a year and a half of resistance has already laid the foundation of revival; we fear no hardship, and dread no danger; we grieve only for this state of Japan, that, by the sacrifice of I know not how much heart's blood and spirit of the reformers of the Meiji, was built into so strong and flourishing a state, and now, the people powerless, the court without authority, the statesmen without integrity or vision, lets a few young officers run counter and do amiss, wildly spend the national strength, shake the national foundation, and walk the whole road of harming others to profit self and slaying men to gratify itself, driving beasts to devour men. In the eyes of these officers there is neither China nor the world; neither law nor rule, nor even their own government; greedy, cruel, and violent, they do as they please. Let this go on, and this state of Japan is in truth in the utmost danger, beyond all imagining. Though we and the Japanese militarists are enemies that cannot stand together, yet we and the Japanese people are, after all, a neighbor people of the same script; and, from her history, thinking on her future, we not only feel her peril, but truly grieve for her.

Comrades, you must know: the enemy now walks frenzied and blind, going ever further onto the road of confusion and the dead end. They have now forgotten their own history, forgotten their own standing; without, they see not the world; within, they see not their own crisis; and, facing us, they know not revolutionary-period China. They have but two thoughts: either, blind to the facts, they wildly hope by cruel terms to force our submission; or they would, by a quick and cunning shortcut, cover the world's eyes and snatch the advantage. This is truly to be a fool oneself and hold all the world's men fools to be gulled; to be cruel oneself and hold that in the world only violence rules all. Even as in the several articles Konoe lists in this statement of his: he would "build a New Order in East Asia" to shut China's door and break the Nine-Power Treaty; by the "East Asian Cooperative Body" and the "economic bloc" shut out the powers of Europe and America in the Far East; by the "stationing of troops in North China" and the "Inner Mongolia special zone" revive the "Twenty-One Demands" he exacted of Yuan Shikai. Taken whole, this set of his so-called "building a New Order in East Asia" and the rest is to force our China herself to break the principle of the Open Door and equal opportunity; to force our China herself to break the Covenant of the League, the Nine-Power Treaty, and even the Sino-Soviet Non-Aggression Pact and every international treaty. He would bind our hands and feet, throttle our blood-vessels, and have our China besides follow him in betraying faith and slighting righteousness to help complete his mad dream of lording it alone over East Asia and even dominating the world. I ask: our China, a state of five thousand years, has ever taken good faith and righteousness for the ground of her statehood—can she be threatened by him into casting away our standing?

The founding spirit of our China is not to insult the widow and the widower, not to fear the strong and violent, and above all not to betray a covenant or forsake good faith and so break the righteousness by which mankind holds together. I remember once, in the early years of the Republic, when Tanaka Giichi came to Shanghai to see our Party Leader—that was the very time of the outbreak of the European war—Tanaka said that we of East Asia should cast off all our existing relations with foreign countries and make instead a new order; and the Party Leader asked him, would this not be to break the international treaties? Tanaka said: "To break the international treaties and shatter the unequal relations—is this not of great profit to China?" The Party Leader firmly refused him, saying: "To abolish the unequal treaties, one must do it too in the open and by lawful and proper procedure; if by unlawful breaking of treaties—such an act, though of profit to our country, we will not do." Comrades, this is the spirit of China; this is the spirit of the Three Principles of the People. By this spirit we resist; by this spirit we withstand all tyranny, might, and violence; and by this spirit, further, we would restore the order of East Asia, and offer it to the lasting peace of the world.

In sum, this war is, in Japan, the running riot of violence, the whole collapse and downfall of spirit and morality; in us, it is the righteous war of one who resolutely takes up the world's charge of justice. Japan's militarists now, having lost their reason and let loose their beast-nature, gallop and dash and can break through every bound, and destroy all the civilization and welfare of mankind. In truth, all the states of the world that bear the responsibility of treaties, to open the darkness and bring back the light, ought all to have the responsibility of sanctioning violence and of upholding the international treaties; but all stand hesitant and looking to one another, and China alone must, sparing no sacrifice, take up this great charge at the pass where righteousness lives or dies and public principle survives or perishes. This resistance of ours is, at home, to complete the aim of the national revolution and win China's independence and freedom and equality; toward the international, it is to uphold righteousness, restore the dignity of treaties, and rebuild the order of peace. This war of resistance of ours is a war of good and evil, of right and wrong; a war of public principle and might; a war of the law-keeper and the law-breaker; a war, too, of righteousness and violence. Our old saying has it: "Virtue is not left an orphan; it must have neighbors." The power of public principle in the world must at last lift its head; all good men must at last cooperate for righteousness. If only we hold fast our standing, fix our goal, set our resolve—the harder, the firmer; the longer, the braver—the whole nation of one heart, striving on, then final victory must be ours. I hope only that our comrades and the whole nation's soldiers and people will strive the harder, that it may be brought to accomplishment.

As to this address of the Director-General Chiang, under the head "Exposing the Enemy State's Plot and Setting Forth the National Policy of Resistance," the true state of things at the time may be understood from the "Memoirs of Chen Bulei"; a fragment is here excerpted for reference.

"During the days at Guilin (writer's note: this was in December of the twenty-seventh year), the Duke Chiang, whenever he had a little leisure, would call me to talk; and what he spoke of was all the essentials of winning by spirit in the second period of resistance. Against the enemy premier Konoe's advocated East Asian Cooperative Body and the interlinked relation of mutual aid, he inveighed with especial force, saying that such a yoke would forever ruin the destiny of the people, worse by far than military occupation, that on no account must we fall into his snare, but ought to fight the harder and wage a war of spirit against him. He dictated it four or five times in all, and bade me set it down in a separate book, in readiness to draft a long piece. (Mr. Chen Bulei's note: it is a pity such words could not be brought to the hearing of Wang Jingwei and Zhou Fohai at the time; else they might not have lost themselves and become traitors.)

"The Duke Chiang further bade me wire Wang and Zhu (Liuxian) to prepare to convene the Fifth Plenum within the first month, and instructed me on the chief items of proposals to be drafted, all of them urgent tasks of party and government construction.

"On the seventh of December I went from Guilin to Chongqing… reaching Yu only toward evening. On reaching Yu I learned that Fohai had gone to Kunming on the fifth, and I lodged for the time in his newly rented house. At nine at night I went to call on Mr. Wang, who asked me again and again my views on the state of the war; and afterward I felt his bearing was not quite natural—yet at the time I did not much mark it.

"The Duke Chiang reached Yu on the eighth, and on the ninth, at Huangshan, invited Kong, Wang, Wang (the foreign minister), Ye, Zhang, and the rest to talk of the essentials of resistance henceforth. When Kong and the rest spoke repeatedly of the international situation, the Duke Chiang stopped them, saying: 'Ask not how the international situation stands; our country must make ready for independent revival and independent struggle.' Wang too had no other word, saying only, 'The enemy state's difficulty lies in ending the war; our country's difficulty lies in how to sustain it.' Afterward the Duke Chiang had a slight illness, and Wang, on the sixteenth, asked to see him alone; the Duke Chiang, still ailing, talked with him in detail for twenty or thirty minutes, yet throughout offered no opinion of peace or war. And in less than a week Wang stole away into Yunnan and left the country.

"After the Duke Chiang recovered, he went to inspect the Northwest, and I did not go with him. On the twenty-third I went to Beibei to visit my family, and after three days' stay returned to Yu…

Mr. Zhang Jiluan (writer's note: chief editorial writer of the Dagongbao, a famed political commentator) called it the first forceful piece of writing of the resistance period. After the Duke Chiang had gone over it four times, it was published on the twenty-eighth; and the next day Wang Jingwei, at Hanoi, published his Yan Telegram in answer to Konoe's statement."

In the above, the times Mr. Chen Bulei sets down are "drafted on the twenty-sixth" and "published on the twenty-eighth," which differ a little from the times commonly recorded. To my seeing, this slight difference of dates is of no importance, and there is no need to make much of textual examination over it.

Writing to this point, the writer cannot but have a certain reflection, and to express it is also to give an account: in order of time, Konoe's third statement was on the twenty-second of December in the twenty-seventh year; the Generalissimo Chiang's address on the twenty-sixth of the same month and year; and Wang Jingwei's "Yan Telegram" on the twenty-ninth of the same month and year. Yet in the first section of this piece I spoke first of the "Yan Telegram," and only in the second section disclosed the "Konoe statement" and respectfully set down the Generalissimo Chiang's address. This is the writer's deliberate arrangement: I hope you, my readers, will first have a notion of the "Yan Telegram," and then, when you have finished reading the "Konoe statement" and the "Generalissimo Chiang's address," read Wang Jingwei's "Yan Telegram" once more, and you will find how he disgraced the traditional spirit of the Chinese people and bowed to the wolfish ambition of Japan!

### 3. No Such Inconceivable Thing Could Ever Have Happened

We read, with all care and reverence, this address the Director-General Chiang had delivered; and besides understanding deeply the wolfish ambition of the Japanese militarists—their studied intent to annex the whole of China and destroy the Chinese people—we felt strengthened besides in our resolve to resist to the end and never compromise midway, and in our faith that final victory must be ours.

Yet we did not understand why not a word was said of Wang Jingwei's turning his back and stealing away.

At this moment those we had sent to Hanoi were already six: Fang Bingxi, Wang Luqiao, Yu Lexing, Cen Jiazhuo, and the writer, with one more comrade, a wireless operator, whom Brother Bingxi was charged to keep in touch with; and though I met him once, I did not yet know his honored name. In the nature of our work this was a common thing, and no wonder at all.

It was mentioned in brief before that the "Hanoi work" was but a temporary grouping, that is, the drawing-off of working comrades from various places for a single tour of duty. Though we held gatherings, they had no form; they were neither a "briefing" nor a "presentation," and had no fixed agenda; to represent them by a "round-table" or a "conversation-meeting" would be the more fitting. Yet it was a very grave and serious business, not a few men huddled together to talk nonsense.

The two brothers Yu Lexing and Cen Jiazhuo had but lately come from Chongqing; their range of contact was the broader, and naturally they had more knowledge of the general enemy situation and of things in the great rear. Of the front-line war news, which changed from time to time, I shall not speak here; but the two great questions—how Wang Jingwei had been able to leave Chongqing wholly unhindered, and to what bottom the Japanese side's basic policy toward China really went—these became the themes of our talk.

I think the two, Yu and Cen, had come only after "taking a lesson"; and it may be, too, that our superiors meant them to convey this understanding to us, though it was not plainly shown.

First, Brother Lexing analyzed Japan's basic policy toward China. What Brother Lexing conveyed at the time was but the gist; and in the wording the writer has again put it a little into order.

On the first of April in the twenty-seventh year, the tenth month after the beginning of China's resistance, the Japanese government had already tasted the flavor of not being able to win the war easily, and so put into force a "National General Mobilization Law."

On the tenth of June of the same year, the Konoe Fumimaro cabinet of Japan was reorganized, and Itagaki Seishirō, who most strongly advocated the aggression against China and teemed with plots, took office as war minister to represent the Japanese "military party." At the instance—or, it may be read, the manipulation—of the new war minister Itagaki, a "Five Ministers' Conference" was set up. The so-called "Five Ministers" included the "war minister," the "navy minister," the "foreign minister," the "finance minister," and the "premier." These "Five Ministers" answer to what we are used to reckoning as the army minister (Japan calling a "ministry" a "province"), the navy minister, the foreign minister, the finance minister, and the cabinet premier.

This was plainly the "power center" within the cabinet, and shows too the domineering of the Japanese soldiery. Yet the Japanese military party (the collective name for Japan's army and navy and the General Staff) gave it the fair title of "the highest organ for the study of national policy." In fact it was far more than study: it was the most powerful policy-deciding organ that planned the annexation of China and designed the aggression of the whole world, and was the more venomous the more it showed itself in the bullying of China.

Since the setting up of the "Five Ministers' Conference," it had first, on the twenty-fourth of June, the twelfth of July, and the twenty-second of July in the twenty-seventh year, held three meetings, and in the meetings drew up three schemes, which were:

One, "The Guiding Principles for the China Affair Henceforth." By the content already understood, there was in it: to concentrate the strength of the state (Japan) and attain the war aim within the year 1938. That is, to defeat China. In the course of the war, should a third country come forward to mediate, this might be considered according to the conditions. That is, if it could meet the demands, peace too might be discussed.

Two, "Stratagems toward Shina in Response to the Times." The Japanese are very fond of juggling stratagems: whatever they do, at every turn they use stratagem; this is what we have in mind as "petty Japan, full of tricks." By what is understood, the "stratagems toward Shina" fixed at the "Five Ministers' Conference" lay chiefly in how to break down China's power of resistance, and their content may roughly be divided into the political and the economic. On the political side: to split the internal concord of the Chinese government, and to entice men of "repute" to concoct a puppet regime; on the economic side: to wreck the system of the fabi, to seize China's assets abroad, and to cut off the material aid of the international community to China.

Three, "The Outline for the Inner Guidance of the Shina Regime." Here the so-called "Shina regime" means the puppet regime Japan fosters in the occupied areas; and the phrase "inner guidance," put another way, is the manipulation behind the scenes. Those who bear this charge do it wholly through the various "advisers" the Japanese military dispatches.

Japan's policy toward China was mostly grounded upon the three points above, with no great change ever after. Later the "Five Ministers' Conference," in its meeting on the ninth of September of the same year, decided upon a "Programmatic Outline for Setting Up a China Federation," drawn up expressly for the several puppet organizations. Its aim lay in how to grasp each puppet organization severally, and make them keep step yet raise no sympathetic resonance.

By the fourth of January in the twenty-eighth year, the Konoe cabinet fell; but Itagaki Seishirō, still in the Hiranuma Kiichirō cabinet, kept his post as war minister, and this "Five Ministers' Conference" went on existing as before.

The above is a rough sketch of what we understood at the time; only as to the state of the secret dealings of Wang Jingwei with the Japanese side we were not very clear. As for the matter mentioned before, of Gao Zongwu, Mei Siping, and the rest going in and out of Chongqing several times, that was all learned only afterward.

This informal round-table of ours touched, too, on the question of how Wang Jingwei had been able to leave Chongqing smoothly, and why his other designs had not been discovered beforehand.

By a rule of our unwritten law, we ought not to have discussed this question—that is, our working activities did not permit such inquiry—but every one of us was still young, and all had a keen curiosity, and, moreover, this had an inseparable connected bearing upon the task we bore. And so we fell to talking of it behind others' backs. Why is the discussing of matters outside one's part forbidden? The chief reason is to avoid dragging in other working units. If our superiors held it needful, they would let us know in various ways; else each unit should mind its own, and our superiors take up the main threads and sum the whole.

Before coming to the question itself, a word of explanation must be made first: the Juntong of the twenty-eighth year was still in its period of "growth"; its working standing, and men's sense of its worth, were not so weighty as in the latter period of the resistance and after the victory. Though it held several working posts besides, there was yet a feeling of being neither up nor down. Perhaps this is not clear enough; then let me put it a step more plainly: the so-called "not up" means that it could not yet win the full trust of the highest authorities, and so got no high grant of power; the so-called "not down" means that it had not struck root downward, and had at most but a thin layer of social base.

At that time, of the units in Chongqing bearing on intelligence and public security, there were, by what is understood: the "Special-Service Regiment" directly under the Juntong; the "Yu Special District" directly under the Juntong's own headquarters; the head of the "Chongqing Municipal Police Bureau," recommended by the Juntong; the head of the "Aviation Inspection Office," recommended by the Juntong; and the like. The "Special-Service Regiment" was in its make-up mostly an armed force, its task leaning toward internal guard and protection, not directly gathering intelligence toward the outside. The "Yu Special District" is the "Chongqing District," a "regular" unit under the Juntong; why the one word "Special" was added, perhaps because Chongqing was the wartime capital and its organization and task the more special. Again, as for the "Chongqing Municipal Police Bureau," though its head was recommended by the Juntong, it had its own fixed charge and range of work, resting of course still on the keeping of local public security, and seldom touching upon political questions. To what organ the "Aviation Inspection Office" belonged, the writer is not yet clear; what may be affirmed is that it was by no means a branch of the Juntong. Whether it belonged to the Chongqing Municipal Police Bureau? the Aviation Commission? or the "Garrison Command" or some other organ? I dare not say for sure. By what the writer knows, the "Inspection Section" of the "Garrison Command" had not yet been set up at that time, so it could not have belonged to the "Inspection Section." Why so sure of this point? Because the writer was himself the formally appointed first head of the "Inspection Section of the Chongqing Garrison Command"—though I never took up the post, yet I remember it very clearly. Lately a friend informed me that after the "Aviation Inspection Office" was set up, it first belonged to a certain section under the Military Affairs Commission. Besides these, there were many more units bearing on the Juntong, such as the "Anti-Smuggling Office," the "Police General Corps," and the like; but these, I suppose, have no bearing on the "Wang case."

The above is something of our understanding; next it will do no harm to give first the conclusion of our round-table, and then analyze the grounds it rested on.

In the Juntong, from Mr. Dai Yunong down, no one knew of any intelligence whatever of Wang Jingwei's dealings with Japan; had there been the least word of it, no one would have dared conceal it, and even were it judged "groundless," it could not have been laid aside undealt-with, but must have been earnestly "handled." What is here called "handling" is an intelligence term. Suppose that at the time a piece of intelligence had come in, saying: "Wang Jingwei, Vice-Director-General of the Nationalist Party, is colluding with the enemy"—what then was to be done? As mere "intelligence-handling," a matter so grave, held in one's mind flatly an impossible thing, could not be dealt with by the ordinary procedure. Were I the man in charge, I would not have gone round again upon the words, referring it back to the reporting unit for re-examination; I would at once have summoned the reporter and questioned him face to face; and if his word had grounds, I ought, together with the reporter, to have gone at once before our superiors and made the oral report to their face, asking their instruction on the steps to be taken thereafter—that would have accorded with sense. Yet one point must be made clear: this so-called "having grounds" means solid proof; and only think, for a matter of this kind, to get solid proof, what a hard thing that would be.

The above speaks of the "operation," and is but a supposition; in fact no such situation ever arose at all.

Summing the result of our few men's talk: by what we knew, at that time not a single unit under the Juntong—including the Chongqing region and the intelligence organizations at Shanghai and Hong Kong—nor any one man, had gotten any word whatever of Wang Jingwei's colluding with the enemy and betraying the state; and even our highest chiefs, Mr. Dai Yunong and Mr. Zheng Jiemin, I believe, knew nothing at all of it. Besides what we few talked of at Hanoi, the writer later asked, in person, the old friends who had charge of intelligence at the Bureau headquarters of the time, and they too said they had never received any report of the kind. To go a step further: there was never a case of such intelligence being seen and then, because the matter was grave and one dared not lightly pass judgment, laid aside.

The fact is very plain: had it been known beforehand that Wang Jingwei had taken up dealings in collusion with Japan, and even that his colluding with the enemy state had shown itself in act, then all the historical traces written down thereafter would be wholly different.

Next let me speak of Wang Jingwei's breaking away from Chongqing. This is not a thing our few men at Hanoi touched on in private talk; but for the convenience of the reader's understanding, it will do no harm to bring the later matter forward:

The late Brother Wang Yunsun, who had once served in the China Aviation Bureau (when the writer was head of the "Beiping Station" in the twenty-second and twenty-third years, Brother Wang Yunsun was the secretary of the "Beiping Station"; he died in Taiwan many years ago. Whether Brother Yunsun held a concurrent post at the "Chongqing Aviation Inspection Office" I do not know; but he was on familiar terms with the men of the "Aviation Inspection Office," and had working ties with them), told me in his lifetime, to this effect:

"For a man of so high a standing as Wang Jingwei, his comings and goings would not, in the ordinary way, be interfered with; we had beforehand received no instruction or hint of any related matter, and afterward there was no move to call anyone to account; and as for a review, that was but the arranging of the course of things into a record.

"I remember it was about nine in the morning on the eighteenth of December when Chen Bijun, together with Zeng Zhongming and two others whose names were unknown—four in the party—reached the Shanhuba airfield; Wang Jingwei did not come with them. Thinking of it afterward, this was of course a deliberate arrangement; but to an outsider's eye, that some come first and some later is a common thing, with nothing worth marking.

"Chen Bijun, in the waiting-hall, kept looking out; now she would bend her head and whisper a few words with Zeng Zhongming, now gesture with her hands as if arranging something; she seemed very anxious—one guessed, of course, because she was awaiting Wang Jingwei to come the sooner.

"Only a few minutes before the plane took off did Wang Jingwei hurry up, attended by a single guard. The time set for takeoff had come, and it should have taken off; for some reason unknown it was again delayed several minutes—was it fueling? a mechanical fault? or waiting for some other person of note? it was never made clear.

"During the time of waiting for takeoff, before they had boarded, Chen Bijun still greeted acquaintances briskly, while Wang Jingwei was especially composed and paced with great strides. It is said Chen Bijun gave out to the airfield's management staff (not the flight-inspection men) that they were escorting Mr. Wang to give an address at the Central Military Academy at Chengdu, and would stay a day and return.

"Wang's party this time was six in all, and lingered within the airfield more than half an hour; in this time, had there been the least breath of rumor, then whatever measure were taken—even referring it up through the layers to our superiors—there would still have been time; but no one did so, because no one guessed there was mischief hid in them. And so Wang Jingwei and his party of six flew smoothly away from Chongqing."

The gist of the above passage is thus; it is the actual scene Brother Wang Yunsun saw with his own eyes at the time, and though he spoke of it only some years after, I think it cannot be far off the mark.

It was just because Wang left so easily, wholly unhindered, that there arose afterward the tale of "deliberate letting-go." And it was not mere talk: what has been set to paper and seen in books and journals is not little either. For instance, what Jin Xiongbai wrote under the pen name Zhu Zijia runs:

"…And in fact, Wang's being about to leave Yu was known not only to Zhou Fohai and the others who took part in the secret councils; Wang himself did not conceal it either, and high and low in the Wang house all knew that Wang, being for peace, had a plan to leave Yu.

"He waited until the middle of December, and then decided to seize the chance and set out on the eighteenth. That morning Wang gave a talk to the household servants, old Song and Ah Wang and the rest, and, at the last, again made plain his attitude and asked each their wish to go or stay; and all the servants of the whole house, down to Pan Jun and Ah Liu, said they were still willing to follow and go on serving Wang. So his secretaries and servants hastily took up their baggage, rode in four motorcars, set out by the highway, and went straight to Hanoi ahead of Wang.

"Wang's flight was beforehand no secret, and the plane tickets were booked two weeks in advance; in the Ministry of Communications, apart from Peng Xuepei (vice-minister of Communications), was there no other who knew of it? If others knew, then how, for so important a personage as Wang, could there be no reason to report it to the authorities? The airfield was thick with soldiers and police; Wang's appearing was seen by all eyes; had the authorities barred his going, then a single telegram would have sufficed, and within the three hours of the flight the plane could still have been ordered to turn back. In the twenty-four hours at Kunming, besides, Long Yun could have been ordered to detain him. Why, in so ample a span of time, was there no move at all?

"There is, besides, another side-proof. Before leaving Yu, Wang said to Chen Gongbo: 'If I am for peace at Chongqing, others will surely mistake it for the government's view, and this is of harm to the government. If I leave Chongqing, then it is my own private view. If the negotiation gives good terms, then the government may accept them.' (See Chen Gongbo's essay 'Recollections of Eight Years.') Seen by this, the meaning of the last two sentences is all too plain: that Wang should come forward to negotiate with Japan; if the terms were bad, Wang alone bore the blame; if good, the government would come forward to accept—might this not indeed be a two-man act played in concert?"

In these several decades past, those who have made such conjectures and held such a view are by no means one or two; by the writer's own contact alone, they are truly not few. Right down to this present day of the nineteen-eighties, though the truth of the matter was long since settled, there are still those who believe it without doubt and hold this view. In the winter of the seventieth year, at a small private gathering, there were among those present not a few figures of note in years past, while the writer, as the youngest, respectfully took the lowest seat; and there at the table was one who talked away with assurance, as though it were truly so. Is it not strange?

Someone once put it to the writer point-blank: "As to Wang's secret activities of suing for peace, did your Juntong really have not the least intelligence beforehand?" The writer answered straightforwardly: "By my own knowledge, it was indeed so; but I am only one of the cadre working staff of the Juntong, and of course I cannot have known everything." Though I said this, the hearer showed himself not much inclined to believe it.

Believe it or not, fact is fact. Next let me excerpt several passages from the "Full Record of Wang Jingwei's Breaking Away from Chongqing," for reference.

The essay "Full Record of Wang Jingwei's Breaking Away from Chongqing" was carried in the "Zhanggu" magazine published at Hong Kong, under the pen name "Yongwu," and appeared about July of the sixty-first year. Not only do I not know Mr. "Yongwu"; I do not even know this gentleman's true name and surname. Though I have examined and inquired, I still dare not say for sure who he is. Yet one point may be affirmed: his relation with Wang was by no means slight.

The following are the fragments from the essay that may serve for reference:

"On the fourteenth (August of the twenty-seventh year), in the forenoon I reached Chongqing; this afternoon I went to the new foreign-style house on the little hill opposite Shangqingsi to see Mr. Wang; it faces the Chuan River, and the scenery is very fine. Mr. Wang had come from Hankou to Yichang, and thence by a small gunboat to Chongqing; after we met, we talked briefly of the scenery of the journey, and, as he was preparing to give an address at the Police Officers' School the next day, it was not convenient to sit long.

"The nineteenth of August: Mr. Wang asked me to supper at his lodging; there came Zhang Pingqun, Gan Naiguang, Xu Jingwei, Zeng Zhongming, Lin Ruheng, Leng Jiesheng, and others; at table the talk was mostly of the scenery of Sichuan, and Mr. Wang was in very good spirits.

"The seventh of September: Mr. Wang invited me to supper; the guests were mostly diplomatic staff, and of the secretariat of the Executive Yuan only two, myself and Duanmu Kai; at table the talk came to the recent Assembly of the League of Nations and other questions bearing on the resistance; Mr. Wang held his wine well, and his spirits too were very high.

"The fourth of October: enemy planes raided Yu for the first time, dropping bombs near the Shangqingsi garden, about ten in the morning. After the alarm was lifted, I went to see Mr. Wang, and talked twenty minutes. Mr. Wang said that Sun Zhesheng, back from Europe, analyzed the European situation none too clearly, holding that Britain would go to war over the Czech question; and he said, too, that Jiang Tingfu's views on diplomacy had something original in them.

"The twenty-fifth of October: I telephoned Zeng Zhongming, and Mr. Wang himself answered, asking what news there was of the times; his voice sounded exceeding troubled.

"The twenty-eighth of November: at three in the afternoon I saw Mr. Wang, who suddenly presented me a recent large photograph, and took up his pen and signed it—I did not grasp what the intent was. In the evening Brother Pingqun and his betrothed gave a feast, which Mr. Wang also attended, and he drank very freely, seeming, too, a little out of his wont.

"The twelfth of December: at eight in the morning I went to the field headquarters to join the Memorial Week; many of the staff of the several organs had been ordered to attend. Chairman Lin presided, and the Generalissimo Chiang spoke, lasting an hour… As the meeting broke up, I saw Mr. Wang from afar come too to hear the address, dressed in a dark-blue Zhongshan tunic, which was also seldom seen.

"The eighteenth of December: in the forenoon I went to the field headquarters to hear the Generalissimo Chiang's special address; he came an hour late, for what reason I do not know.

"The twenty-first of December: Mr. Wang, on Sunday (the eighteenth), suddenly and secretly left Chongqing; only by yesterday did the news gradually spread abroad. This morning I went specially to the Central Party Headquarters to ask Brother Naiguang, and he too was at a loss. Two weeks ago my wife told me that our maidservant said the maidservants long employed at the Wang mansion had now all been dismissed, because Mr. Wang would soon go abroad and reside in Chongqing no more. When I heard these words at the time, I still held them groundless—little knowing they were in fact the truth. Yesterday Wei Bocong (Daoming) asked me whether Mr. Wang had reached Chengdu, and I could make no answer. This afternoon, toward evening, Mr. Wang's nephew Brother Yanci telephoned to ask me to number seventeen, Meizhuanxiao Street, Mr. Wang's lodging, to talk; and after we met, I learned that Madam Wang and Zeng Zhongming and Mr. Wang's private staff had all gone with Mr. Wang, and that he too would go to Kunming the next morning; save for a few guards, the whole household was left with him alone. When I asked him the reason for Mr. Wang's flight, he guessed it was because of a clash of views over the Communist question; what the substance was, he naturally could not tell. Yanci said, too, that Mr. Wang's move this time was in the strictest secret, known to but a few, and begged me not to let it out. In the gathering dusk we parted sorrowfully before the gate, wishing each other well, not knowing what day we should meet again.

"The twenty-third of December: in the forenoon I went again to the Shangqingsi garden to study with Brother Gan Naiguang the question of Mr. Wang's flight. By Brother Naiguang's view, Mr. Wang's flight was perhaps not only for a clash of views over the Communist question; for a year past Mr. Wang had held no power in the government, and none in the party either; though he held the place of Vice-Director-General, it was but an empty name, and in many important measures he was never consulted—this point, most likely, galled him most.

"The twenty-fourth of December: the news of Mr. Wang's flight was today openly published in the newspapers, but said that, traveling to Kunming, his old ailment had recurred, and he had gone to Hanoi for treatment and could not for a time return to Yu. It was reported yesterday that he had reached Hong Kong, but this too could not be confirmed.

"The twenty-seventh of December: again I talked with Brother Naiguang of the effect of Mr. Wang's leaving Chongqing, which for the time seemed still not to be seen clearly. By Brother Naiguang's account, Chen Shuren said Mr. Wang's flight had two great causes: one the Communist question, the other the question of peace talks with Japan. How much Chen Shuren knew of the inside of Mr. Wang's flight is a question; but that Mr. Wang was pessimistic about the resistance and for peace talks with Japan was indeed of long standing. When the government was still at Nanjing, the Wang mansion was already full of a pessimistic, defeatist air; Madam Wang and their children, in their talk, often took a mocking and sneering tone toward the resistance, and Mr. Wang seemed to show agreement with their words. For instance, when the war went ill, and the newspapers, instead of 'defeat and retreat,' said 'shifting to advance,' this was matter for laughter in the Wang mansion.

"The twenty-eighth of December: Brother Naiguang said that Mr. Chiang, on the anniversary of his escape from the Xi'an Incident (the twenty-fifth of December), gave a feast to the Central Committee members, and in the course of it made a talk, saying: the fall of Song and of Ming lost but a dynasty, not the nation; the Yuan and the Qing, though of alien race, entered and ruled China, yet were assimilated by China; the two dynasties of Song and Ming had military and economic strength more than enough to withstand outward calamity, only because a few men in power, their spirit cowed by the foreign foe, had soldiers yet could not use them, had the latent power to resist yet could not bring it forth. The present resistance is the resistance of the whole nation, and there is no dynasty to be lost; if our spirit can but be unmoved by the enemy's threat, we can set in motion men and matériel to sustain a long resistance, and so win final victory. These words were plainly aimed at Mr. Wang's arguments and his flight.

"The thirty-first of December: in the afternoon, a Reuters dispatch from Hong Kong—Mr. Wang has published a circular telegram advocating peace talks with Japan (the Yan Telegram), and it is in answer to the enemy premier Konoe Fumimaro's address of the twenty-second; this has stirred all quarters greatly. Among friends of the Executive Yuan, some said Mr. Wang's daring was truly no small thing, and some said peace talks could never come to pass, that Mr. Wang's move only showed his own private view and advocacy."

Mr. "Yongwu's" work is very long; of the matter of Wang's flight, I cite only to this point. The writer believes what Mr. "Yongwu" wrote is reasonable and just, all true and honest words.

The above—materials from every quarter—is all to prove that no such inconceivable thing could ever have happened. (End of this chapter.)

The "Hanoi work," which was in its origin the meeting of a chance emergency, had beforehand no full preparation, and at the time no fixed rule; this was, in truth and in name, an "emergency action."

To trail a quarry abroad is like flying blind: beside meeting the moment as it comes, there is only charging headlong.

Had there not been a certain special personage secretly helping with all his strength, not only would it have been hard to push the work forward, but we might well have knocked our noses flat and blacked our eyes; and who was this special personage? In the book it cannot be said outright, for the larger good must be kept whole. Yet between the lines there are traces enough to trace; mark a little, and one may guess eight or nine parts of it.

Just as our disposition was not yet complete, and we still sought an "inside line," rumor without spread thick that Wang Jingwei was about to leave Vietnam for elsewhere; the thing had its cause, and was no wind from an empty cave. A wire came from our superiors to verify it, and the upshot was but "the stair heard creaking, and no one coming down." Had he heeded the counsel and made his tour of Europe then, this whole case, I suppose, would have ended there, and would not have grown a branch outside the knot, or the worse turned worse. Which shows that the lust of power most bewitches the heart's cavity—the more so with a meddling busybody at his side making mischief.

Thereafter our superiors picked and dispatched a number of able working comrades, who reached Hanoi in batches by sea, land, and air; and what gave us a start was that they brought a great quantity of arms and ammunition. Mark it well: this did not mean that any resolute decision had already been made; it was a first move that Mr. Dai had laid down beforehand of his own accord. He instructed plainly: "It is hoped you will make the needful dispositions; but before an order is received, on no account act rashly."
