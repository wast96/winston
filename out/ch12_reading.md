## A Letter from Afar

New Year's Eve, in the morning. Chen Qianli turned off beside Chengzhong Middle School into Xuejiabang Road. Small peddlers had set out a whole row of ground-stalls along the wall, and Chen Qianli, for all the world like a man with nothing to do, kept stopping to look about him. Across from the gate of the Xiahai Temple, at the Maohai Road corner, a gong clanged beneath a telegraph pole: someone had brought out a monkey that tumbled and turned somersaults on the ground, and in no time a crowd had gathered round. He worked his way into the press, shouldering left and right, and came out the other side of it soon enough, having shaken off the tail behind him.

He went into a secondhand-clothes shop, and came out with a gray felt homburg in place of his hat. That morning he had put on, on purpose, a conspicuous russet-brown hat and scarf. “Give them one striking feature first, and their attention will fix on it.” So his instructor had said in training. He crossed the road at one side of the gate of Tilanqiao Prison and turned into a little street paved with granite chips.

On the north side of the street ran a row of red-brick houses, dormer windows opening to the morning sun in their roofs; thick smoke rolled up at the roadside, where an old foreign woman with a kerchief round her head crouched by a coal-ball stove, a palm-leaf fan in her hand. An old man pushed open a door and came out, clutching a liquor bottle wrapped in old newspaper, a large and dubious stain on his little orange skullcap. He came out furtively — very likely hoping to seize the chance to slip away — but the old woman spotted him at a glance and at once set up a squalling.

Chen Qianli checked the number on the door, and was just about to go up the steps when the old woman suddenly broke off her cursing and watched him warily.

“I'm looking for Chen Qianyuan,” Chen Qianli told her.

“The tingzijian,” the old woman brought out suddenly, in Shanghainese.

Chen Qianyuan had not expected that the man who knocked and came in would be his own brother, still less that his brother was the very comrade sent down from above to make contact, of whom Old Yi had spoken. This elder brother with whom he had once shared every day and night — the lean frame had filled out and grown strong, faint lines had come at the corners of the eyes, and at a glance he seemed another man altogether.

“Are Father and Mother both well?” Chen Qianli asked, looking at his younger brother.

Chen Qianyuan's eyes moistened. “They're both well. After you left, the organization moved them back to the old home in the country.”

“A good many foreign residents live hereabouts?”

“These past years, a lot of foreigners have come to Shanghai and settled together in this quarter.”

“Mm. That's a kind of cover, at least.”

“Though there's no place in Shanghai now that's truly safe.”

Chen Qianli was silent a long while, then let out a long breath. “Nekrasov — do you still read him, these years?”

Chen Qianyuan started, straightened up, and recited a line: “They say the storm is coming — and I cannot help but smile.”

This was their own private countersign; for a time they had loved to use the line to prove their youth and their ardor. Every time Chen Qianli came home from the Russian tutorial class and knocked late at night, the two of them would exchange it through the door. After Qianyuan moved into the dormitory at Chengzhong Middle School and came home each weekend, they exchanged it still, each saying half the line, no matter who began. No, Chen Qianli said to himself — not the countersign of two, but of three; there had been Ye Tao as well.

They say the storm is coming, and I cannot help but smile. Of course he remembered the poem; he had once written it out, neat and careful, with a brush on a sheet of Duoyunxuan letter-paper, and given it to Ye Tao. The paper was printed with a spray of peach blossom.

“Where did you go?” It was Qianyuan speaking.

The training school had once been the manor of some old aristocrat; standing at the wire fence at the edge of the grounds and looking out, you saw the Siberian forest stretching away without end. Chen Qianli had lived there three years.

Once winter came, when the day's training was done he would get through the long endless nights on Nekrasov's poems. Sitting by the stove, he read them aloud, recited them, or turned them over in silence, until his head was full of their sound, until the figures of Ye Tao and his brother rose out of memory.

Chen Qianli felt a little dazed, a softness in his heart, a feeling that had not come to him in a long time. He held it in check and slowly turned his mind to other things. He looked about him: the room was swept and neat, not the Qianyuan he remembered — he remembered Qianyuan's room as always in a mess, yet now the clothes hung tidily on their rack, and there was a red scarf, too. Was it his?

“That day you came back from Nanjing and told me that Sister Ye Tao had given her life. You said you'd come to see me, to say a few words, and would leave at once — that they would come to arrest you.”

For Chen Qianli knew they would say that he had killed Ye Tao, would set the Municipal Police to arrest him; they knew where he and his brother lived in Shanghai. In the last moment of her life Ye Tao had told him to go at once and find the Party organization; it was the organization that had sent him back to Shanghai to find a certain man and carry a message to him. That man had told Chen Qianli: the organization has decided — you are to set out for the Soviet Union at once. Later he learned who the man was: Comrade Shaoshan.

He had given his brother all the money he could lay hands on, saying that he would be away for a time, gone far off, that men would come to arrest him, and that his brother had best move house too, and rent a room somewhere else.

“I went to the Soviet Union,” he said.

“The day after you left, the Municipal Police came. They said you'd killed a man in Nanjing, that you were a wanted Communist, and they carried off all your things. I wanted to get that book of poems back — do you remember? — the one with Sister Ye Tao's portrait tucked inside it. A pencil sketch; all three of us were there when it was drawn. The man who drew it said he could sketch a person with a single line, and get a good likeness too, and so he did. They took the picture away. Everything to do with her vanished, as though there had never been such a person at all.”

“Of course there was,” said Chen Qianli, smiling.

“But to this day I still don't understand how it happened — why did she die? Back then you told me it was because of her father. You said that one day you'd settle accounts with him.”

But just now Chen Qianli did not want to speak of it; he did not want to remember — perhaps the time when he could remember had not yet come.

“And Old Fang? Have you seen Old Fang? Was it he who sent you to find me?”

“Old Fang is dead,” Chen Qianli said calmly. “His son was taken by the agents too.”

Chen Qianyuan was just then about to put the book on the table back on the shelf. It slipped from his hand and fell, knocking over a teacup; Chen Qianli reached out and caught the book, and set it back on the shelf.

“Old Yi told me the news when we made contact. He'd learned of it through an inside source.” Nor did Chen Qianli tell his brother that he himself had been there at the time.

“There must be an enemy mole inside the Party organization,” Chen Qianyuan said, voicing his own conclusion.

“A temporary action group secretly thrown together is usually mixed in its makeup,” Chen Qianli said, looking at his brother.

“Then the man is among us?”

Chen Qianli was just about to speak —

Someone opened the door with a key: it was Dong Huiwen. She had never met this visitor, nor he her. But she knew who he was — Qianyuan's brother. She had a memory for faces she had seen, and she had seen him countless times in a photograph.

“This is Huiwen.”

Chen Qianli smiled at her. Chen Qianyuan told her that his brother was the comrade sent from above to make contact with Yi Junnian. They shook hands. Her face was rounder than Ye Tao's, and she was not so tall. She taught in a primary school — Chen Qianli recalled what Old Fang had told him.

Dong Huiwen set an embroidered cloth bag on the table and took out gauze, cotton wool and ointment, and cleaned Chen Qianyuan's wound afresh, dressed it, then bound it up with the gauze, listening quietly to the two of them talk.

“They wanted to know who was acquainted with whom. That You Tianxiao seemed to know that these comrades had all been called together on short notice. Later they asked me whether Old Yi was the man sent from above to convey the task. I thought: this is bad, something has surely gone wrong on the inside. Luckily the agents came in before the meeting had begun. Thank heaven someone jumped from the building to give the warning. Do you know who the comrade was who gave his life?”

Chen Qianli shook his head. There was intelligence that the man who had fallen from the building that day was a Chinese constable of the Settlement police. From one report and another, Old Fang had judged him to be one of their own. Before the enemy ran him down, Old Fang had reported the matter to his superiors, asking who the man was, a comrade of which line; but he had had no answer back. Perhaps the man had taken orders to lie deep undercover for a long time, and very few knew of him. Perhaps in that long concealment he had lost touch with his superiors, the comrades of his group had fallen, and his line of work had been cut. Such things happened often enough.

The trouble lies on the inside — Chen Qianyuan said it again, as if to himself, seemingly still in the grip of the shock the conclusion had dealt him. Chen Qianli watched his brother. When Old Fang had given him the list of those at the meeting, he had been a little surprised. His memory of his brother broke off in that summer; he had never thought that the boy too would grow up, still less that Qianyuan, like himself, would be forced to ripen fast under the pressure of a cruel struggle. Only now, the two of them face to face, did he realize that the year he left Shanghai he had been about the age Qianyuan was now. Qianyuan was just as he had been then: he saw the danger, he faced the danger, and yet he could not truly grasp the danger. How simple his own ideas had been in those days.

“— We got a message out to the organization, from the lockup.” It was Dong Huiwen speaking, his brother's sweetheart. Qianyuan was the very image of himself: the same age, a sweetheart of the same age, the same ardor kindling higher in the bitter cold. He listened quietly, and all at once felt that something was not right.

“— Sister Ling said we had to report it to the higher-ups.”

“Haohan?”

“Yes. That fellow You suddenly lost his temper and asked me whether I knew where Haohan was. Of course I knew who Haohan was. The enemy is hunting Comrade Haohan — that was news the organization had to be told.”

“You mean the secret letter you wrote to Old Fang mentioned Haohan as well?”

“It was Sister Ling who wrote the letter. Time was very short; Miss Tao was about to be released, the warder just outside the door telling her to gather her things quickly, and she had no small heap of them.

“Sister Ling decided to set about the letter at once. We'd agreed we must seize this chance to pass the news out. Sister Ling understood such things; we had long since made ready the rice-water for the secret writing. With the warder hurrying us from outside the door, I pretended to help Miss Tao sort her clothes, to drag out the time as long as I could.

“I had no chance to see the letter, but Sister Ling said she had put the dice and the names into it.”

The letter had been switched by the enemy. Chen Qianli understood at once.

“When did this happen?”

“The day after the interrogation. Before I was taken up for questioning, the warder had already called Miss Tao out; during the interrogation I heard a woman laughing outside the window, and I thought it was Miss Tao's voice. She got into a motorcar and drove off. That night, the moment she came back into the cell, she told us she'd soon be let out.

“I think it was from then that Sister Ling began to turn it over in her mind; she was very decisive. After that she talked with Miss Tao without letup — I could see she was working to grow closer to her, and I helped her at it. Miss Tao mightn't have seemed a respectable sort, but she had a strong sense of honor, and she promised that once out she'd pass our letter on for us. All she'd have to do was post it into a certain letter-box, Sister Ling told her. That made it easier still, Miss Tao said.”

“Why did the Judge Advocate's office arrest her?”

“It seems she'd offended someone. She said it was a man who ran a bank — and never mind that he was only a bank boss, the backing behind him was enough to frighten you to death. At first she wouldn't go on, and Sister Ling goaded her, until at last she said the bank boss's elder brother was a great official in Nanjing, that even the Ministry of Finance was as good as run by their family. She said the bank boss had taken a fancy to her, and she'd taken up with him; but then she'd wanted to marry him — she only wanted to be a concubine, she said — and he flatly refused.

“Then she tried to force his hand — said she was pregnant, said she'd expose the whole thing in the newspapers — and so they had her thrown into the lockup. They only wanted to bring me to my senses, was how she put it. And very soon she was let out.”

Dong Huiwen glanced at the calendar pasted on the wall by the table. “I remember she said, all pleased, that letting her out on a Saturday must have been worked out on purpose, because the bank boss usually came to her on Sundays. So — that would have been the nineteenth of the last month.”

On the evening of the eighteenth of the last month, his superiors had sent a man disguised as a visitor to find him aboard the ship at Qingdao, to have him turn aside to Shanghai and take up a new assignment; the visitor had told him there was word that the arrested comrades were about to be released.

That letter was switched by the enemy, Chen Qianli thought; very likely they had posted men around the private box at the post office, meaning to seize whatever comrade came to collect it, and had failed. That they altered the contents of the secret letter showed they did not want Old Fang to know the letter had mentioned Haohan.

That Old Fang would speak to the other comrades of the matter of the dice was because it was the recognition sign; once the meeting began, all who came to it would know “Old Kai.” But why should the agents press these comrades for news of Comrade Haohan? Were the agents who went to the Fourth Avenue market the same lot who went to Pu'enjishi Road to seize Haohan? Had they recognized that the man who fired on Pu'enjishi Road to warn Haohan was Old Fang? The matter of Comrade Haohan was of the first importance; he must see “Old Kai” as soon as he could.

Just how many people knew the address of that barbershop? Did Cui Wentai know it? He was the courier; Old Fang trusted him, they met often, and secrets slip out unawares. Yet if something were wrong with him, Old Fang would surely have been arrested long before.

“What day did you come out of the lockup?”

“Last Wednesday,” Chen Qianyuan answered.

“No — the day of the release was a Tuesday, the twenty-second of the last month.” Dong Huiwen recalled it carefully. “Sister Ling and I came out in the morning, you in the afternoon; and afterward Cui Wentai even drove over to bring you wound-medicine — Liang Shichao had told him you'd been put to torture, that your injuries were bad.”

After Old Fang was killed, the enemy released them. In Chen Qianli's mind there was a line of time; he thought over what had happened, and beneath every surface appearance lay the enemy's thinking. He set these things into that line of time and weighed his opponent's intent.

“You've been in touch often these last few days?”

“We decided to set up a provisional Party branch — until we find our superiors, we're to organize ourselves first,” Chen Qianyuan told his brother.

“Whose idea was that?”

“Tian Fei said he'd talked it over with Cui Wentai some time before,” Chen Qianyuan said. “The day of the release they put it to us, and we all agreed heartily.”

“Who is in the provisional branch?”

“Myself, Huiwen, Ling Wen — Sister Ling — Old Yi, Tian Fei,” Chen Qianyuan counted on his fingers, “Cui Wentai, Dr. Qin, and Lin Shi and Liang Shichao.”

“And the other two?”

“Who?”

“There were two more who went to the market that day. They've not joined the provisional branch?”

“One comrade can't be reached; the other — Old Yi says he's wavered a little, and means to find time for a talk with him.” Chen Qianyuan remembered something. “Last night Sister Ling let us know there's to be a meeting of the provisional branch today. Now that the higher-ups have sent you, won't you go, and tell everyone what we should do next?”

“When?”

“This evening, at seven.”

Chen Qianli remembered what Wei Dafu had told him. “I hear the comrades are somewhat on edge — something has happened at the clinic?”

“They've taken Lin Shi in hand.” Chen Qianyuan turned to Dong Huiwen. “You tell it — Sister Ling, when she told Huiwen about the meeting, told her all that had happened at the clinic besides. Old Yi says this meeting simply must be held.”

“Where is the meeting to be?”

“At the clinic itself; it's very safe there,” said Dong Huiwen. “Sister Ling says the consulting rooms are up over the lane archway, a whole row of windows with a fine wide view, so you can see any stir in the street. The front and back buildings are well hidden, both with back doors, and there's a rooftop terrace — plenty of ways to withdraw.”

“Have you been there?”

“I went with Qianyuan; Dr. Qin looked at his wound and wrote out a prescription.”

Chen Qianli pressed her. “Did Sister Ling say who it was that seized Lin Shi and questioned him?”

“Sister Ling said Tian Fei from the library was the most hot-headed. Sister Ling happened to go to the clinic that morning —”

“What did she go to the clinic for?”

“I don't know, she didn't say — perhaps to look in on the wounded comrade. She saw that Dr. Qin was knitting his brows, all care and gloom — that was her phrase, care and gloom. When she heard what was afoot, Sister Ling said, leave it to me. That's the sort she is: leave everything to her. She went over to stop them — you can't do that, you can't go suspecting a comrade for no reason. That fellow named Tian —”

“Tian Fei,” Chen Qianli prompted her.

“Yes, Tian Fei. Sister Ling said he was the most hot-headed, dead set against letting Lin Shi loose —”

“Loose?”

“They'd tied Lin Shi to a chair.”

Anxiety and suspicion were one thing; a bank safe-deposit box was another. Chen Qianli realized that on that line of time in his mind, the enemy was running faster than he. He would have to think of something in a very short space. He guessed the enemy had long since had the clinic under watch — that a great many agents might well lie in ambush around it.

He had a dim sense that someone was shadowing him, too. The enemy had not released these comrades at all; they had only moved them from a visible prison into an invisible one. This invisible prison was more dangerous than the Longhua lockup: the enemy without was hard to make out, and the enemy within harder still to tell apart.

Dong Huiwen went downstairs to boil tangyuan, and Chen Qianli picked up a sheaf of manuscript from the table.

“I'm practicing my translation,” Chen Qianyuan said.

On the first page of the manuscript a title was written in pen: First Letter: The First Stage of the First Revolution.

“Letters from Afar?”

It was the first Russian work Chen Qianli had ever read. The Russian tutorial class. A mimeographed journal with yellowing pages. He had taken it to his teacher, Ye Qinian.

In those days he went every day to Xinzha Road, where Ye Qinian lived. A lane house; downstairs was a magazine office, and in the evenings an Esperanto study group met there too.

In those days Teacher Ye was still a scholar, and a believer in anarchism. In those days Chen Qianli worshipped him; Teacher Ye was a figure like a star, voluble, brimming with passion. His house was forever full of distinguished guests, and he, for his part, had always been fond of Chen Qianli. In those days Ye Tao would now and then come downstairs and stand quietly by, listening.

He brought Letters from Afar to Teacher Ye and, all excitement, showed it to him — and it proved, against all his expectation, the beginning of the rift between master and pupil. Don't read those Russian books, they're of no use; the world of the future will have but one language. Such differences grew, little by little, more and more numerous.

Later, even his going up to Ye Tao's side room became a difficulty. First Teacher Ye put on a stern face and said to him quietly: you two are no longer the children you once were; you mustn't be forever running up to her. Then he took Ye Tao to task; and later still he declared to him that he was never again to set foot inside the house on Xinzha Road. Yet not many days after, Ye Tao came to see him.

He and Teacher Ye drifted further and further apart. In those days he could never quite tell the one thing from the other; he always supposed the change in Teacher Ye sprang from some obsessive feeling — a father refusing to let him come near his daughter.
