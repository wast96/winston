### Gunshots off Avenue Joffre (Part 2)

### The Relentless Pursuit of the Traitor Bai Xin

When word got out that Peng Pai and the four others had been cruelly killed, it stirred grief through the whole Party. On behalf of the Party Center, Zhou Enlai at once drafted a proclamation to the people titled "Answer Counter-Revolution's Butchery with the Revolutionary Struggle of the Masses," laying bare in fury the crimes of the Kuomintang reactionaries in slaughtering Peng Pai and the others, mourning with a heavy heart the martyrs who had given their lives for the revolution, and calling on the people to learn from the martyrs' noble character and to answer counter-revolution's butchery with deeds. At that time Peng Pai's mother, old Mrs. Zhou, was drifting in exile in Macau.

Zhou Enlai at once sent people to help the old woman move to Shanghai, to comfort and look after her, and saw to the safe keeping of Peng Hong, the young son who had been at Peng Pai's side. Ke Lin has said: "When Peng Pai was working in Shanghai, his wife Xu Yuqing lived there with him. They had a boy, called Peng Xiaopei. After Peng Pai fell, Xu Yuqing held to the revolution and asked the Party Center to send her back to Hailufeng to work; the Center granted her request. Before she left she placed her own flesh and blood, little Pei, in a nursery run by the Party's central organs under the name of the Catholic Church, in the care of Li Lisan's wife. After Gu Shunzhang defected to the enemy, little Peng Xiaopei too was lost without a trace. Xu Yuqing was later killed by the enemy in the Danan Mountains of Guangdong, a heroic death. From 1931 to 1935, while I was in Hong Kong, Peng Pai's mother Zhou Feng often came to my house with Peng Pai's son Peng Shilu to help with small chores. In those days the Party organization gave them a living allowance every month."

The killing of Peng Pai and the four others stirred everyone all the more to a boundless hatred of the traitor Bai Xin, and the watch on Bai Xin was tightened. In his guise as the Kuomintang Central Organization Department's Investigation Section special commissioner resident in Shanghai, Yang Dengying found his lodging and talked with him, asking what plans he still had, what leads. From Bai Xin's own mouth he learned at once that the man meant to go to Nanjing, sell the Party's secrets, and come back to do the Party great harm. Chen Geng then had Yang Dengying keep close watch on Bai Xin's movements — where he was each morning, what he did each afternoon, what he was up to at night — and report in time, so as to guard against the traitor's leaving Shanghai.

At this time the Kuomintang reactionaries deliberately laid a smokescreen, doing all they could to cover the truth that Bai Xin had informed on and sold out Peng Pai, and to shield this traitor. On September 14, 1929, when the Shanghai Kuomintang organ Minguo Ribao carried the news of the shooting of Peng Pai, Yang Yin, Yan Changyi, and Xing Shizhen, it deliberately ran alongside it another item saying that Bai Xin "was once a graduate of the Whampoa academy, and having earlier been duped into wrongly falling in with the Communists, his offense being the lighter, has been vouched for and released on the responsibility of Chairman Chiang (Chiang Kai-shek was then chairman of the Nanjing National Government — author's insertion), and was taken to Nanjing the day before yesterday, there to earn merit and redeem his guilt and prove himself henceforth."

Bai Xin did make one trip to Nanjing, but had long since returned to Shanghai.

As told earlier, Ke Lin at that time went by the assumed name Ke Dawen and, using his physician's calling as cover, together with He Cheng — who went by the name He Yusheng — ran the "Dasheng Hospital" on Sichuan Road in Shanghai to screen the activity of the CCP central organs.

Ke Lin had known Bai Xin for years; his medical skill was high, and Bai Xin, trusting it, often came to the Dasheng Hospital for treatment and felt kindly toward Ke Lin. But Bai Xin did not know Ke Lin's true identity, still less the truth about the hospital. Chen Geng and Li Qiang both knew of this connection between Bai Xin and Ke Lin, and once it was established that the traitor who had sold out Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and the others was Bai Xin, it occurred to them that Bai Xin, then suffering from a head ailment, would likely come to Ke Lin for treatment, and that here was the best chance to take him.

On the morning after Peng Pai's arrest, Guan Xiangying came to Ke Lin's house and told him of Bai Xin's betrayal of Comrade Peng Pai. He told Ke Lin too: Bai Xin has a head ailment just now, and will likely come to you for treatment; you should be prepared. Before long Chen Geng came and told Ke Lin: I have posted a liaison man near your hospital; if anything comes up, go to him. Chen Geng also left the number of his own room at the New World Hotel; whether or not there was anything to report, Ke Lin was to go to the hotel every night.

About ten days on, when Peng Pai and the others had already been killed, Bai Xin turned up at Ke Lin's for treatment, dressed to the nines in Western suit and leather shoes and full of swagger, two bodyguards with him. Ke Lin treated him with one part of his mind while turning over with the other how to alert the liaison man; when he had finished he pretended to go downstairs in search of a medicine and slipped out the back to find the man. By the time he came back, Bai Xin had already made off. That evening, when Ke Lin went to the hotel to report, Chen Geng told him to keep the shop open as usual the next day: "If you hear shots, run at once." He then sent the Red Squad's Tan Zhongyu and Wang Deming to the neighborhood of Ke Lin's surgery and laid careful plans to seize Bai Xin. Every day they lay in wait there, waiting a whole week, and Bai Xin never showed his face. Chen Geng reckoned Bai Xin might, fearing trouble, ask Ke Lin to come to his house on a call.

Sure enough, another two weeks on, Bai Xin telephoned to ask Ke Lin to come and treat him at the White Palace Hotel in the French Concession. When Ke Lin saw to him there, Bai Xin's wife and bodyguards were on hand, and so was his protector, Fan Zhengbo.

Of how he treated Bai Xin this time, Ke Lin has said: "I reached the White Palace Hotel and treated Bai Xin as usual. Bai asked me, 'Who among your acquaintances has come to you for treatment lately?' I said, 'Very few.' I turned it back on him: 'Do you want to see them?' He said, 'I do.' When I had finished this time, he gave me a good deal of money, and a letter of thanks besides, all tucked into my medicine case. Coming out of the White Palace Hotel, I waited at home until it was pitch dark, and when I saw that night had fallen I took a car at once to Chengdu Road — or was it Xinzha Road — and found Gu Shunzhang (who was later to defect to the enemy), the head of our 'Dog-Beating Squad,' and Chen Geng, and reported how the meeting with Bai Xin that day had gone. They told me to hold my nerve and treat Bai Xin as before, reporting anything as it came up. From the way things then stood, we reckoned Bai Xin would come to the hospital again for treatment."

So Chen Geng decided to rent, for the time being, a two-story Western house in the French Concession as Ke Lin's residence. The ground floor was a Frenchman's home, the second floor Ke Lin's quarters, and the third the place where the Central Special Section's "Red Squad" would lie in wait. With the trap thus set, the Section waited only for Bai Xin to take the bait: the moment he appeared, they would finish him. But a stretch of time passed with no sign of Bai Xin's shadow.

Another two weeks on, Bai Xin telephoned from the residence of Fan Zhengbo on Avenue Joffre (today Central Huaihai Road) in the French Concession, where he was staying, to ask Ke Lin to come and treat him. When Ke Lin came back he reported what he had learned to Gu Shunzhang and Chen Geng: Fan Zhengbo's residence stood at No. 43 in the fourth lane of Hehe Fang on Avenue Joffre; the brothers Fan Zhengbo and Fan Zhengluo lived on the second floor and Bai Xin on the third. Chen Geng had men rent two rooms in the last house on Bai Xin's lane and on the third floor of No. 27, hard against Bai Xin's lodging, and set a watch there. The back door of No. 27 was diagonally across from the front door of No. 43, and from this commanding height one could see with perfect clarity every movement inside No. 43. There the Section's men kept close watch on Bai Xin, ready to act at any moment.

As for Bai Xin, he knew well how hard the Red Squad came down on traitors, and felt keenly how perilous his own position was, his life scarcely safe. He begged his masters to approve his fleeing to Italy to wait out the danger, his departure fixed for November 11, while giving out that he was bound for Nanjing, to throw people off the scent. Because Bai Xin had once been secretary of the Central Military Commission and knew many of the Party's secrets, and because after selling out Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and the others he was still working to wreck the Party — the very picture of unpardonable evil — he could on no account be let escape the punishment he had earned. Zhou Enlai decided to have him put to death on the day he set out. Having heard Chen Geng's report, Zhou Enlai went over the plan for this operation once more with Gu Shunzhang, Chen Geng, and the others, and to make sure it went without a hitch he went himself into the Hehe Fang lane where Bai Xin lived to look over the ground. Seeing that the Hehe Fang where Bai Xin lay hidden had its front door on Avenue Joffre and its back door onto Rue Bourgeat, both under patrolmen's guard, he weighed it against the intelligence of the past days and worked out a careful plan of action, which he handed to the Special Section to carry out.

He also had the Special Section rent, at Hehe Fang, a shopfront room whose front door opened on the main street and whose back door gave onto the lane, so that if the mouth of the lane should be blocked by the enemy, they could withdraw this way.

### The Traitor Falls Under the Red Squad's Guns

Very soon our side had a complete grasp of the pattern of Bai Xin's movements. We knew beforehand not only the time he would set out and the steamer he would take, but such details as Fan Zhengbo sending a car to see Bai Xin off to the dock. Before Bai Xin left, the enemy's guard was tight: besides strengthening the watch at Fan Zhengbo's house, they had armed patrolmen on the round at the two entrances of Hehe Fang. On the day of his departure the people at Bai Xin's lodging came and went in great tension, and a servant was seen buying back a great deal of fruit — proof that the intelligence was sound. But Chen Geng was still a little uneasy. Just before the strike he sent Yang Dengying specially to No. 43 to make sure whether Bai Xin was still at Fan Zhengbo's at that moment. As it happened, Chen Lifu's confidant Zhang Daofan was about to go to Fan Zhengbo's to make contact with Bai Xin, and asked Yang Dengying along; Yang Dengying went with him, saw Bai Xin at Fan Zhengbo's house, and made certain that Bai Xin was indeed leaving Shanghai that night. On learning all this, Chen Geng decided to act on the plan as laid.

That afternoon, under the direction of Gu Shunzhang and Chen Geng, the Red Squad's Tan Zhongyu, Shao Dafu (also known as Shao Fumin), Wang Deming, Zhao Yifan, Wu Lanfu, Chen Yongjia, Zuo Guangyu, and others lay in wait in the lanes around No. 43. Past ten at night, Fan Zhengbo's car was seen to drive into the lane, and his bodyguard Han Yunxiu went to the Suzhou Motor Company on Avenue Joffre and hired a car, number 6730. Both cars were parked at the back gate of Hehe Fang on Rue Bourgeat, and before long the bodyguards and servants were carrying the luggage out to load. Toward eleven o'clock Bai Xin came out the back door of No. 43 — in dark blue Western trousers, a gray serge gown over them, the black leather shoes on his feet polished to a shine — accompanied by the Fan brothers and by Wang Rongchuan, who had come to see him off, with the bodyguards and the rest behind him, seven in all, and made for where the cars stood. Just as they reached the mouth of the fifth east lane, the Red Squad men burst suddenly out of the dark of the opposite lane. A great shout of "Don't move!" — and the Red Squad men fired straight at Bai Xin. Because Bai Xin's party was large the first shot missed, and the traitor took to his heels, trying to break clear. The bodyguard Han Yunxiu drew and returned fire, but before his round left the barrel he was hit by the Red Squad men, a bullet entering at the right temple and boring through his skull, and he fell dead on the spot. The rest scrambled for their lives; in the wild firing Fan Zhengbo took three rounds, two of them — one driven through the chest from behind, one entering at the waist — leaving him down and gravely wounded; Fan Zhengbo's brother Fan Zhengluo took six, a bullet in the belly, and died where he stood; Wang Rongchuan, hit in the left foot, dropped to the ground and played dead. The gate patrolmen Lin Hanchen and Wang Dingyuan came running to stop it; Lin was shot dead on the spot by the Red Squad men, and Wang fled with his arms over his head. Bai Xin now ran north for his life, drawing his own pistol and firing back. The Red Squad men tracked him hard and at last shot the arch-criminal traitor dead at the foot of the door of No. 71, "a bullet boring from his forehead through the back of his head, the brains bursting out," so that he pitched to the earth and died at once. Having killed Bai Xin, the Red Squad withdrew from the scene at once. The fight had lasted a good while, and the Red Squad had fired in all more than ninety rounds. But by the Shanghai papers' report, "About an hour after the affair, the police station first sent detectives to inspect the ground"; by the time the patrolmen and detectives came up, the Red Squad was long gone, without a shadow or a trace. On November 12 the Shanghai English-language paper The China Press reported this "street battle" in a prominent place, saying that the men who carried out the attack "all escaped, leaving not the slightest trace of their movements behind."

By this time some two months and more had passed since Peng Pai and the others were killed. The news of this heart-gladdening suppression of a traitor shook Shanghai at once. The Concession authorities and the Kuomintang reactionaries were stricken with dread. At first they did all they could to keep the news from spreading, forbidding the papers to print anything about it; the day after the affair, only the Concession's foreign-language papers reacted strongly, while every Chinese paper that "reported this news left a blank window, keeping nothing but the headline." Thus the top item on the front page of the second sheet, seventh section, of the Shi Bao of November 12 kept only the headline "Assassination Case on Avenue Joffre Last Night," and an item on page fourteen of the fourth section of the Shen Bao kept only the headline "Ruffians' Ambush Last Night." But paper can never wrap fire, nor could the Kuomintang cover the sky with its hand; by the third day (November 13) the several dozen Chinese and foreign papers published in Shanghai burst the floodgates at last like a torrent and reported in full detail how the traitor Bai Xin had been suppressed, many of them writing it up vividly and putting the news in a prominent place. Some ran it under great red headlines, calling it "the East's one great assassination case."

Though the Kuomintang papers ran the case at endless length and wrote up the details vividly, none of them dared breathe a word of its political character. Only the "true account of the Avenue Joffre assassination case of the night before last" published by the Shi Bao of November 13 let slip a little of the case's "political background." The paper's reporter wrote:

Toward ten o'clock the night before last, an assassination case occurred within Hehe Fang on Avenue Joffre in the French Concession, leaving five dead and three wounded, one gravely, two lightly. We set down here, in order, what our inquiries have gathered:

At No. 43 in the fourth lane of Hehe Fang on Avenue Joffre lived Fan Zhengbo, standing committee member of the Shanghai Special Municipal Party Branch (this year twenty-eight), who went daily to the branch on business and kept his own car. Fan worked hard at the branch and often dealt severely with the Communists and their like for their wrecking of the revolution, and so drew much resentment and fear; there were often threatening reports laid against him, which Fan brushed off with a laugh. Toward ten the night before last, after Fan had dined at home, two visitors — a Mr. Bai (said to be Bai Xin) and a Mr. Wang — came calling, and they talked with pleasure. Toward eleven the guests all took their leave, and Fan, with his younger brother Zhengluo (this year twenty-two) and others, saw them out the door.

Fan, Bai, and the rest, seven in all — Wang first, Fan's bodyguard next, then Bai — went out and reached the mouth of the fifth east lane, when seven or eight assassins darted from the dark of the opposite lane and opened fire. Fan's bodyguard Han Yunxiu drew and returned fire, but before his round left the barrel he took several bullets, hit in the head, and fell dead at once. The Fan brothers too were hit one after another and went down at the sound, the bullets in chest and belly. Their friend Mr. Wang (said to be named Rongchuan), hit in the left foot, was in no great danger. The lane gatekeeper-patrolman Lin Hanchen, hearing the noise, came running and was shot dead by the south wall of the fifth west lane, while Wang got clear to one side in time. After the men had fallen, Fan's friend and Mr. Bai broke away to the north, drawing their own pistols and firing back, but against such numbers they were overborne and shot dead by the ruffians at the foot of the door of No. 71, "a bullet boring from the forehead through the back of the head, the brains bursting out," so that they pitched to the earth and died. In all the ruffians fired better than ninety rounds.

About an hour after the affair, the police station first sent detectives hurrying to inspect the ground, and had the wounded taken to hospital. Fan Zhengbo was sent to the Guangci Hospital, Fan Zhengluo and the patrolman Lin and Mr. Wang to the Renji Hospital. The patrolman Lin, hit in the head, died the moment he reached the hospital (note: an earlier passage says "the patrolman Lin too was shot dead by the south wall of the fifth west lane" — the two do not agree); Fan Zhengluo, gravely hurt, with six bullets in him, lingered until yesterday morning and died there too.

Yesterday morning Mayor Zhang, Commissioner Pan, Commissioner Chen, and party officials such as Zhu Yingpeng went to the Guangci Hospital (to see Fan Zhengbo), and were courteously turned away by the doctors there. By the doctors' account: Fan took three rounds, two of them — one driven through the chest from behind, one entering at the side of the waist — the bullets not yet taken out, the wounds fairly grave; within these three or four days, should the inside of the chest not turn septic, there may be hope. The one course for now is to keep Fan lying still and not let him be moved, for he has lost much blood and his temperature is not too high.

The station's men, meanwhile, had the several dead conveyed to the Tongren Fuyuantang morgue, where yesterday afternoon, on the report of the criminal section of the French police station to the Mixed Court, the presiding magistrate came with the French consul Du to examine and identify them, and then the municipal party branch and the Public Security Bureau sent men to take away the two bodies of Bai and Fan.

On hearing the news, this reporter drove to the spot and saw Fan Zhengbo's bodyguard Han Yunxiu lying face up at the mouth of the fifth east lane, the bullet entered at his right temple and out at the left, the wound the size of a coin, the red blood pooling and running, the right eye shut and the left slightly open; being fat, his belly rose like a mound; he wore a gray cloth Sun Yat-sen tunic, a felt cap on his head and leather shoes on his feet… Bai Xin lay curled on his side at the door of No. 71, his back against the doorpost, his right hand still holding a pistol, the forefinger crooked upon the trigger as though to fire; he wore a gray serge gown and dark blue silk Western trousers and black leather shoes, the wound at the back of the head, the white brains and red blood flowing of themselves from where the bullet came out — a most piteous sight.

This item also, following the enemy's put-about story that Bai Xin was going to Nanjing, added an account of the circumstances "before the assassination": "Mr. Bai, having a wire from a certain Nanjing office, was to go up to the capital to take a post. Bai lodged at Fan's; the two Fan brothers lived on the second floor, Bai on the third (note: an earlier passage says Fan had 'visitors, a Mr. Bai … came calling, after dining'). When it came time to set out, fearing that Fan's own car could carry passengers but not goods, some minutes before leaving he had Fan's bodyguard Han Yunxiu hire a car from the Suzhou Motor Company on Avenue Joffre, its number 6730. The car was parked at the back-lane gate of Hehe Fang on Rue Bourgeat, and Fan's servant and a bodyguard were loading the luggage. Just then, it is said, all was quiet within the lane, hardly a figure about, and Fan, Bai, and the rest came out, when the assassins struck suddenly. Witnesses say there were seven or eight, some say more than ten. If so, they had come and lain hidden beforehand in the dark of the lane. Most puzzling of all: how did the ruffians know Bai would set out that night to flee to the capital? And how did they know that Fan Zhengbo and the others would go with him?" This report, though it did not truly lay bare the case's "truth," raised a question worth pondering and left the reader to guess.

The foreign-language papers, for their part, mostly took it for a "political assassination case," "possibly a Communist reprisal." On November 13 the British-run North China Daily News of Shanghai reported the case in a prominent place under the great headline "Political Murder in Shanghai," with the subhead "Four Killed in a Mysterious Shooting Affair in an Alleyway Off Avenue Joffre: Alleged Communist Revenge." The full text ran:

The French police are investigating an unprecedented assassination case. Toward ten on Monday night, in a lane leading onto Avenue Joffre (not far from the "Peacock Oriental Theatre"), a group of persons of unknown identity shot dead four men on the spot and wounded three others. Three suspects have been arrested, and it is thought more will be taken within the next 48 hours. This is believed to be a case of political assassination; of those killed, the most important is Mr. Bai Xin. He was chief of the detective bureau of the Songhu Gendarmerie Command at Longhua and head of the anti-Communist headquarters for the Chinese city of Shanghai.

The case occurred in a lane called "Hehe Fang," one end of which leads onto Avenue Joffre and the other onto Rue Bourgeat; at No. 43 lived Mr. Bai Xin. Just as he was leaving his door with his family, his secretary, and his bodyguard to take the night train to Nanjing from the railway station, a hail of bullets was loosed at him. Those who fired were a group of persons who had hidden themselves beforehand in the shadows, and whose identity is still unknown. After firing two volleys, the party got clear of the scene, leaving no trace behind.

The shooting lasted only a few seconds, but when the neighbors came out to look, they found four dead and three wounded. Among the dead were Mr. Wang and his bodyguard, along with the watchman guarding the lane and a cook employed by a neighbor. The wounded were Mr. Fan Zhengbo (a member of the Kuomintang party branch of the Shanghai region) and Fan's brother, and Mr. Bai's secretary.

Within minutes of the shooting the French police reached the scene and at once searched the surrounding area, arresting three suspects.

The investigation continued yesterday, and the arrested suspects were questioned, to get to the bottom of the case. The police hold that the assassins were hired by the Communists, the aim being reprisal, since Mr. Bai had lately, over the past several months, seized many Communists.

Reports of the case in yesterday's Chinese papers all "left a blank window," keeping only one headline, "An Assassination Case in the French Concession," to give the reader a little word of it.

The Kuomintang reactionaries were badly shaken that Bai Xin and the rest had been put to death by our side. The Concession authorities racked their brains and still could not crack the case. The Kuomintang government, deeply dissatisfied with the Concession, set off a spell of dog-eat-dog wrangling. On November 9 the Kuomintang-run Shanghai Minguo Ribao carried a report by Wu Kaixian openly reproaching that in this case "the Concession authorities beforehand had heard nothing of it at all, and afterward could not seize the killers within any short span — no wonder either."

To meet the blows of the Red Squad (called in those days the "Dog-Beating Squad"), the enemy at once raised the patrolmen and agents of the French Concession, the International Settlement, and the Chinese city from 500 to more than 1,700, and still found themselves helpless. The American intelligence service too set great store by the case and at once sent an agent chief named Ross to the police station of the Shanghai International Settlement to "strengthen the guidance." But these measures were of little use: at Ross's side was a confidant, a man in touch with us, who supplied a good deal of valuable intelligence and drew a monthly subsidy of 500 yuan from our side.

### Tan Zhongyu, the Hero Who Executed Traitors

The man who led the Red Squad in carrying out this task on November 11 was Tan Zhongyu, deputy to Gu Shunzhang in the Third Section of the Central Special Section. That afternoon, on the plan of action Zhou Enlai had settled beforehand, under the direction of Gu Shunzhang and Chen Geng, he led ten Red Squad men, each got up as a small peddler, a cobbler, a clothes-deliverer, or a passerby, and one after another they slipped into the neighborhood of Bai Xin's lodging in the Hehe Fang lane. That night they struck all at once and suppressed the traitor Bai Xin, whose death was more than his crimes deserved. Ke Lin, who took part in the case, has said gladly: "This fight was directed by Chen Geng in person. It was a beautiful piece of work. We executed the four cleanly and neatly — the traitor Bai Xin and three others, and not one of us in the Section was hurt. When the news of the traitor Bai Xin's execution appeared in the papers, it shook Shanghai for a time; revolutionary comrades clapped their hands for joy, while the class enemy was thrown into a panic." Ke Lin said too: "Peng Pai was my fellow townsman. When we studied at the Haifeng middle school, Peng Pai was three years ahead of me." It was Peng Pai who had led Ke Lin into the revolution, and the two had fought shoulder to shoulder ever since. At this time Ke Lin felt a deep comfort that he could play a part in avenging Peng Pai and the other comrades.

Tan Zhongyu (1909–1933), whose original name was Tan Hongjiang and who was also called Zhongyu, Zhongyu, and Dan Zhongyu, was a native of Baoshan county, Shanghai. In his boyhood, the family being too poor to pay for school, he was apprenticed at a rice shop; a younger sister of his worked in a cotton mill. He joined the revolution in the days of the Great Revolution. In the late 1970s, when I was writing General Chen Geng, I visited Li Qiang many times to learn about the Central Special Section of those years, and heard him speak of Tan Zhongyu's deeds. On November 23, 1979, I wrote to him once more to ask about Tan Zhongyu. In my letter I wrote: "As to whether Comrade Dan Zhongyu was surnamed Tan or Dan, I should like to lay a few things before you. In my Dragon's Pool, Tiger's Den (note: a chapter of General Chen Geng), I already, on your view, changed it to Dan. This time, on the manuscript you sent to the National Committee of the CPPCC (note: the book Comrade Chen Geng in Shanghai, which the CPPCC's Selected Historical Materials commissioned me to write), you noted that he was a Suzhou man. Lately the comrades of the Collected Works of Zhou Enlai office say that the Premier's essays too mention him, written as 'Tan Zhongyu.' They asked Comrade Zhongyu's wife, Comrade Zhou Huinian, and she says he was surnamed 'Tan.' She says Comrade Zhongyu's first wife and Gu Shunzhang's wife were sisters; after his first wife died, he married her on the organization's introduction. She says too that Tan's old home was in the outskirts of Shanghai. By her account, the surname differs from yours and the native place differs too — could they be two men? Or might it be that he was sometimes surnamed 'Dan' and sometimes changed it to 'Tan'?" Li Qiang wrote back in the margin of this letter: "The surname and given name went through quite a few characters — even Zhongyu, and Zhongyu again. In secret work one was forever changing name and surname. As for his being a Suzhou man and a rice-shop apprentice by origin, that was Gu Shunzhang told me. Gu Shunzhang's sister-in-law was called 'Abao' as a child. In those days they lived in a row of little Western houses on the west side of the south end of Chengdu Road — the number I cannot recall, but near the bottom of the lane."

Early in 1927 Tan Zhongyu served as a courier in the Central Shanghai District Committee of the CCP. He took part in the third armed uprising of the Shanghai workers and soon joined the Chinese Communist Party. Late in 1927, after the Central Special Section was founded in Shanghai, he was transferred to it, one of the chief responsible men of the Red Squad and deputy to Gu Shunzhang, head of the Third Section. In August 1930, when the Third Section chief Cai Fei (nicknamed "the Old White Face") was arrested (and later defected), Tan Zhongyu became chief of the Third Section. From 1927 to 1931, in the work of leading the Red Squad in suppressing traitors, rescuing arrested comrades, and raising funds for the Party, he was active and brave and made a fine record. Besides this suppression of Bai Xin, he took a leading part in, or led, the Red Squad through a whole series of cases: the suppression of the traitor Chen Weinian that same year, and of the traitor Huang Dihong in 1930.

After Gu Shunzhang was arrested and turned traitor in April 1931, the Central Special Section was reorganized, and most of its former chief personnel were moved elsewhere. At this time Chen Yun led the Section, with Kang Sheng holding the post of Third Section chief concurrently and Tan Zhongyu as deputy chief. Tan Zhongyu went on leading the Red Squad in suppressing traitors and guarding the Party's safety. At this time, with the organization's approval, he married Zhou Huinian, who worked in the Third Section. Zhou Huinian was a native of Xinyang, Henan; her family was still counted well off when she was born, but with her father's early death the household fell swiftly into decline. Huinian loved to read, and her mother scrimped on food and clothing to the utmost to keep her in school, until hardship forced her to leave off midway. In 1926, when the Northern Expeditionary army reached Henan, a supplementary school was set up in her home village, Tanjiahe town. The school was free, and it not only gave her a fresh chance to study but brought her into contact with Communist Party members among the teachers. Under their guidance she came little by little to understand a good deal of revolutionary reason, her thinking awoke by the day, and before long she joined the Chinese Communist Party. Shuttling and toiling between Xinyang and Wuhan through every hardship, once arrested by the reactionaries, she was tempered in the struggle. Later the organization moved her to Shanghai to take part in the underground struggle. In Shanghai, where the White Terror was severe, she lived a life of the bitterest poverty and hardship, taking on every kind of task the Party assigned and showing at every turn a boundless loyalty to the Party and a brave, forward spirit, and in 1930 was moved into the more dangerous work of the Central Special Section. Assigned to the Third Section, the work in her charge was to guard the organs, keep the weapons, sometimes carry ammunition for the Red Squad on its missions, and sometimes pass on orders and intelligence about operations. These tasks were extraordinarily dangerous: to hide or carry firearms in the Concession, if one were caught, could mean the extreme penalty. Zhou Huinian has said: "In those days, when we went to carry out a task, we had to be careful upon careful. First we took a tram, then went into a park and circled a few times, then took a rickshaw winding left and right, and only when we were sure there was no 'tail' behind did we go on to the contact point or the place of the task." It is said that some women comrades, carrying out such tasks for the first time, had their legs trembling; but Zhou Huinian pressed ever forward, fearing nothing, and could always beat the enemy and see the task through.

In November 1931, when Wang Shide, a Special Section worker closely tied to Tan Zhongyu, was arrested and turned traitor, Tan Zhongyu could no longer move about in Shanghai. The Party organization decided to move Tan Zhongyu and Zhou Huinian out of Shanghai to work in the Hubei-Henan soviet area. Tan Zhongyu became political commissar of the Red Army's Twenty-sixth Division, and Zhou Huinian was assigned to the women's work committee of the soviet's special committee, charged with mobilizing the people to join the Red Army and rousing the women to give active support to the fighting at the front. Tan Zhongyu led his troops in battle against the enemy around Hubei, Xiangyang, and Zaoyang, but with the enemy strong and ourselves weak, the odds too uneven, the base area was lost, and Tan Zhongyu and Zhou Huinian were forced back to Shanghai.

Not long after their return, the Party organization sent Tan Zhongyu to study in the Soviet Union (on reaching Moscow he changed his name to Zhou Ming). Zhou Huinian, having just given birth, stayed in Shanghai, where the organization arranged for her to live together with the "revolutionary mother" Granny Xia (note: the mother of Xia Zhixu) and keep watch over one of the CCP central organs. Tan Zhongyu had lost his chance at schooling from boyhood, and study in the Soviet Union came very hard to him. But he overcame every difficulty and did very well. In 1933 the Party sent Tan Zhongyu back with a radio set to find the Red Army. Because he carried the set, and feared the Kuomintang would find it, he did not take the train but crossed the border on foot in secret; on the way he was set upon and surrounded by the enemy, and fell, a hero, in the fight.
