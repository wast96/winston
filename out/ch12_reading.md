## The Celestial Maiden of Yoshino

On the twenty-seventh day of the second month of Bunroku 3, the Taikō Hideyoshi went out to Mount Yoshino to view the blossoms, his palanquin drawn up in a line with those of his adopted son Hidetsugu, of Ieyasu, and of Toshiie. The pacification of the Korean war was going at last from bad to worse, and word of it had set a faint unease stirring in the hearts of the people of the home provinces. No doubt Hideyoshi meant by this progress to show Ieyasu and Toshiie the grandeur of his own spirit, and at the same time to make a display before the common folk of how secure the Toyotomi rule stood; and to that end the blossom-viewing feast was got up with a magnificence beyond anything ever seen.
His adopted son Hidetsugu had already been ceded the office of Kampaku, back in the twelfth month of Tenshō 19; it was then that Hideyoshi first took the title of Taikō. Yet this same Hidetsugu, the year after the Yoshino blossom-viewing, was abruptly charged with treason and exposed at the Sanjō riverbed, his head set up beside those of five retainers and twenty-nine of his consorts; and Hideyoshi, who had ordered the executions, was himself dead of illness a bare four years after the year of the flowers. And Ieyasu, the Naifu, whom he led by the hand beneath the blossoms that day, crushed the Ishida faction at Sekigahara in Mino only six years later.
On this day, when Hideyoshi's glory blazed out its last bright flare, Tsuzura Jūzō too was there, in secret, beneath the flowers of Yoshino. His purpose was not assassination. What Jūzō wanted was to see Hideyoshi's true face, once, in a good clear light.
From dawn on the twenty-seventh, the narrow pilgrim road that runs along the ridge of Mount Yoshino was swept clean of every speck of dust under the direction of the monks of the Zaō Hall, and the guard-samurai went minutely over the sub-temples and dwellings packed along both sides of the way, checking each man and his weapons. Every last person who would kneel in the dust had been vetted beforehand by the officials posted at the great gate, so that a single stranger among them would have shown up, to any eye, like a poppy seed dropped on white ground. To come within arm's reach of Hideyoshi through all this, a man would have had no recourse left but to turn himself into grass or tree.
And in fact, on the eve of the blossom-viewing, the top of a great camphor tree that stood beside the Saigyō hermitage — a peak a ri of mountain path from the Zaō Hall — stirred, though there was no wind.
It was Jūzō. He had got himself up as a kudzu-peddler from some far province, but there was no getting through to the town along the pilgrim road, and so he had resolved to pass the night in the hollow of this old camphor on the peak, out at the edge of the cordon. After dark he climbed into the crown of the tree and looked out over the mountains far below him; the watch-fires of the guards, filling every peak and valley, were like the lamps of a great Ten-Thousand-Lantern rite.
(Reckon I've made too little of this,)
Perched on a branch, Jūzō gave a wry smile in spite of himself. There was nothing to be done. By night, he had no small confidence he could pass in and out of even a cordon like this at will; but the hour when Hideyoshi would leave his palanquin near the Kissui-in and climb the mountain road on foot would not come until the next morning, with the sun well up. Jūzō meant, at that hour, to slip into the ranks of the kneeling crowd and steal a look at Hideyoshi's face.
But as things stood, even getting near the town would be no easy matter.
(No help for it, then.)
Something in him spoke the words to himself.
It was a way of making one man vanish from the earth for a while. He would change himself into that man, and, in broad day, deceive the fixed stare of the crowd. For that he had to be ready to stake his life.
It was only twenty days before this that Hideyoshi had summoned his companions — Oda Urakusai, Furuta Oribe-no-shō, Yamaoka Dōami and the rest — and commanded them: "The realm itself is to admire the flowers. See the feast is got up to match." There was, in short, no time. Ōsaka Castle and the daimyō mansions around Tenma were thrown into a commotion like a battle, over furnishings and retinues.
Talk in Ōsaka reaches the capital in a day. Jūzō, who heard of Hideyoshi's flower-viewing at Kuroami's house in Kyoto, thought it a windfall. Here, at last, was the day he could study Hideyoshi's features at his leisure beneath the blossoms. But how to get past the eyes of the tens of thousands of guardsmen ringing him? Even Jūzō had no confidence he could brave that and come near. Kuroami tried to talk him out of the folly:
"Don't go trying any such nonsense. Your life is the thing that matters."
"And do you think I can get in to him later, some day, without so much as knowing his face?"
"You can reckon it near enough. A face like his comes one in ten thousand, they say."
"That's not enough to go on. Isn't there some trick for it?"
"There is — if your heart is so set on it. But once you're seen through, they'll cut your body to a hash."
What Kuroami meant was this: among the entertainments got up for the blossom feast was a torchlight Noh beneath the cherry trees, in the charge of the Kanze master. Why not slip in among the players, and from up on the stage look his fill at Hideyoshi?
"Down at Shimogawara there's the house of a man called Kanze Otojirō. Every night, they say, the ones who'll dance at Yoshino rehearse there. Steal into the house, and in the ten days or so we've got, filch the movements."
"The movements?"
"Just so. But put a foot wrong on that stage, and the whole mountain is the Taikō's men — there'll be no way left to slip off."
"No knowing till it's tried. What's the piece?"
"'The Celestial Maiden of Yoshino,' or so I hear."
In the Kanze house this is reckoned a third-category piece. Being bound up with Yoshino and with the blossoms, it had no doubt been chosen on purpose to grace the flower-viewing. The shite, the principal, is a heavenly maiden. She appears first in the guise of a village woman; the waki is a man from the capital; and two attendants come on besides, as the waki's companions.
It opens as the man of the capital, his attendants behind him, drifts onstage: "Now it is the spring of flowers — let us go and gaze on the thousand cherry trees." When his party reaches Mount Yoshino, they fall in with a lone village woman beneath the blossoms. She tells of herself, "I pass my days on the mountain where spring has come, and live among its wilds with the flowers alone for company"; and the man, deeply moved, answers, "Truly, a bond made with the flowers is the deepest of all our lives to come," and, forgetting the road home, lingers to gaze at the blossoms — until, one night, the woman turns to him: "If tonight you will lodge here and keep faith with me, I will show you the ancient Gosechi dance of old, the sleeves of the sacred robe thrown back, a revel under the moon." And of a sudden she takes on the form of a celestial being, and shows the wonder of her dancing in play among the flowers. Such is the gist of it.
That night, stealing into the broad garden of the Kanze house at Shimogawara, Jūzō and Kuroami saw, against the backdrop of a small tea-house-style main hall, and within a boundary marked off by four watch-fires blazing red at the corners, a knot of shadows in Noh costume moving busily about.
The practice of performing Noh at night by torchlight was one seen, as a rule, nowhere but at the sacred Noh of Kasuga, in Nara. Every year, entering the second month, it was held by custom for seven days on the lawn before the Great South Gate of Kōfuku-ji. For this Yoshino flower-viewing it had no doubt been chosen especially, to lend the affair the charm of night. On the black night air pouring down off Higashiyama came the low, penetrating voice of the chant. In time with it, a figure danced slowly, drew back a few paces, and stood as still as a driven stake, waiting its entrance. Now and then the wind stirred and the torch-flames leapt as if licking at the sky, and each time the shadows on the ground wove an intricate pattern.
"There — look at the figure that has just risen."
Kuroami whispered it close by Jūzō's ear.
"The waki's attendant, is it."
"Just so. In build and bearing, much like yourself."
"Will he do?"
Jūzō, sitting cross-legged in the cover of a planting, watched with the air of a patron of ten years' standing.
"He'll do. Little movement in the part.—— Though whether you can bring it off, now."
"By the look of it, I can."

…and out from within, the gorgeous Noh costumes, once they came out into the precinct, were of a sudden lit by the torch-fire, stirring up the very dreamlike unreality that suited the theme of the Celestial Maiden of Yoshino. Perhaps the darkness of the road had been contrived on purpose, to that very end.
But the misfortune of Mikajirō, who was to play the waki's attendant, had its root in that darkness of the road.
With the bell of the sixth hour of evening, the Kanze troupe set out from the Hōzen-in.
Mikajirō lagged behind the party, walking a dozen-odd paces back from the lantern of the servant who led the way. Just as the last of the preliminary strokes of the sixth-hour bell died away, the world outside this man abruptly lost its light — he had been knocked senseless. In the same instant he was dragged off into the grass at the roadside.
It was Kuroami who had dragged him in. Quickly he forced a gag into the man's mouth, stripped off his costume, and, having finished dressing the waiting Jūzō in it,
"It suits you,"
he said, with a grin. Leaning back with satisfaction to look Jūzō's costume over,
"Off you go, then. Though this may well be our parting for this life."
"Don't say such ill-omened things."
Mask on, Jūzō went unhurried after the party.
Presently the Noh began. Following the movements of the Kanze master, Jūzō matched the actions of the other attendant, the second of the waki's companions. That alone was all he need do; and yet, hidden inside the mask, Jūzō's face grew steadily less easy.
Jūzō had made a miscalculation. Looking out from within the bright, spark-scattering ring of the torch-fires, he realized at last that the seat of the Shikan-in, where Hideyoshi ought to be, was unexpectedly dark.
True enough, the latticed shutter of the Shikan-in had been raised high. But the space beneath it was dark as a cave. Within that curtain of backlight the daimyō and grandees were ranged close together like a thousand ranked Buddhas — but was the Taikō Hideyoshi in fact among them? And if he was, which of the shadows in the front and center was he? Try as he might to search, the world seen through the mask was small, and, worse, his gaze was fixed forward in the set stare of the dance and could not be moved.
And all the while the Noh moved on without mercy. By the time the celestial maiden, won over by the capital man's devotion, was about to reveal her true form, Jūzō had at last given up the hope he had come with.
(All that labor wasted…)
Dancing, Jūzō gave a wry smile. When he stared hard, there was indeed a dark human outline in the seat that seemed to be Hideyoshi's, front and center. But to make out the face he would need a little more light. There was nothing for it now but to go on with the dance. The play moved forward: the village woman changed into the robes of the celestial maiden and, throwing back the sleeves of the sacred robe, began to dance the Gosechi dance alone. Following her, the waki and his two companions drew back to the edge of the torch-fires and watched the maiden's dance in wonder and amazement. As he had learned the part, Jūzō set both knees to the ground and, as though entranced by the dance, raised the chin of his mask a little — but by about this time a strange wave had begun to rise in his heart.
The gaze was not coming from one direction only. From the side, too, Jūzō felt a stare stabbing in at about the root of his left ear.
(…Two of them, then.)
And the two did not seem to be of one company. One was soft; the other sharp.
(Who can they be…)
A shudder ran over Jūzō's skin.
If he were seen through, there would be no way of escape. Around the not-large precinct, but for the performance stage ringed by the torch-fires, the people were packed without a gap.
If a challenge were raised, his one means of escape would be to knock over the four torch-stands first of all. He would have to plunge the place into darkness and lose himself in the crowd of onlookers — but if the temple gates were shut in that interval, he was finished. It was a night of stars thick enough to rain down. And to try to climb the wall would end only in showing himself to every eye. So — of course Jūzō too would follow the players. He would pass before the crowd seated below, cutting his stride into small steps.
Before long the Noh ended, and the players melted away one by one, threading out of the precinct.
As he went, Jūzō felt the sweat on his back. Still someone was watching him. The gaze clung to his body with a stubborn, sticky grip, at every step he took. When at last he had passed before the spectators and ducked through the four-legged gate at the rear, the tension went out of his limbs all at once. From here on it was his own world, the world of the ninja. Before him, the darkness that wrapped the mountains and the fields spread away to the very ends of the sky.
Naturally Jūzō broke into a trot; and at his very heels a voice, like a nail driven home, sounded suddenly behind him.
"You there…"
It was a low voice, as if crushed flat. Jūzō pretended not to hear and pressed on up the little path through the thicket.
"Can't you hear me?"
Jūzō resigned himself to it. Already he had begun to loosen every one of the ties of the Noh costume. Beneath it was black ninja garb, and beneath that again he was armored in a shirt of chain mail. Only — he carried not a single blade.
"Stop, or I cut you down."
As the other spoke, Jūzō, with deliberate slowness and pointedly without turning around, said:
"Cut me down, then, if you can."
Before the words were out, a long sword came down in an arc and grazed Jūzō's skin.
It sliced the Noh costume clean in two, from collar to hem. The costume stood a moment in the road like a living thing, then folded and collapsed with a soft flop.
An instant before, Jūzō's body had slipped out of the costume and been sucked away into the thicket beyond the roadside.
The man gave chase.
A few petals drifted down onto his face, though there was no wind.
Jūzō fled through the cherry grove, choosing the upward slope.
When he had come halfway up, a great half-moon, red as blood, chanced to rise wavering over the ridge of the mountain. As if the moon had spat it out, a single black shadow came crawling up beside Jūzō, and —
"Master Jūzō, it's me."
"Ah, Kuroami. You've come at a good moment."
With that, Jūzō drew, all at once, the sword slung at Kuroami's back, and —
"I'm being followed. I'll cut him down here. Kuroami — you're in the way. Get yourself off somewhere."
Jūzō let the sword hang loose and peered down the slope. Seeing this, Kuroami bared his white teeth and —
"What careless talk. That, I tell you, is Master Gohei of the Kazama."
"Gohei?——"
"A while back, when he called out to stop you just past the four-legged gate, I got a good clear look at his face. By the look of it, he's been on us ever since we left the capital, no doubt of it. It's just the right chance. Cut him down and have done."
But for all the spirit of Kuroami's words, when Jūzō —
"Right. You block off Gohei's line of escape, then."
— when Jūzō gave the order,
"Ah — no, that I cannot do,"
and his face changed color.
"Oh?"
"Master Gohei — I'm frightened of him past all bearing. I'd be cut down for certain. I'll just go on ahead of you, back to the capital."
Jūzō dropped flat and, as if he had already forgotten Kuroami was there, narrowed his eyes and watched, with all his mind, the movements of Gohei coming up from below. Presently the shrubs just beneath him stirred faintly, and he knew Gohei had drawn near.
"Gohei."
"So you've noticed."
He did not show himself, but the voice leaked out from the shrubs at Jūzō's feet.
"I'll wait until you've found your footing. Luckily the moon is up. We'll settle it in a fair fight."
said Jūzō. But Gohei's voice came back as if in mockery:
"If I meant to fight fair, I'd hardly have trailed you all this way. Cut you down or be cut down — either way there's nothing in it for me."
Jūzō fell silent. The more words they traded, the more he felt himself sinking into Gohei's cunning.
The moon pulled free, all at once, of the crown of an old cherry on the peak. Its light began to slant down as if scattering powder, and laid a deep umber shadow along every feature of the hillside.
Jūzō gripped the hilt of the bared blade. A quivering ran up it toward the point, like something fluttering. By whatever means, he had to kill Gohei. The time for it had come. So long as he left Gohei alive, Jūzō would in the end be boiled down in the cauldron of the man's schemes — that was where it would come to.
The moon was at Jūzō's back; Gohei faced into its glare. The distance between them was perhaps three ken, with a few shrubs tangled in the space between. With the shrubs between them, the two crept slowly, tracing the whorl of a tomoe, circling as they went.
Both killed every sound.
Which meant that neither could tell where the other was. The fear that a blade might be thrust in from the side at any moment wrapped them both. Unable to bear it, perhaps, Gohei opened his mouth to probe. His voice, at least, he kept deliberately leisurely:
"Jūzō — that moon at your back is a nuisance to me."
The moment the words were out he moved, swiftly. Jūzō did not answer. Ear pressed to the skin of the mountain, he quietly hunted the sounds Gohei made. The wind pouring down off the peak had begun to set the grass hissing steadily. In his eardrums there was that sound, and nothing else.
A dry little clatter.——
A pebble had fallen beside Jūzō's right hand. Another dropped by his left foot. The pebbles came one after another, clearing the shrubs in slow arcs. Gohei was searching out Jūzō's position. First he would tell the sound of one falling in the brush from one falling on bare ground; and when at last a pebble struck Jūzō's body, the sound would be swallowed by his clothing and never come back to Gohei's ear. That would be the moment Gohei learned where Jūzō was.
Jūzō grinned, and instead fixed the direction the stones were coming from. Sure enough, that direction shifted with every stone, and was hard to pin down.
That it came, more or less, from just below him was all he could tell.—— Jūzō laid his palm quietly over a stone near at hand, a stone big as an armful.
The moment, he judged, was right. Gohei was most likely directly below. Jūzō let the stone drop — but the stone, which he shoved off with all his strength, he sent deliberately to the right of where Gohei was. It began to fall, tearing up the earth as it went.
Jūzō himself slipped quickly down around the shrubs on the side away from the falling stone, and, coming round at Gohei's back, brought his blade hewing down through the night air over the head below.
(Jūzō?)
It was the moment Gohei, startled, had his attention snatched away by the falling stone.
With a slither, Gohei's body slipped and went tumbling on down the slope.
From the ground where Gohei had been there rose, sure enough, the smell of blood. But of a sudden Jūzō made a sour face.
(A misjudged stroke,)
A young cherry sapling had been lopped clean through, two shaku from the ground. Gohei, unable to dodge the blade entirely, had taken a slight wound; then, no doubt, he had caught hold of the cherry Jūzō had cut down, hidden himself among its branches, and slid away down the slope with it.
Jūzō had no memory, in eye or in hand, of having cut a cherry at that moment. It seemed that Gohei, the very instant he sensed the killing intent, had swiftly hidden himself behind the tree.
(No ordinary man, after all.)
Once again Jūzō felt his eyes widen at the Iga art of measure-and-concealment that Gohei had at his command.
All the more reason, then, to be rid of him quickly.
And yet Jūzō did not go down the slope after him. Call it a ninja's discipline. Gohei had surely not slid down there merely to flee. In all likelihood he was lying in wait for Jūzō with some fresh trick of transformation.
Bare blade in hand, Jūzō climbed toward the peak. At the top, as if in dread of some slim chance of attack from the wounded Gohei, he plunged on flying feet into a stand of cedars.
Cross three peaks from here and one came out at the Saigyō hermitage. He was pulling back to the hermitage for a purpose: to bury the ninja tools he had hidden in the hollow of the old camphor.
Once they were buried, he had to be out of Yoshino as fast as he could. By now, surely, the senseless Noh player had been found in the grass near the Shikan-in. And once that was known, the guard on Mount Yoshino would boil over into a sudden frenzy of activity.
When he had made his way to the peak of the Saigyō hermitage, Jūzō put his hand into the hollow of the old camphor, and a puzzled look crossed his face.
They were gone.
(Did Kuroami see to them?)
He tried the thought, but that man would never have cleared the tools away while Jūzō was still on the mountain.
When, on an impulse, he looked over the ground around him, there was the mark of earth freshly turned and filled in again, still raw.
(Someone.—— And if not Kuroami…)
Gooseflesh rose over Jūzō's skin all at once. He had an intuition. He had remembered that other gaze, the one that had watched him during the torchlight Noh.
(Surely not — Dōgen of Kōga…?)
No, the quality of that gaze, he thought, was not Mari Dōgen of Kōga's.
But whoever's work this was, that person was surely watching Jūzō as he stood now beside the turned earth, staring fixedly out of the dark of some stand of trees.
Feeling the danger to himself, Jūzō sprang back from the earth by reflex.
At that moment, out of the trees, came the cry of an owl.
It was no owl's cry, Jūzō knew, but the sound of an owl-whistle, blown by a ninja.
Bare blade lowered, Jūzō looked about him. As ill luck would have it, this spot where the old camphor had its roots was a narrow grassy clearing. Dense woods pressed in all around it, and between him and the trees there was not a scrap of cover.
The moon was already climbing toward the zenith. The clearing where Jūzō stood lay now in a sea of blue light.
He could make a dash for the trees and hide himself, but he felt no inclination to try it at once. What lay concealed within the woods, he had, as things stood, no way of guessing.
The proof was that the owl had not cried only once.
A cry in the east, and the west answered it. Before long the whole ring of woods gave up owl-cries, as if they were welling out of the ground.
Jūzō gave up any thought of moving.
There was nothing for it now but to fold his arms and stand stock-still, like a wooden puppet, and see what was about to begin.
Some missile, most likely, would come. That was Jūzō's guess. But he was neatly wrong.
The first thing to come at Jūzō, from behind, was a single sasumata.
He knocked it aside and cut down across the shoulder. But the blade met the chain mail and did not bite, and the attacker went down from the shock alone. The fallen man was dressed in ninja garb.
(Meaning to take me alive, are they?)
To be honest, Jūzō was relieved. Had they meant to finish him with a thrown weapon, there could have been no finer target than Jūzō just then.
But somehow their numbers had grown, and a dozen and more ninja had begun to ring Jūzō round. Armed with sasumata, staves, rakes and the like, in utter silence they drew the circle slowly tighter.
(Ah — Kōga men.…)
At the orderly movement of this band Jūzō let out, in spite of himself, a groan of admiration. The men of Iga excelled at the work of the lone individual; but at the massed, coordinated function of the men of Kōga — in a siege, in a field engagement — they fell far short.
One of the men behind him flung a blinding-charge at Jūzō. He dodged it by a hair, and it burst with a great crack against the trunk of the camphor at his back.
Powder flew. Stay where he was and his eyes would be done for. Jūzō shut his lids, held his breath, abandoned the shelter of the camphor at his back, and charged into the wind.
In an instant it was a melee. Before he knew it Jūzō had thrown away his bared blade and seized a three-ken staff to fight with; but he had barely managed to thrust one man down, and no more.
Waiting for the blinding-powder to settle, he fell back once more beneath the camphor and caught his breath. His opponents, cowed perhaps by this Iga man's unlooked-for skill, would not close with him easily now.
Jūzō put a hand inside his breast. Then, dropping suddenly low, he sent a throwing-wheel, its star-shaped blades jutting out, flying up into the sky.
Without even a scream, a man fell from the crown of the camphor. A casting-net clutched in his arms, he smashed uselessly to the ground.
Everything was silent.
When the man before Jūzō, back bent, staff at the ready, sank his head lower and lower in that same posture and at last toppled to the ground, not a sound came from any of the band.
Next, the man to Jūzō's right folded and collapsed as quietly as if it were a dance, or the movement of some rite.
Only when the third man fell did a sound at last break from the band, like a groan held back and back.
"F— fall back!"
At that one word, all those men gathered up their wounded and vanished from before Jūzō's eyes like a dream.
"That you, Kuroami?"
Throwing down the staff, Jūzō called into the thicket nearby.
"Aye."
Kuroami stood up out of the thicket, a short bow in his hand.
"So you didn't go back to the capital after all."
"It preyed on me a little, you see."
Timid as he was, this Kuroami seemed to have an uncanny sixth sense, like a shrine medium's. Running along the mountain road that cuts through to Shimoichi, he had felt, all at once, a foreboding about Jūzō, and had turned back — or so he said.
Jūzō made to take the lead and follow the path into the woods. But Kuroami tugged sharply at his sleeve:
"Have a look at that."
Looking at the ground, he saw that over the path, in the brush, as far as a groping hand could reach, caltrops had been strewn thick everywhere. Step on one and it would run up into the sole of the foot. They were a special device, used only by ninja.
"The whole floor of this wood, I should think, is sown with them. A fearful enemy. Had you tried to flee into the trees, you'd have trodden on these and been taken with no trouble at all."
As he spoke, Kuroami pulled up a handful of grass and with its tips swept the caltrops rattling aside.
"Our enemy — Kōga men, were they not?"
"Aye. Kōga men."
"And who was it set them on?"
"Ho.…"
Kuroami stopped his sweeping and looked at Jūzō's face.
"You don't know? —— That was Kohagi."
"What — Kohagi?"
"Just so. I saw her, plain, with these own eyes."
"Kohagi, you say."
"The very same. In the shadow of the trees, east-and-south of that camphor, dressed — a woman, of all things — in ninja garb, and giving out orders this way and that."
"Why should the woman want to take me alive?"
"It'll hardly be a lovers' quarrel between the two of you, I suppose."
Kuroami, with a sardonic smile, went on:
"It's your living body that's of use to them. Ishida, the Jibu-no-shō, reckons that Imai Sōkyū's plot is being worked at the prompting of the Tokugawa Naifu. He means to take you for living proof, lay the plot bare before the realm, and press the Taikō to a resolve to destroy the Tokugawa. Kohagi is that Jibu-no-shō's ninja. There will be more of this to come. Don't lose your head over a woman and be the ruin of yourself."
It was just so.
So Jūzō thought. And at the same time he felt rise in him something close to awe at Kohagi's ferocity as a ninja.
So the other gaze he had felt in the midst of the torchlight Noh had been Kohagi's after all. What was folded into that gaze was, in all likelihood, no longing for Jūzō. It could have been nothing but the greedy interest of a woman made over — sorrowfully, and wholly — into her profession, an interest fixed on the object of her work. Was the ninja, in the end, a keshō, a thing of no human birth?
Seeing with his own eyes the ferocity of Kohagi's transformation, Jūzō thought so now more than ever. Unlike Gohei, Jūzō meant to live out his whole life as a ninja of Iga, and toward the keshō in Kohagi he felt, if anything, a pleasant kinship. Among ninja fated to die out, as a calling, with the coming of peace to the world, that woman, it seemed to him, carried on the last of the true ninja — carried it on with a vivid life.
That night at Otogi Pass, the whole night through, he had listened, in the bed of the hermitage, to Kohagi's sweet voice.
Kohagi had seemed a woman utterly given over, naked and unguarded. Had she not, in fact, gone so far as to say that the two of them should throw off the ninja life together?
"Let us flee to some place we do not know, and live out a quiet, happy life, the way ordinary people do."
In Kohagi's voice, as she whispered it over and over, there had been even tears. For one trained so far into a keshō to come back so far toward a raw, living human being could not be done, surely, without passing through a great longing and a great suffering. And yet Jūzō refused what Kohagi asked.
The fleetingness of the love between man and woman the Iga ninja knows through and through, as a reasoned tradition. A passion of the moment holds within it, from the instant it springs up, the seed of its own ruin. Rather than commit himself to this foolish passion, Jūzō chose to commit himself into the keshō that was the ninja's.
The moment Jūzō made that choice, Kohagi too returned to her own true self. Tonight's attack on the peak of the Saigyō hermitage was, most likely, a declaration to Jūzō that she had recovered herself — and it seemed to Jūzō that he, at least, could read it so.
From the peak of the Saigyō hermitage they went by byways down into the valley of the Yoshino river, and, once down at the hamlet of Sanade, they took separate roundabout roads to the capital — Jūzō by way of Takatori, Kuroami by way of Takada.
Jūzō was already back in the traveling dress of a samurai. Kuroami had got himself up in the travel-clothes of an honest small tradesman, and they parted before the gate of a plain country temple of the True Pure Land faith at Sanade.
As Jūzō was on the point of walking off, Kuroami thrust his chin in under the brim of Jūzō's woven traveling-hat and said:
"Once you're back in the capital, you must make an end of Kohagi. Do away with Dōgen and Master Gohei too, the whole lot of them. The day to kill the Taikō is drawing near."
"And you? What will you do?"
"I'll gather up the rappa who've scattered, and start the thieving again. The shop behind Hōkō-ji has grown chancy, by the look of it, so I mean before long to shift my lodging to the ruined temple in Rakshasa Valley, or somewhere of the kind."
Baring his teeth, walking the village road in the pale of daybreak, he gave a bleak, white laugh. Somewhere in it showed a weariness of years there was no rubbing out.
