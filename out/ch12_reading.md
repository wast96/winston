## Chapter Twelve. A Purge in the Red-Light District

### 1. Shots at the "Little Garden"

At 7:35 on the evening of June 14, 1933, just as the red lanterns went up and the banners of pleasure were flying along Shanghai's Sima Road (today Fuzhou Road), a burst of vicious gunfire suddenly rang out of the main lane of the "Little Garden," and Ma Shaowu, an inspector of the Municipal Public Security Bureau, staggered and went down in a spray of blood. In the words of the *China Times*, it came to this: "Lü Keqin, inspector of the special-service group sent to Shanghai by the central authorities, took a bullet in the pleasure quarter; his evening's tryst at the Sweetheart brothel of the Little Garden unconsummated, he met with ruffians, was shot through a vital spot, and died on the instant."

"The pleasure quarter" here renders beili, "the northern ward," an old name for the brothel district. It comes from Sun Qi's Records of the Northern Ward, which tells of the Ping'kang ward in the north of Chang'an in the high Tang, where the singing-houses and courtesans' halls stood packed wall to wall and the famous beauties vied in bloom. From that the "northern ward" became a byword for the singing-houses and the brothels. Xin Wenfang's Lives of the Tang Poets has the line, too: "In high spirits to the northern ward, where at every visit he'd write a verse for the girls."

"Lü Keqin" was Ma Shaowu, who was also Shi Jimei. Ma Shaowu had once been a member of the "Red Squad" of the Central Special Branch himself; after he turned traitor and went over to the enemy, he became a faithful hound of Xu Enzeng, chief of the Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department.

Zhang Guodong writes: "In 1931 the Zhongtong kept only a single special commissioner in Shanghai, the post held by Yang Dengying. Only in 1932 was a formal 'Shanghai Action District' set up, with Ma Shaowu as district chief and Xu Zuxin (Qing) as his deputy. After Ma Shaowu's death in 1933, Xu Zuxin served for a time as acting chief as well." (Zhang Guodong, 1992)

Of Ma Shaowu's death, Xu Enzeng wrote in his memoirs: "The man I had posted to run our work in Shanghai, Shi Jimei, was one of my ablest officers. He came back to Nanjing to report in June 1933, and because of the run of incidents in Shanghai, and remembering his outstanding record of service—Xiang Zhongfa, the Noulens couple who were the Comintern Labor International's representatives in China, and other important cases besides had all been cracked by his design—I judged that the Communists must hate him to the bone, and meant to transfer him out of Shanghai to let the heat die down. But he would not agree to the arrangement, and insisted on going back to his old post. I could only urge him to mind his safety and let him go. As it happened, on the very afternoon he returned to Shanghai, wanting to hurry to a dinner he was himself hosting, he got off the train and went straight to the meeting-place; and just as he stepped down from the car and started up the steps, six ruffians lying in wait for him there closed in and attacked, and he died with seven bullets in him." (Xu Enzeng, 1992)

And so Xu Enzeng ordered Gu Shunzhang to Shanghai at once to work the case, and to get to the bottom of Ma Shaowu's violent death.

Early on June 16 Gu Shunzhang's car reached Shanghai. He first called on Wen Hong'en, chief of the Municipal Public Security Bureau, and the mayor, Wu Tiecheng, and then drove out to Sima Road to inspect the scene of Ma Shaowu's shooting in person.

Qian Huafo, in Shanghai Over Thirty Years (1984), says that the "shady, tree-thick" Little Garden had once "truly been a quiet, fine spot—elms and willows lining the lanes, no end of green in sight, orioles hidden away in spring, cicadas shrilling in summer, crows dotting the cold of autumn and winter, a painterly scene worthy of Ni Zan." Only later, as the Sima Road that pressed up against it slid into a foul, corrupt street of powder and paint, a red-light district, did the Little Garden lose "any trace of a garden's colors or a flower's fragrance; all the eye could find were wanton women and painted vamps, all the ear could catch was the lewd music of Zheng and Wei."

The Little Garden was no strange ground to Gu Shunzhang. A lecher by nature, he had haunted the place often enough back before he changed masters, and knew the surrounding streets well; so the moment his car drew near the east exit on Zhejiang Road he had the driver pull over and stop at the curb across the street, in front of the Yongtai women's shoe shop.

Gu Shunzhang pushed open the door and got out. First he walked the lane through, through the main lane of the "Little Garden," out to the west exit on Guangxi Road; then he turned and walked back, retracing Ma Shaowu's path, west to east, over again, and as he walked he pictured the scene from the accounts of the parties and the witnesses.

By the account of Ma Shaowu's driver, Zhu Shaoxiang, the car Ma Shaowu rode that night was a police vehicle carrying two plates: one, No. 4223, issued specially by the Municipal Public Security Bureau, and one, No. 1038, issued in the citywide series.

Zhu Shaoxiang also said he stopped the car at the west exit on Guangxi Road. Before getting out, Ma Shaowu told him to park and then go to the second lane, to the prostitute Sweetheart's, to collect his fare-and-meal money.

The witnesses, for their part, said Ma Shaowu walked into the lane after getting out and headed alone for the prostitute Sweetheart's in the second lane; at the corner of the second lane four or five men set on him, "drawing and firing." Ma Shaowu "was hit in four or five places on the spot, and cut his way toward the east exit of the lane in flight for his life; a few steps on, badly wounded, he fell at the back door of the Eastern Hotel in the lane, blood welling like a spring, at his last gasp."

The witnesses said further that after it happened, "the people of the Sweetheart brothel and the girls of the brothels nearby came running at the alarm; there was a great cry of alarmed voices filling the lane. When word reached the Laozha police station, detectives were sent at once, an ambulance was called, and Ma Shaowu was rushed to Renji Hospital; but his wounds were too grave, and he died toward eight o'clock."

His survey of the scene done, Gu Shunzhang went on to the forensics room of the Municipal Public Security Bureau and carefully examined the several bullets that had been taken out of Ma Shaowu's body.

Gu Shunzhang found that the bullets taken from Ma Shaowu, and several others recovered in two other shooting cases, had been fired from one and the same gun. His instinct told him that behind the "Little Garden" bloodshed lay, without a doubt, the life-and-death struggle of the two parties, Nationalist and Communist.

Gu Shunzhang took up his aides and his creatures, joined forces with the concession patrolmen, and struck out on every side, hunting through the whole city for his old comrades-in-arms, the members of the "Red Squad" of the Central Special Branch.

Almost at the same moment there raced through Shanghai the sensational news that the famous left-wing writer Ding Ling, missing now for some while, had been ordered "shot" by the authorities for her supposed part in Ma Shaowu's killing.

### 2. License Plate 1469

Ding Ling had disappeared on May 14, 1933.

First an unidentified man's body was found at the foot of No. 7 Kunshan Garden Road; then the man proved to be Ying Xiuren, propaganda chief of the CCP's Jiangsu Provincial Committee and a well-known poet of the Lakeside school; then it emerged that Ying Xiuren had gone up to the flat at No. 7 before his terrible death; and finally it emerged that Ding Ling, who lodged in that flat at No. 7, had vanished along with Ying Xiuren's death in the street.

Ding Ling, originally named Jiang Wei and also called Jiang Bingzhi, was born in 1904, a native of Linli in Hunan. In 1925 she married Hu Yepin; in 1927 she published the work that made her name, *The Diary of Miss Sophia*; in 1930 she joined the League of Left-Wing Writers; in 1931 she edited *The Big Dipper*; and in 1932 she joined the Chinese Communist Party, becoming that same year secretary of the League's Party group.

Song Qingling, Cai Yuanpei, Lu Xun, Yang Xingfo and others got wind that Ding Ling's disappearance was a secret kidnapping by the reactionary authorities, and at once launched a rescue campaign on a grand scale.

To rescue Ding Ling and Pan Zinian, who had vanished at the same time as she, Song Qingling, Cai Yuanpei, Lu Xun, Yang Xingfo and others set up a "Ding-Pan Defense Committee" through the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights.

Agnes Smedley recalled: "In 1931 China's first League for the Protection of Civil Rights was founded. Dr. Cai Yuanpei was a vice-chairman; Mr. Yang Quan was vice-chairman and executive secretary. Madame Sun, Song Qingling, was chairman; Lin Yutang, Lu Xun and others were on the executive. There were two foreigners in the League, one the young American reporter Harold Isaacs, the other myself. Lin Yutang, Isaacs and I, the three of us, handled the English-language press work together." (Smedley, 1985)

Yang Quan was Yang Xingfo. Smedley called Yang Xingfo "one of the first rank of Chinese scholars, a man of real learning who lived up to his name," "a statesman, a scholar, and more than that a gifted organizer and administrator." As executive secretary of the Civil Rights League, Yang Xingfo "spared no effort, running everywhere," for "Ding Ling and another writer, Pan Zinian," in their kidnapping, "and issued statements calling on the public to speak out."

Others who "issued statements calling on the public to speak out" included the "Ding-Pan Rescue Association" got up by Lin Yutang, Hu Yuzhi and others, and the League of Left-Wing Writers. Lin Yutang, Hu Yuzhi and others wired the Nanjing National Government jointly, demanding the truth and the swift release of the abducted. The League of Left-Wing Writers, in its Declaration on the Arrest of Ding and Pan and Against the Kuomintang's White Terror, laid open the crimes of the reactionary authorities—selling out the country to foreigners abroad, running a fascist dictatorship at home—demanded the release of political prisoners, and called on the working classes and progressive people of every nation to back the Chinese people's struggle against the Kuomintang's White Terror.

Faced with the huge pressure of public opinion, the Nanjing National Government first held its tongue and then flatly denied everything. Wang Jingwei, president of the Executive Yuan, wired back to Song Qingling, chairman of the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights: "The Public Security Bureau replies that there has been no arrest of Ding Ling; as to her disappearance, therefore, we are truly in the dark…"

Hu Shi, too, in his *Independent Critic*, ran a telegram from the Shanghai mayor, Wu Tiecheng, which said: "The reported arrest of Miss Ding Ling is without foundation. Here, all reactionary cases whatsoever, if not remitted to the Center, are sent to the local courts. With all eyes upon us, we would never dare let human rights be trampled unlawfully."

Smedley, however, already held hard evidence—enough to prove that the solemn vows of Wang Jingwei and Wu Tiecheng were not worth a cent, and that the rumor of Ding Ling and the others suffering the authorities' "unlawful trampling" under those very "watching eyes" was no wind out of an empty cave.

Smedley's evidence was the testimony of an "engineer" surnamed Li who had by luck slipped the clutches of the agents. That "engineer" surnamed Li (whom Ding Ling always took for a "shameless traitor" named Hu Lei; in fact he was Xia Caixi, using the aliases Li Fei, Li Fei and Li Jucun, who had joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1925 and served in turn as CCP county secretary of Qingpu, county secretary of Song-Jin-Qing, special-committee secretary of Yangzhou, city secretary of Nanjing, propaganda chief of the Jiangsu Provincial Committee, and secretary of the West Shanghai District Working Committee) said that on the day it happened, "two agents and a driver" "shoved him into" a car "with License Plate 1469," and brought him to Ding Ling's home, which a "coward and traitor" had given up.

Ding Ling "opened at the knock," and was at once "seized" and "marched down the stairs" and away.

Then a man "tall and thin, keyed up," came into Ding Ling's home and "got into a scuffle" with one of the two agents staking the place out. The "tall" but "brittle" man "kicked over the agent, then vaulted onto the windowsill and threw himself down from the fourth floor." The agents scrambled "out the door to collect the body, afraid that word of a kidnapping in the foreign concession would get about."

Smedley said the "coward and traitor" was Feng Da, Ding Ling's "private secretary and translator."

Ding Ling said Feng Da had quietly "come into" her "life" after her husband Hu Yepin was killed, at her "loneliest and most desolate" time. Ding Ling said: "He was a stranger; I didn't know him at all. He helped me with a steady sort of way of living. He had no warmth and no light, and he couldn't draw me to him, but he didn't frighten me and he didn't disturb me. He was a bachelor who had never been in love; he only worked, quietly. He had been Smedley's private secretary, an ordinary member of the League of Left-Wing Social Scientists. He had already joined the Party. He had had a good salary once, a hundred yuan a month. Later he gave the post up and went to work at the Workers' and *Peasants' News* Agency under the Party Center's propaganda department, drawing fifteen yuan a month for his keep." (Ding Ling, 1984)

Jiang Zulin, the son of Hu Yepin and Ding Ling, recalled: "On the morning of May 14, 1933, before Feng Da went out, he made a promise to my mother: to be home by twelve noon at the latest. If he was not back by then, she was not to wait but to leave at once and to notify the organization and the comrades concerned. So when noon had passed and my mother still saw no sign of him, she meant to get away from the house as fast as she could—but then Pan Zinian came. He came and then wouldn't go; he heard my mother out, and still was in no hurry to leave; he just sat himself down on the sofa reading the paper, and my mother couldn't very well hurry him along, and could only keep him company and chat."

Ding Ling recalled: "A moment later I suddenly heard a jumble of footsteps on the stairs, and I knew at once that things had gone wrong. The door banged open, and three strangers crowded in together. I understood; Pan Zinian understood too. We both sat still and said nothing. The one at their head, a tall man, went straight to my writing desk—my desk was by the window. One man kept guard at the door, and one went through the bookshelves. Later I learned that the agent at their head was Ma Shaowu, a great traitor."

Ma Shaowu first took Ding Ling to a "black den" "by the Huangpu River." "At dawn the next day" she was "sent onto a train," "with a great retinue front and back," and taken under guard to Nanjing.

Ding Ling recalled: "Around noon the train pulled into Nanjing station. Nanjing was the seat of the Kuomintang's central government, the headquarters for the slaughter of the revolutionary people. As though welcoming some Kuomintang dignitary, a great crowd surged up and pressed in close to gawk at me, like people at a monkey. My escorts bundled me into a big bus, and it drove to the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters."

Jiang Zulin said: "After my mother was taken under guard to Nanjing, Ma Shaowu never appeared again—until the 'Red Squad's' purge killed Ma Shaowu, and the papers dragged the two of them together once more."

### 3. Rumor Kills

Later in 1933, in "a room in a Soviet sanatorium in the Caucasus," Smedley saw "License Plate 1469" turn up again.

Smedley said that while "leafing through the latest Western-language papers from Shanghai," she came upon "a Kuomintang intelligence officer famous for 'going into the tiger's den to track down and hunt Communists'"—that is, the "lapsed Communist" Ma Shaowu—who, "as the car he was riding in passed the Baochaiyuan in the International Settlement, was shot dead on the spot along with his driver." Four days later, "four Blue Shirt ruffians" used quick-firing pistols to shoot down her "friend" Yang Xingfo "on the steps of the Academia Sinica."

Smedley said Ma Shaowu rode "a large sedan, license number 1469." This is at odds with the facts—at least, with the facts as the papers of the day had them. At the time the papers quoted Ma Shaowu's driver (who had not, as Smedley had it, been "shot dead on the spot" alongside Ma Shaowu) as saying that the car Ma Shaowu rode that night was a police vehicle carrying two plates: one, No. 4223, issued specially by the Municipal Public Security Bureau, and one, No. 1038, issued in the citywide series.

Ding Ling learned that Ma Shaowu was dead no sooner than Smedley did. She said she was being held at the time by Xu Enzeng in Nanjing, shut up in "a desolate courtyard grown over everywhere with a layer of green moss," with Gu Shunzhang in charge of her. Xu Enzeng let her read some "old classic novels" and "new magazines." On an out-of-date sheet of *Commercial News* used "to wrap a book," she read a piece of "news" about herself that was "sheer fabrication, long and very detailed." Ding Ling said: "This piece made up the lie that after my arrest I had not only turned myself in but had taken up with Ma Shaowu, the traitor and agent who had come to arrest me; that Ma Shaowu was later dealt with by the Communists and died outside the door of a prostitute he was involved with on Sanma Road in Shanghai; and that this assassination too had something to do with me. It said all sorts of things about me now, painting me as a shameless, degraded woman."

I could not find the sheet of *Commercial News* that Ding Ling "remembered so clearly." But in the *L'Impartial* of June 25, 1933, I found the grounds for "Ding Ling already shot": that "on her arrest Ding Ling at once declared her willingness to turn herself in, and lured Ma Shaowu into cohabiting with her"; that "in the killing of Ma Shaowu, the threads of the thing implicated Ding." And in the *China Times* of June 28, 1933, I found that paper's rebuttal of "Ma Shaowu cohabited with Ding as soon as he arrested her" and "in the killing of Ma Shaowu, the threads implicated Ding." The *China Times* held that the claim "that Ding's killing fell right after she took up with Ma Shaowu" was "purely an attempt, by base means, to cover up his own crimes under the criminal law."

Ding Ling said: "The Kuomintang has butchered patriots and revolutionary youth by the thousands and tens of thousands with broadswords and machine guns; and now they mean to use vile, venomous rumors to murder in spirit an unarmed woman of letters, a revolutionary woman writer with a name in society. These venomous, vile tricks are plainly staged and manufactured by someone behind the scenes."

The *China Times* said: "In slandering Miss Ding, they seek only to cover their own crimes."

In fact the "venomous, vile" "slander"—"on her arrest Ding Ling at once declared her willingness to turn herself in, and lured Ma Shaowu into cohabiting with her," and the rest—was indeed, as Ding Ling and the *China Times* said, "staged and manufactured by someone behind the scenes." The "behind-the-scenes" hands that "staged and manufactured" that "venomous, vile" "slander" were Xu Enzeng, Gu Shunzhang and their kind.

Xu Enzeng, Gu Shunzhang and their kind, even as they turned black to white and muddied the public's eyes and ears, flinging basin after basin of filth at "an unarmed woman of letters, a revolutionary woman writer with a name in society," were assigning another high agent, cold and ruthless, to take Ma Shaowu's place and "succeed to his post."

That man was Qian Yizhang.

But "about two months on—August the twelfth," Xu Enzeng lamented, not without dejection: "Mr. Qian went to a hotel to meet someone; just as he was about to step into the elevator, ruffians lying in wait attacked him and he died."

### 4. A Place to Die

Xu Enzeng said: "This string of casualties—the last two cases above all, which struck directly at the chiefs we had sent out—was in every case worked out and set up by close calculation, the place and time chosen so as to be almost impossible to guard against. It threw the rest of the staff into unease, and everyone's nerves were badly on edge. Those who had come over from the Communist side, or had taken part in the wrecking of Communist underground bodies, feared for their lives all the more; they dared not stir out of doors all day, for none could tell when he might become the Red Squad's next target. Everyone lived in taut dread, hard put to look to himself, and of course quite lost the power to hit back at the enemy."

No one saw more clearly than Xu Enzeng what the deaths of Ma Shaowu and his successor meant. Xu Enzeng said: "The deaths of the two, Mr. Ma and Mr. Qian, were plainly the Communists serving warning on the whole body of my colleagues: 'Shanghai is your place to die; whoever comes, this is how it will be.'"

Xu Enzeng had no wish to stand by and watch his subordinates blanch at the mere name of the tiger, lose their nerve at a rumor, and be so wrought up in "taut dread" as to refuse a Shanghai posting. So he racked his brains and, at great pains, played a move more sinister and more venomous still. The move was this: he "chose four bold, careful operatives, split them into two teams, and gave them a task—to get close to the figures of the underworld." Xu Enzeng "gave them some money and had them eat and drink to the full, hit the gambling dens, chase women, brawl over face, and put on a show of not caring a whit."

Xu Enzeng said: "A month and a half later, one of the teams had indeed struck up an acquaintance with a Red Squad section leader, 'Little Shandong.' Having this 'foot in the door,' they worked hard to hold on to 'Little Shandong,' and, half by design and half not, did him a good many favors by the code of the underworld, so that in 'Little Shandong's' eyes they passed for staunch, loyal friends worth having; and so the two grew closer by the day, and the friendship deeper."

Jing Shenghong, a professor of history at Nanjing Normal University, has another version: "The Zhongtong's Shanghai district sent in a small agent named Zhang Asi, who came on as a factory worker, feigned revolution, bored his way into the CCP's underground bodies, and got assigned to 'Red Squad' work. As an ordinary member, Zhang Asi could not go about the streets as he pleased; ordinarily he had to stay hidden alone in the quarters the 'Red Squad' arranged for him, and only when there was a task would someone be sent, at short notice, to tell him to come along. So although he took part in several operations, he neither understood the 'Red Squad's' dispositions and plans nor knew where its leaders lived." (Jing Shenghong, 2004)

By this time it was already sometime in July or August of 1934.

Xu Enzeng decided to draw in the net, with "Little Shandong" as the breach.

A young man named "Xiong Guohua" now surfaced, and at dawn on September 15 of that year he hurried into the Qianji Hotel on Shanghai's Sima Road and asked the front desk for a single room on the second floor.

On the origins of "Xiong Guohua," accounts vary. Yang Guanghua, for instance, says he was "Old Gong"; Zhang Shenchuan says he was Weng Ying (real name Zhu Wenyuan, a native of Changshu, Jiangsu), the first political commissar of the Third Bureau (Communications and Liaison) of the CCP Central Military Commission; Jing Shenghong, again, says he came from "the Northeast." Jing Shenghong writes: "In fact 'Xiong Guohua' was only the man's temporary alias. He had joined a CCP body in his early years. After the September 18 Incident the organization sent him to the Northeast to join the anti-Japanese volunteer army. Later he fled to Shanghai, wormed his way into the CCP's underground body, and took on the underground body's all-important work as 'lookout.' But the man was lazy in his work and fond of good food and drink. On June 26, 1934, through his slack and careless work, he brought about an extremely grave wrecking and an incalculable loss to the leading organs of the CCP's Shanghai underground."

The "extremely grave wrecking and incalculable loss" Jing Shenghong speaks of is the arrest of Li Zhusheng, secretary of the CCP's Shanghai Central Bureau, Li Dezhao, its secretary-general, and others.

Jing Shenghong says Li Zhusheng, Li Dezhao and the others were arrested because Xiong Guohua "drank alone," got himself "dead drunk" by himself, and failed to pass on "to the Central Bureau" in time the "extremely important arrest intelligence and list of names to be taken" that "an intelligence officer planted in the concession's special-district court" had sent out ahead.

Jing Shenghong writes: "Having made so grave an error, Xiong Guohua showed not a shred of self-blame or repentance, but instead resisted the organization's criticism and review, threatened and bullied the Party body, and declared he would inform on it to the enemy. Before long he did indeed slip off and turn himself in to the Zhongtong's Shanghai agency. Only because the CCP underground people in touch with him mostly used aliases, mostly kept single-line contact, and had already taken some precautions and shifts, his defection did not at once do great harm. So Ji Yuanpu, Han Da and the other heads of the Zhongtong's Shanghai district instructed Xiong Guohua not to give away that he was a traitor, but to stay on inside the CCP body to spy—to be a mole—and to help the Zhongtong wreck the newly formed CCP Shanghai Bureau and the CCP Jiangsu Provincial Committee."

Xu Enzeng said that, using Xiong Guohua as bait, he laid a trap and "deliberately put it about" that Xiong Guohua had already "secretly" sworn "allegiance" to him and was "quietly guiding the security men in wrecking the Communists' 'central organs.'"

Chen Tongsheng, for his part, says Xiong Guohua had long since "been arrested by the Kuomintang and, under the enemy's threats and inducements, betrayed the revolution—giving up the addresses and leaders of the Party's organs and serving as the enemy agency's eyes, to carry out wrecking against the Party. To protect the safety of the Party's head organs, the 'dog-beating corps' led by comrades Zhao Xuan, Kuang Hui'an, Meng Huating and others resolved to send Hu Lingwu to carry out the task of putting the traitor to death." (Chen Tongsheng, 1959)

Jing Shenghong says Zhao Xuan, Kuang Hui'an, Meng Huating and the others "drew up a careful assassination plan aimed at Xiong Guohua's recent secretive, wary habits. They had an underground Party member known by the alias 'Baben,' who was on familiar and standing terms with Xiong Guohua, send word to him through an intermediary in coded language, saying the Shanghai Bureau's new leader wished to meet and talk with him in person; Xiong was asked to take a single room at the Qianji Hotel in Zhaojin Lane on Sima Road in the British Concession on September 15 and wait there, under the name 'Xiong Guohua,' and at some hour in those few days the Shanghai Bureau's new leader would come secretly to the room to meet him and hold an important talk."

Xiong Guohua went gladly, and stayed a full day in the Qianji Hotel, going out only once, in the evening.

The next day Xiong Guohua again locked himself in and shut himself up for a day. Only at eleven at night did he have the hotel's tea-boy bring in a bowl of shredded-pork noodle soup.

Half an hour later, two strapping men in black short jackets came to the Qianji Hotel. They went straight upstairs, knocked open Room 34, the single suite where Xiong Guohua was staying, and then came a double burst of pistol fire, a hail of shots, and Xiong Guohua, bleary with sleep, was riddled bloody and dropped where he lay, not to rise.

That was the version in the papers of the day.

Chen Tongsheng's version is that only one man went, Hu Lingwu. "Hu himself was a gendarme by background, an opportunist who had wormed into the Party; he fired one shot at Xiong Guohua but missed the vital spot, and fled in a panic."

### 5. As Through an Empty Land

Chen Tongsheng's version is close to Xu Enzeng's. Xu Enzeng said Xiong Guohua "was ambushed in his lodgings, but the bullet took him in the leg and he did not die."

Jing Shenghong's version runs: "The Red Squad party that carried out the killing of Xiong Guohua at the Qianji Hotel numbered five in all, led by Kuang Hui'an himself; the other four were Zhao Xuan, Meng Huating, Zhu Jinming, and Zhang Asi (nicknamed Pockmark Zhang), a Red Squad member called in at the last to help."

By Jing Shenghong's account, this Zhang Asi nicknamed "Pockmark Zhang" must be the very "small agent" who was sent in by the "Zhongtong's Shanghai district," "came on as a factory worker, feigned revolution, bored his way into the CCP's underground bodies, and got assigned to 'Red Squad' work."

All the big papers of the day said Xiong Guohua, hit by three bullets, was rushed to Renji Hospital and put in Bed 18, Ward 145, on the ground floor, with three men keeping guard in shifts at the door and "special detectives keeping watch twenty-four hours a day, every day."

Chen Tongsheng said: "The imperialists' and the Kuomintang's police, gendarmes and agents reckoned this a fine chance to uncover our Party's underground armed body. They took Xiong Guohua to Renji Hospital and posted plenty of spies to watch our movements. What the enemy went in for was the trick of 'spreading the net and waiting for the fish,' meaning to wreck our underground armed body. And Sheng Zongliang, because Xiong Guohua knew him and he feared Xiong would bring the enemy to seize him—for his own personal safety, giving no thought to the safety of the Special Branch comrades—used his authority as secretary to order the Special Branch comrades to finish the task of putting Xiong Guohua to death within a week."

Sheng Zongliang, that is Sheng Zhongliang, also called Sheng Yue and also Faqiao, had studied and worked six years at Moscow Sun Yat-sen University, returned to China in January 1933, and was at the time secretary of the CCP's Shanghai Central Bureau.

Sheng Zhongliang said: "As soon as I got back to Shanghai from Russia in January 1933, I became head of the propaganda department and took part in the work of the Central Shanghai Bureau." (Sheng Yue, 1980)

Huang Yaomian said that after Sheng Zhongliang turned traitor he once "gave an anti-Communist speech at the French police station in Shanghai." Speaking as "acting secretary of the Communist Central Bureau," he lectured them—the men of the "Youth League Central Bureau": "By now the Party's Central Bureau and the League's Central Bureau are both all but wrecked; even the barest funds for activity are hard to keep up, now that the tie with the international side is broken. So I advise you to leave the Communist Party early and look for another way to live. Otherwise you'll freeze or starve to death, or be shot; and who will count you martyrs then?" (Huang Yaomian, 1987)

Chen Tongsheng said that to carry out Sheng Zhongliang's order, Kuang Hui'an, Meng Huating and the others "held many discussions. After repeated close reconnaissance, they made a very careful arrangement."

The "careful arrangement" was this: "Kuang Hui'an was overall commander of the operation; Meng Huating was to provide cover; Little Zhu was to snipe at any police-station patrol car so that Zhao Xuan could get away."

Chen Tongsheng said: "On September 26, comrade Zhao Xuan went alone into Renji Hospital, where the enemy had spread its net wide. He got himself up as a rich merchant, rode in a private rickshaw, carried fresh flowers in his hands, and strode grandly upstairs to visit the patient. Comrade Zhao Xuan went into the first-class ward where Xiong Guohua lay, and with a single shot killed Xiong on the spot. A 'bodyguard' protecting Xiong nearby had only just drawn his gun when Zhao dropped him. As Zhao walked out the hospital gate, a gate patrolman was flustered, at a loss what to do; Zhao Xuan grabbed hold of him and said, 'There's been an assassination upstairs—quick, go phone the police station!' Zhao Xuan walked off hand in hand with the patrolman, and of course no one suspected him, and he left the scene without the least trouble and got clean away."

Chen Tongsheng also said: "Because Zhao Xuan had done the job so quickly, they all shifted safely to a wine-house on Sima Road to watch the patrolmen searching the passersby. These dull, blockheaded patrolmen made a fuss for an hour or two and of course got nothing."

But the more consistent version in the papers of the day is that around 3:40 that afternoon four "Red Squad" men came to the inpatient wing of Renji Hospital. They were seen to be "two in Zhongshan suits, one in a long black gown, one in a long blue cotton gown, aged about twenty to thirty." When the gatekeeper of the inpatient wing asked them to show their little wooden visitor's tags, two of them at once drew pistols, forced the gatekeeper into the porter's room, and, "together with the staff in the telephone room and the rest, held them under guard, letting no one stir, and jammed the telephone to cut off all calls." The other two "went straight up to the first floor, burst into Ward 145, and stepped up to Bed 18."

At the time Xiong Guohua "was still lying on the bed; seeing men enter and just about to challenge them, he was met as the two drew their pistols and each fired a shot into him, both to the head."

On September 27, 1934, a report in *Shen Bao* dwelt in particular on how "a detective and a patrolman were also cruelly killed." *Shen Bao* said that Detective No. 253 of the Laozha police station, on guard over Ward 145 on the ground floor, was at the time "sitting idle in the reception room below when he suddenly heard shots from Ward 145, and, knowing something was wrong, snatched out the pistol at his side and stepped out of the reception room," running smack into the two "Red Squad" men coming out of Ward 145; in the narrow passage he was shot through a vital spot on the spot by their "merciless fire." Then another patrolman, No. 721 of the French concession police, seeing which way things were going, "turned to flee," and was shot dead by three rounds "clean through the chest" at the flower bed in front of the ward building. "By the time the station got word and rushed a full detail of armed Chinese and Western detectives to the chase on every side," the four "Red Squad" men had long since "passed as through an empty land, walking calmly out of the hospital," "like the wild swan winging off into the far sky," and had vanished into the boundless sea of people.

### 6. The Crack Shot

The whole concession was scared out of its wits by the deafening gunfire over the busy streets.

Xu Enzeng said: "The International Settlement's public order, which had always prided itself on prosperity and calm, was so thrown by this disturbance that it was at a complete loss; the whole city was in an uproar at once, and the papers, Chinese and foreign, filled with reports and discussion of the affair. The British, French and Japanese concession authorities held an emergency meeting the moment it happened, and agreed as one that to crack this political organization swiftly and thoroughly they must have the cooperation of the Chinese security authorities, since, judging by past cases, they believed that on matters of this kind we held more material and experience. So they sent men to negotiate with us; and we were of course willing to accept the concession authorities' request and help them break the case."

The two sides thereupon "signed" a "written agreement of temporary cooperation."

Under the "agreement," "all the detectives of the British and French concessions" would, "within the twenty-four hours of the day the case was worked," come completely "under" Xu Enzeng's "command."

Under the "agreement," the concession authorities would, "within one week of the operation," hand over "all prisoners" for Xu Enzeng to dispose of.

The concession authorities, forever making a show of fairness, detachment, and strict neutrality, now stripped off the mask of hypocrisy altogether.

Xu Enzeng then showed his true, murderous face, ordering Ji Yuanpu, Han Da and the others of the Shanghai district to send in two more agents disguised as peddlers to lie in wait near Zhang Asi's home.

Jing Shenghong says the two agents "one got up as a fruit-seller, one as a shoe-mender. Zhang Asi would pretend to buy fruit at the fruit stall and take the chance to pass word and swap intelligence. In this way they finally got one thing clear: the head of the 'Red Squad' was a Cantonese named Kuang Hui'an."

Kuang Hui'an, whose real name was Gong Changrong, was a "crack shot." He fired with both hands, and never missed; his nickname was "Old Cantonese."

One day Kuang Hui'an came to see Zhang Asi on some errand. Zhang Asi signaled the agent got up as a shoe-mender to follow him.

Jing Shenghong says the agent disguised as a shoe-mender "found that Kuang Hui'an, ahead of him, was walking a zigzag. This was a way of walking the CCP's underground workers often used at the time: now onto the sidewalk on this side of the street, now onto the sidewalk on that side, so as to spot a tail and shake it off. The cunning, seasoned agent kept after Kuang Hui'an on the zigzag without turning a hair, and never let Kuang Hui'an notice a thing. And so he at last found one of Kuang Hui'an's haunts."

Kuang Hui'an and his wife Lin Ermei lived on the third floor of the Xinwenxiang silver shop at 112 Rue Ratard in the French Concession; this was the "Red Squad's" secret arms cache, holding a large store of light and heavy weapons and a good deal of printed propaganda besides.

On September 27, 1934, Kuang Hui'an and his comrades were dealt a shattering blow.

Xu Enzeng said: "The operation that day began at seven in the morning and ended at half past eleven, carried out at the same time in six different places. If one gave this on-the-ground material to a first-rate playwright, it would not be hard to make several splendid 'cops-and-robbers bloodbath' film stories out of it—above all the scene of taking the Red Squad chief Kuang Hui'an, at which the English friend who drove the car for us stared dumbstruck at the time."

Kuang Hui'an, umbrella in hand, walked out of the silver shop and was at once shadowed hard by two agents. The tailing agent signaled the other two agents in a car parked by the road to follow up in support and try to make a kidnapping. Kuang Hui'an gave a flick of his umbrella, broke into a long stride, turned his head and hurried off, running fast toward Avenue Joffre with its thronging crowds—only to be surrounded on all sides by a dozen agents lying in wait, his way blocked. Kuang Hui'an did not flinch in the peril; he fought back with all his strength, knocking several agents to the ground one after another, and was taken only when the odds against him were too great.

Jing Shenghong said: "The agents and patrolmen marched Kuang Hui'an off and rushed his lodgings on the third floor of the Xinwenxiang silver shop. This was the Red Squad's arms store, so the moment Kuang was marched up to the third floor he began shouting to warn his wife, Lin Ermei. The agents found the room where he lived. Kuang's wife Lin Ermei leapt to the bedside in a single bound, tore open the mattress and pulled out a Mauser pistol, ready to resist arrest. A Western patrolman sprang and pinned Lin Ermei to the floor, wrested the weapon from her hand, and cuffed her. Then they set about the search and turned up a great quantity of arms and ammunition."

Chen Tongsheng, for his part, says the arrest of Kuang Hui'an and the others had a direct bearing on Sheng Zhongliang's defection. Chen Tongsheng said: "The killing of the traitor Xiong Guohua threw the enemy into a spell of alarm, and after Sheng Zongliang and the others defected they gave the enemy further leads. Once, several Special Branch people were meeting at home under cover of a mahjong game when the enemy surrounded them. Because it was a surprise attack there was no resisting; the alarm signal was not taken down, so those who arrived later were arrested one after another too, and the Special Branch's main cadres all suffered losses."

### 7. The Martyr Forever Praised

After Kuang Hui'an and his comrades were arrested, they underwent, again and again, bamboo needles, the whip, the tiger bench, the "safety umbrella," chili water and other tortures. But though their flesh split and their bodies were one wound, Kuang Hui'an and the others held firm and would not yield.

The agents brought Zhang Asi in to confront him; Kuang Hui'an glared, eyes wide, and roared: "Get out! Get out of my sight!" The other "Red Squad" men said too: "We're the Communist Party's dog-beating squad! Kill us if you're going to—why all the questions!"

An agent boss said: "Just tell us your central organs, tell us who gives you your intelligence, and we'll guarantee you a bright future and your whole family reunited."

Kuang Hui'an said with a cold laugh: "Your own futures aren't bright—what have you got to give anyone else?"

Chen Tongsheng said: "After the Special Branch comrades were arrested, apart from that Hu Lingwu, a gendarme by background, who defected, every comrade was set on death. The enemy handed them over from the concession to the Kuomintang's Public Security Bureau. The Kuomintang hoped to get from their mouths material on our Party's underground body. In Shanghai they had undergone every kind of torture—Huating alone was put to the electric shock eight times. They said not a word and wrote not a character."

Jing Shenghong, though, says the one who "could not bear the torture and broke and confessed" was Chen Junming.

On October 29, 1934, the criminal court of the Second Branch of the Shanghai High Court formally convened to "try in open court" Kuang Hui'an and the others, charging them with "grave capital crimes," including "twice plotting the murder of Xiong Guohua," the successive "killing" of a detective and a patrolman, and the premeditated shooting of "the former political secret agent of the Municipal Public Security Bureau, Ma Shaowu."

When the judge asked Kuang Hui'an and the others whether they acknowledged their guilt, confessed, and submitted, their answer was: "To rid the people of a scourge—where is the crime in that? In killing Ma Shaowu and Xiong Guohua we most certainly were not venting private spite or avenging private wrongs. We were upholding justice for our cause. We were exacting a blood debt for our comrades. Ma Shaowu was a great agent; his two hands were covered in our comrades' blood. Xiong Guohua was a shameful traitor. He harbored dark designs and maliciously framed revolutionary comrades—truly a monster of wickedness, his crimes brimming over."

On December 6 the criminal court of the Second Branch of the Shanghai High Court formally sentenced the four—Kuang Hui'an, Zhao Xuan, Meng Huating and Zhu Jinming—to death, and afterward handed them over to the Nanjing National Government, taking them under guard from Shanghai to Nanjing.

Hearing that Kuang Hui'an and the others had arrived, Xu Enzeng came in person at once to urge them to surrender, hoping they might have a change of heart and be of use to him. Kuang Hui'an, catching him off guard, snatched the pistol from the guard beside him and fired—but the round jammed and would not go off. Kuang Hui'an threw the pistol down, not without regret, and said to Xu Enzeng coldly: "Lucky for you—you've dodged a bullet!"

Having dodged a bullet, Xu Enzeng did not give up, and still saw to it that his men treated Kuang Hui'an and the others well.

Chen Tongsheng said: "Once they were brought to Nanjing, they began to be 'well treated.' A representative of the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters gave them his word that they would not be sentenced, and could even be assigned work—saying they should put their talents to use against the Japanese collaborators. Huating and Zhao Xuan thought this so much lying nonsense, but Kuang Hui'an held that if the chance came, and they talked their way out, they could surely do the Party some more meaningful work. He proposed a feigned surrender, to make up some plausible stuff to deceive the enemy. Huating and Zhao Xuan were never much in favor. Kuang was the one in charge, and left it all to him."

Kuang Hui'an was too naïve, and fell into the enemy's trap.

Their feigned surrender did not deceive the sinister, treacherous enemy.

Having forced them to give up what was of real value, the sinister, treacherous enemy took them to the military-justice office.

The military judge said: "Every one of you is up for several death sentences. If you want to live, your only hope is a pardon from the National Government. If you want us to plead for you and ask the National Government for a pardon, you must first help us take all the leaders of the Shanghai Communist Party."

Kuang Hui'an cursed roundly on the spot—cursing the enemy, and cursing himself.

Meng Huating comforted him earnestly.

That night Meng Huating tossed and turned, unable to sleep, and could not help waking Chen Tongsheng, who shared his cell, and telling him the whole thing from beginning to end.

Meng Huating said, sick at heart: "In this fight of ours, a few of us have played Jiang Boyue. But I don't believe that rubbish about 'man proposes, heaven disposes'; the main thing was that our fighting experience was too thin, and that certain comrades still held some illusions about the enemy. This is a bitter, bitter lesson!"

Chen Tongsheng said he could not bring himself to believe Kuang Hui'an would make so elementary a mistake.

Meng Huating said Kuang Hui'an, "though a worker by origin, had been away from production too long; he'd knocked about the world and had too many illusions."

Meng Huating said again: "We ought to give our lives for the Party; but that we should bring the Party some disgrace before we do—that is what I cannot be reconciled to." And so he wished he could cut open his heart and show it to Chen Tongsheng, insisting that Chen Tongsheng believe in their innocence, and stressing over and over: "The enemy got from our mouths not one scrap of material bearing on the safety of the Party's secret body. Our hearts are with the Party forever."

Chen Tongsheng felt as though a knife were turning in him, and could not hold back his tears.

Meng Huating told him to remember hard: "Whatever happens, you must not shed tears in front of the enemy, and you must not grieve over a comrade's death. What our people need, always, is not to pity one another but to hearten one another!"

Chen Tongsheng did not know what words he could find to hearten Meng Huating. Chen Tongsheng said: "You don't need me to hearten you—you hearten me, always. And believe this too: without you, without me, without all of us who have been arrested, the Chinese revolution will still win. We march on the bloody tracks of the comrades who fell, and I believe still more people will follow us. However brutal the enemy, they are forever the fewest of the few. We fight for the interests of the great majority; we will not perish, and we will surely win!"

Meng Huating nodded and said, from the heart: "Maybe the road we've walked has not been quite right, but our general direction is right. To overthrow imperialism, to root out the feudal forces, to strive for socialism and for communism—that direction is correct. As for the road we walk, it seems we still have to feel our way, still have to pay a price. I believe that if we feel it out right, we'll be close to victory. What a pity, though—I've read too little, and too little of what is of use to the revolution. It has cost me dear!"

At four in the afternoon on April 13, 1935, the four—Kuang Hui'an, Zhao Xuan, Meng Huating and Zhu Jinming—were "bound with hemp rope and garrotted at the stake" on the execution ground of the military-justice office of the Nanjing Gendarmerie Command.

Chen Tongsheng said: "As they took their leave of us, the whole place fell silent, and saw them off with tears."

Zhao Xuan raised his two cuffed hands to his heart and, with his eyes, paid everyone a last salute.

Meng Huating said: "Such a pity! We aren't taking leave of you all to go to the battlefield—though the Japanese bandits have already taken nearly half of China, and the battlefield too needs people like us."

Just then the whole place was so still that a single falling leaf could be heard; only the chaplain murmured his prayer to one side: "May the good return to the presence of the Lord…"

His prayer done, the chaplain went to sprinkle holy water on Kuang Hui'an and the rest and have them repent of their sins; they cried out with one voice: "Down with imperialism!"

They said, too, that going together they would not be lonely. Their one last word was that they be buried together in one place, true to the pledge of a shared cell in life and a shared grave in death.

Only then did the chaplain believe that there really were, in this world, "men greater than Christ at his Passion."

Everyone heard him tell it to whomever he met, and asked him to explain what so moved him.

The chaplain said, with feeling: "You've read the Bible. Before his Passion Christ had already fainted, and did not even know when others set the crown of thorns on him. Today I saw these men take their leave of us, clear-headed, every one, as though we would meet again tomorrow. What they believe must be a truth mightier than the doctrine of Christ. There truly is in people an unimaginable goodness and greatness; and those who destroy men greater than Christ are to be cursed through all the ages. As the martyr is forever praised."
