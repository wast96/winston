## Chapter 12

We went by way of North Sichuan Road to the station—a quarter I had not once set foot in since the fall of Greater Shanghai. I saw the hoardings advertising enemy goods, the enemy's sentries, and the grey shadow of ruined walls. The nation's rage and grief rose all at once into my heart; I felt a heavy remorse, and repented of the dissipation of my recent life. So it was that, in the first-class carriage, I sank into a dispirited silence.

Beyond the window lay fields I knew well. Years ago, how many times had I sat in a carriage like this under a bright field-sun, leaning at the window to gaze at the deep-blue sky and the emerald fields. I thought of the people there, among whom were my own kin and friends; they tilled the soil in peace, the farmers singing, the farm-women carrying rice-baskets, the herd-boys astride the backs of oxen cheering at the passing train; and there were the softly flowing streams where, in summer, how many children swam and fished, and the water-wheels along the banks, where men trod the axles to irrigate the fields. In the far woods lay quiet villages, and when the train went by, the women in the farmyards at the village edge would shade their eyes against the sky-light and gaze into the distance, seeming each time to be waving to me. Now the rails and the train were a world set apart from the fields; barbed wire fenced off the train's passage; on board were the enemy's gunmen, forever on guard against a raid by the peasants—and we, sitting face to face in such a train, going to Hangzhou to while away our discontent: was this a thing that could be forgiven?

Just as I was thinking this, an enemy gendarme came to inspect our passes, and more shame and remorse welled up in me; I laid all the blame on Mei Yingzi's absurd travelling scheme.

But Hangzhou was reached at last. Getting off the train, we went straight to the Xiling Hotel; and I looked out on the lake and hills I had so long been parted from, where once I had laid up my loves and my dreams, where so many friendships and so many poems lay silent. The moon tonight was near the full, and the stars too were brilliant; how many scenes of the same mood there were for me to call to mind! Of the kin and friends of those days, most were now scattered—some dead, some gone to work in the rear, some at the front killing the enemy. Their houses were burned, their bedchambers turned into stables for the enemy's horses; how many changes there were among them, for me to grieve over and mourn.

At the hotel Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping shared a room, while Stephen and I each had one of our own; the night was already far gone, and we went early to our separate beds.

The weariness of travel, the desolation of my mood, and the wine of supper together sent me quickly to sleep. When I woke it was already eight; the sun shone straight into my room through the window, and a springtime gladness gave me a comfort past telling. Longing for the beauty of the lake and hills, I clung no longer to my dreams, but rose, washed, drank a cup of tea, and, seeing that the others still seemed asleep, walked out of the hotel alone, and quietly took the way toward Geling.

The discontent of how many years of city life found, only now, an easy breath; the spring frost on the grass was just melting, and a peculiar moisture and tenderness nestled close about me, so that I longed to strip off shoes and socks and taste again the feeling of my childhood. On the trees a green was already hanging out the tide of spring, and the sparrows chattered wildly on the branches, tasting in the sunlight the gladness of the season. Not a soul was on the mountain road; entranced, I rambled along it, and without knowing it had come a good way. Coming out from a thicket, I caught sight of the lake and hills on my right, and felt a longing to reach the summit and take in the old, famous view at a glance. Unawares I quickened my pace, and climbed straight on up. But after two turnings I suddenly saw that someone was ahead of me, going up at a slow pace, only to be hidden at once by the trees. As though spurred by a childhood spirit of rivalry, I hurried faster to catch up.

At last I made out the person again: it was a woman, and she too wore a great loose overcoat of yellow camel-hair—how like the costume of Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping! Could it be, then, Mei Yingzi or Bai Ping herself? I climbed faster. I could tell already that it must be one of the two; and so I slackened my pace, and, going by the judgement I had formed the night before at the Golden Gate of their build and proportions, tried to make out whether it was Bai Ping or Mei Yingzi. But the observation would not hold; at almost every step I changed my guess, and in the end I still could not be sure—I needed to look closer. So I quickened my pace, and when I was only a few feet behind her I saw the ring on her hand, and knew beyond doubt that it was Bai Ping. She seemed to be looking about her on every side, as if of a mind to turn her head; I crouched at once behind a tree, spied on her until she had gone straight on, and only then came out and hurried swiftly forward, hoping I might steal right up before her and give her a surprise on her climb. But there seemed no other passable path anywhere near, so I ran up dodging and darting, hoping to be found only when I could all but touch her; and at last, working my way up the left-hand slope by clinging to the tree-trunks, while she gazed off at the lake and hills on the right, I flew up the mountain path, stood at her right side, reached an arm round her body, took hold of her arm, and, my eyes on the lake and hills, said low:

"Bai Ping."

"…" She started a little, but turned her head, and said coolly: "So it's you!"

It was a voice, rich and drawling, that I did not know; a little startled, I turned to look—ah, it was Mei Yingzi.

"It's you!" I said, and felt of a sudden a great awkwardness, for to treat Mei Yingzi in such a posture was indeed to overstep the distance between us. I loosened my left hand and, with the greatest reluctance, took it from her; but she turned, and walked on ahead with her back to me, and so I followed her, going up on her left.

"You are up so early?" I asked.

"You are none too late yourself," she said gravely, her eyes on the way ahead.

"Did you sleep well last night?"

"Thanks to you, very well." She was cool and grave, her eyes glancing down at the ground and then lifting to the path ahead.

I seemed unable to find anything to say, and we walked in silence, step following step, slowly. The sun was warm and pleasant on me, the open space all round gave my eyes a sense of brightness, and the fresh air seemed to float away the foulness in my breast; but a bewitching fragrance filled me with a sweetness past telling, and I felt I must break the silence, come what might. I said:

"Did you often come to Hangzhou before?"

"Yes."

"You have not come for a long time?"

"That is so."

"You love such a lake and hills?"

She turned on me all at once a glance of extraordinary sharpness, and said with a mocking laugh:

"I love them as I love Bai Ping."

"…" I hung my head a long while, and thought out a more fitting thing to say: "Is it my rudeness just now, in calling you the wrong name?"

"What nonsense," she said, laughing, with a sharper mockery still.

"I see nothing to laugh at," I said. "When the two of you are dressed exactly alike, it is the commonest thing in the world for me to mistake you."

"But where is the rudeness in that?" she said.

"Then you have no cause to mock me," I said.

"It is only because I love Bai Ping," she said. "If on my account you were not to love Bai Ping, that would be a very laughable thing."

"I do not love you," I said; "but not to love you does not oblige me to love Bai Ping."

"If you have never loved Bai Ping, then you ought not to have crossed with her the sort of distance that lies between you and me."

I knew she meant the posture in which I had greeted her. But then she reached out her fingers gently and asked:

"Is this the ring you gave her?"

Mei Yingzi's hands, like narcissus flowers, truly added to the worth of my ring, and I felt even a longing to kiss them. I said:

"Yes—how does it come to be on your hand?"

"I said the ring was set in a charming way, and wanted to trade her a larger one of mine for it; she would not, but agreed to exchange them for a few days' wear." She held out her hand, made the ring flash, looked at it herself, and went on: "She would not part with it, saying it was because you had given it to her. But since you do not love her, have you the right to give her a ring?"

"But," I said, "do you suppose that to give a dancing-girl a ring must always carry some special meaning?"

"I had not thought you too were that kind of man," she said. "So toying with women is the theoretical foundation of your bachelorhood."

"I would not have you insult me so."

"But in any case,"—she seemed not to have heard me, and spoke in a gentler tone—"I hope you will not look on Bai Ping as you would on an ordinary dancing-girl."

"How I regard Bai Ping is not for you to know."

"But," she said, "the matter comes to only one point. If you love her—love her; give up your bachelorhood, take her into the interior, and live a more solid life. If you do not love her, then have less of this close intimacy with her."

"I do not know by what right or duty you meddle in my relations with Bai Ping."

"It is because I care for you both,"—her manner had grown very gentle—"and for Bai Ping most of all. She is a very young and clever child, and cares for you not a little. She knows a great many Japanese; if her feelings were deepened, and in the end she were dealt some blow, what road would be left for her to take?" She paused a moment, then all at once changed her tone, and said, clear and grave: "Have you thought of her livelihood? Can her income keep up such spending as ours?"

"…" I seemed to have something to say, but could bring nothing out, and fell silent. For a long, long while I felt an emptiness nothing could fill, and drew a deep sigh. Mei Yingzi's manner softened suddenly then; she took my arm, and said, warm and affectionate:

"Xu, I know you are a very clever man; so keep my words well in your heart, and think them over from time to time. Now let us make an end of this talk." As though nothing had passed, she recovered her ordinary manner, gripped my arm tighter, and quickened her pace toward the summit.

My thoughts still circled about what she had just said, but my feelings began to have room to spare for the open sky, the mellow sunlight, and the fresh scenery all around.

"Have you never made such an outing with Bai Ping?" Mei Yingzi asked suddenly.

"No," I answered lightly.

"I know now that the elements of love take in the whole of a life," she said, with feeling, as if to herself, and slowed her steps.

"Do you mean that such an outing can make people who do not love each other fall in love?"

"At the least it can give people who do not love each other the chance of it," she said.

"That is what everyone who pursues the other sex goes seeking."

"But for some it is easy to find," she said, "and for others hard."

"That is a matter bound up with money."

"And bound up with politics besides," she said, and made her pace slower still.

"So today's outing is the work of your political power?" And when I had done laughing I added: "Why not say it is the work of your love?"

"In Bai Ping's case the power of love, though it might well bring her here easily enough herself, could never so easily bring you here."

"You mean to say…"

"I mean that the man who got these passes for us brought Bai Ping here on an outing a month ago," she said, walking on with a laugh.

"And that has made you jealous?" I laughed deeper than she.

"The one who should be jealous is you," she said.

"I have told you already that I do not love her."

"Then was your jealousy over me yesterday a sign that you love me?"

"What I mean is this,"—I paid her words no heed, and went on—"that Bai Ping travels with people I detest for the sake of money, and travels with me for pleasure; and that, on the contrary, sets me at ease."

"But in fact, Bai Ping's feeling for you too arose out of your money."

"Money, money, politics—say what you please," I said. "But all these things can only help love, help happiness; they cannot buy love, nor can they buy happiness."

"Nor can they buy honour or learning," she said.

"Nor the heart, nor wisdom," I said.

"But they can buy a diamond ring."

"…" I made no sound.

"—And can make her believe you love her, and take an outing with you for pleasure."

"And what else?" I asked.

"This, though it is not political power, is political finesse."

"Go on," I said, seeing her pause a moment, yet seeming to have more to say.

"But,"—she softened her tone—"since a nimble political finesse has triumphed over political power, why use it only in the contests of love, and not in the contests of politics?"

"Xu!"

"Mei Yingzi!"

Someone was calling behind us; our talk broke off, and Mei Yingzi and I turned our heads. It was Bai Ping and Stephen—Bai Ping with Stephen's walking-stick in her hand—climbing the hill at a great pace.

We stood still to wait for them. The sun was high now, the scene all round most brilliant, and I felt at ease and warm. I took off my overcoat, found a rock nearby, spread the coat over it, and asked Mei Yingzi to sit. I sat beside her and watched Bai Ping and the others come up.

The sky was deep blue, and one or two white clouds drifted idly across it; the grey-green all around enclosed them now on this side, now on that. This was a beautiful pair in their youth, hastening on the spring colours of nature herself; they were nature's adornment, and nature was theirs.

I was reckoning up the day's itinerary, for today was my day to be host.
