## A Brief Account of Huang Jinrong

{d} Xiang Bo

Huang Jinrong, styled Jinyong, was the foremost of the "three big bosses" who lorded it for a time over the Bund of old Shanghai. He was born in Suzhou on 14 December 1868 (the first day of the eleventh month of the seventh year of the Qing Tongzhi reign).
The Huang family's ancestral home was Yuyao in Zhejiang. Huang's father, Bingquan, had been a constable of some note in the Yuyao county yamen. During the Tongzhi reign he was called to Suzhou to crack a case, and stayed on there as head constable of Suzhou prefecture, marrying a local woman née Zou.
Huang Jinrong's milk-name was "Monk," and because his face bore a few large pockmarks, the nicknames "Monk" and "Pockmarked Jinrong" arose from it.
In 1880, when Huang Jinrong was twelve, Huang Bingquan moved the family to Nanshi in Shanghai and opened a small teahouse at Sanpailou.
Huang Jinrong disliked study from boyhood, and as he grew he fell in among the local hoodlums, thieves, and gambling sharps, often stealing clothes from home to pawn them, and gathering others to gamble whenever he had a little money in hand, or carousing with prostitutes. In the end he joined in robbing the Cuihuatang mounting shop at the City God Temple, where he was serving his apprenticeship, and for this the owner dismissed him.
In 1892, at twenty-four, through his father's old connections, Huang Jinrong became a detective in the French Concession patrol house. His chief haunt was the "Jubao Teahouse," opened by a disciple of his near Zhengjiaqiao. Each morning he would sit in his fixed seat, brew a pot of tea, and those with leads to hand over, intelligence to pass, or terms to strike would come thronging in. He directed the "Thirty-Six Gang" of the wharves, the "Eight Gang" of the concession, and his own "Hundred and Eight Generals" to spy out and run down suspects in the service of the concession authorities; at the same time he arranged smuggling, kidnapping, and the like from this teahouse to line his own pockets. In the Shanghai French Concession the power of imperialism and the power of the gangs were thus fused into one, and police and hoodlums cast together into a single body.
Huang Jinrong's shrewd capability won the high regard of the concession authorities, who praised him as "the Great Wall of the concession's peace." In fact his protection of the foreign masters' persons and property reached far beyond the Shanghai French Concession. Once, a clerk of the French consulate-general in Shanghai, Fan Erdi, took his wife Fan Juli on an outing to Lake Tai and was carried off by the bandits who ranged over the lake. When the French Concession authorities asked Huang to arrange a rescue, Huang, after consulting Du Yuesheng and others, sent Gao Xinbao, who had ties to the Lake Tai bandit chiefs, to "pay respects to the mountain." In the end the bandit chiefs "Taibao Ah Shu" and "Zhulou Ah Mei" took not a cent and handed over the two French hostages.
After Huang Jinrong entered the patrol house, he rose from detective to detective-superior to inspector, and at last to the sole Chinese chief inspector in the French Concession police, deeply relied on by the concession authorities. After retiring in 1925, he continued to be retained as an adviser to the police. To reward his services, the Governor of Annam, France's plenipotentiary for East Asia, three times conferred on him first- and second-class gold and silver Orders of the Precious Star.
Shanghai in the 1920s was the chief land-and-water port for buying arms, raising war funds, gathering intelligence, procuring foreign goods, and trafficking opium; and so the Beiyang government, the inland warlords, the revolutionaries, and the mounted bandits all had more or less to do with the "three big bosses" Huang Jinrong, Du Yuesheng, and Zhang Xiaolin, while Huang and the others in turn saw to the comfort and safety in Shanghai of warlords from every quarter and of politicians down on their luck. When six cases of paintings, porcelain, and other antiques belonging to Yang Zhihou, chief of staff to the Fujian military governor Zhou Yinren, were stolen wholesale on reaching the Shanghai wharves, the Songhu garrison commissioner He Fenglin asked Huang Jinrong to help recover them, and in a mere three hours the whole lot was traced and returned. In June 1923, after Li Yuanhong was forced to resign the presidency and went south, he stayed three months in Shanghai as Huang Jinrong's guest, with Du Yuesheng and his "Little Eight Gang" assigned by Huang to guard him; the grateful former president had ten pure-gold medals struck to distribute to those concerned as thanks. After the Qi-Lu War of September 1924, the warlords ruling Shanghai changed hands often, but whoever held power, Huang and the others were courted by them all, engaged as councillors or appointed advisers. The Beiyang government once conferred on Huang Jinrong a fifth-class order of merit and the rank of colonel.
Trading on his position and power, and with the tacit consent of the concession authorities, Huang Jinrong had his followers escort opium shipments for the Shanghai opium dealers, reaping enormous profits. Later, with Du Yuesheng, Zhang Xiaolin, and others, he set up the "Sanxin Company" as a joint venture, and under the armed guard of the French Concession patrol house monopolized the opium trade of the concession; even He Fenglin and the police-and-army boss Yu Yefeng bought shares in turn. Protected as one by the warlords, the concession authorities, and the police and army, the Sanxin Company grew ever more prosperous and amassed a great fortune for Huang Jinrong.
Another of his ways of piling up wealth was to run gambling houses. As early as before 1917 he had opened, with his followers, the Xinjili gambling table, and it grew until they were jointly running five or six large tables; besides his monthly share of the profits from each, every table had to pay him a tribute of 100 yuan a day. Moreover, the hundreds of servants of the Huang household drew no wages from Huang Jinrong but went to the gambling tables for their pay. At the same time he opened, one after another, the Rixin and Daguanyuan bathhouses, the Rongji Grand Stage, the Huangjin Grand Theater, the Gongwutai, and others. In 1924, to enlarge the site of the Gongwutai, Huang, offering a removal fee of a mere two piculs' worth of rice money, demanded that Ye Qingxiang, owner of a hardware store on Central Jinling Road, move out within half a month. When Ye politely declined, Huang flew into a rage, cursed him roundly for failing to know when he was well off — refusing the toast only to drink the forfeit — and within three days Ye had been seized by the French patrol house. His wife ran everywhere to save him, but no one dared step forward to help, and in the end she could only go to the Huang mansion to weep and beg pardon and agree to give up the shop to the Gongwutai, whereupon Ye Qingxiang got his freedom back.
An empty-handed hoodlum, by way of opium, gambling, and extortion, came to own not only all the properties named above but dozens of lane-houses at Yuanchengli and Junpeili in Shanghai, and several hundred mu of good land in Weiting township outside Suzhou — a great magnate worth a vast fortune.
Huang Jinrong's followers reached into every corner and every trade of Shanghai, from warlords, politicians, lawyers, and pressmen and great merchants at the top down to menials, rickshaw men, and street hoodlums at the bottom; those who entered the Huang gate numbered in the thousands. In fact Huang had never formally joined either the Green Gang or the Hong Gang, yet he took name-cards and enrolled students in the gangs' name. Strictly speaking this broke the gang rules and ought to have brought severe punishment, and someone did once write to charge him with daring to pass himself off as a disciple of the Green Gang "Da"-generation man Zhang Jinghu to win an empty name; but relying on his standing among the Shanghai hoodlums, he came to no great harm by it. Only at the time of his retirement did Huang Jinrong get Du Yuesheng to press Zhang Jinghu into accepting his name-card and an initiation gift of 20,000 silver yuan, and so this "hollow one" Huang Jinrong at last turned the sham into fact and became a "Tong"-generation man of the Green Gang.
In March 1927, on the eve of Chiang Kai-shek's counter-revolutionary April 12 coup, Chiang sent Yang Hu, Chen Qun, and Wang Bailing in turn, on secret missions and in disguise, to slip into the Huang mansion and meet with Huang, Du, and Zhang to plot the anti-Communist purge. Though Huang Jinrong had by then retired to his home, he still saw serving Chiang Kai-shek as a splendid opportunity, declaring again and again that he would "help with all his might and never look back," would "do his utmost," and would "assist the Kuomintang central authorities in the general purge of the Communists." Following a set plan, they organized the "China Mutual Progress Society" to gather their partisans and drew up a plan of operations, at the same time commissioning foreign-firm compradors to buy up handguns and rifles and light and heavy machine guns. Trading further on his old ties with the French patrol house, Huang Jinrong made his mansion a hiding-place for the arms. On the night of April 11, on the pretext of important business to discuss, they lured Wang Shouhua, chairman of the Shanghai General Labor Union, to Du Yuesheng's house and murdered him. That night Huang, Du, and Zhang, with Yang Hu, Chen Qun, and Wang Bailing, made sacrifice to heaven and earth before a picture of "Liu, Guan, and Zhang swearing brotherhood in the Peach Garden" and drank the oath of brotherhood. On the morning of April 12, when the Mutual Progress Society attacked the strongpoints of the Shanghai workers' pickets, Huang Jinrong and the others even went to the front in person to direct the fighting and hearten their followers. The Shanghai Green and Hong gangs under Huang's command and the National Revolutionary Army directly commanded by Bai Chongxi — the one charging the workers' pickets with sword and gun in front, the other tricking the pickets into surrendering their arms under cover of "mediation" behind — seized the fruits of the Shanghai workers' three armed risings and buried the victory of the Northern Expedition. To reward Huang Jinrong and his followers for their signal service against the Communists, Chiang Kai-shek appointed Huang, Du Yuesheng, and Zhang Xiaolin major-general councillors of the National Revolutionary Army headquarters, and they became new grandees of the party-state and local men of note.
Toward Huang Jinrong, who had risen high by following Chiang Kai-shek in the purge, the French Concession authorities showed all the more esteem, and agreed to let him go on running gambling houses in the concession. Not long after April 12, Huang Jinrong, Zhang Xiaolin, and others opened the largest and most luxurious gambling house in Shanghai at No. 181 Avenue Foch (today's Central Yan'an Road), fully furnished with roulette, fantan, pai gow, mahjong, dice, and every other game, with pretty girls in attendance to wait on the players and pack their opium. Once this money-pit opened its doors, carriages and horses came thronging and gamblers gathered in crowds, giving Huang Jinrong and the others yet another source of wealth. In 1931, at Caohejing in Shanghai's southern suburbs, Huang Jinrong spent 3,500,000 silver yuan to build the Huang Family Garden, covering more than sixty mu and elegantly laid out with towers, halls, and pavilions, and Chiang Kai-shek even sent a specially brushed inscription reading "Learning, Conduct, Loyalty, Faith." From then on Huang Jinrong lived in seclusion and gave out that he had retired and would not meddle in outside affairs. In fact he never gave up his old trade of directing things from the shadows and taking his cut. The most notorious instance was the 1933 case in which Huang's grand-disciple Wang Wenkui kidnapped the young son of the great Shanghai pigment merchant Bei; Huang Jinrong came forward to "talk terms," and the Bei family paid 20,000 silver yuan to ransom the boy back, and gave Huang Jinrong a motorcar besides.
On 13 August 1937, when the Japanese army attacked Shanghai and the people of Shanghai rose in a stirring patriotic movement of resistance, Huang actually refused to let the Great World amusement park be used as a refugee shelter, and drove out the Shanghai Relief Association that had been set up there, keeping his doors open for business. He even put about such talk as that China's fighting Japan was "a chick going up against a weasel — how could it not lose?"
By the summer of 1938 Shanghai had become an "isolated island," and the Japanese naval attaché's office in China sent Rear Admiral Satō to the Huang house to invite him to prepare a puppet government for Shanghai. Huang pleaded illness and would not see him, but on Satō's second visit the two reached two agreements: first, that Huang would support the puppet government to the utmost and send his followers and partisans to join the collaborationist organizations; second, that Huang would traffic opium for the Japanese invading army to serve as military funds. Thereupon large numbers of Huang's followers began to join the collaborationist organizations.
In 1940, when Wang Jingwei set up his puppet government at Nanjing, Huang Jinrong sent Gong Tianjian hurrying to Nanjing to offer, on his behalf, congratulations on the "grand founding ceremony," with a secret letter conveying his full support.
In 1942, when Huang's sworn brother Chen Qun became governor of Jiangsu under the Wang Jingwei regime, Huang went in person, leading a great crowd of followers by special train, to Suzhou to lend his countenance. Under the arrangements of Huang Jinrong and Chen Qun, collaborationist offices at every level throughout Jiangsu were packed with Huang's disciples — such as Shen Jinghua, magistrate of Wu county; Wei Changzhen, magistrate of Jiangyin; Yang Shijie, magistrate of Songjiang; and Zhou Afu, head of the Wuxi county security corps — all followers of Huang's, who each month paid him rich tribute.
Beyond this, Huang Jinrong had great influence too in the "Peace Army" of the Wang Jingwei regime. His sworn brother, the veteran Lake Tai bandit Xu Lincheng, was commander of the Second Army of the Peace Army's First Group Army; his disciple Hao Pengju was security commander of Huaihai province; and his adopted son Tao Xuesheng was commander of the Songhu Railway Guard Regiment. Some of the Peace Army chiefs stationed in northern Jiangsu to deal specially with the New Fourth Army — such as the group-army commander Li Changjiang, the army commander Yan Xiuwu, the 36th Division commander Tian Tiefu, and the 28th Division commander Ding Jutang — were all of Huang's following. Huang Jinrong's capable disciple Lu Jinchen was director of the 36th Division's Shanghai office, in charge of procuring military supplies to equip the puppet troops and of wrecking the people's resistance to Japan.
Huang Jinrong was on close terms with the leading men of the Japanese-puppet regime; the Wang Jingwei government's minister of social affairs, Ding Mocun, and its minister of industry and commerce, Mei Siping, were both regular guests at the Huang house. Some collaborators said that although Pockmarked Jinrong held no open title in the Nanjing government, his power was no less than Zhou Fohai's.
In August 1945, when Japan announced its unconditional surrender, Huang Jinrong turned his coat and, in the guise of a local elder, was received by Chiang Kai-shek in person. Chiang wanted him to organize the gang forces to keep local order and shore up the Kuomintang's rule at the grass roots. On 23 February 1946, Huang Jinrong organized the "Rong Society," with his senior disciple Qiu Zishan and his son Huang Yuantao (a Zhongtong agent) serving as directors and supervisors, and tried to set up branches at Hangzhou, Suzhou, Nanjing, Wuxi, and elsewhere; but because the People's Liberation Army swept south so swiftly, the scheme came to nothing.
On the eve of Shanghai's liberation, Huang Jinrong secretly converted his household's movable property and his firms' funds into gold and American dollars and had his eldest daughter-in-law, Li Zhiqing, take them, together with the title deeds and business licenses, to Hong Kong, and buy houses and property in Hong Kong, Macao, and elsewhere. After Shanghai's liberation, under the guidance of the Military Control Commission, Huang Jinrong publicly confessed his crimes in the press, and the People's Government dealt with him leniently.
In 1953 Huang Jinrong died at his own residence.
