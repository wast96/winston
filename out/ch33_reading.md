## Chapter 1. Reviving the Ailing, a Second Start

Not long ago a spokesman for the United States government made a statement to the effect that: “An internal rebellion within a country cannot be settled by negotiation.” This was said with reference to the conciliatory policy the Philippines is at present pursuing toward the Filipino Communists. True enough—we hold this view to be entirely correct; only, so far as our own Republic of China is concerned, it comes a full forty years too late!

In the latter part of the War of Resistance the Chinese Communists were already bent upon rebellion against the Central Government, and after the victory they grew only the more unbridled. In the thirty-fifth year of the Republic the United States stepped forward to mediate, sending a special envoy to China to force my government into peace talks with the Communists. From that time pressure was applied again and again, and there were matters in which my country had no choice but to yield; and the result? We were duped outright; and then, when things had reached a pass beyond mending, the mediator turned on his heel and left, whereupon the whole situation grew the more unfavorable to us still.

The one thing mirrors the other; dare we fail to take the lesson to heart!

In the seventh month of the thirty-sixth year the Lizhi Class began its training in the capital, Nanjing, and the Leader, the Honorable Chiang, came in person to address us, saying: “The name and aim of this class, and the plan for its organization and training, were set in motion and decided by me myself. I hold that though a revolution may have a correct doctrine, yet lacking the revolutionary spirit and the revolutionary skill it cannot do battle with those counter-revolutionaries. Whether this training succeeds or fails is a very hinge on which the survival or extinction of our Party and State depends.” The Leader, the Honorable Chiang, went on to point out, with earnest emphasis: “Nothing has grieved me more than the decay of the special organization I raised with my own hands over twenty years… Today I mean, with you young men, to set the revolution marching forth anew.” Herewith a special-operations unit of a new pattern came into being—namely the Ministry of National Defense Pacification Corps, formed up after training in the Lizhi Class; and the writer was the brigade commander of its First Brigade, stationed in the region of Hebei, Chahar, Suiyuan, Beiping, and Tianjin. What is set down in this book is precisely the true record of how all of us of the corps, blade against very blade with the Chinese Communists, fought them in a bloody grapple throughout the said region.

Today what we take pride in is that this book is written out of experience paid for in blood; it is no idle campaigning on paper.

Into this chapter I have woven, here and there, some small matters of my own daily round—and not all of it is mere padding; for hereafter, what may prove hardest of all to come by are just such faded traces of history.

In the latter part of the chapter I introduce three of the brigade’s senior officers—I have called them “comrades in battle, masters at the work.” Some may mistake them for no more than obscure little men of whom the histories say nothing; but it is not so. Though they had no fame in this floating world, they were the revolutionary backbone of a great age; their aims and their conduct, their labors and their deeds, have a greatness all their own, and it is only that, their calling being other than the common worldly sort, the world outside never came to know of them.

### 1. The Birth of a New Special-Operations Unit

The victory in the War of Resistance had not come cheaply, and among the soldiery and people of the whole nation, who did not long to draw a breath and rest? Little did any think that this gang of Chinese Communists would reckon their hour had come: seizing the moment when the Central Government was busy taking the surrender of the Japanese forces, they fell to storming cities and seizing territory through the gaps, and openly broke away from the Central Government and rose in revolt.

The state of things at that time, at home and abroad, cannot be made plain in a phrase or two; but in sum, had the United States government not, for good or ill, stepped in to mediate between us, and had the settling been left to ourselves, then—I do believe—it would not have dragged on overlong before the Communist disorder was put down, and step by step wiped out. This is by no means talking to my own conceit: it may be proved from our taking of Yan’an, the Communists’ old lair, at a single stroke.

But as ill luck would have it, the United States government behaved as though it were under a spell: cheated by the Chinese Communists and never knowing it, it went so far as to sympathize with them and to help them, and would by all means force my government to make peace with the Communists—or else it would halt this and withhold that, applying pressure again and again. And my country, in the aftermath of war, its vital strength grievously drained and a thousand things awaiting to be set right, lacking now this and now that, was in truth in a case where it had no choice but to lean upon American support; and so, swallowing its pride to keep the whole, it accepted the American proposals.

At the beginning of the thirty-fifth year of the Republic the United States sent the well-reputed Marshall as its special envoy, to come and mediate the fighting in my country. The writer’s learning does not reach so far, and I cannot say whether this was or was not an interference in another country’s internal affairs; but, say what one will, it was a most uncommon thing in the political history of the world.

Now as to the manner of the mediation and its carrying-out: at the very top a “Committee of Three” was formed, made up of the American special envoy Marshall, my government’s representative Zhang Qun, and the Communist representative Zhou Enlai. After this committee had held many meetings, it formally issued the order that “all fighting shall cease fire at once, and all troops alike shall halt their movements.” For the effective enforcement of the truce, there was then formed, beneath the “Committee of Three,” a “Military Mediation Executive Headquarters” at Beiping, made up of three men—the American representative Colonel Robertson, my government’s representative Lieutenant-General Zheng Jiemin, and the Communist representative Ye Jianying (chief of staff of the Communist forces)—to further its execution. Yet the Communist side, from the very first, would not abide by the rules: they kept singling out the sectors where our forces were thin and went on attacking them without cease; and in their treachery and cunning they showed to the full their fickleness and their wolfish ambition. The American side, for its part, turned a blind eye to the Communists’ breaches of faith and broken covenants, bent as it was upon indulging and shielding them; and, heedless besides of our view that it was hard for us to go on negotiating, it held fast to its fixed policy that no armed force was to be used. Thus bound hand and foot, we were not only checked in what we might do, but the Communists were suffered to grow the greater by it; and so the whole situation went from bad to worse day by day.

At that time Mr. Zheng Jiemin, who sat on the “Military Mediation Executive Headquarters,” was serving as Chief of the Second Bureau of the Ministry of National Defense; and after Mr. Dai Yunong met his death in the middle third of the third month of the thirty-fifth year, he had for a time acted as head of the “Juntong Bureau” as well. Mr. Zheng’s knowledge and understanding of the Chinese Communists was profound indeed; and it was owing to his reports, his analyses, and his recommendations that, a little later, the “Lizhi Plan” was promulgated and, in the end, handed down to be carried out.

The “Lizhi Plan” was drawn up by the Leader in person; the whole of it ran to 3,500 words. Mr. Zheng Jiemin once conveyed its main points to me by word of mouth—the writer never set eyes on the original. As to the putting of the “Lizhi Plan” into practice, there is a passage of record in Fleeting Glimpses of a Floating Life, the posthumous work of Mr. Liu Peichu, Corps Commander of the “Pacification Corps” (published in the tenth month of the fifty-seventh year of the Republic, distributed by the Cheng Chung Book Company), which runs: “Zheng Jiemin said to me: ‘The Leader feels the hardness of the bandit-suppression work, and, calling to mind the work of the Special Operations Corps in the early days of the Jiangxi bandit-suppression, wishes to set up within the Ministry of National Defense a Pacification Corps under its direct command. I have already recommended you to the Leader as its Corps Commander; make haste to hand over the People’s Service Corps and come to make ready this new corps. The newly chosen cadres must first of all be sternly trained; it has now been decided to set up a Lizhi Training Class within the Central Training Corps, over which I shall preside, and the whole body of trainees you shall lead; when the training is done, they shall one and all be members of the Pacification Corps. As for all the particulars and how it is to proceed, settle them with the two deputy chiefs, comrades Zhang Yanyuan and Hou Teng.’” I remember that when, having come out of prison and gone from Shanghai to Nanjing, I saw Mr. Zheng Jiemin for the first time, he said: “Go to the Lizhi Class and take a month’s training first, then lead the corps up to North China to make war on the Communist forces.” And he said further: “I have been ordered to hold the post of director of the Lizhi Class besides, and have already drawn up a whole scheme of work; let us go into it in detail once we are at the class.” This was talked over in haste, in the car, as I was seeing him partway to the class. As I got down, Mr. Zheng added: “You may have a talk with Deputy Director Zhang.” It was in the office of the deputy director of the “Lizhi Class” that I met Mr. Zhang Yanyuan (Binghua); the one thing I cared about most was just where in North China I was to be sent, and when Mr. Binghua told me it was Beiping, I was overjoyed indeed—for it suited both public duty and private wish, and in a month or so more I should be able to be reunited with my family.

The “Lizhi Class” was set up within the “Central Training Corps,” and nominally belonged to it; but in reality the “Central Training Corps” did not much concern itself with the training and management of the “Lizhi Class”—or, one might say, did so in form only.

The “Lizhi Class” had Bureau Chief Zheng Jiemin as its director, holding the post besides, and Deputy Bureau Chief Zhang Yanyuan as deputy director, likewise besides; the class headquarters had its several sections for instruction and for general affairs. Beneath it were one trainee corps and five brigades (by another account, one trainee brigade and five companies). Each brigade numbered about 120 men, and the whole about six hundred (by another account, upwards of four hundred all told). Whether I was at the time a brigade commander or a company commander, even I cannot clearly recall—which, told aloud, is a real absurdity. The only explanation is that forty years now lie between.

The Corps Commander was Liu Peichu, and beneath him it divided into five brigades: the First Brigade commander was Chen Gongshu, the Second Chen Zhenshan, the Third Liu Renhua, the Fourth Guo Chongxin, the Fifth Jin Yifu—all five brigade commanders were men bred at the Whampoa Military Academy. Beneath the brigades were district companies, whose commanders were drawn from among the higher-ranking of the trainees; the highest-ranking of the trainees at that time were lieutenant-colonels. On the twentieth day of the seventh month of the thirty-sixth year the first session of the “Lizhi Class” held its opening ceremony, and the Leader came in person to address it. By Mr. Liu Peichu’s record in Fleeting Glimpses of a Floating Life, at the opening ceremony the Leader instructed us earnestly, saying: “The name and aim of this class, and the plan for its organization and training, were set in motion and decided by me myself. I hold that though a revolution may have a correct doctrine, yet lacking the revolutionary spirit and skill it cannot do battle with those counter-revolutionaries. Whether this training succeeds or fails is a very hinge on which the survival or extinction of our Party and State depends.” And again, by the recollection of Wang Zhaofen, district-company commander of the First Company (a lieutenant-colonel, and colonel commanding officer in the First Pacification Brigade), the Leader in his address at the opening ceremony spoke also the following passage, the gist of which was: “At the reverses of the war of suppression I do not grieve; the failures of politics I can yet bear, and the loosening of the Party I too can endure; but nothing has grieved me more than the decay of the special organization I raised with my own hands over twenty years. I call to mind how at Whampoa, with five hundred young men and five hundred rifles, I accomplished the Eastern Expedition and the Northern Expedition; and today, with you young men, I mean to set the revolution marching forth anew.” From these two passages of address one may understand how much the Leader at that time looked for from the “Pacification Corps” that the “Lizhi Class” was about to form.

In the course of the training, all those who came to the class to speak or to give lessons were great personages and men of note; but I always felt it did not much square with the practical. If one asked whether we had learned anything, I think the trainees would have found it hard to give any positive answer. In the last chapter of the fourth book there is a sentence that reads: “Chief of the General Staff General Bai Chongxi also came to the class and spoke twice”—this sentence is in error: General Bai Chongxi was the Minister of National Defense, and it is General Chen Cheng who should be the Chief of the General Staff.

Here it may be worth setting down once more that General Bai Chongxi, in his capacity as Minister of National Defense, in addressing the trainees actually assailed, without the least concealment, a number of past and present military measures—which left us all, who heard it, greatly astonished, nor could we make out on the spot what lay behind his words. Yet even were there any difference of view with the Chief of the General Staff, it ought never to have been vented so nakedly before a company of guileless young officers. It reflected, too, the discord and want of unity at the higher levels at that time.

The trainees were of a fairly even composition, being chosen for the most part, respectively, from the several divisions of the “Youth Army,” from cadres of every grade in the “People’s Service Corps,” and from the junior and middle-ranking officers of the “Officers’ Corps.” By military rank they ranged from second lieutenant to lieutenant-colonel, but were mostly captains and lieutenants. As to quality, those from the Youth Army were the most unspoiled, their average age being just past twenty; those from the “People’s Service Corps” were much the same; while those from the “Officers’ Corps” were somewhat older, most of them men well weathered by hardship. In sum, all were highly malleable, and given able leadership might of course make much of themselves.

In the month-long training there is no lack of tales worth telling, and an amusing one is the manner of management of the Corps Commander, Mr. Liu Peichu. Of this there is, in his posthumous work Fleeting Glimpses of a Floating Life, a passage that may well have been a piece he was pleased with; the original runs: “Instruction in training was allotted but two hours in the whole course, so I had no choice but to lay hold of the extra hours—morning assembly, evening assembly, the dead of night—to draw as close to them as I could, to know them, to temper them, and to work our spirit of leadership into them through the work itself. Each morning, at the drill hour the corps had appointed, I would always lead the whole body of trainees to the hill country near Xiaolingwei and put my own methods of activity into practice: after a few days of gymnastic drill came hill-climbing contests and singing lessons, and at the last I used the six words ‘climb, leap, roll, twist, strike, sing,’ by every sort of means, to quicken their minds and bodies, to draw out the vigor of every man, and to make each day’s morning drill a thing of joy to them. The evening assemblies took as their themes rambling talk, debate, discussion of current affairs, and the study of techniques of struggle against the bandits. For the night musters—at least once a week, and never with any warning beforehand—after the assembly bugle had sounded I would order them to muster and answer the roll at the Linggu Temple, or at the Ming Xiaoling, or the Sun Yat-sen Mausoleum; the way there was all rough mountain track, yet they had to arrive within a set time. Having checked the numbers, I would bid them dress their ranks toward the martyrs in the Shrine of the Revolutionary Martyrs at the Linggu Temple, and would raise a slogan besides—to turn black night into broad day, to turn deep vale and great river into level ground. I put forward the eight-word slogan ‘march in the dark, eat coarse fare, be quick, hit the mark,’ and I (Liu Peichu) said: ‘If we would be able to wipe out the Communists, we must do these things in very earnest—otherwise we are not fit to be the Communists’ foes.’” The passage above is a true likeness of what Mr. Liu Peichu, in his capacity as Corps Commander, then performed. Of these methods of his I greatly disapproved, and indeed felt no little repugnance. Look first at his temper and his bearing: where was this a training? It was plainly the tormenting of men; if he was not a petty warlord ordering all about with a lift of the chin, then he was like a man whose nerves had been unhinged by some shock. And further, since the “Central Training Corps” had a fixed timetable of work and rest, that timetable ought to have been kept to; besides which, the trainees were one and all men who had undergone strict military training and had experience of real combat, and there was no need at all, at the morning-drill hour, to march the ranks off to the hillside to climb and leap and roll and brawl, and by his barbarous methods to carry on such pointless exertions.

Mr. Peichu’s slogans, too, were most marvelous—all this of “turning black night into broad day, turning deep vale and great river into level ground.” To my mind, he had better have turned himself into a Heavenly Dog and swallowed the Communists whole—would not that have been finer still?

These perverse and childish doings were, at the time, a truly sickening business; but by now they have turned into a pleasant tale worth the recollecting.

The themes the “Lizhi Class” set forth included “four great tasks,” namely: “pacification,” “intelligence,” “action,” and “assault.” My own explanation of them is as follows. “Assault” is the sudden striking of an enemy strongpoint—one might say, dealing the enemy a blow when he least expects it. Once it has succeeded, hold the ground if you can; if you cannot, take the spoils of the fight and go.

“Action” may be divided into two parts. The slaying of the bandit chieftains is a thing one may chance upon but cannot seek out at will; the weight of it lies rather in the wrecking of transport and communications, of grain and fodder depots, and of military installations generally. And the enemy’s loss is just so much of our gain.

“Intelligence” should be divided into two levels: of the material gathered in hand, once it has been worked up, the one kind is for our own use, the other is furnished to our superiors for their reference.

“Pacification,” now, was no simple matter, for in the past it had seldom been set down as an item of work, and so there was as yet no settled notion of it. Mr. Liu Peichu’s explanation of “pacification” ran thus; he said: “Pacification means that on reaching a place—whether it be a brigade, a sub-brigade, or a section—one is at once to organize all the people of that place, men and women, old and young alike, and to make of a wholly unorganized society an organized one; to make of a society where the strong oppress the weak and the many do violence to the few, a society luxurious and frivolous, disordered and dark, a society still and pure and pressing forward toward betterment.” To my mind this can only be called an ideal, a goal to be gazed at but not reached, and it does not make plain the concrete way of doing the “pacification” work; to say that “to organize at once all the men and women, old and young, of a place” is “pacification”—that is surely too hollow. I, for my part, likened the “pacification” work to a road-clearing machine and a street-sweeper: on the principle of not meddling in the local administration, it stood only in a helping place, no more. In the conceiving of it, two cases were supposed: the one, a combat zone where we were at close quarters with the Communist forces; the other, a recovered area once seized by the Communist forces and then retaken. As for places quiet and untroubled, there was no need for us to go at all, and no need to suppose anything. Among the items of work may be reckoned the organizing and training of the populace, the checking of household registers, the soothing of refugees, the rooting out of Communist spies, and the aiding of the local government in keeping order and restoring the peace of society, and so forth. This had to take the objective circumstances and the subjective conditions as its measure, and to decide, in the face of the real state of things, what should be done and what should not. Otherwise it would either not work at all, or find no welcome.

The “Lizhi Class” was held in Nanjing over three sessions in all, and the above is the outline of its first session. Measured by present-day standards it seems to have nothing especially outstanding about it; but forty years ago it counted for something fairly advanced.

During the training of the first session of the “Lizhi Class” the Leader came four times: he addressed us as a body, called individuals by name, received us in groups, and, with the greatest earnestness, urged us to become nameless heroes. From this one may see how much store was set by it.

### 2. Return to Old Ground, Faintly Familiar

I was born in Beiping; and though I grew up amid wandering and dispersal, yet from the third year of the Republic onward I came and went so many times that I can no longer clearly recall how many.

Strange to say, at every interval of time there would always come some great upheaval; and each upheaval, without fail, governed my going or staying—and how it would be this time, that too could not be foreknown. Yet whenever Beiping is spoken of, say what one will, I love the place still, and love it dearly; for it has indeed a mighty power to draw a man and to hold him—above all that quality peculiar to the natives here: that unhurried, easeful, leisurely, contented savor of life. To one engaged in secret-service work, forever in tension, in toil, in bustle and anxiety, that is of course a temptation. Take myself for instance: I always hoped that one day I too might pass a few quiet days after the manner of a “Beiping man.”

The last time I came to Beiping was in the twenty-seventh year of the Republic. The War of Resistance had by then entered its second year, Beiping had long since fallen, and I had come, under orders, from Tianjin to sanction Wang Kemin, the number-one head of the North China puppet régime. Once in Beiping, I lodged at first with my maternal uncle in the northern city; but the place was too out of the way—it had not even a telephone, and was most inconvenient—and so I owed it to the kindness of Mr. Mao Wanli, acting Chief of the “Beiping District,” that he invited me to move to his house. His house, in truth, was none other than the headquarters of the “Beiping District.” By our rules I ought never to have been let to know of the place, let alone to lodge within it; and sure enough the Wang Kemin case did bring the “Beiping District” no end of trouble—happily without imperiling its safety, that was all. The “Beiping District” in the time of Brother Wanli’s charge had its address at Meizha Hutong in the eastern city, and the scene of the action to sanction Wang Kemin chanced to lie at the two ends of one and the same hutong—a thing wholly of coincidence, not of any deliberate arrangement.

At that time, by report, Wang Kemin often went to Meizha Hutong (the eastern end) to meet the chief of the Japanese special-service organ and several of his important advisers; only later did I learn that the place was a guest-house belonging to the Ping-Han Railway Bureau. The guest-houses of that age were, for the most part, trading-halls that served the turns of political dealing. I remember that the writer himself loitered and spied outside the gate of this great mansion many a time, and all he could ever see was the red-lacquered gate and, now and again, a police guard on station—the thing that struck me most being that the police carried no arms.

Reckoning back from the thirty-sixth year, this was already a thing of ten years before.

Now when the first session of the “Lizhi Class” came to its end, I was granted leave not to take the second session’s training, but to set out ahead to Beiping, to make ready the establishment of the “First Pacification Brigade Headquarters.” At that moment, save for a few stretches, the whole line of the Jin-Pu Railway had long been cut; and to go from Nanjing to Beiping, the quickest way was to reach Shanghai first and take a plane—whereas to go by sea-steamer to Tianjin and thence to Beiping might drag out ten days or a fortnight.

In Shanghai I dared not tarry long: my longing to return to Beiping and be reunited with my family was urgent indeed as a shooting star—the more so, coming as I did from prison.

The plane came down at Xiyuan airfield. In those days the flights were few and the seats limited; and though there were passengers enough on the field, and folk come to meet the plane, it was of course nothing so lively as now.

He who came to meet me was Brother Wu Anzhi, and he had brought along my son, a boy of three or four. That kindness I have not forgotten to this day.

I remember that, once in at Xizhimen, we passed along Di’anmen Boulevard, turned in at Jiaodaokou, went along Andingmen Boulevard, and one turn more brought us to Mayuan Hutong.

My home was in this very hutong. Mayuan Hutong is a narrow lane; a car cannot drive in, nor, of course, reach the gate. Getting down to walk, one had still to go a good many dozen paces more. Two small leaves of a gate, and pasted upon them the faded door-gods—to ward off petty thieves and keep the peace—in the hope that those two might long guard my house.

Stepping into the courtyard, I saw it was a little three-sided compound: to the north a row of three main rooms, people living in the two flanking ones and the middle serving as parlor and dining-room together; to the west a kitchen of a room and a half, to the east a wing-room of a room and a half—by “a room and a half” being meant an ill-proportioned pairing of one large and one small.

The courtyard was small, its floor bare earth, with no flowers or plants set out—bald and barren, without a spark of life.

Though households that lived well had long since fitted flush toilets, ours was still the “squat pit” that the old families used. The “squat pit” was a little thatched shed, at the turn of the wall beside the kitchen, and could hold but one person at a time. After each easing of the bowels one had to cover it over with “stove-ash”; and every day or two there would always come a “night-soil man,” a wooden bucket on his back and a long-handled iron ladle in his hand, to clear it out once—at the festivals and the New Year a few coppers of tip would content him. This being the thirty-sixth year, when the marketplace had long since entered the age of new American fittings, there were still some backward quarters of Beiping that remained just so.

This was my first impression on coming home—no great matter; after all I still had a home, and I had come home in the company of “freedom.”

What most puzzled me was this: not having seen one another for years, my family, on meeting, ought surely to have been warm and fond—why did they all look at me with such cool eyes, and seem so distant? But little by little the talk grew freer.

My wife told me: “The house is rented; before the victory the rent was reckoned in so many sacks of flour, and now it is reckoned in money. These two years past we have lived wholly on the help of old friends—above all after Bai Shiwei and old Qi and the rest came back.” My wife said further: “While you were inside, they were all in great anxiety, forever clamoring to write a joint petition under all their names to bail you out; it was not till these last few months that it was at last sent up, with more than twenty names signed and sealed.” I asked who they were. She told me all she knew, but some of them she did not so much as recognize. Such a thing, with us, was against all rule, and no one had ever dared do it before. This once, it may be because Mr. Zheng Jiemin was a generous man and easier to deal with! For my part, of course, my gratitude was past all reckoning, graven forever in my heart. For the present, all I could do to show it was to go first, being near at hand, and give my thanks, and think of some fuller repayment by and by.

I went and gave my thanks to Mr. Ma Hansan. By report, after the victory Mr. Dai had appointed him director of the “Beiping Office,” and afterward he had passed to an open post, being now Chief of the Bureau of Civil Affairs of the Beiping Municipal Government. On inquiry, he lived at No. 20 Meizha Hutong in the eastern city—to which, at the time, I paid no heed.

When I came to the gate and looked, of a sudden it struck me: was this not the very scene where, ten years before, a few of us at the hazard of our lives had come to sanction Wang Kemin? In the old days I could only slip past the gate in secret; now I could, in all openness, send in my name and pay my respects to the master of the place.

Stepping into the courtyard—flowers and trees in graceful profusion, a winding path leading into deep seclusion, a covered gallery on the left, a pavilion and kiosk on the right—turning this way and that I was led at last to the reception hall: truly rich and splendid, a great show of state. No wonder men like Wang Kemin were forever gathering here. As for how Mr. Ma Hansan had come by this place for his office, and how it had become a private residence besides—therein, no doubt, lay a tale of twists and turns.

At this moment I was much bewildered, feelings crowding thick upon me, not knowing what it were best to think. When I had steadied myself, made my thanks, and put my emotions in some order, I thought again, quietly: does the world’s good fortune, after all, come round by turns?

In these two days, while the trainees had not yet arrived, I resolved to go to Tianjin to see Bai Shiwei and Qi Qingbin. Though I did not say so aloud, I went, of course, full of gratitude, to give them my thanks.

Brother Shiwei was now a major-general, chief of the inspection department of the “Tianjin Garrison Command,” and lived in the former British Concession (the several foreign concessions of old—British, French, Japanese—had by now all been recovered). Brother Qi Qingbin had formerly been a major-general, chief of the inspection department of the “Beiping Garrison Command,” and had now been transferred to be deputy chief of the police bureau of Tianjin municipality—the chief being, I think, Li Hanyuan—and lived near the police bureau. Though I stayed two days at his house, I cannot recall now just where the place was.

We talked of everything, save only those matters that, heard again, would stir up unpleasantness. From our talk I learned that the friends who, before and after the War of Resistance, had done secret work at Beiping and Tianjin and thereabouts, had nearly all passed into the intelligence and security organs, and taken up open posts.

Besides Brother Ma Hansan, Brother Shiwei, and Brother Qingbin, named above, there was Brother Wu Anzhi, who had come to Xiyuan airfield a few days before to meet me, now chief of the police department of the “Ping-Han Railway Administration Bureau.” Brother Zhang Jiaquan, who in the twenty-second or twenty-third year of the Republic had been leader of the “Cangzhou Section,” was now a major-general, chief of the Second Section of the “Beiping Field Headquarters.” In days to come, when I went to the Field Headquarters to report and hand in my papers, it was with him that I most often had dealings.

There was also Brother Chen Xianzhou and Brother Shi Hong, who in the twenty-second or twenty-third year had been directly subordinate couriers of the “Beiping Station,” and who were now both in the north: the former chief of the police department of the “Hebei Provincial Government,” with his office at Baoding; the latter a major-general, chief of the Second Section of the “North China Bandit Suppression Headquarters,” at Zhangjiakou, I believe.

Likewise in the twenty-second or twenty-third year, Brother Lou Zhaoyuan, who had been leader of the military section of the “Beiping Station,” was now dean of studies at the “Police Officers’ School” in Beiping. I am still not clear whether this “Police Officers’ School” was a central one or a local one.

Brother Wang Yunsun, who in the old days, when I was Chief of the “Beiping Station,” had served as “secretary,” was now director of the Beiping office of the “China Aviation Company”—which made the buying of plane tickets much the easier. Then again, Zhang Fengyi, who had been an action-man of the “Beiping Station,” and comrade Tong Ronggong, its liaison-man, had now each in turn become a colonel and chief inspector of an inspection department.

These good friends and comrades named above were, for the most part, the very men who had signed the joint bail for me. The rest were either in the Northeast or in the South; to them, here, I bow once more, all together, and send my regards. Writing to this point, let me take the chance it gives me and be allowed to say a few words that overstep my station: presumptuous though they may seem, there is no ill will in them, still less any design or ulterior end.

I said just now that the comrades who had done secret work before and after the War of Resistance had all taken up public office. In truth this had nothing to do with personal glory: think of it—for a dozen years and more, going out to death and coming back to life, and now to get some rank or half a rank of office, where is the harm? And what does it amount to? To speak of it as a reward for merit would be more unseemly still. The more so as each of them, in the post he now held, could fill it and be content; and some, kept down in a deputy’s place, had, to my mind, even suffered a wrong.

In truth, that most of the cadres of the “Juntong Bureau” came out into open posts was all Mr. Dai Yunong’s planned arrangement—so it was at Beiping and Tianjin, and much the same elsewhere. Plainly, to hold the intelligence and security organs of the whole country in his grasp was one of his first aims. As for the turning of the secret toward the open, that was what the times required, and there was no help for it. Yet he had already made ready to raise up a second generation of secret workers. Where, then, was his next aim to lie? Upon that question everyone may guess at will, by his own understanding and observation. The writer too has heard it said again and again: “Had Dai Li not died, thus and thus would it have been.” And how, in the end, would it have been? There will be, I suppose, no answer now.

Mr. Zheng Jiemin inclined toward the “regularizing of secret work” and the “systematizing of personnel,” seeking only free growth, and caring not to grasp all under heaven in one hand.

And so in his pace he was the more conservative; yet one must own, all the same, that he had achievements of his own.

Mr. Mao Renfeng, for his part, had a strong mind to inherit Mr. Dai’s mantle and even to carry it to greater glory; but, as ill luck would have it, in the conditions both within and without he fell too far short. Of all that Mr. Dai had had, Mr. Mao had but two or three parts in ten.

And so the “Juntong spirit” withered, and was seen no more!

Note this: it was said above that at the opening ceremony of the first session of the “Lizhi Class,” in the Leader’s address, there was a sentence that ran: “Nothing has grieved me more than the decay of the special organization I raised with my own hands over twenty years.” This must have had its meaning; and though it need not have pointed at the “Juntong” alone, yet the “Juntong” was precisely one of the “special organizations,” and the largest at that.

My journey to Tianjin left but the slightest impression, and it was this: two years had passed since the concessions were taken back, yet when you passed through those quarters it was as though a concession were a concession still—clean and trim, unchanged in the least; whereas the so-called Chinese sector, such as Hebei Boulevard, was in the same old hubbub as ever.

### 3. Tangled Personnel Ties and Overlapping Duties

To begin with, it is needful first to give a brief account of certain tangled systems of work and ties of personnel; not to speak of the outsider, even I myself must think it well over before I can set it down.

In the first through the fourth of these poor books of mine, the writer’s working unit was always the “Juntong Bureau”; that is to say, the writer’s standing was throughout that of a working cadre under the leadership of the “Juntong Bureau.” And what is set down in those books is all of it the course of the work of the “Juntong Bureau’s” comrades, and the valiant deeds they wrought.

By the fifth book—which is just this one now beginning—the writer’s working standing was already somewhat other than before, and what the book sets down is no longer wholly the course of the “Juntong Bureau’s” work. To make it plainer, I may offer some further explanation, as follows.

When, in the thirty-fifth year of the Republic, the writer’s sentence was confirmed and he was delivered to Shanghai’s “Tilanqiao Prison” to serve it, it was as good as being driven out of the gate by the “Juntong Bureau,” and of course I no longer bore the standing of a “comrade.” Plainly, though such a thing was nowhere set down in writing, it was not quite the same as when one who had broken the “house law” was shut up in the gaol that the Bureau headquarters had leave to keep of its own. (As for what is meant by “house law,” I shall treat of it in another place, should the chance offer.) During my term of punishment at “Tilanqiao,” the writer was of course a convict serving his sentence, and naturally held no duty or post.

From the first ten days of the seventh month of the thirty-sixth year of the Republic, when I came out of “Tilanqiao,” to the twentieth day of the seventh month, when I was formally installed in office—that span of days, some ten or so, was it not?—was a blank; having no name for it, let me call it that of “a free man under constraint.”

On the “personnel record card,” from the seventh to the eighth month of the thirty-sixth year, my post was that of colonel and brigade commander of the “Lizhi Training Class of the Central Training Corps”—this by formal appointment, an officer of the state beyond all ambiguity.

Most to be regretted is this: as to the unprecedented handling of my case—the remission of the remainder of my sentence, my early release, my being taken again into service and given a military post—I have never to this day known in what words Mr. Zheng Jiemin signed the request for the sanction of those above, still less how those above endorsed it. By rights there must surely be a document; but it is one that will never be seen.

This span, on the “personnel record card,” and on the “military service roll” as well, is a “gap”; for one could not fill in that from such a month of such a year to such a month of such a year he had served a prison term at “Tilanqiao.” And so the friends in charge of “personnel,” in the dates of appointment and removal, worked a little juggling of before and after, and patched it over without a seam. Here a note is needed, and it is this: there was in it nothing of the sort of “pulling of strings” or “favoritism.”

When the month’s training of the “Lizhi Class” was done, all the trainees were forthwith formed into the “Ministry of National Defense Pacification Corps.” The writer was at the same time appointed colonel and brigade commander of the First Brigade of the “Pacification Corps.” This “Pacification Corps” came directly under the Ministry of National Defense, while its business was overseen by the “Second Bureau”; or, to put it plainer, though the “Pacification Corps” nominally came directly under the Ministry of National Defense, in reality it was a field unit of the “Second Bureau.”

Between the “Pacification Corps,” overseen by the “Second Bureau,” and the “Baomiju,” which nominally belonged to the Ministry of National Defense but in reality worked on its own, there was no connection—one might as well say that each went its own way. Since the writer’s roots ran deep with the “Juntong Bureau,” the forerunner of the “Baomiju,” and since it was the “Juntong Bureau” that had sentenced me, my coming out this time was a special case, and it seemed I ought to go back to the Bureau and pay my respects, lest I give a handle for any talk. Little did I think that Mr. Mao Renfeng, head of the “Baomiju,” without a word more, at once gave a “verbal order” that I hold besides the post of leader of the “Beiping directly subordinate section,” and give myself wholly to the gathering of intelligence. In my helplessness I had no choice but, swallowing much, to accept.

Writing to this point, let me at last breathe out the breath I have held pent these several decades! At that time Mr. Mao Renfeng said to me, in a tone all but of command: “The Bureau gives you the title of leader of a directly subordinate section; pray keep up your efforts.” It sounded courteous enough, and still counted me as one of their own, and seemed even to raise me up; yet I, questioning my own heart, said within me: “Am I then bound by any such duty?” I had had my fill of bitterness in very truth; and besides, I had already been assigned my post, and this hardly suited well either. I was just about to find some pretext to put it off, when Mr. Mao Renfeng went on: “As to communications and liaison, I shall have the Beiping comrades send you a wireless set and a set of ciphers for your sole use; hereafter I hope you will keep up a full correspondence.” It sounded as though the thing were already decided.

I was a man but newly come through affliction, frail in body and mind past the bearing of a single blow; whence should I find the spirit to set myself against authority? And so, do it I must, whether I would or no—let me take it as being “pressed into forced labor.” By reason I might have declined it gently, or refused it flatly, or paid the whole business no heed at all; for since they had driven me out before, I now had, as it were, not the least tie of organization left, and on what ground, then, was I bound to accept this post? Yet in my heart I knew well that once things were let to turn stiff, the days thereafter would be hard indeed—and against whom could I hope to prevail?

Thereafter I reported to Mr. Zheng, face to face, this matter of Mr. Mao’s appointing me a section leader; whether I did it or not still waited on his decision. If Mr. Zheng agreed, I would do it and see; if Mr. Zheng showed himself against it, then there was no need to trouble myself further. In the old-style officialdom such a case was often called “standing with a foot in two boats,” whereby a man might take his profit on either hand; but my holding two posts at once was rather like driving a nail in atop a rivet—it was tightness upon tightness.

In intelligence work, to take office on two sides may be called a “double relationship,” or, to be more exact, one might as well call it an “internal double relationship.” It is commonly held a great taboo, and a thing well-nigh unheard of; in a case like mine, the best construction were rather to call it a “double mission.” If one cast away the narrow feeling and opened one’s breast, and reckoned that one was valued and had ability in one, would not the heart then be at peace?

Here let me take the occasion to mention one matter of factional trouble, of which the rumors ran thick and various: when I had reported to Mr. Zheng the whole course of how Mr. Mao Renfeng had bidden me take the post of leader of the “Beiping directly subordinate section,” and he had heard it, he said, in the lightest of tones: “Then just do as the Bureau means you to.”

At this time the “Second Bureau” in Mr. Zheng’s charge and the “Baomiju” in Mr. Mao’s charge, though both alike were organs under the Ministry of National Defense, had between them neither a vertical line of command nor a horizontal line of liaison: the two had nothing to do with one another. To draw the distinction strictly, the “Second Bureau” was a business unit and the “Baomiju” a service unit, each with its own charge, each forming a system of its own. And, understood a layer deeper, their “backgrounds” too differed a little. For a time it was rumored abroad that Messrs. Zheng and Mao did not cooperate over-well, and even had their differences of view; and there were meddlesome folk besides who would insist on ranging the old Juntong cadres as this man’s men and that man’s men, as though each had his allegiance. And how, in the end, did the truth stand? Because such rumors arose after Mr. Dai’s death, when the writer was just then in prison, I truly do not know the inside of it; but to my own thinking, Mr. Zheng’s standing in the work was a good deal higher than Mr. Mao’s—he had, moreover, acted as head of the “Juntong Bureau,” and had once been Mao’s superior—so it can hardly have come to that, I suppose. And from the fact that Mr. Zheng, without minding it, allowed me to work for the “Baomiju,” how great a largeness of spirit was there in him!

Yet I know myself very well. As to the work relation, Mr. Zheng Jiemin and I had been together since the twenty-second year of the Republic: he was the Bureau’s “special commissioner” resident at Beiping, and I was Chief of the “Beiping Station”; he had the right to command me, and I was of course his subordinate; and as to friendship, we had our side of private and frequent intercourse too. My relation with Mr. Mao Renfeng was much of a kind: in the twenty-eighth year of the Republic, at Chongqing, we worked in one small office together—Mr. Mao’s post being, I think, secretary and concurrently head of the First Section (?), and mine acting head of the Third Section—and we ate together at little eating-houses, played mahjong, and dodged the air-raids, and got on well enough; I remember he even showed me how to countersign official business. But by the thirty-sixth year of the Republic things were no longer what they had been; and moreover I had been cut off from Messrs. Zheng and Mao so long that I had not even the chance to come near them—so how could there be any talk of whose man was whose, or of factions and their bounds?

Pardon me if I make two things plain for the record: I have an independent character of my own; I am an officer bred up by the government; in my duties I am a worker of the unit I belong to; and I will by no means own myself to be any private man’s man.

To speak further of reason and feeling: Mr. Zheng Jiemin was good to me out of a regard for my ability in the work; and in bailing me out of gaol he hoped that I, with a body yet of use, might once more serve the state. And I, for my part, of course knew how to requite kindness with gratitude; and the only way of that requital was to labor hard and fulfill my task. In the last chapter of the fourth book I mentioned that, when the case in which the Bureau headquarters’ court-martial department at first instance sentenced me to twelve years’ imprisonment came up for Mr. Mao’s review, he wrote in a note of opinion on the document, “recommend the death penalty” (by another account it was “recommend life imprisonment or the death penalty”). Yet here I must point out particularly that at the time, in the thirty-sixth year, I did not yet know of these many turns, and so in my mind bore no rancor at all; and when in later days I heard there had been such a peril, so long a time had passed that things gone by I had long since taken lightly. Why Mr. Mao Renfeng should have been so hard—to speak by reason, there was in it certainly no private grudge; and since it was no private grudge, then he had, of course, reasons of his own that he held to. As for what reasons, I cannot puzzle them out; and even had I some guess, it is now past all proving.

For the four gentlemen who knew the inside of it—Dai, Zheng, Mao, and Pan (Qiwu)—have all been dead these many years; and in the great sealed heap of the archives there will be no record of this either. To leave a question behind for men to turn over in their minds—is that not well too?

To sum up the foregoing account, its aim is to make plain that the work henceforth, though unchanged in essence, was yet a little other than before in its nature. To put it another way, this was no longer a task carried out simply under the leadership of the “Juntong.”

The writer’s state of mind at the time was this: the post of commander of the “Pacification Brigade” I received willingly and with a glad heart, and would do it with all my heart and mind; the post of leader of the “Beiping directly subordinate section” was one I did against my will—yet, since it could not be shaken off, neither could I do it by halves and be done. Of all the vexing things under heaven, this, I suppose, is the chief.

Next, let me say a little of the relation between me and the Corps Commander, Liu Peichu.

Mr. Liu Peichu was a man of Daye in Hubei. He was of the fifth class of the Whampoa Military Academy, and we were classmates of the same class; in the twenty-eighth year of the Republic, at Chongqing, Mr. Liu Peichu was director of the inspectorate office at the Bureau headquarters, and I was acting head of the Third Section, so we were colleagues. Before that we had not been acquainted. What struck me most was that he had once fallen into a dispute with Mr. He Zhiyuan, then head of the Second Section, neither yielding to the other, and it was, in the end, only after Mr. Dai Yunong said a few words that it was smoothed over. As for what they disputed, I no longer clearly recall; dimly it seems to have been, again, over official business.

Though we were colleagues, we had no intercourse, for our ways of life were widely unlike—or rather, I was by no means so straight-laced as Mr. Peichu. For instance, in those days we still played mahjong on the sly; had he caught sight of it, there would have been the devil to pay, and it would have gone beyond a mere bout of shaming. Mr. Liu Peichu was a type apart: if one would describe him, he was rather like an ascetic monk—bearing hardship and enduring toil, plain and unadorned—and to serve as Corps Commander of the “Pacification Corps” he was in truth the best of choices.

He was the Corps Commander, and I a brigade commander beneath the corps; he was, of course, my immediate superior, out and out. Were anyone to ask me how I regarded him—by reason of a month’s unbroken contact and cool observation—there were many of his doings that truly could not win my heartfelt submission. Yet, after all, this was an organized body, and one must respect the system as is right.

As for how Mr. Peichu was toward me, one may say that on the whole it was well enough; deeper than that, at this present moment, I could not yet make out.

Besides, the “Second Bureau” had two deputy chiefs: the one Mr. Hou Teng (Feixia), the other Mr. Zhang Yanyuan (Binghua).

Deputy Chief Hou I had met, though in the work I had little contact with him.

Deputy Chief Zhang—Mr. Binghua—was at this time also deputy director of the “Lizhi Class.” We were, one may say, friends who had shared adversity, like an elder brother and a younger. This tie I have spoken of in the first of these poor books, Rooting Out Traitors in the North: how we trained in the same class and lodged in the same room and passed together a spell of bitter days. Afterward Mr. Binghua of a sudden was gone; it turned out he had already joined Mr. Dai’s work and set out ahead of me, none knew whither.

That this time I could come out of prison early—that Mr. Binghua worked to shield me in it goes, of course, without saying.

Writing to this point, let me by the way add a piece of later matter: in the forty-first year of the Republic Mr. Binghua and I met abroad, and though each had his own task, we still came and went from time to time. In the forty-sixth year Mr. Binghua took over as head of the “Intelligence Bureau,” and employed me as a “directly subordinate courier” resident in Japan; on the way from Hong Kong to Japan, passing through Taiwan, Mr. Binghua came in person to Songshan airfield to see me off and to hand me my task face to face. In the third month of the next year Mr. Zheng Jiemin (head of the “National Security Bureau”) bade me by international telephone return to Taiwan that very day, and thus it was that I came back to the “Intelligence Bureau” and became head of the Second Section (in charge of intelligence).

What is set down above is too tangled, and gives one much the feeling of growing the more confused the more one reads—above all in the order of time, which, all topsy-turvy, is not easy to make clear. Better to sort it out plainly, once over, thus, in the main: from the ninth month of the thirty-sixth year I was colonel and brigade commander of the “First Brigade of the Ministry of National Defense Pacification Corps.”

At the same time I was leader of the “Beiping directly subordinate section of the Baomiju”—which leadership carried no established rank, being an honorary post.

The “First Brigade of the Pacification Corps” was under the control and oversight of the “Second Bureau,” its officers of connection being Bureau Chief Zheng Jiemin, Deputy Chief Zhang Yanyuan, and Deputy Chief Hou Teng.

Beneath the “Pacification Corps” were established seven brigades and three directly subordinate companies, the Corps Commander being Mr. Liu Peichu. The “First Brigade” was but one of the ten field units belonging to the corps, ranked first, no more.

On the other hand, Mr. Mao Renfeng, head of the “Baomiju,” “in the standing and posture of the heir to Mr. Dai Yunong,” commanded, or controlled from afar, all the working units.

Beyond this, once arrived at each place of work, on the side of the “Pacification Brigade,” by the rules, one had to establish a “relation of attachment” with the highest military and civil authority of the locality; and on the side of the “directly subordinate section,” one had also to keep secret liaison with the local working units. At times one had, besides, to take part in the “joint briefings” of the “intelligence and security organs.”

### 4. Mentors and Friends: Comrades in Battle, Masters at the Work

A month’s training was in truth too short: not to speak of the trainees learning little, even the leaders who were soon to bear the real responsibility had, all alike, no standard to go by. And the more so because this task of ours was well-nigh a thing newly founded, and no man, whoever he might be, could speak of experience in it; and so there was nothing for it but to feel our way forward.

In such a case what one leaned upon was that all should be near in view, and of one step, and—the hardest thing of all—cooperate in true sincerity. These conditions our First Pacification Brigade very nearly all possessed, and it was for this that we came to have doings out of the common run.

First let me here introduce our deputy brigade commander, Mr. Li Yulin. Mr. Yulin was born in the seventh year before the Republic, being five years my elder. He was a man of Luan county in Hebei, and I of Ninghe in Hebei; our old homes were both in East Hebei, so that we might be reckoned near fellow-townsmen. It is a pity we had not been acquainted before, and met for the first time only while training at the “Lizhi Class.” Yet, to tell it, we had long since served together—this was in the twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth years of the Republic, during the War of Resistance: at that time Mr. Yulin was waging guerrilla war behind the enemy’s lines, having gathered no small force of men and arms, and through the guidance of Mr. Zheng Enpu was formed into the Sixth Route Army of the “North China Loyal and Patriotic Army,” with Mr. Zheng Enpu as commander and Mr. Li Yulin as chief of staff.

To speak of the “North China Loyal and Patriotic Army” is to call to mind the “Luan-Yu Guerrilla General Headquarters.” In the fifth chapter of the first of these poor books, Rooting Out Traitors in the North, I gave a brief account of it, wherein it was said: “In the twenty-seventh year Mr. Dai sent a telegraphed order to open up guerrilla work, the original text running, in brief: ‘The Generalissimo has by express order appointed you and the rest guerrilla commanders of Luan-Yu; pray set about the organizing at once, take firm hold of the several units, and carry on every sort of work to throw the enemy into confusion and to wreck him—this being at present the one and only way to serve the state in loyalty.’” What the telegram called “by express order appointed you and the rest” meant that the Military Affairs Commission had appointed Wang Tianmu, Chief of the “Beiping District,” as “Luan-Yu Guerrilla Commander-in-Chief,” and Chen Gongshu, Chief of the “Tianjin Station,” as “Luan-Yu Deputy Commander-in-Chief of the Guerrillas.” As for what was meant by an “express order,” I neither paid it heed at the time nor rightly understood it. Not until the fifty-first year of the Republic did I chance, among the personnel records of the “Intelligence Bureau,” upon a “commission of appointment.” I remember it was in an envelope a little larger than the common sort, on the face of which were printed flat characters in blue, the first few reading “Military Affairs Commission” and the like, while the characters below I did not look at closely. As for what was written on the “commission of appointment,” apart from my own name I cannot recall it. This memorable thing, after a single glance, I knew not to cherish, and then, past all understanding, could not find again.

Let me now explain the two words “Luan-Yu.” “Luan” is Luan county; “Yu” is Linyu county—commonly written with the single character 榆—and what it points to is the well-known Shanhaiguan. The two counties of Luan and Yu lie no great way apart, their compass of activity was narrow, and it was, as the saying goes, poor hills and wild waste. For the waging of guerrilla war it served only for concealment, but was no easy place to subsist in; and so we could not but expand outward. Before long, unlooked-for, we had spread along the line of the Jin-Pu, and along the line of the Ping-Han, as far as the northern bank of the Yellow River. And it was for this very reason that, by the decision of our superiors, the “Luan-Yu General Headquarters” was abolished, and, uniting its name with that of the “Loyal and Patriotic Army” that was active south of the Yangtze, was changed to the “North China Loyal and Patriotic Army,” with Mr. Wang Tianmu and myself still holding, respectively, the posts of commander and deputy commander.

By the first month of the twenty-eighth year, the writer was transferred away from Tianjin to Hong Kong and thence by way of Vietnam, to take up another task; the “North China Loyal and Patriotic Army” still held a number of strongpoints and unceasingly gave the Japanese forces play. At that time the “Sixth Route Army of the North China Loyal and Patriotic” was the staunchest and most active of the armed forces of resistance. Little did I know that Mr. Li Yulin was the very chief of staff of that “Sixth Route Army.” Told thus, we had long been comrades on one line of battle.

In the “brief record” of Mr. Li Yulin there is a passage that runs, in part: “Thereafter formed into the Sixth Route Army of the North China Loyal and Patriotic Army, the gentleman served as chief of staff, and in East Hebei and North Hebei dealt the bandit foe heavy blows. In the twenty-eighth year of the Republic he went to Tianjin to convene a secret meeting; the Japanese enemy got wind of it, and many comrades were seized, while the gentleman, disguised, slipped away and by good hap escaped the peril. Afterward, to rescue his comrades, he went again, alone, to Beiping; the Japanese secret agents learned of it, and he was taken and cast into prison. In gaol he suffered cruel tortures to the full, dying and reviving some scores of times; yet the gentleman was resolved to die, never to yield, and asked only for a speedy death. In one interrogation he struck and wounded a Japanese sergeant-major with a wooden chair, and swallowed a quantity of tincture of iodine besides, meaning to die, yet did not. At first condemned to death, he was then sentenced to life; in gaol he was numbered No. 5, and, sharing his clothes and his food with his fellow-prisoners, was called by all ‘Fifth Brother.’ On the victory in the War of Resistance he was released, and appointed Chief of the Police Bureau of Tangshan municipality. In the thirty-sixth year he came to the Lizhi Class of the Central Training Corps at Nanjing to train; when the Ministry of National Defense set up the Pacification Corps, he led the trainees back to Beiping and served as deputy commander of the First Brigade, opening an all-out struggle against the bandits.” By what the writer knows, around the time of Mr. Li Yulin’s arrest in the autumn and winter of the twenty-eighth year, both my “Beiping District” and “Tianjin Station” were broken up by the Japanese enemy: Zeng Che, acting Chief of the “Tianjin Station,” and Chen Ziyi and Zhou Shiguang and many others, station chiefs belonging to the “Beiping District,” were seized; Wang Wen, leader of the “Tianjin Station action section” and concurrently directly subordinate unit commander of the “North China Loyal and Patriotic Army,” was seized too; and most grievous of all, all these comrades named above have already, in gallantry, met their deaths.

I learn besides that Mr. Zheng Enpu, leader of the “Tianjin Station military section” and concurrently commander of the “Sixth Route Army of the North China Loyal and Patriotic Army,” was seized at this same juncture; whether at the same time as Mr. Li Yulin, though, remains to be looked into. By the close of the twenty-eighth year the case at Beiping and Tianjin was perilous, and most of our working comrades could no longer find a footing; whereupon Mr. Wang Tianmu, commander of the “North China Loyal and Patriotic Army,” Brother Qi Qingbin, its chief of staff, and Brother Zhang Zuoxing, director of its political department, made their several escapes and went south—of which the third of these poor books has told. Thereafter the “North China Loyal and Patriotic Army” fell silent, far from the brilliance of the “Loyal and Patriotic Army” that was active in Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Anhui and did its work with such color and éclat.

Because the “North China Loyal and Patriotic Army” had lost its center of leadership, the units belonging to it were, piecemeal, “swallowed up” or “absorbed” by the Communist forces—such men as Li Yunchang of East Hebei and Lü Zhengcao of Central Hebei were of them, and all became, in after days, figures of corps-commander rank in the Communist army. And this batch of men was the very ones we now faced, coming to Beiping and Tianjin for the “pacification” and the “suppression of rebellion”; shameful to tell, when Mr. Yulin and I spoke of these bygone matters, it did but the more spur our resolve to have one grapple with the Communist forces.

Mr. Li Yulin was a man of true feeling; even were one not to serve with him, but merely to have him for a friend, that too were a good thing. Mr. Yulin, though rich in wit and resource, never schemed against others—a thing especially hard to come by. And then he was practiced and polished in his dealings with the world, with nothing stale or stiff about him; sure in what he did, and never given to bluster. We were together two years, cooperating without a rift, and never once fell into a quarrel of views, still less into open dispute. He was several years my elder, yet always addressed me with the respectful “you”—for he who can honor others must himself receive men’s honor. This is no feigned modesty, nor a saying of what sounds well, nor need I trick it out: he had, in truth, more of merit than I and less of fault; and what one most admires and cannot forget is that he could put things into practice with his own hands, and that spirit of his of buckling down to hard, quiet work.

Today I would tell in all sincerity those classmates and comrades of the First Brigade yet living, that Mr. Yulin gave more to the First Brigade than I, and the contribution he made was, of course, greater than mine.

Next, let me introduce our political director, Mr. Luo Jing. From the first of the Nameless Heroes books, Rooting Out Traitors in the North, onward, it was this same Mr. Luo Jing whom, book after book, we asked to write the cover, and book after book asked to inscribe the title-leaf. That we asked Mr. Luo Jing to inscribe it was not only because his hand shows the mastery of long labor; more meaningful still, it betokens the boundless reverence we bear him from the very heart!

Mr. Luo Jing in his person was all in order and all upright, a gentleman of the utmost sincerity. To praise him as a perfect man were no excess.

Of Mr. Luo Jing’s past he has an account in his own hand, which I here excerpt as follows: “In the twenty-sixth year of the Republic I graduated from the Department of Chinese Literature in the College of Letters of Fu Jen University at Beiping. The May examinations just done, I was introduced to a post in the Administration Bureau of the Ping-Sui Railway. Before long the fighting at the Marco Polo Bridge broke out, and Beiping and Tianjin fell one after the other. A young man, on the very spot and beholding the misery of it, my heart was as if aflame. My colleague Xing Guangmo introduced one He Qixiang, said to be a man sent by the Central Government; we met several times, but I did not know him well. In the eighth month of the twenty-seventh year Mr. He met with mishap and fled to Nanjing, and I was arrested by the Japanese gendarmerie and held in confinement a hundred days and more, being released for want of evidence. It falling out that the Institute of Chinese History at Fu Jen University was taking in students, I was admitted, and pursued my studies further.

“One day Shen Jianshi, dean of the College of Letters, introduced me to Zhou Shiguang, who in turn passed me on to Mr. Qiao Jiacai, and I resolved firmly to go to the great rear and train. I recall that on the eve of my leaving Beiping Dean Shen gave a farewell dinner at Yuhuatai (the writer’s note: Yuhuatai was in Xila Hutong at Bamianzao, a famous eating-house of Beiping; its specialty dishes included dry-braised crucian carp, walnut cream, and soup dumplings, and the like), at which were present Secretary-General Ying Qianli, Dean Zhang Huai, Mr. Zhou Shiguang, and Mr. Wei Nanchang.

“On the way I passed through Baotou in Suiyuan, entered upon the great grasslands, Shanba, and Ningxia, and in the eleventh month reached Lanzhou. I trained a year and more (the writer’s note: that training unit was the ‘Lanzhou Training Class’ run by our own Bureau, reckoned as the second session of the ‘Special Police Class’), and in the third month of the thirtieth year completed the course, being assigned to the personnel section of the Bureau headquarters at Chongqing, marked down to a specialist unit; the section head was Gong Xianfang, the deputy section head Liu Qinli.

“The business of the Bureau headquarters grew heavier by the day, and there was a proposal to enlarge the secretariat; the heads of the several units recommended men to sit an examination for assistant secretary, and Mr. Liu Qirui recommended me. A few days later Mr. Liu said: ‘You have passed; you may wind up your work, hand over to the section head, and report to Chamber A.’ The director of Chamber A was Zhang Yifu; there were six men in all, Secretary Pan Qiwu among them, and the great desk in the middle was Mr. Dai’s seat.

“At Chongqing, from the twenty-ninth year on, there were air-raid warnings well-nigh every day, and the dropping of bombs was a thing grown common. By the fifth month of the thirtieth year it was fiercer still. In the fifth month several thousand were suffocated to death in the Great Tunnel—a mighty calamity. Early in the seventh month the Bureau headquarters moved its several units, by night, to a silk-reeling mill in the outskirts. The eleventh day of the eighth month was the grand meeting to welcome home two comrades from Shanghai who had escaped peril. I was just in the personnel chamber, making ready to hand over, when of a sudden I heard someone cry, ‘Alarm! Alarm!’ Looking up, I saw men running out; Liu Qinli and I ran to the gallery behind the office and stood beside the washbasins, watching the sky’s edge—no aircraft to be seen, but a droning heard. Mr. Liu crouched by my left leg. He tugged at my trouser-cuff and said, ‘Down, quick’—the word not yet out, and I was already struck by a shell-splinter, and lay upon Mr. Liu, my head streaming blood, senseless in a swoon. Waking a little after, I saw only the trees bald and leafless, and swooned again. Waking once more, several comrades were bearing me, and others followed behind—it seemed midway on a mountain road—and I swooned again; and when I woke, I lay upon a bed. So it was for twenty days and more, lodged at the Sun Yat-sen Hospital.

“Comrade Zhang of the personnel chamber said: ‘Your head was wounded by the blast, the blood would not stop, and one towel on the washstand was not enough—several were changed.’ My Fu Jen fellow-student Geng said: ‘When you were wounded, Mr. Dai was giving an oral examination to the foreign-affairs class students, and he said: “Send him first to the infirmary; if he cannot be cured there, he may go in my sedan-chair” (the writer’s note: a rude bamboo chair borne by two men, handy for going up and down steep slopes) “to the Sun Yat-sen Hospital on Gele Mountain; I shall write to Dean Shen Kefei that he must by all means save his life.” On the way to the hospital we met a Japanese air-raid again, and set you down beneath a small tree, and took cover ourselves. When the aircraft were gone, the rain poured down in torrents; seeing your hands and feet trembling, we made our way with much ado to a mountain household and borrowed old clothes to keep you warm—truly a heaping of calamity on calamity.’ Zhang, my fellow-student who handled general affairs, said: ‘The second day in hospital was the most perilous of all; your face went black and you foamed at the mouth. I ordered a great coffin at Chongqing, but could find no grave-clothes of the large size, and bought besides a bolt of white silk—you being fond of letters, if death should wrap you round, you might yet scribble upon it.’ Having lost too much blood from my wound, I was parched and longed to drink; waking at midnight in the sickroom, I saw on a small table to the right of the bed a teapot and cup, but the right half of my body could not stir and my left hand trembled without cease, and in the struggle the pot and cup fell to the floor, and I swooned to the ground. The nurse, seeing it, did but bind my right hand with gauze to the bed-rail, herself not knowing how grave the moment was.

“A month and more later, I could little by little eat, and learned to sit, to stand, to walk; the hospital gave notice of discharge, and I lodged for a time in one of three rooms above the silk-reeling mill, someone bringing me meals each day. One day Mr. He Zhiyuan came, at Mr. Dai’s order, to see me, minded to send me to Chengdu for treatment by a foreign doctor, and bringing a bottle of American-made vitamins. Two days after, Mr. Fu Youquan, my instructor during training, came to see me and said: ‘The Queen Mary Hospital in Hong Kong is well equipped; to be able to get to Hong Kong is the best course.’ Who it was that reported this notion to Mr. Dai I do not know; but one day at midnight someone bade me dress, get into the sedan-chair, go down the mountain and take a car to Chongqing. He who tended me said Mr. Dai had sent men to see me to Hong Kong for treatment—there was a noon flight; today being Sunday, the Bureau headquarters was in session with Mr. Dai presiding, and I was to wait at the door to take my leave of Mr. Dai.

“Mr. Dai came down, and said: ‘The plane has been held up two hours at Chengdu; go you and wait at the field.’ The waiting-shed at Shanhuba was thronged with people; the guard whispered that Mr. Dai was seeing me off, that I was not to speak but to rise softly to my feet. Mr. Dai came from the right, made one turn about the table, fixed his eyes on me, and went. Getting aboard, I slept at once, and was waked to find we had reached Hong Kong. At Hong Kong I lodged for a time at the ‘Kelaimen’ Hotel (sold by the English to our side to run), and the next day was taken to the Queen Mary Hospital; two days after, an English doctor came bringing more than twenty students of the Hong Kong Medical College—there were foreign students, South Seas students, Cantonese students—and of them only Wang Zhitong, a Tianjin man, could speak the national tongue. I, being dumb, communicated in writing; Wang Zhitong said the doctor was in the midst of studying the diagnosis.

“From my wounding on the eleventh of the eighth month I was in a daze, not knowing the month or the day, unable to read a newspaper; about the beginning of the twelfth month of the thirtieth year, someone said Japan had bombed Pearl Harbor, and two days after someone said Japanese aircraft were reconnoitering over Hong Kong, and someone said the Canadian troops (the Hong Kong garrison) had surrendered to Japan. The hospital declared the patients discharged; my liaison man came, and barefoot I left the hospital and crossed the water to Kowloon, still lodging at the same hotel. The Japanese fought four days, and Kowloon fell; by the seventeenth day Hong Kong surrendered, and pitiful were the sufferings, past all telling, of the Chinese who could not get away.

“The guests left at the hotel were all folk connected with the Bureau headquarters; at first there was a sack of rice for all to eat, and, with no vegetables, we ate salted eggs, and when the salted eggs were gone, fermented bean-curd, and afterward only three or five of us remained. Japanese troops upstairs drove us down to move into the basement, and a Japanese secret agent came and said, this hotel was opened by the English—why was the proprietor a Chinese? Happily, in the end they only drove us out of doors.

“Leaving the hotel, I rented, on the third floor of a certain number on Shanghai Street in Kowloon, a share of a couch in a picture-mounter’s shop, the rent five Hong Kong dollars a month. Upstairs there was no privy, and for every easing one had to go down—which was a hardship to me.

“In the confusion some of the comrades left Hong Kong one by one, some going on foot to Canton and thence to Qujiang, the travel-money issued being 1,500 yuan of fabi to each man; I could not walk, yet was issued the 1,500 all the same. At that time a money-shop gave ten Hong Kong notes for a hundred yuan of fabi—how many days could so paltry a sum keep one alive? For me, a mute, it was a very dead end.

“The dead in Hong Kong and Kowloon were all too many, and flies covered the ground; the Japanese, fearing pestilence, sent doctors here and there to inoculate the passers-by. One day, going to the privy, I was herded by Japanese soldiers into a line, and after waiting more than an hour chanced upon Wang Zhitong, the intern of the Queen Mary Hospital. He said: ‘President Li of the Hebei Fellow-Townsmen’s Association put a notice in the papers seeking you, but there was never any word. I too, after the Medical College was dissolved, cannot leave Hong Kong, and am lodging for the while at the Fellow-Townsmen’s Association; leave your address, and going back tonight I shall tell them, and tomorrow we come to fetch you.’ The next day I was received at the Fellow-Townsmen’s Association. It turned out my second uncle at Tianjin had written to Mr. Wang at Shanghai; Mr. Wang had entrusted it to President Li at Hong Kong; and President Li had further introduced me to the Kwong Wah Hospital, where, while being treated, I made my plans to leave Hong Kong, and at the same time got into liaison with the Bureau headquarters. A few days later I bought a ticket on a Japanese steamer to Shanghai; who would have thought I should meet a typhoon again, and in great wind and waves eight days on the way to Shanghai, where I stayed a dozen days and more, and took the train back to Tianjin and thence to Beiping.

“Reaching Beiping, I dared not rashly make contact with my family, but first sought out my old friend Liu Luoyi to take counsel, and lodged a while in the municipal hospital. In the second month of the thirty-second year I moved to a small room at my third brother’s house by Dongzhimen to convalesce, seeing first Dr. Guan Songtao and afterward Dr. Feng ——. Dr. Guan said: ‘The shell-splinter in the brain cannot be taken out, for fear the ailment should hereafter break forth from it; and were an operation ventured to take it out, the right hand and foot would still not work nimbly.’ So I resolved to seek no more treatment, and to overcome all by my own resolve. Thenceforth I learned to make sounds, learned to speak, practiced thinking, practiced gymnastics, and made progress by the day.

“One day a friend came, and through him I learned how things stood at Chongqing, and so made ready to return to the ranks. On the twentieth day of the tenth month of the thirty-second year I reached Kaifeng, and by way of Jiekou and Zhengzhou went to Xi’an. I heard that Mr. Dai was at Lanzhou and would soon come to Xi’an. Mr. Dai, seeing me come to call, was greatly pleased and said: ‘Go you first to Chongqing and make ready to take part in next year’s April First assembly; I have business in eastern Gansu.’ Afterward I was posted to work at the ‘April First Library.’

“In the thirty-fourth year Japan surrendered, and the Bureau headquarters approved my being sent with Mr. Ma Hansan to Beiping; but it falling on the eighth of the twelfth month that my eldest son Zhe’er was born at the Chongqing municipal hospital, I asked leave to delay, and by the next spring, when I reached southern Shaanxi, the Communist forces were running wild all along the Long-Hai Railway, and I was held up at Baoji several months.

“I recall that in the training days of the twenty-eighth year I saw Mr. Liu Peichu, close-shaven of head and in a gray cotton Sun Yat-sen tunic, forever slipping in among the ranks to hear the comrades talk—simple, lively, without airs, easy of approach. On the seventeenth day of the third month of the thirty-fifth year Mr. Dai met his death, and Mr. Liu Peichu was transferred from his post as administrative inspector-commissioner of Hebei to be Corps Commander of the Ministry of National Defense ‘People’s Service Corps,’ and began work in the counties of the eastern region of Henan, his headquarters set at Zhengzhou. Learning that I was held up at Baoji, he summoned me and forthwith made me an overseer of the Second Brigade, with the counties of Lanfeng, Shangqiu, Heze and the rest under my charge. Each day I walked some scores of li, looking into how the populace was organized, and at night must hold a review-meeting besides—it was the hardest work of my life. Afterward, when it was found that a certain brigade commander had played false in the moving of funds, and after two months of setting-to-rights, I came to feel little sympathy with Mr. Liu Peichu’s use of men. In the seventh month of the next year Mr. Liu passed to be Corps Commander of the Ministry of National Defense ‘Pacification Corps,’ and I went with him to Nanjing to help make it ready. During the training at the ‘Lizhi Class’ I was posted director of the Fourth Brigade; and when Li Yulin, director of the First Brigade, went to Beiping to see to the recruiting for the second session, I was thereupon transferred to the First Brigade as its director, and lodged in one room with the brigade commander a month and more. From then we served together two years, in all things, within and without, in peace and concord.”

The above is the brief life that Mr. Luo Jing lately wrote out for me in his own hand; the script of the original is beautiful in the extreme, every character a pearl, and I shall keep it well and treasure it.

Before this, I had not known Mr. Luo’s past in such detail; and having read it, I truly admire his unresting self-strengthening, and that valiant spirit which grappled with ill fortune his whole life long.

Mr. Luo Jing, in the brigade, was a steadying force firm as bedrock; I gave into his hands the assessment of personnel, promotions and transfers and replacements, leave and the lending of money, the airing of grievances and the pouring-out of hardships. Because he held throughout to the great impartiality, weighing both reason and feeling, the whole brigade, high and low, trusted him without exception; and it was thus that we could come to be of one virtue and one heart, and, gathering our strength, all together open up the work.

To say it once more: Mr. Luo Jing was the steadying force that kept our First Brigade free of any trouble of personnel whatever.

Yet had he seen the other side of my life—no need to ask—he too would surely have shaken his head.

Next to be introduced is Mr. Liu Yuanshen. His working place in the brigade was, on the one hand, that of chief secretary, and on the other he served besides as a field commanding officer.

To speak plainly, in the early time his title in the brigade was chief secretary, but in reality he managed the affairs of the “Beiping directly subordinate section of the Baomiju”—this an expedient of my own arranging, which by rights was not quite to be justified; happily, high and low, none spoke a word against it.

Mr. Liu Yuanshen was born in the sixth year of the Republic, a man of Yishui in Shandong. He graduated from the “Linli Training Class” run by the “Juntong Bureau,” whose formal name was the first session of the Special Police Class of the Central Police Officers’ Academy. In the class he was a student of high parts, ranking at the head in every examination and assessment; on graduating he was assigned to work in the “Shanghai District,” rising from editor and assistant clerk to field action-brigade commander. As for this passage of his career in the “Shanghai District,” the third and fourth of these poor Nameless Heroes books tell of it at length, and I shall not repeat it here.

Mr. Liu Yuanshen is, moreover, the collator of all five of these poor Nameless Heroes books; and to this “collating” of his, besides its being true to the name, one ought to add the word “by special engagement,” for as to the collating of this book, apart from him no second man is to be thought of—only it has troubled him, and put him to hard toil.

Mr. Yuanshen had not only to read every page of the manuscript, but to strike out the ill-fitting words and phrases, correct the errors and add notes, polish the wording, and at times to exchange views with me face to face and weigh them out with care. Besides which, in the past second, third, and fourth books, some chapters were, in their whole text, written by Mr. Yuanshen. In a case like this, unless the two men be deep friends, how could it ever be done?

In the eighth month of the thirty-fourth year of the Republic, on the victory in the War of Resistance, Brother Qi Qingbin, by order of his superiors, gathered at Shanghai all the working units of our Bureau whose standing had already been exposed or half-opened, together with the advance parties of the “Loyal and Patriotic Army” arriving one after another at Shanghai, and set up the “Shanghai Provisional Working Committee” on Ju’erdian Road of the French Concession, opening for business on the first day of the ninth month. This committee had Brother Qingbin as its chairman-member, Mr. Yuanshen as member and concurrently secretary, and the men in charge of the several units all as members. The committee’s chief tasks were: first, to take the whole charge of funds and pay and the needful supplies, all to be pooled and disbursed together; second, to make the pace uniform and uphold discipline; third, to keep watch over, and to soothe, the few among our Bureau who during the War of Resistance had gone over to the traitors, pending their disposal; fourth, to gather intelligence, and to make ready beforehand for the future work of rooting out traitors. And it was to give close heed to Communist activity besides, against their sowing disorder as the chance offered.

Plainly, the “Shanghai Provisional Working Committee” was, in the transitional stage before demobilization, the highest leading organ that the Juntong Bureau set in the Shanghai region to take charge of command and liaison; the weight of its responsibility, and the press and multitude of its work, may well be imagined. Before long, Brother Qi Qingbin, who had already been named chief of the inspection department of the “Beiping Garrison Command,” invited him to go and serve as chief inspector of that inspection department; but before the thing, though decided, had been carried out, Bi Gaokui, formerly a section leader of the “Shanghai District,” chanced to become chief inspector of the inspection department of the “Songhu Garrison Command,” and, being near at hand, drew him along to meet the department chief Cheng Yiming (Cheng had once been an instructor of the Special Police Class), and forthwith named him inspection-brigade commander of the Pudong District (with the two counties of Chuansha and Fengxian under it); and the tie of friendship being hard to refuse, he stayed to work at the Shanghai Garrison headquarters and did not, after all, go.

About the eighth month of the thirty-sixth year, when the Lizhi Class training was done and the writer flew by way of Shanghai to Beiping, I got to see Mr. Yuanshen; and because we had cooperated some years and shared adversity besides, close as water and milk, I firmly invited him to come to Beiping, to make together a fresh start in the work; and he generously consented, and we fixed a date for our next meeting.

It was about the tenth month, I think, that Mr. Liu Yuanshen, together with Wang Zhibin, Qi Zhenping, and Guo Zizhong, came to Beiping by the same road. Mr. Yuanshen stayed at the brigade headquarters in Beiping, while the three—Wang, Qi, and Guo—set up a directly subordinate intelligence section of the brigade and were sent to Tianjin; and thereafter they too had, each of them, a tale of their own.

Mr. Yuanshen’s gifts stood out above the rest, and were looked up to by all who knew him; only he suffered for want of a chance to spread out his powers. In the fourth book I mentioned that, in the summer of the thirtieth year of the Republic, our superiors telegraphed an order transferring Mr. Yuanshen from Shanghai to Chengdu, meaning to lay some weightier charge upon him; but I “kept him back by force,” an act that was in truth all a minding of my own gain and loss with no regard for another’s good or hurt—though at the time how could I have understood this reasoning? Not long after, Mr. Yuanshen, by ill hap, met with mishap and was seized, and, falling thus a single step behind, was left behind his fellows: the wrong he suffered was my fault.

This time, in inviting Mr. Yuanshen north, I hoped, of course, that we should all achieve something; for, over and above the fulfilling of our task, who does not wish to make a little scene of his own wherein he may spread out his aspirations?

The above has been, of set purpose, to introduce the three important pillars of my First Brigade, and to serve besides as the prelude to all the hard and bitter struggles that were to come.
