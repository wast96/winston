## Chapter 33

Mrs. Manfield beside me, the car drove along the smooth road; the country scene was dim, the street lamps strangely bright—we were far from the city now, already at Jiangwan.

Umetake's official residence was a gray Western-style house not far from what had once been our Municipal Government Building; before the war it had blazed with crystal light, and to what high official or rich merchant it had belonged I did not know—now it was occupied by Umetake. The house stood some twenty feet back from the road, approached by a drive wide enough for two cars abreast, leading up to the entrance. Along both sides of this drive grew trim rows of holly, and tonight the holly trees were hung all over with colored electric lights; the mouth of the drive was thick with Japanese soldiers and puppet police, and cars had to stop there. Two smartly uniformed Japanese soldiers came up and questioned us gravely; I showed one the invitation, and he directed me to drive in. At the end of the holly drive was a great round court, its whole circumference already lined with parked cars, as trim as a military array of tanks, all nose to the center and tail to the rim. By the time I arrived the second ring was nearly full, and I parked in a gap beside a 1940 Buick. At the center of the round court stood a tall cypress, tonight dressed up into a dazzling, rich and lovely Christmas tree. The moment I got out I noticed that Mei Yingzi's red car was not there—so she had not yet come? Mrs. Manfield very naturally took my arm. A page in green came over, bowed, and led us across the arc of the court and up the stone steps, in through the brilliantly lit doorway.

The drawing room was very spacious, and there were already many people in it. Rear Admiral Umetake, in full naval uniform, came over to usher us in; he did not introduce me to the guests one by one, but exchanged a few words with me, told me to sit where I pleased, and walked off. Going to the far end of the room, I found several Japanese army officers whom I had known before. Honsa Jiro and his party were not in the room (were they not coming?); of the many puppet officials I knew only three or four; but among a few Westerners I saw Dr. Philip and his wife. This was truly strange: I was not on especially close terms with Dr. Philip, yet seeing him now I really had the feeling of seeing one of my own kin. I was subconsciously aware that in this world everyone was my enemy, and only Philip was my friend. After a general exchange of greetings with the company, I shook hands with Philip. Philip seemed to notice that I was too warm; he shook my hand with a respectful bearing, while his stern eyes refused my show of affection, and I realized at once that I was not composed enough. So I checked myself, and drew in the too-thick smile at the corners of my mouth. In a cool and solemn tone I said in a low voice:

"Mrs. Stephen—"

"She's not coming," he said. "Unwell."

He was very cold, and the moment he had spoken he turned to talk with Mrs. Manfield. So I had no choice but to go and greet the others.

The host's hospitality could not be called poor, but the atmosphere was gloomy. The Japanese officers wore the very faces of victory; the puppet officials smiled their fawning smiles; everyone talked polite nothings in hushed, careful voices, striving to make every sentence noncommittal—expressing no opinion, betraying no feeling, carrying no argument—and yet one had to speak! Striving to make every smile express no joy, no mockery, and no comfort either—and yet one had to smile! Such was the world, such the air, that here and now I wished a bomb might utterly destroy it.

I believed that everyone present felt as I did; and what gave me a slight comfort was that my conscience had no remorse in it, for my mission and my work were precisely to destroy such a world—though for that very reason my mind bore one more burden.

The guests kept arriving, and the drawing room slowly filled. I was waiting to catch sight of Mei Yingzi early, but still Mei Yingzi did not come.

Suddenly a loud, ringing laugh came from outside, and the whole air began to stir; everyone seized the chance to break off their tedious small talk and turned their eyes to the door. I heard Umetake talking loudly outside, in Japanese, which I did not understand. But how much complacency was in that tone, how much pride, how much excitement!

My heart leapt all at once, for I judged this was beyond doubt Mei Yingzi arriving; I hardly blinked an eye, gazing at the door—but the one who came in was Bai Ping.

Bai Ping, eyes lowered, her head almost slightly bowed, wore a long-furred silver-fox coat, a snow-white evening gown trailing beneath it, one hand hooked on the arm of Colonel Arita's resplendent army uniform, while Umetake, in his handsome naval uniform, stood on the other side talking with her; I saw on her hand the diamond ring I had given her. She said not a word, only smiling and lowering her head, for all the world like a bride going up before the minister; it was the first time I had seen her in such a posture—demure, bashful, still, serene. Was it feigned? I wondered.

Umetake took Bai Ping's coat; many Japanese officers came over to greet her, and Bai Ping began slowly to draw away from Arita's arm, greeting them as though bashful, as though delicate, and as though sacredly inviolable, and only at last responded to the acquaintances around her. In a tone courteous, haughty and faintly commanding, she greeted me coolly and lightly:

"You've come? And where are Honsa and the rest?"

"I don't know," I said. "I came as Mrs. Manfield's escort."

Then Bai Ping fell to greeting Mrs. Manfield, and I began to see a rather more distinct smile on her, and to hear her softly lowered voice.

For a while the air of the drawing room flowed more freely; Bai Ping was like a spring current scattering the dead-still duckweed. The puppet officials stood in a knot by twos and threes, their wives in another knot, a few Westerners in another, a few Japanese merchants, officers and staff officers now scattering, now gathering in another. Around Bai Ping the most smiles always gathered; she talked and laughed with each group with perfect ease, and even among the group of puppet officials' wives, whom she scarcely knew, her social touch was exactly right.

The guests still kept arriving; Honsa Jiro and his party came too, bringing women I did not know, and I very naturally fell in with them. Here I had nothing to say to the Japanese officers, and was not well enough acquainted with the puppet officials—both sides were on their guard; Philip had refused my affection, Bai Ping was engaged in society on every side; it was Honsa Jiro and his party, after all, who were both familiar and free of entanglements, with whom one could chat at ease. He told me that Umetake had lived a long time in England, had once been a military attaché at the legation, and was a thoroughly Europeanized figure. He told me too that the Japanese girl he had brought was a teahouse hostess from North Sichuan Road, and that if I liked her he could yield her to me; then he suddenly pointed to a woman with an oval face among the puppet officials' wives and asked whether I knew her. I said I did not, and he told me she had once been a dance-hostess at the White Palace, and that he had once amused himself with her—Honsa was a merchant who had lived in Shanghai for over ten years and might be said to have been assimilated by China; he had not the least love of "country." Though he did not oppose Japan's invasion of China, he was forever grumbling about the Japanese army's control of trade and prices and the like. Apart from business he was very good at pleasure and spent money generously, using it to keep up connections with the Japanese officers, so that both sides found their advantage in him. My heart bore a heavy load of care, and being with him was no more than a way to cover my lonely and awkward situation; so his high-spirited talk could rouse in me neither sympathy nor interest, and I did not take it all in.

I now saw that Mei Yingzi's estimate had been right: to keep watch on Bai Ping was an utterly impossible thing. I was no more than a frog, and Bai Ping a crucian carp; to bid me keep watch on Bai Ping here was like bidding a frog keep watch on a crucian carp at the bottom of a river—and, moreover, not to let others see that I was watching her, which was of course an utterly impossible thing. Now, in a single drawing room, I could indeed notice Bai Ping's every movement; but if Bai Ping went out on Arita's or Umetake's arm, I had little means of following her all the way. I anxiously hoped Mei Yingzi would come; I wanted to tell her this, and at the same time to urge her that, if things were too difficult, she should give up tonight's work and carry it out later when the chance came. Strange to say, at our earlier meetings I had never once put this idea to her. I suppose that when a man's mind is set wholly on the positive side, he will not turn back to reconsider; the whole plan had centered on the question of "how best to do it," and had never thought of the question of "when best to do it." I felt this work need not be confined to today at all, and so I hoped Mei Yingzi would come quickly, that I might offer her my view.

I heard many people asking one another after Mei Yingzi; and Bai Ping, with a very natural air, had in the course of conversation asked different people why Mei Yingzi had still not come—already for the seventh time, and I fancied the eighth was about to begin.

"Miss Mei Yingzi!" someone cried outside.

As Umetake went out to meet her, Mei Yingzi had already come in like light itself, four Japanese army men clustered about her and two naval officers following after; Umetake, with great gravity and courtesy, went to shake her hand. Having shaken hands with Umetake, Mei Yingzi lifted her head and looked ahead, sweeping the whole crowd of the hall with the brightest, sweetest glance; at this the room fell silent as death, everyone's attention concentrated upon her. She wore a plain white long-furred coat, so pure that it had not a trace of color, and beneath it the gown that stirred was the down of a white bird fresh from its bath; most striking of all was the collar of pure white pearls hung about her bare throat. Just as I was watching her face, she smiled; and no sooner had she let people see her smile than she made the company a bow of twenty degrees. I am sure everyone in the hall believed her glance had lit on him alone, and everyone believed her sweet smile a gift meant for him alone; needless to say, everyone believed too that this bow of twenty degrees was a greeting to himself, and as if by agreement everyone returned it with a bow of forty-five degrees. I found that in an instant Mei Yingzi had become an empress. Mei Yingzi raised her head, handed her coat to Umetake, and first of all shook hands intimately with Bai Ping, pouring out endlessly her longing after their long parting; then she turned to the cluster of Westerners and greeted them one by one in English, after which she made polite talk with the puppet officials in graceful Mandarin—I marveled that she should be on familiar terms with every one of them—and at last paused among the Japanese naval officers, conversing in fluent Japanese.

The servants brought cocktails, and when everyone held a glass, Umetake raised his high and said:

"Drink this glass to the beauty of our Mei Yingzi."

After all had drained their glasses, the servants brought a second. Then Mei Yingzi circled to the center, holding her glass high; and now for the first time our eyes met, and I found she was signaling something to me. She said:

"I beg the host's gracious leave that we drink this glass in blessing of Miss Bai Ping, and name her president of tonight's gathering."

"…" Bai Ping seemed to be saying something. But the cheers on every side drowned her out, and everyone raised their glasses and drank.

At the third glass, Bai Ping, shielded between two officers, raised her glass and said:

"To the peace of Greater East Asia, and to this first festive gathering of Sino-Japanese friendship, we name Miss Mei Yingzi the goddess of peace."

These words grated harshly on the ear, but they seemed to be a hint from the Japanese officers, for thereupon someone brought a garland woven of fresh flowers, and Umetake set his wineglass on the table, solemnly lifted the garland onto Mei Yingzi's head, then raised his glass anew and, before Mei Yingzi, said:

"In this first festive gathering of Sino-Japanese friendship, let us bless the everlasting radiance of the goddess of peace."

Then Umetake drank to Mei Yingzi, Bai Ping cheered and drank after him, and then everyone drank. A roar of applause drowned out all else.

The air, dull and dreary and forced a moment before, was now dissolved in the smiles of Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping. Through all the social talk Bai Ping deferred throughout to Mei Yingzi, and Mei Yingzi always took the part of a man championing Bai Ping and championed her; between the two of them there was scarcely a sign of vying for supremacy—so much so that, had I not been one of the circle, I should surely have taken them for a pair who set each other off to advantage.

At last Umetake ushered us to the ballroom, a great hall got up for the occasion. Where the heavy woolen curtains parted, in an anteroom, a band of seven was playing. After we went in, ten or so gorgeous Japanese women came in at the heels of six or seven naval officers. Umetake invited Bai Ping, commander of the evening, to open the dancing; everyone applauded, and so Mei Yingzi pushed Rear Admiral Umetake to lead Bai Ping out, and we fell to dancing after them.

I had to partner Mei Yingzi as early as possible, so as to say a few words, but no chance ever came; Mei Yingzi, I thought, must also feel the need to speak with me, for when the music stopped she came over to my side to talk, and this gave me the chance to dance with her.

Long used to talking at leisure and unhurried, I now had to talk in a brief snatch of time, and did not know where to begin—as though a crowd of words were welling up in my narrow throat, jammed like the doors of a theater when the film lets out, unable to get through. It was Mei Yingzi who spoke first:

"You must believe now that a dragon in the sea cannot be kept watch upon."

"A frog truly cannot keep watch on a crucian carp at the bottom of a river," I said. "So am I to—"

"You're treading on my dress." Mei Yingzi cut me off, at the same time gripping my hand; only then did I notice a Japanese soldier dancing past beside us, and so, seizing the chance, she went on: "In everything, one must think it all out beforehand; afterward one can only meet things as they come and leave the rest to Heaven."

"Mei Yingzi," I said, dancing as I drew her to a corner of the room, "could we not gather all today's feelings into gladness alone? There seem to be days enough yet; how would it be to sail our boat again when the wind is fair?"

"This is a shoal," she said. "It is seldom there is water to sail in; perhaps when the wind is fair there will be, but for the most part they are waterless days."

"Won't you think it over once more?"

"I have thought it over very carefully," she said.

"Mrs. Manfield came with me," I reminded her—meaning, of course, to ask whether there was any use to which Mrs. Manfield might be put.

But Mei Yingzi paid no heed; then, as if lost in thought, she suddenly seized a chance to say to me:

"By and by, when Bai Ping goes upstairs to gamble, you had better join in, and gamble hard with her as far as you can; I think this is your most glorious chance." She laughed happily: "At dancing you're no match."

"Thank you for pointing me to so good a chance."

The music stopped, and our talk broke off there.

I kept my eye on Bai Ping; Bai Ping was busy with the company, and I thought there was no need for me to keep watch on her—these men would naturally so beset her that she could hardly leave the place.

Just then I noticed a Japanese woman who seemed familiar, and who was also watching me. I felt sure we must have met somewhere, but do what I would I could not recall it; at last I went to ask her to dance, and on the floor I asked:

"Excuse me, miss—have we met somewhere before?"

"Of course," she said with a shy laugh. "I know you."

This astonished me, and I said:

"Then who am I?"

"A good friend of Miss Mei Yingzi's."

These words made me wonder, but I said in jest:

"Who here is not a friend of Miss Mei Yingzi's? Ah, that does not prove you know me."

"You," she laughed, "are the man who at the Standford asked me to sing 'The Setting Sun over the Huangpu.'"

I remembered that it was she, but I did not know her name. I said:

"Was it that day you didn't tell me your name?"

"My name is Miko." (What nationality this name is I do not know; here I merely set down the sound of it.)

"Forgive me," I said. "I am a very dull fellow; anyone who has not told me their name I can never afterward recall."

She laughed, a laugh with a touch of affectation in it, though its essence was innocent and sweet. I felt that Miko was a simple, pure, docile child; after keeping company with such deep and complicated girls as Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping, I found, on the contrary, that talking with a child like Miko was rather easier and more comfortable.
