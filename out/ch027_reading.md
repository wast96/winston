### Human Rabbits

To call a man a "rabbit" is contempt at its very lowest. The fashion for playing with "rabbits" once flourished in the north. Shameless fellows in plenty — a great many of them — would of their own will debase themselves to this vile trade of the boy-actor and the catamite. Yet not all of it was willing; no small number were driven to it by circumstance (just as not every woman who sells herself does so of her own accord). Since the founding of the Republic this vile trade has slowly fallen away.

Shanghai's "human rabbits" were already to be seen about the second year of the Republic. I had just then entered the newspaper world, and one day the editorial office received, by the postman's hand, a handbill got up like an invitation card: on one side was printed a little photograph of a "human rabbit," on the other a notice, fulsome stuff meant to pass for wit, and below it the address of the "rabbit-warren." The notice did no more than tell how comely this "human rabbit" was and how attentive its service, their drift being to bid us come and try. In those days the tide of vice on the Shanghai shore was not so fierce as now, nor the varieties of the trade so many, and who would care to take up the playing of "rabbits"? So before long they furled their flags and stilled their drums, and vanished without a trace.

But in these last five or six years, unlooked-for, this "male fashion" has come into great vogue again. Their get-up is by now almost wholly womanish — they rouge and powder themselves too, in shapes coquettish and grotesque, so that at first sight one can hardly tell the cock from the hen. And, borrowing the streetwalkers' own methods, they go pacing to and fro in the amusement halls and the public parks, casting inviting glances, waiting on the favour of the cut-sleeve fancier and the merely curious. They print little cards, just such as the kiln-girls use, and send men round to hand them out at the houses and hotels; the card gives the whereabouts of the "rabbit-warren," and the player may follow it like a map to the door and pay his tea-visit. In a hotel or a tavern, too, one may send round a note and have the rabbit fetched, for a bit of casual sport.

The Settlement authorities, to keep up public decency, spare no effort to arrest and punish any "human rabbit" they come upon. But arrest as they will, this evil fashion shows no sign of dying out; it springs from the press of livelihood, or from other wretched circumstances, and those who truly debase themselves of their own free choice are, I dare say, not many after all.
