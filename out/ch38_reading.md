## Chapter 38

Over high mountains, across lofty ridges, up sheer steeps and down headlong slopes—the hardship and weariness of our lives show themselves not in the time of our labour, but in the rest that comes after it. So it was with my wounds: only after I had lain down on the sickbed as if in collapse did I feel, about the effort of a moment before, a kind of incredible miracle.

Mei Yingzi sat down and said a few words of comfort; then Philip came in with another doctor, motioned her to go out, and after that I did not see her again for five weeks. This was because my operation was performed that afternoon, and for a long time after it I was mostly in a stupor; the doctors would not let anyone come to disturb me, and still less would they let me force myself into too much talk. So for sixty hours I was wholly at the doctors' disposal.

On the morning of the third day my mind was clearer; sunlight came in at the window, and the room was as bright as spring, several clusters of fresh flowers set here and there—the red roses were Mei Yingzi's, the camellias Bai Ping's, the daisies I supposed were...? and there was still... but I did not care to guess. I began to think of Bai Ping, to think of Mei Yingzi, to think of the many questions surrounding my coming into hospital.

For instance: what sort of person, after all, was Bai Ping? Mei Yingzi had already concluded she was an enemy spy; then why should she shoot a man she took to be a traitor to the country? For instance: had Philip come to save me on word from Bai Ping, or was it because Mei Yingzi had long been keeping watch on my movements? And for instance, that document—after Bai Ping found it missing, what had Mei Yingzi done with it? And for instance, did my family know...? In short, I was tormented by these tangled, insoluble threads of thought, and I very much hoped one of them would come to see me. I asked the nurse, and she told me that at present the doctor absolutely forbade any outside visits—could my wounds really be so serious, then? I asked her, and she answered no further. My left shoulder ached dully, and at the least stir it hurt sharply a long while; I believed the wound there had already begun to fester.

At nine o'clock a doctor came to examine me. At ten Dr. Philip came in and told me another operation was to be performed that afternoon. The last time, under the anaesthetic, I had supposed the bullets in both my wounds had been taken out; only now did I learn that that day's operation had been only on the left arm, and that today's would be for the left shoulder.

Philip did not talk much with me; he told me to set my mind at ease about everything, and went out. At noon I had no lunch, and moved my bowels besides; at two o'clock I was first carried to the X-ray room, and after being examined by X-ray was carried on to the operating theatre. My eyes passed over the white rooms, the white people; the doctors were all washing their hands; the instrument case bubbled and hissed on the spirit lamp. I was carried into an inner room, where many white-clad nurses stood round the operating table and settled me onto it; and soon, under the anaesthetic, I lost consciousness.

When I woke I was already lying on the sickbed, and I felt a heavy pain in my left shoulder, sharper than before. There seemed to be a very high fever in my head; the nurse came to take my temperature, but did not answer my questions. She gave me milk, orange juice, eggs, toast; I was very hungry, but could not eat much, and after that I sank again into a deep sleep.

The next day my sharp pain had not eased, nor on the third day and the fourth; on the fifth day, after the dressing was changed, Dr. Philip came to consult with me, saying the wound in the left shoulder must be operated on again. This truly dismayed me: the second operation had already left me with an unspeakable weight, and now there must be a third—I really did not know what to do.

"I've had enough," I said.

"But this has been carefully considered and discussed by us."

"And if there is no operation?"

"We doctors cannot answer that."

"Will a further operation be certain to leave no more trouble?"

"That, too, only God can answer you."

"Then will this be the last operation?"

"So we think."

"If science holds it right," I said at last, "I leave the decision to you."

That day's supper too was stopped; I put out the light and went to bed very early, but could not sleep. I fretted, I was anxious, uneasy, feeling my loneliness and emptiness and the dimness of my life on the morrow; but the moonlight beyond the sky was purest clear, and in an instant it poured in through the window frame and slowly spread over my bed, as if to soothe my wounds. My soul felt watered; I felt I ought to receive my fate in prayer and thanksgiving; and so, softly stroking my shoulder wound, I fell peacefully asleep.

At five in the morning I was woken to have my temperature taken and my medicine given, and moved my bowels again; at seven, with a leaping heart, I was carried once more to the operating theatre.

I do not know how much time passed before I woke on the bed out of the anaesthetic, and found myself in a state of high fever and pain, everything grey. I no longer had the strength to notice my surroundings; a long bolster propped the left side of my body, and the nurse charged me not to put the least burden on my left shoulder; I lay on the bed like a cripple.

The continuance of this pain seemed without end; in my sleep I woke again and again with the ache, and the right side of my body too had gone numb from lying still. A day was as long as a lifetime; I endured the day and then endured the night, watching the sun come in at the window and watching the sun go out, watching the stars twinkle and vanish in the dark sky, watching the moon waste thinner, sending out night after night a faint sorrow among the tree-clumps. Sometimes the wind soughed, and rain and snow drifted down, keeping my scattered thoughts company as they waited for the whiteness at the sky's edge.

But the days were at last got through, and I had a fresher, easier spirit for noticing my world. Almost every day fresh flowers were sent to the room; Mei Yingzi's were always red roses, Bai Ping's always pale or agate-coloured camellias; besides these there were the red-hearted, purple-petalled lotus-daisies, the wintersweet with its yellow flowers and brown stems, the nandina with its red dots and green leaves, the narcissus threaded with kingfisher-green and white; and I began to think that the world had not, after all, forgotten me.

The doctor had in fact allowed me to sit up quietly rather earlier, but because it was so hard to get up from lying down, and my head was so muddled, and I wanted to lie down again not long after sitting up, I had afterwards not wanted to get up any more. Now I made a second attempt; the nurse helped me down from the bed and put the dressing gown on me—that was Mei Yingzi's Christmas present, the very gown that had gone through the gunfire with me and been stained with my blood; the bloodstains had now been washed away, but the bullet holes remained. I put only my right arm through, the left merely laid across, and sat on the sofa, an indescribable feeling rising in my heart—a feeling mingled of the disorder of my mind and a momentary calm, of an unresolved hidden pain and the pleasure of a long torment just easing.

Today my spirits were better; I believed my fever had nearly gone. On the chair I ate the lunch the hospital supplied me, and ate a piece of chocolate sent by I knew not whom, and found it all very good to the taste.

Beyond the long window the sunlight was just right; on the benches under the bare trees the off-duty nurses sat reading, on the yellow-purple lawn people came and went, and on the far side of the corridor someone was whitewashing a wall—what a peaceful, quiet world it was. The furnishings of the room were very simple: besides the sickbed and its little bedside table were a small cabinet, a small table and the sofa; and on the cabinet, the table, the stand, and all around the window ledges, bunches of flowers were set. And among these bunches of flowers I happened to see a bunch of pure white roses, and instinctively felt a nameless excitement: I understood that Helen must already be back from Qingdao.

When I had first come into the hospital I had a thousand urgent longings to see Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping; but after the grievous pain and the long span of days, though on the one hand I still wanted to see them, on the other I was in truth a little afraid to face them—it was like being afraid to take up again a tangled task that had run aground; bound up with the very thought of them was string upon string of endless, boundless questions, and to touch one corner set the whole in motion. For the sake of ease and a moment's comfort, over the past days I had thought of them time and again and always shied from thinking further; whereas now I had a thousand yearnings to see Helen.

If she had not gone travelling to Qingdao, but had come to the party that evening, then—so I now imagined—the document might well have fallen into Mei Yingzi's hands, and I should have had no need to steal from Bai Ping, and perhaps this wounding of mine might likewise have been spared. So all these turns of fortune seemed to have been decided in the moment of a single change of Helen's mind; the mystery of life might lie just there!

But my wish now to see Helen was not to lay before her the whole chain of cause and effect, nor to discuss with her this mystery of life. What I felt now was only that she alone could sit across from me without speaking of the questions before me or of the reality upon my shoulder; that with her our talk could turn to the pure world of music and philosophy—and this, just now, was so much what I needed.

The nurse came in, and I asked:

"Do you know who sent me these flowers?"

"The white roses?" she said. "It was Mrs. Manfield."

"Is this the first time she has sent white roses?"

"Yes," she said. "Why? Do you feel..."

"I was thinking... ah? Do you happen to know her daughter?"

"I do," she said. "Mrs. Manfield told me that letter was from her daughter to you."

"A letter?"

"I put it there for you." So saying, she went to the bedside and handed me the letter from the drawer of the bedside table.

It was a white envelope lined with blue, with no stamp affixed; so plainly Helen had already returned to Shanghai, had learned that her going to Qingdao was the cause of my being wounded, and, hearing besides that I could not yet receive visitors, had written this letter to me first.

I opened the letter, and was just about to read it when Dr. Philip suddenly came in; I slipped the letter into the pocket of my dressing gown—it was the first time I had used that pocket.

Philip, my case history in his hand and a tin of Philip Morris besides, came radiant to my side.

"Congratulations," he said. "You've recovered faster than I ever expected."

"Really?"

"A few days I don't come to see you, and you're well." So saying, he handed me the tin of cigarettes: "I think you need this now."

"Thank you." I took his gift and said: "Have you time to sit a while?"

He sat down beside me, looked about him, and said:

"Just now I telephoned Dr. Gaolang and learned you'd recovered wonderfully these last few days. So I've brought you this tin of cigarettes. Had I known it would be so, I should have told them two days ago to come and see you."

Seeing that I held the tin of cigarettes in my right hand, he took it and opened it for me, drew one out and gave it to me; then he took out his own pipe and lighter, and we sat facing each other and smoked. He went on:

"From tomorrow, I can allow one person a day to talk with you for two hours."

"Is it still as serious as that?"

"You've lost too much blood, and ought to have a good rest," he said. "The medicine you're taking now is all a tonic, too."

"Thank you," I said.

"This time it was truly a piece of luck," he said. "Ten days ago I still feared your left arm would be crippled."

"And now?"

"Set your mind quite at rest—it's well," he said. "But perhaps you won't be able to use it too hard."

"And Mei Yingzi? Is she well?"

"Tomorrow I'll permit her to come and see you."

"And Mrs. Stephen?"

"She telephones to ask after you every day," he said. "Haven't you seen the fresh flowers she sends you daily?"

"Please thank her for me first," I said. "Have you heard that Miss Manfield is back?"

"That I've not heard."

Just then I thought of a question that had long lain on my heart, and asked:

"To this day I still don't know—after I was wounded that day, who was it, after all, that told you to come and save me? Was it Bai Ping?"

"She telephoned me, told me you'd been shot and hurt by a Japanese officer, and told me to come and see you at once. But I didn't know your address. Afterwards I telephoned Mei Yingzi, and only so found it out."

"I'd been shot and hurt by a Japanese officer? Yes—I was shot and hurt by a Japanese officer."

"Was it in a car?"

"I was truly muddled with drink," I said. "I expect Bai Ping knows the details better."

"She has told me in detail," he said. "You came out from Umetake's, went on to a bar to drink, and afterwards she left. The next morning she went to see you, and that Japanese officer was there in your room; somehow you fell to quarrelling, and he fired."

"Yes. I think that was it," I said. "But now I can't remember a thing of it. Afterwards, I suppose, the officer saw I was hurt, threw down the gun and ran."

"And so Bai Ping telephoned me."

I said no more. Bai Ping's lie was perhaps well told. But it raised in me all the more doubts. Did no third person, then, yet know the whole of Bai Ping's affair? But what of Mei Yingzi? She still held Bai Ping's document in her hands. I did not know whether Bai Ping's lie was for a momentary concealment or for a permanent one. Could she have known in advance that I would not tell the truth once I was in hospital? Had Philip not spoken first today, might I not very easily have told out the whole thing? To my mind, since Bai Ping was not, after all, the Japanese hireling Mei Yingzi supposed her to be, only a full and truthful confession could dissolve every knot and misunderstanding. And now—how, after all, was this misunderstanding to be dissolved?

Philip soon took his leave; he told me not to sit too long, and so I went back to bed. In an instant a thousand thoughts took possession of my soul; it dawned on me suddenly that Bai Ping might, after all, still be a Japanese spy, and have shot me for stealing her document, and have deliberately used the opposite method to fix the crime on me. Yet in the instant of my wounding, private friendship, the conscience of a nation, and my respect for her had moved her, made her feel shame and remorse, so that she came out and had Philip fetched to save me. Then the knot of this question was, after all, not the simple and hopeful thing I had imagined.—

All these thoughts and reckonings, doubts and worries, in an instant beset my weary body; I could neither resolve them nor save myself from them, and at last I could only resolve to forget them for a while. I gazed off out the window and saw the white roses on the window ledge; I thought of Helen; I asked the nurse to give me the letter from the pocket of my dressing gown, and I began to read Helen's slender, delicate hand.
