## Edo Diary

"No — I'll go as I am."

{j} When Toshizō made to go down east in a plain rōnin's dress, Kondō stopped him.

"Along the road it's arranged that you'll be billeted ahead and lodge at the honjin. In that get-up the rules won't hold. Go in proper dress, according to your rank."

It was only reasonable.

A stay at the honjin in rōnin's dress would not do. The honjin took none but daimyō, court nobles, hatamoto, and those of rank above the privilege of audience.

Toshizō got himself up in grand style.

A war-hat of blue-beaten lacquer, its lining a ring of open metalwork, its white cords drawn tight beneath the chin; and for attendants he had a young retainer, a sandal-bearer, a spear-bearer, and a groom to lead the horse, and so went down the highway.

To these were added five corps men.

(It's all rather like a play.)

At first it made him self-conscious.

When he reached the honjin, before the gate,

"Lodging of Hijikata Toshizō"

{j} such a placard on hōsho paper was posted up, and the post-station officials came to pay their respects.

(A funny thing.)

By the time he crossed Hakone, he had grown quite easy in the part.

(Feel awkward about it, and you'll look out of joint to others' eyes too. That's how it is.)

He set his nerve to it.

Once he had, then, being a tall man with a cool cast of face and a touch of the astringent about the eyes and mouth, he looked far grander than a hatamoto of generations' standing.

"Master Hijikata — well, I must say."

{j} The corps men, though they said nothing aloud, were astonished, and it showed in their eyes.

All the way he wore a single-layer robe.

For this autumn of the third year of Keiō, the heat dragged on and on, unbearably.

When the sea at Shinagawa began to glitter on his right hand, Toshizō, at last,

(I've come home.)

{j} felt the truth of it. That had been in the third year of Bunkyū, in the second month, still cold, when he set out from Edo. This was his homecoming, in the fifth year since, counting inclusively.

Toshizō's party entered the Great Barrier-gate of Edo.

After walking a while they rested at a teahouse by the foot of the Kanasugi bridge. He was not particularly tired, but he wished to sit on the bench and savor the feeling of having come back to Edo.

(Edo has changed.)

Not in its bustle.

The townsfolk, the teahouse master, his wife, even the serving-girls — there was something distant and cold in their faces.

But Toshizō soon saw the reason.

(How absurd. It's these clothes of mine.)

The townsmen met Toshizō the hatamoto with the face and bearing that befitted him. It was not Edo that had changed, but Toshizō.

"Master."

{j} Call to him so, and he gave no ready answer, but put on a look of appealing to the young retainer.

"Here — Komoda-kun."

{j} said Toshizō to the rank-and-file man who was with him.

"Tell that old fellow to make me a bit of small talk, this and that."

Even to himself it seemed comical.

The old man thawed at last.

"My lord, Edo has changed a good deal this past year, it has."

{j} said the old man. He took Toshizō, no doubt, for one come back from garrison duty at Osaka or the like.

"Sitting here looking out at it, it doesn't seem so."

"No, my lord — you should go and see for yourself. Outside the Hitotsubashi gate there's gone up a monstrous great building they call the Foreigners' Training Institute; and on the site of the Naval Drill Ground at Teppōzu they've been raising, since this summer, a foreigners' lodging called a 'hotel' or some such — so vast a thing that the folk of nearby Jikken-chō make a half-joking uproar that they can no longer see the sky beyond it."

"Is that so."

It stirred deep feelings in Toshizō, too.

When once he had set out from Edo,

"we'll be the vanguard of expelling the barbarian,"

{j} that, surely, was the word he had gone out on.

And now the shogunate itself, the very thing, against the will of the barbarian-expelling Court at Kyoto, was opening the country by degrees.

Treaties, too, were now made not with the first-rank powers only; this very month, so Toshizō had heard, they had come to be concluded even with the second-rank nations — Portugal, Spain, Belgium, Denmark.

(No wonder Itō Kashitarō the barbarian-expeller is in a rage.)

For Toshizō there was neither expelling the barbarian nor opening the country.

Matters having come this far, he was resolved to defend the Tokugawa shogunate to the very last.

Toshizō and his party set off from the teahouse.

Afterward the old man cocked his head.

(Somehow, a face I've seen before.)

The old man was born in Hino, in the South Tama district, and was called Kichimatsu. Hino-juku was near Toshizō's birth-home.

"Who was that gentleman?"

{j} he asked his wife.

"A captain of the Great Guard, and named Hijikata Toshizō, they say."

"Why — Toshi!"

{j} It came back to him.

Was it not the Toshi who, burnt black by the sun, used to loaf about the whole stretch from the Asakawa embankment along the bank of the Tama and the Kōshū Highway — the ibaragaki, the young rascal?

"That Toshi — what a game he's playing at."

The old man's eyes went wide. He thought, no doubt, that Toshi was traveling up and down the Tōkaidō as a sham hatamoto.

***

Toshizō took off his straw sandals at the mansion in Ushigome Nijikki-chō that Kondō had lately bought.

When Kondō had gone back east earlier, he had closed up the old dōjō at Yanagichō in Kohinata, Koishikawa, and bought this mansion, suited to his new standing.

In this spacious mansion lived the bedridden Kondō Shūsai, Isami's wife Otsune, and their only daughter Tamako, as though the world had forgotten them.

(A fine mansion, sure enough.)

Edo had changed, Toshizō thought, with some irony. Kondō Isami, risen from the Bushū peasantry, owned a mansion like this in Edo.

Old Shūsai was worn to skin and bone, and his sight, it seemed, was already gone.

"How do you fare?"

{j} Toshizō sat down beside the bedding, but the old man's eyes were merely open; he seemed not to see.

Even so, come night he rallied a little, and,

"Toshi. I'm a man who changed wives nine times in his life, but this time it looks bad,"

{j} he said, in a small voice.

Isami's wife Otsune was as sullen as ever, and showed no gladness at seeing him.

"Are you keeping well?"

{j} said Toshizō, and,

"In body, at least."

{j} she answered. Even such a woman as this, living cast off by Kondō, seemed to feel anger like anyone else, and her face had grown more dangerous than before.

"I'll borrow your lodging for a while."

"Aye."

{j} Otsune nodded, scratching about her belly.

She was, by no stretch, a woman one could call the lady of a great hatamoto.

Toshizō meant to make this mansion his base for recruiting corps men.

"There'll be some coming and going of people; I ask you to bear with it."

From the next day he had the corps men carry a manifesto and call at every dōjō in Edo, one after another.

Great and small, there were some three hundred of them.

He chose, so far as he could, the small dōjō of obscure schools, and did not send them to the great ones — Chiba, Momonoi, Saitō.

Among the pupils of the great dōjō, many had turned loyalist.

The Shinsengumi had had its fill, by now, of the likes of Kiyokawa Hachirō, Yamanami Keisuke, Tōdō Heisuke, and Itō Kashitarō.

"The small schools are best. And best of all a man of common stock — peasant, townsman — with his backbone well set,"

{j} said Toshizō to the corps men in charge of recruiting.

"Look at Chōshū's Kiheitai."

Risen, every one, from peasants and townsmen, yet now it had become the strongest corps upholding the Chōshū army.

From houses grown sated, generation on generation, on a hereditary stipend, no decent samurai comes.

The word spread at once through the dōjō of Edo, and the swordsmen who came to call at Kondō's mansion in Nijikki-chō never let up.

The interviews he left to the corps men.

When a corps man took a liking to a man, he would see him politely to the entrance and tell him the day of assembly.

Toshizō showed his face not at all, but stayed in an inner room, only hearing the day's report from the corps men in charge.

"Why do you not meet them yourself?"

{j} a corps man once asked.

"I'm a man for whom, one look at a face, and like or dislike comes first. Can a fellow like that be trusted to choose men that matter?"

"I see,"

{j} the corps men murmured to one another afterward.

"That man knows himself, it seems, in his own way,"

{j} said some; while,

"No — I thought so on this journey: that man has grown, as a person. He no longer fusses over little things,"

so said others. How it came about is not clear, but from this Edo journey on, Toshizō's reputation markedly improved.

Toshizō himself, too — this, no doubt, was a thing he did not notice in himself. It may, perhaps, have had something to do with his intimacy with Oyuki back in Kyoto—

If there had been here a corps man of sharp eye for human nature,

"a man who's come this far single, scarcely holding a woman, his blood running hot; and lately he's got himself a good one somewhere, and has come to feel the pity in each separate life of others — that'll be it,"

he might, perhaps, have said.

***

By a story that survives in the Satō house at Hino, once, during this stay in Edo, Toshizō visited his birth-home, the Satō house, and others.

He came, it seems, in an anpotsu palanquin — the kind that in those days none but a samurai house of rank might ride in.

In the neighborhood of Hino-juku, this was not much liked.

"He's grown a stiff neck, has he,"

{j} so they said.

Even his brother-in-law Satō Hikogorō,

"Toshi, you may be a lordling now, but you mustn't forget the old days,"

{j} chid him, roundabout.

"I'm the same Toshi,"

{j} said Toshizō, without so much as a smile.

Toshizō had of old passed, in this country, for a surly man. That base metal of his, he was saying, was the same now as ever.

"But, Toshi, you've come home in glory, after all. Everyone's glad that you and Isami are the great success stories of the three Tama districts. They've all got that feeling ready in them, so you must answer to it properly."

"Hm?"

{j} he said, with a sour face.

"And how am I to do that?"

"Show them a smile, a bit of a smile, will you. The folk hereabouts are simple souls; ah, they'll say, a man who rises to greatness is a different thing, so humble — and everyone will be pleased. Or do you begrudge them a smile as much as all that?"

"I don't begrudge it, but—"

Toshizō did not understand.

"I can't laugh when there's nothing to laugh at."

And yet, for all that, there was in him, they say, a curiously attentive, gentle streak.

At his birth-home out at Ishida there was a niece, a girl named Nui.

She had been a child still when Toshizō set out for Kyoto, but had since gone into a daimyō's Edo mansion to learn manners in service.

Before long she had married into a neighboring house, but for her sickliness the match had been broken off and she had come back home — a rumor Toshizō had heard even in Kyoto.

For this Nui alone he brought gifts — when he had found the time to buy them, none knew — Kyoto combs and hairpins, picture-books, and the like.

"There's more to Toshi than you'd think,"

{j} his blind brother Tamesaburō said, in admiration.

There was, besides, talk of a match.

It was brought by his sister Onobu (Satō Hikogorō's wife), and his blind brother Tamesaburō pressed it on him too, almost past bearing.

"The girl — I know her myself — she's a pretty one. A man with his eyes might say so and it'd be one thing; but when a man who can't see says it, there's nothing surer."

She was a girl of Totsuka village.

Her house was a distant kin of the Hijikata, and a great property-holder in the village too, but through the last head's indulgence it kept a shamisen shop besides.

"Ah — that house."

{j} Toshizō remembered it, dimly. The place had a crossbar gate and a maple hedge round it, and one corner alone, facing the highway, was built with a fine lattice and called "the shop."

There they sold shamisen.

"That'll be Okoto."

{j} Toshizō broke into a laugh.

It was here, it seems, that he laughed for the first time.

Okoto was the reigning beauty of the Totsuka neighborhood, and, above all, played the shamisen well. She had been fifteen or sixteen about the time Toshizō set out for Kyoto, so she would be past twenty by now.

"Toshi — you've a fancy for her."

{j} His blind brother cocked his head. By the feel of a thing, it seemed, he could read a person's mind.

"Take her to wife. You're the youngest of this house, but count it on your fingers and you're thirty-three now. As a man you're past your prime."

"Past it, and then some. A widower and a bachelor at thirty-three is no good anymore. To have a woman stuck to me around the clock, as a wife is — brr, it makes me shudder. And besides — an ordinary samurai may draw his stipend and idle his time away, but I have work to do."

"What work have you?"

"The Shinsengumi."

That was the end of the marriage talk.

Toshizō stayed only a few days in his home country, and went back at once to Edo.

In Edo he met, among others, Okita Sōji's brother-in-law — one Rintarō, of the same Okita surname, who had become a petty officer of the Shinchōgumi — and his wife Omitsu, Sōji's own elder sister.

Omitsu asked, over and over, of nothing but Sōji's health.

"Now, there's no cause for worry,"

{j} said Toshizō; but in truth Sōji had come to keep to his bed half the days of the month.

For medicine he took what the doctor prescribed, but he took, too, a remedy handed down in Toshizō's birth-home.

The Hijikata house had, besides the bruise-medicine "Ishida Powder" that Toshizō had once carried on his back and peddled, a remedy by the name of "Koryōsan," said to answer for consumption; and Toshizō would send all the way to Kyoto for it and dose Okita with it.

When Toshizō brewed it for him,

"Oh, I hate this,"

{j} he would drink it, unwilling.

"I'm drinking it for your sake, Hijikata-san,"

{j} he would say, making a favor of it.

"Omitsu-san, this time too I'll take some back with me. It works, that does."

By the habit of his medicine-peddling days, he said such things with confidence. Nay, Toshizō himself was fully persuaded that his medicine worked. In all things, he was a man of that temper.

The corps men, picked over, came to twenty-eight.

Before dawn on the twenty-first of the tenth month they gathered at Kondō's mansion and set out from Edo.

Some days before that, on the fourteenth, the shogun Yoshinobu had returned the government to the throne — such was the fact — but it had not yet reached the ears of Toshizō in Edo.

He heard of it at the honjin in Odawara.

At that, Toshizō, without a change of color,

"Never mind — the Shinsengumi's work is all still to come,"

{j} said, and no more than that.

On the fourth of the eleventh month, they reached Kyoto.

As they made to cross the Sanjō Great Bridge, the wind and rain that had run since the night before grew fiercer still, and the far bank showed dark through the mist.

Toshizō stood on the bridge, in a daze. Never had he seen Kyoto wear so fearful a face.
