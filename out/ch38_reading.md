## Chapter 6. Right and Wrong Made Plain, yet Ever Fickle

To work in concert with a defector who had made some small name for himself, and together to carry on the work of pacification and the suppression of rebellion—this, indeed, was a wholly new problem.

The writer himself had no experience of the kind, and the officers of the whole unit were naturally making their first attempt at it as well; and the pity of it was that we failed to lead him into the right path, so that in the end he turned back the way he had come.

A case such as this is not often met with; and this chapter, divided into four sections, shall set down the whole affair from first to last, exactly as it was.

At the outset of the eight-year War of Resistance, Zhu Zhankui, stirred by patriotic ardor, gathered together his fellow-villagers and stood forth to fight the Japanese. Taken captive by the Japanese army in one of their mopping-up sweeps, he had the good fortune to leap from the train on the way under escort and make his escape, suffering only a slight hurt and not death.

A little healed, he went south, meaning to fight the Japanese still; but not knowing the true state of things, he threw in his lot by mistake with the Chinese Communist forces under Lü Zhengcao. At that time the Nationalists and Communists were in concert against the foreign foe, and Zhu, too, made himself content with wherever he found himself.

Some years later Zhu Zhankui had scrambled up to be commander of the “Fifth Military Subdistrict” under the “Central Hebei Military District,” which belonged in turn to the Chinese Communists’ “Jin-Cha-Ji Border Region.”

Before long he was transferred to Yan’an for training at the “Anti-Japanese University,” and presently sent back again to Central Hebei. Zhu Zhankui’s fortunes were by no means all smooth sailing: this time, none knows quite how it came about, he was in the end “dealt with” on such charges as “capitulationism” and “petty-bourgeois consciousness.” It is said that Zhu, in his indignation, once threw himself from a cliff to make an end of himself, but failed, and was hurt in the fall a second time.

After the victory in the War of Resistance, the bandit chieftain He Long sent him to help Xiao Ke in their marauding about East Hebei, on both sides of the Great Wall and elsewhere; there he was taken prisoner by the Nationalist army and sent under escort to Nanjing. At that time Zhu’s post was equal to that of a brigade commander.

After Zhu had come over, his conduct being good, the government treated him with indulgence. He was first kept on in the Ministry of National Defense, and in the autumn of the thirty-sixth year of the Republic went north with the “First Brigade of the Pacification Corps” to take up the post of magistrate of Anci county. From this time he was promoted out of the usual order, and made administrative inspector-commissioner of the “——th Special District of Hebei Province,” with the additional office of security commander.

Our own unit was ordered to work in close concert with him, and in the “Beiping-Tianjin-Baoding triangle” we fought side by side on many an occasion, with results tolerably good.

But by the end of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic, when the situation had turned about and Zhu grew unsteady, he went so far as to use the “widening of the guerrilla front” for a pretext to lure our “Second Command Room” and “directly subordinate assault team” deep into bandit-held territory, meaning to carry them off by force into the bandits’ camp; in this he did not succeed, and the next day it was confirmed: Zhu Zhankui had turned traitor.

Afterward there were differing opinions and differing views. Some raised a warning, saying: “From this affair we may deeply learn that the Communist Party is the Communist Party, and that its very nature is always hard to change. This is a bloody fact, and may serve us as a bitter lesson in our struggle against the Communists.” Others held that: “The Chinese Communists are Chinese too, and if the poison has not gone deep, the most of them will come to their senses. Those who would cling to that signboard and never for their lives let go—it is not for any doctrine or belief at all, but, in a word, only for power and place.” And there were those, leaning to the secret-service point of view, who held that: “A man of this sort, who has come over to us—even if we use him, we ought to keep a secret watch upon him; had we but watched him close, this thing would never have happened.” The writer’s own view is this: that this is a matter of a “political” character, and that in handling affairs of this kind there is no harm in opening wide one’s breast and keeping the bearing of a gentleman.

### 1. Had Japan Not Invaded, None of This Would Have Happened

In the third chapter above, in treating of the doings of our own directly subordinate First Group in Anci county, the name “Zhu Zhankui” was mentioned. It is also written, in another form, with a different character for the final syllable, but it is, in fact, one and the same man.

Upon this man’s person certain things befell—vivid, and full of twists and turns—and his is likewise an instance of the fickleness of fortune.

In an age so troubled and so changeful, a man like him is no more than a small piece of flotsam in a great whirlpool. Drifting with the waves and following the current, now sinking and now rising, round and round he is drawn down at last to the bottom, never to surface again.

Zhu Zhankui was first, within the Chinese Communists’ sham administrative system, a member of the “Seventh Special Commissioner’s Office” under the “Central Hebei Administrative Office,” which belonged to the “Jin-Cha-Ji Border Region Administrative Committee” (the sham commissioner being Dong Ying); while in the military system he was commander of the “Fifth Military Subdistrict” under the “Central Hebei Military District,” his area of activity lying in the counties of Yongqing, Gu’an, Anci, and Jinghai within the “Beiping-Tianjin-Baoding triangle.” His force numbered about 3,500 men.

After the victory in the War of Resistance, the Communist army rose in revolt, and in one of the bandit-suppression actions Zhu Zhankui was captured by our army and sent to Nanjing; his “conduct” being good, he was thereupon given important employment.

In September of the thirty-sixth year of the Republic, by the sanction of the Ministry of National Defense, Zhu Zhankui was appointed to work in concert with the “First Brigade of the Pacification Corps,” and went north together with it; he was first made magistrate of Anci county in Hebei Province, and afterward promoted to be administrative inspector-commissioner of the “Third Special Commissioner’s Office of Hebei Province,” with the additional office of security commander, his rank being that of major general.

The various matters touching Zhu Zhankui I shall set down below, each upon the ground of its own fact.—First let me tell my own personal impression of him: it inclines, to be sure, toward the subjective, and yet all that I say is the truth.

I cannot clearly recall how many times Zhu Zhankui and I met; each time it was a word or two, the matter settled, with no idle talk, still less any exchange of courtesies. Whenever he came from his post to Beiping, he would almost always look in at brigade headquarters; only once, when I had gone to Jinghai on the Jin-Pu line to inspect the work of the “Second Command Room,” did he too come riding up on horseback to bid me welcome.

He seldom wore uniform, and even when he did he wore no major general’s collar-tabs. There was one small point, laughable to tell of: though he had been granted the rank of major general by our Ministry of National Defense, yet whenever he saw me—a colonel, one grade below him—he would always be the first to raise his hand in salute, or to take off his cap and bow; which, on the contrary, put me greatly to the blush. He had no gift for honeyed and artful words, and was even somewhat slow and halting of speech; I could not see in this any affectation, and thought him rather a plain and honest man. And yet—was there in it some measure of self-restraint? Because I had not enough to do with him, I could not for the moment see clean through him.

Zhu Zhankui came of a farming family that might be reckoned among the poor; from childhood he had no schooling to speak of, and of any degree of learning there could of course be no question. Though he could take up a brush and write, that was something he had learned little by little in later years. It is said that when he first stepped out into the world he had worked for a time as a chuigushou.

This “chuigushou” of his was one who truly blew upon the horn, and no mere figure of speech for a maker of propaganda.

Among the towns and villages of our North, the “chuigushou” too counts as a trade; one must, I suppose, take a master and be taught, for it is by no means a thing where a man can pick up a horn and blow away at random. In those days there were bands of several men matched together to play; and there were also single men who, a small horn tucked under the armpit, would make the rounds of the weddings and funerals and feast-days of the poorer households, blowing their hearts out for the sake of a meal or two—and Zhu Zhankui belonged, it may be, to the latter sort. In truth this too is a free profession, with nothing low or mean about it; my aim is but to show the circumstances of his boyhood, and I by no means make it a ground for slighting him.

Touching a passage of Zhu Zhankui’s past, my fellow-student of the Lizhi Class, Wang Zhiyi, has furnished a piece entitled “The Story of Zhu Zhankui,” which I here copy out in the original as follows: “After the Double-Seventh Incident of the twenty-sixth year of the Republic, the wrathful tide of resistance surged high in every quarter, and hot-blooded young men came forward in throngs to offer their service. But because the situation in North China changed too fast—Beiping and Tianjin falling one after the other, and the Nationalist army withdrawing in haste—many suddenly felt that they had no road by which to serve their country; and so they gathered the young men of their own places, and each formed a local anti-Japanese band, taking the enemy and the puppets for their mark, and opening a guerrilla war.

“Zhu Zhankui was a farmer’s son from the country of Xx county in Hebei Province, with no schooling to speak of—as for his degree of learning, he could barely tell one character from another. Out of patriotic ardor he gathered some scores of the local young men, of much of an age with one another, and after a fashion put together a band of anti-Japanese guerrillas, which grew, if you please, little by little in strength, until after a year and more of development it had swelled to more than a thousand men. Unhappily, in one of the Japanese invaders’ ‘mopping-up’ sweeps, this band, unable to bear the weight of it, was scattered and broken, and Zhu Zhankui himself was taken captive by the Japanese.

“When Zhu was under interrogation at the Japanese military headquarters in the Haiguang Temple at Tianjin, he suffered every kind of cruel torture and humiliation; yet beyond that he knew only that he had resisted invasion, he had nothing to tell them.

“And here fell out a thing beyond all looking-for: on the very way, being taken under escort from Tianjin to Beiping, Zhu Zhankui, seizing a moment when the Japanese soldiers were off their guard, leapt clean out from the window of the train’s privy—another mischance, this—and hurt only his leg, so that he could not walk, though it was no great harm; and crawling and rolling, he hid among the ridges of the fields, and so, after all, escaped that peril. Afterward the villagers found him, and hearing that his was a local accent, were all glad to give him help.

“By now Zhu Zhankui’s valiant deeds against the Japanese were told and retold throughout the Beiping-Tianjin-Baoding triangle, and men looked on him as a hero of the resistance; so that after his rescue he was well cared for, and when in a few months his wounds were healed, he went south in disguise, and coming to the hill country near Shijiazhuang (of the Taihang range), he joined, all in a muddle, the forces of Lü Zhengcao. At that time it was the very season of the “joint Nationalist-Communist resistance,” and in Zhu Zhankui’s mind there was no knowing what was the Kuomintang and what the Communist Party—so it was against the Japanese, that was enough. After a time, Lü Zhengcao sent Zhu Zhankui up, under warrant, to the “Anti-Japanese University” run by the Chinese Communists at Yan’an, there to be trained; and in the course of it he passed through many a session of self-examination and criticism, being charged with “individual heroism,” “petty-bourgeois consciousness,” and the like. In truth this was what they call “thought training,” wherein first all that a man has is denied him, and only then are other things discussed.

“His training done, he was sent back again to Lü Zhengcao’s forces to go on being active in the “Jin-Cha-Ji Border Region,” and by degrees was raised in rank, being made commander of the “Fifth Military Subdistrict” under the Chinese Communists’ “Central Hebei Military District,” and holding by usurpation the counties of Yongqing, Gu’an, and Anci in Hebei Province—no longer fighting the Japanese now.

“By what is understood, it was very likely at this time that Zhu Zhankui joined the Communist Party.

“Perhaps it was that Zhu Zhankui’s conduct did not square with the Communist Party’s rule and measure; a second time he was summoned to Yan’an to be examined, and besides being charged with “capitulationism,” was found, in short, to be good for nothing at all; whereupon in a mountain hollow they held a meeting to struggle against him, and the bandit cadres by turns reviled and abused him. Zhu Zhankui could bear it no longer, and in his extremity, with a great cry, he leapt into a mountain gully—this time a worse fall than his leap from the train—and lay a long while recovering in a cave-dwelling before he could walk again.

“The Communist Party gave him no work to do, but bade him go on studying at the “Anti-Japanese University.” On the morning of the eleventh of August in the thirty-fourth year of the Republic, Zhu Zhankui went over to He Long’s cave-dwelling to “pay a call,” and coming in at the door, saw He Long pacing to and fro with his hands behind his back, his face wearing a look of anxious unrest; and seeing someone enter he gave no greeting, but went on turning round and round about the room. Zhu Zhankui asked: ‘Commander He, what is it that keeps you so troubled?’ He Long answered in a great flurry: ‘It is a disaster! A disaster! The Japanese have surrendered!’ Zhu asked in wonder: ‘The Japanese surrender is a good thing—why do you call it a disaster?’ He Long said: ‘This thing has come too suddenly, and caught us off our guard; our main forces are all in the border regions of the several provinces—how are we to come up in time to take over the great cities the Japanese have held? Think of it—how can I but be anxious?’ Two days later, Zhu Zhankui, for the sake of his own disposal, went again to ask He Long’s instructions, and He Long ordered him to the Hebei-Rehe border region to help Xiao Ke; and so Zhu Zhankui came once more to the country about Xifengkou and Dushan.”—All the foregoing passages are Zhu Zhankui’s own account of himself, made when, after he had come over to the Nationalist army, in August of the thirty-sixth year of the Republic, he spoke before the whole body of trainees (besides the “Lizhi Class” there were other training units as well) at the “Central Training Corps.” Here I set down the substance of it; its credibility can be reckoned at no more than half-and-half.

“After the victory in the War of Resistance, Zhu served as a senior cadre in the bandit army under Xiao Ke, marauding about the Hebei-Rehe border region on both sides of the Great Wall; and when the Nationalist army fell upon them with a hard blow and the bandit army was routed and scattered, Zhu Zhankui was taken prisoner. The Nationalist army had sent aircraft to scatter abroad Zhu’s “call to surrender,” and many of the bandit army came over bearing their arms; his conduct being good, the government granted him by special favor the rank of major general, and in September of the thirty-sixth year of the Republic he went north from Nanjing with the “First Brigade of the Pacification Corps of the Ministry of National Defense,” charged to raise a separate local armed force, and, in concert with the First Brigade, to open the struggle against the bandits.

“In the winter of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic, when the Northeast had fallen and the situation in North China had turned about, Zhu Zhankui trimmed his sails to the wind—and it may be too that he was worked upon by threats and by inducements—and in one of the joint actions with our side he laid a snare to lure us into the trap, while he himself led his men off to the bandits.

“This is a bloody fact, and a bitter lesson to us as well!” Here ends “The Story of Zhu Zhankui” furnished by my fellow-student Wang Zhiyi, for which I here give thanks. As for how Zhu Zhankui laid out his array to lure us, that shall be told separately below.

The writer will first append to the foregoing some explanatory notes, as follows.—One. He Long was commander of the “120th Division of the Eighteenth Group Army”; this designation was one bestowed by the government for the sake of a united front against the foreign foe.

In the early period of the War of Resistance the main body of this 120th Division was garrisoned in northwest Shanxi, its other elements spread over the Shaan-Gan-Ning border region and the country of Suiyuan and North Hebei, with its divisional headquarters set in Shaanxi. Its whole strength was some fifty or sixty thousand men. By the middle period of the War of Resistance, when the Chinese Communists were of set purpose breaking away from the Central Government, all their armed forces cast off the designations they had borne and set up, if you please, on their own, establishing “Military Districts” each under the “Border Region Governments” set up in the several border regions. Among the three divisions under the “Eighteenth Group Army” (the “Eighth Route Army”)—the “115th Division under Lin Biao,” the “129th Division under Liu Bocheng,” and the “120th Division under He Long”—He Long’s was the weakest; the other bandit forces all grew and spread, and only He Long’s single force was still off skirmishing in the country about Shanxi and Suiyuan. Right down to the later period of the War of Resistance, though a sham “Jin-Sui Border Region Government” was got up after a fashion, its armed strength remained the feeblest of all. To the Communist Party, He Long, in this life of his, had indeed laid up no small store of “meritorious service in the field”; yet in the turmoil of the “Red Guards,” even he could not escape being struggled against.

Two. Xiao Ke was originally He Long’s second, having served as deputy commander of the 120th Division; about the time of the victory in the War of Resistance he led his men in skirmishing about Cha-Sui and North Hebei. It was for this connection that He Long bade Zhu Zhankui go into Xiao Ke’s forces.

Three. The so-called “Anti-Japanese University”—it was founded after the Mao Communists had fled in rout to Yan’an in October of the twenty-fourth year of the Republic, and was at first named the “Red Army University,” being renamed in the twenty-sixth year the “Anti-Japanese Military and Political University,” “Kangda” for short—was something like a “training camp for guerrilla cadres,” and in some part too like a “madhouse.” Its levels were all uneven, and its standard of learning was in no way worthy of the name of “university.” On the face of it military and political studies were held of equal weight, but strip it open and it was no more than a thing set up expressly for the “building of a faction.”—Four. When Zhu Zhankui was taken by our army, his military post was equal to that of a “brigade commander”; for the Chinese Communists at that time made little of rank, and reckoned out it might be equal to a “colonel.”

Five. Last, touching the writer’s own thoughts upon that scene of Zhu Zhankui’s leaping from the train on the road while being taken under escort from Tianjin to Beiping, I may as well mention them here in passing. In the winter of the thirtieth year of the Republic, the writer, after being arrested in Shanghai, was likewise taken under escort by the Japanese gendarmes aboard a train bound for Nanjing—much like Zhu Zhankui’s being taken from Tianjin to Beiping, though not wholly the same. At the time the writer too conceived the notion of leaping from the train; but my misgivings were too many, and my courage not enough, and I did not do it. In my poor book Nameless Heroes, in the second chapter of the Fourth Part, “Counter-Espionage Work in the Later Period of the War of Resistance,” there is a passage set down thus: “Before and after boarding I had drunk very little water, and had made water once already a dozen minutes before, yet somehow I needed to again. The last time I went to the privy, it was Nakano who went first to see whether anyone was within, and then followed behind me; and when I had gone in, he signed with his hand that I was not to shut the door—which is to say, no motion of mine might pass out of his sight. Because this was a first-class carriage, unless someone were passing by, an open door was no matter of seemliness or unseemliness. I had noted long since that in the privy there was a small window, shut at first, which needed only a push upward to open, and whose height and breadth were just enough to let a man of middling build squeeze out. I gazed at this small window in a daze, and thought how good it would be if I had the sort of passing-through art of the knight-errant tales—yet never had I seen it told of any knight-errant that he leapt from a train. In an instant I came back to reality, and knew that if one wished to make an end of oneself by leaping down, one might well try it, but if it were to escape with one’s life, then there was no need at all.” To “leap” from a train one ought to land on one’s feet; if a man were first to poke his head out of the window and then fling himself out, think of it—not to speak of a swift-moving train, even standing stock-still he would be half killed by the fall. I call to mind that about the seventeenth or eighteenth year of the Republic, when there were already trams within the city of Beiping, I would often see, as the tram ran on, a ticket-inspector suddenly swing aboard, and when he had torn through the tickets one by one, step lightly down without waiting for it to stop, in a posture so graceful that it was a wonder to behold. That a man could get on and off so freely must surely have some knack in it—else how could it be so deft? Out of curiosity I too made trial of it: when the car ran fast, I dared not leap; when it slowed, I mimicked the way of those others, landing left foot first and the right close after to keep my balance—yet my skill was not up to the mark, and still I staggered several steps before I could stand firm. I remember that in my early training days I had walked the “swinging log” as well; and though on the ever-swaying “swinging log” I could turn and advance and retreat as on level ground, yet that knack was, I fear, not much like a train’s.

How was it that Zhu Zhankui leapt down? and under what case did he leap? It is a pity I never asked him to his face; but by conjecture, Zhu reckoned that he was a dead man in any event, and that if by chance he lived, it would be so much gained—and thus he could bring himself to that resolve. And the most crucial thing of all lay yet in his seizing the moment when his guards were off their watch. So much for a first introduction of this man Zhu Zhankui: at the outset of the War of Resistance against Japan he was a patriotic youth; once taken captive by the Japanese army, he made a hazardous escape, in which he was unhappily hurt; a little healed, he threw in his lot by mistake with the Communist forces—and thence was brought about this affair of shifts and turnings. In what follows I shall, in several sections, set down this winding tale of a thing that befell amid pacification and the suppression of rebellion.

### 2. Would That Every Unit Were So Steadfast

Our First Brigade had at first no “assault team” upon its establishment; but after the one battle at “Lishuiqiao” by the “North-Suburb directly subordinate Group,” we came to see by experience that, for the pushing forward of the work and the keeping of the work’s safety, there was real need of such a formation, and that, could it be used with mobility, it would surely bring a greater function into play. And so, having sought and gained the sanction of the higher authority, we set up the “directly subordinate assault team.”

Most to be counted a blessing is that the last three commanders of the “directly subordinate assault team” are all now in Taiwan; and though every one of them is past his sixtieth year, each is still as lively as a dragon and lusty as a tiger; and should the higher authority but speak the word and call them to return to the mainland and do it over once more, I am sure they would buckle on their armor with high glee and be willing to fight the Communist army to the death!

Before this, my three fellow-students Wang Hongzhu, Chang Shaozeng, and Wu Chunxiang—that is, the three successive commanders of the “directly subordinate assault team”—at the writer’s request, each made, out of such past matters as he could remember, a passage of record; and I here, in their order, copy out the original texts they gave, exactly as they stand. Where there is here and there some repeating or overlapping in the texts, I do not mean to abridge it; it is little enough, happily, and the touch of each man’s telling is different besides.

Part the First: my fellow-student Wang Hongzhu’s account, “The Forming of the Assault Team and a Brief Record of It.”—“Looking back upon the things of forty years ago, truly a hundred feelings throng together, and blood and tears run mingled. The members of the First Brigade of the Pacification Corps were one and all young men of high patriotic feeling. I remember above all that during our training in the ‘Lizhi Class of the Central Training Corps’ at Nanjing, President Chiang came each week to instruct us, and several times called the roll and dined with us; the depth of his care for our class, and the earnestness of his hopes of it, could in truth no further go.

“The ‘spiritual talks’ given to us by the class director, General Zheng Jiemin, and the deputy director, General Zhang Yanyuan, and the kindly care of us by the officers of the corps such as Lieutenant Colonel Zhang Luying—good masters and helpful friends, all such as a man can never in his life forget.

“The instructors invited to the class were likewise the choice of their day—such men as Mr. Ren Zhuoxuan and Mr. Xu Foguan and the like; and fresh in my memory is the exceedingly droll manner of teaching of our demolition instructor, who said: ‘Spring flowers unred are no better than grass, a youth who mounts not is no better than the old; the black-powder formula runs just so—one of niter, two of sulfur, three of charcoal, and of each not a jot may be short.’ This came near, to be sure, to the ‘patter-rhyme of the storyteller’s tongue,’ and yet, mixed afterward according to the recipe, it never once failed in the trial.

“I remember further the instructor of the weapons course, who would take endless pains to see that every one of us, with the ‘carbine,’ the ‘revolver,’ the ‘M1,’ the ‘MB,’ the ‘FTs,’ and the rest, could not only use them but take them apart and put them together again before he would count us fit.

“The courses were many, and we learned not a little. Once, in the pitch-black dead of night, at the sound of a single command we students each, in silence, with a map of one part in fifty thousand and a simple compass, were bound to move alone, from the Central Training Corps to a muster at the Ming Xiaoling.

We were every one of us in high spirit, and felt our way along. As the day was about to break, we saw that the Corps Commander, Mr. Liu Peichu, had long been waiting at the Linggu Temple pagoda. He called the roll from the register and gave us a talk, his words full of encouragement.

“On the day of our graduation, the ‘News Class’ and the second term of the ‘Lizhi Class’ held their graduation ceremony jointly, at which the late President Chiang presided in person, calling each man by name and earnestly exhorting us; and he instructed us thus: ‘Comrades, you are revolutionary youths engaged in the very act of the struggle against the bandits; you must hold fast the dauntless spirit of sacrifice and struggle, press boldly forward, and, going deep behind the enemy, deal the Communist bandits a heavy blow. Know this: that youth is the wellspring of revolution, and that this very hour is the golden time for you to serve your country and offer up your hearts’ strength; and it is my hope that my comrades will, with one accord, strive together to wipe out the Communist bandits and to make the Three Principles of the People a reality.’ In November of the thirty-sixth year of the Republic, not long after we had been assigned to the First Brigade at Beiping and opened our work, there came word that fellow-workers had already risked and given their lives—such as the five fellow-students Ji Ruicheng, Zhao Pengfei, and the rest, of the ‘East Hebei Command Room’; and, of the ‘Chahar Command Room,’ my fellow-student Zhong Ningshou, who in the battle of Zigoubao fought bravely with his gun and came alive out of death’s jaws, though his upper limbs were left maimed. Besides these, there were of course others I do not know of; and all such things, one and all, made manifest the striving spirit of revolutionary youth.

“I here set forth in detail the forming of the assault team and its development. In the beginning of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic we were granted leave to establish the assault team, whose chief cadres were all filled by fellow-students of the first and second terms of the Lizhi Class. The conditions for enrolling members were set comparatively high: in principle a man must be a graduate of senior middle school, above twenty and under thirty years of age, of sturdy build, staunch in mind, and resolved to serve his country; next came demobilized young soldiers who volunteered to serve the country once more; and there were besides the excellent youths recommended by the several military units. In the end, the number of places being limited, those who met the conditions far exceeded what we had imagined. (The writer cannot forbear to put in a word here: “Conditions such as the above, set down in the Taiwan of these latter years, would hardly, I fear, draw a single man.”) When the quota was filled, we gave them political and military training; and because their quality was from the first very high, and training was joined besides with actual action, the making of the force was hastened. This band was not only high in morale and fine in equipment, but in regular tactics, guerrilla tactics, small-group assault, and separate action would use each by turns as the case required, showing forth a new spirit of battle, and constraining the ‘Headquarters of the Eleventh War Zone’ and the ‘North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters’ to own that it had indeed a fresh and eye-opening ‘quickening effect’ upon the situation of that time.

“I set forth its general state under heads as follows. Organization and establishment: the assault team belonged to the ‘First Brigade of the Pacification Corps of the Ministry of National Defense’; the team had one commander, one deputy commander, one instructor, and three instructor’s assistants, who had charge severally of finance and of affairs. The team held under it four district companies, each district company having one district-company commander and one adjutant. Each district company held four sub-brigades, each sub-brigade having one sub-brigade leader and one deputy; each sub-brigade held four small groups, each small group having one group leader; and each small group was made up of five men. Personnel: the assault-team commander, my fellow-student Xu Lide, nicknamed ‘the Old Mongol,’ held the post of commander but a single month during the training before illness made him leave it; the deputy commander was my fellow-student Wang Hongzhu, and after Commander Xu left, Wang the deputy was raised to the place. The instructor was my fellow-student Yang Shiyi. The first district-company commander Dou Yulin, the second Zhang Baoquan, and the third Jia Shuming were all fellow-students of the Lizhi Class; the fourth, Zhao Kuoting, was one whom Wang Hongzhu had drawn in and re-formed into the ranks.

“Task: with fine light equipment and a vigorous will to battle, to fall upon the bandits where they looked not for it and to strike them unprepared, dealing them a heavy blow; to bring into play the valiant spirit of the few striking the many and the small overcoming the great; and by force and by daring to overawe the bandit army, thereby to storm and take its strongpoints, so as to capture its inner documents for a strong means of gathering intelligence. This was one of the most practically effective methods our unit had come by out of its accumulated experience of the work, and in our own jargon we called it ‘beating out intelligence.’ Besides this, there was the covering of our unit in the carrying-out of its other tasks, and the working in concert with the fighting forces in reconnaissance and search, and so forth.

“The engagement with the bandits’ ‘Changping County Detachment’ at Wuchongshan: in the fifth month of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic, brigade headquarters received intelligence that the bandit army’s ‘Changping County Detachment,’ some hundreds strong, was gathering at Wuchongshan village, about sixty li to the northwest of the city of Beiping, with intent to make trouble in the near suburbs. Thereupon the deputy brigade commander Li Yulin, together with the assault-team commander Wang Hongzhu, got into touch with the ‘North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters,’ and having gained its consent, was granted the strength of one battalion of the ‘208th Division of the Youth Army’ (the divisional headquarters of the 208th being at Qinghe town, thirty li north of Beiping) and one brigade, about three hundred men, of the ‘Wanping County self-defense corps,’ which reached Wuchongshan village before dawn the next day. After a little coordination, the 208th Division blocked the way on the northwest, the Wanping self-defense corps cut in from the south side, and our assault team, as the main attack, drove strongly into the village from the east; and just as our demolition group set off its charge with a roar that shook the very sky, we saw the bandits, in knots of three and five, seize hold of the villagers, old and young alike, and flee in panic, the common people wailing and crying aloud—a scene most painful to behold. Such a case made our men afraid to fire, and made it ill to press the pursuit—and herein lies the very hatefulness of the Communist Party. With one voice they cry, ‘for the people’—but that, indeed, is a tale to cheat men with.

“The seizing of stores at Baifang village: on a day in the sixth month of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic, the assault team got trustworthy intelligence that a bandit transport train would pass through the bounds of Changping county, with armed militia escorting it. At the appointed time there did indeed come a transport train, carrying requisitioned mixed grain, cotton, and the like in ten great carts, setting out eastward from a certain village at about ten in the evening. Our assault team, led in person by Commander Wang Hongzhu with one sub-brigade, broke itself up whole into parts and lay hidden severally at the appointed spots, while another sub-brigade, at the appointed time, gathered and held a small height east of the village to bar the way, the two answering each other by a settled signal. When the bandits discovered them, they were already at close quarters, and gun and shot went off together and struck them all unready; and heedless now of the carts, every man of them scuttled off with his head in his hands and fled across the wilds, some slipping away into the tunnels. Afterward, on reckoning up these dozen-odd great carts and the stores scattered on the ground, there were in all forty-eight sacks of grain, thirty-odd flashlights, several tens of boxes of dry batteries, several tens of bolts of cloth, a number of bags of cotton, and sundry articles of daily use besides. There were also several rubber-tired wooden-board carts and six lean horses. This being reported to brigade headquarters, we were granted leave to sell them off and buy ten horses with the price, and so set up a cavalry group; and thereat the fame and force of the assault team waxed mightily.

“Shifting garrison to Shunyi county to help the locality open a traitor-rooting action: in the autumn of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic, at the invitation of the ‘Ping-Gu Line Command Room,’ we were ordered to move into the county town of Shunyi. It was reported that there was a puppet ‘political commissar of Shunyi county,’ one Wang, who under the guise of a schoolteacher openly gave out his orders, levied harshly and exacted cruelly, and oppressed the common people; and though the people hated him to the marrow, none dared to deal with him, for dread of drawing down revenge—which too was one of the strange sights of that time. One day Commander Wang Hongzhu, with three of the team dressed up as farmers, was led by a local guide across a small river, and going eight li on foot came to a village; and at a sign from the guide the mark was found. In an instant they first stripped this Wang of his pistol, and thereupon made known who we were; Wang, without a word, turned and took to his heels, and because he was native-born and bred and knew the ground, in the twinkling of an eye he was gone. The two men who had gone with Commander Wang hurried off, each his own way, to the neighborhood of the dwellings, when suddenly they saw a chicken standing on the top of a wall; and a thought striking them, they climbed over the wall, and sure enough found Wang crouching at the foot of it, and took him alive. Brought back to the team’s post, at the first questioning he told the truth, and was thereupon sent under escort to the Bandit-Suppression Headquarters to be dealt with by law.

“The breaking-up of an arms-repair shop: in the autumn of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic, not long after the assault team was stationed in Shunyi county, the deputy brigade commander Mr. Li Yulin came in person to the county town, and there and then ordered our team, joined with Group Leader Zhang Peizhi of the ‘Shunyi Working Group,’ and having sought besides a company sent out by the ‘Shunyi Niulanshan self-defense corps,’ to march by night and come first to Niulanshan north of the town (in which town there was garrisoned Wang Hongjun, a directly subordinate man dispatched by the ‘Fourth Command Room’). After a little inquiry and a checking-over of what was known, they set out again northward, and crossed a river waist-deep in water, the air by then being already chill enough. Coming ashore, they divided the tasks: the self-defense corps took the left wing, one sub-brigade of our assault team served as the vanguard, and another took the right wing and the rearguard; and marching on about an hour, they came to their goal as day was about to break. Straightway they began the attack; the demolition group and the sniper group, quick in their movements, caught the bandits unprepared at last, and in knots of three and five they fled in disorder. A sentry was seized in passing, and at his guidance, within a small cluster of dwellings, there were brought out several hundred rifles of every kind, and many pieces besides of machines, tools, iron bedsteads, and the like; and these, being past carrying off, were destroyed and burned on the spot. By the time the bandit army of a neighboring village fired their guns and came to the rescue, our team had already crossed the river in safety and returned to its former place. This was a destructive assault action driven deep into bandit-held territory; and we found the bandit army in truth a very common, ordinary thing, not worth the speaking of.

“Ordered to Jinghai and Duliu, to skirmish on the northern reach of the Jin-Pu railway: at the end of the autumn of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic, ordered to pass through Beiping for a little regrouping and training, our assault team, a company of some three hundred men, boarded a special train of the Bei-Ning railway and transferred to the Jin-Pu line, and after a whole day’s journey reached Duliu station. During this time of awaiting orders we pressed on with our training and made ourselves acquainted with the general state of a hundred li and more round about—of the many places where the bandit army and its militia went in and out, such as Shangguantun, Xiaguantun, Tangguantun, Wangjiakou, the county town of Jinghai, and the rest—and we helped the ‘Jin-Pu Line Command Room’ to muster and drill the villagers and to set up civilian watch-posts, with results plainly seen. Some weeks later the deputy brigade commander Li came on a tour of inspection, and we learned for the first time that our team would in time bear a still harder task: it was disclosed that we were to draw near to the coastal delta and there aid in the coast defense. At Tangguantun the deputy brigade commander Li conveyed the transfer order: Hongzhu was recalled to brigade headquarters to be operations staff officer, and the post of assault-team commander was taken over by my fellow-student Chang Shaozeng.”—Writer’s note: the passage of record furnished by my fellow-student Wang Hongzhu does not end here; there follows a general account of how the assault team covered the whole brigade in its withdrawal, winding some thousands of li, to south of the Yangtze and across the sea to Magong island—a thing rich alike in feeling and in style, and worth the recalling. The writer purposes, in the last two chapters, to draw upon it again. As for the battles of the assault team, and their fruits, they are, in the great scheme of things, a trifling matter, and there is nothing to boast of in them; what is of moment is to look upon the other side—the cruel and violent doings of the Communist Party, as in their seizing of the common people for a shield to cover their own flight—a thing in truth to make a man’s hair stand on end.

By what is understood, this too was one of their “training subjects”; as for what the people’s welfare might be, in their minds there was no such thing at all.

In his text my fellow-student Wang Hongzhu made no mention of matters of “logistics and supply” or of “pay and allowances”; and yet these too are weighty questions in any military body. The reason he did not touch on them is that the comrades of the whole team did not “reckon over” such things—or one might say, at that time they were none of them a problem. As for matters of equipment such as “fine weapons” and “powerful firepower,” something shall be said of them in the record of the third commander, my fellow-student Wu Chunxiang.

In the text, certain small place-names and precise dates could not, in truth, be called to mind.

The writer has no mind, nor any need, to sing the praises of anyone; and yet, in an outpouring of true feeling, I must say: “Our deputy brigade commander, Mr. Li Yulin, going before his men and shrinking from no hardship or peril, walked through every one of the dispatched units; for this body of troops he spent himself far more than I did.”

### 3. Or Did the Tide Turn and Their Resolve Fail?

My fellow-student Chang Shaozeng was originally group leader of our unit’s “directly subordinate West-Suburb Group”; and when it was decided to disband the “West-Suburb Group,” Chang Shaozeng was appointed in Wang Hongzhu’s stead to be commander of the “directly subordinate assault team,” the second to hold the post. He held the command of the assault team but a short while before he was promoted and transferred to be commanding officer of the “Second Command Room,” the assault team being then attached under the command room’s oversight.

The two sections that follow set down first the assault team and then the command room, the focus still resting upon Zhu Zhankui’s turning traitor.

Part the Second: my fellow-student Chang Shaozeng’s account, “Taking Over the Brigade’s Directly Subordinate Assault Team.”—“In the middle of the Xth month of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic, I was ordered to leave the ‘West-Suburb Group’ and take over the ‘directly subordinate assault team.’

“I was truly unversed in the ways of the world, and knew only that it is the soldier’s bounden duty to obey his orders; nor did I stop to think whether I was equal to it, but went off to Tangguantun in Jinghai county to take up the charge. At that time the force was well trained and finely equipped, its members all of middle-school schooling and above, and in thought, in knowledge, and in fighting skill alike, under the careful management of the former commander Wang Hongzhu, a firm foundation had been laid. The district-company commanders Li Baozhang, Jia Shuming, and Zhang Tongfu were all standard officers, accomplished alike in skill and in virtue, in letters and in arms; and rarest of all, they were greatly beloved of their comrades, of one heart and mind, and strict and clear in discipline, so that wherever they went they were not only welcomed of the people but honored of the allied forces, and men called this a political force hard as iron and steel.

“Commissioner Zhu Zhankui was the administrative commissioner of this district, with the additional office of security commander, and was commonly called ‘Commander Zhu.’ Before Shaozeng set out, the deputy brigade commander Li charged him again and again to ‘work in close concert with Commander Zhu’; and Commander Zhu, too, made much ‘account’ of our force, and was very courteous to every one of the men. This, to be sure, is not enough to prove anything.

“Each time we went out on a mopping-up task, our assault team for the most part took the key point of the attack, and would often fire with the ‘M1 rifle’ or the ‘Browning machine gun’; and at the first sound of the guns the bandit army, hearing that the sound was different, dared not resist but at once drew off. Yet Commander Zhu, who ‘directed the fighting,’ never once gave the order to pursue—whereas by the principles of tactics, save in special cases, one ought to press the pursuit while the advantage holds.

“In the middle of the Xth month of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic, on one occasion of a joint sortie with Zhu Zhankui’s force, the true state of things was this. That night, toward three in the small hours, we set out from Xiaguantun, our course to the southwest, and pushed deep into the bandit country about Qing and Cang. Reaching the target ground, we entered a certain village in silence and by batches, and completed the encirclement on three sides—north, west, and east; and having first made our footing sure, after a little while we began the attack. The gunfight lasted an hour and more; the enemy did not waver in the least, and twice besides fell upon us in a counter-thrust, seeking to break out from the eastern side, but without success. Commander Zhu Zhankui thereupon asked our team to pour a dense fire from the northwest corner toward the southeast with machine guns and M1 rifles; and hard upon the sound of the guns, our brothers, packs on their backs and steel helmets on their heads, at once leapt up onto the wall (the village wall, like a city wall); and thereat the main body came on behind and stormed into the village. But when they had entered the streets by their several ways, no trace of a soul was to be seen—the common people had hidden themselves; and the bandit army? They must surely have had another way of escape. Nothing was left but to give the order to look for the mouth of the tunnels, and to open a search on every side.

“About noon our team’s first district company was the first to find the mouth of a tunnel; and after the district-company commander Li Baozhang came to report it, he there and then led that company into the tunnel to search. Of the first sub-brigade, Chen Junxiang and Ren Deqin, each with a pistol, went deep in, and after them Ren Deqin’s group of three reached the inmost part. On the spot they took several tens of rifles (among them the Japanese-made Type 38 and the Hanyang-made 7.9), a number of grenade-throwers, a heap of home-made firearms, and a dozen-odd bundles of yellow explosive charges.

“We lit brushwood fires and threw them into the several tunnel mouths, and sent others besides to look for the exits nearby; and having found them, we kept watch a good while, yet saw no bandit soldier come out—they must, we supposed, have long since fled away.

“We found besides, within the tunnels, that at many a corner there was set a flanking-defense device—that is, a half-cover fit to hold one man; and that at the entrances there were placed a grenade or two, for use in a sudden turn of need. Such contrivances, rude as they all were, might yet catch a man off his guard and take him in; it is a thing one must not fail to know.

“The above material is drawn from a letter of information sent by my comrade Chen Junxiang, who had himself taken part in this action; it is wholly true to the fact, and shows how deep the impression was upon him.

“Afterward our team reported to brigade headquarters, for its record, the course of the sortie and the captured firearms and the rest.

“Not long after the report was sent, Commander Zhu Zhankui came in person to the team’s post and asked that those firearms and munitions be given over for their use; and I had no choice but to put him off gently, saying we were a step too late.”—So much for the record of my fellow-student Chang Shaozeng, during his time as commander of the directly subordinate assault team, of an action carried on in concert with Zhu Zhankui’s force.

Writer’s note: my fellow-student Chang Shaozeng held the post of assault-team commander but a short time, and so there is not much that can be recorded of it.

In this joint sortie, though Zhu Zhankui’s conduct was a little out of the ordinary, yet since we were fighting side by side with him, we did not think the worst of it.

As with his pointing out the target, and bidding us pour a dense fire in a certain direction, while afterward the other side had neither dead nor wounded—that was a thing past understanding; and when our team had stormed into the place, the bandit army had fled clean away, not one left behind—herein too was something strange.

Afterward Zhu asked us to hand over the spoils of the fight to him; this might indeed be explained, and yet it could not be taken as wholly aboveboard either.

What was wanting was this: whether Zhu Zhankui had already some link or tacit understanding with the bandit army on the other side, of that we knew nothing at all.

By now, in fact, there were already threads and traces to be traced; that we failed to spy them out in time and to guard against them—for this, at the very least, we must bear the blame of an oversight.

Writing thus far, I shall for the moment halt the matter of the assault team and Zhu Zhankui, and speak in brief of another—the working unit our force dispatched to garrison this district, the “Second Command Room.” The “Second Command Room” moved about the northern reach of the Jin-Pu railway; at first the hope was to push south from Qing and Cang to De county in Shandong, so as to link up with the Third Brigade; and next, to open through the Tianjin-Baoding line and join with our own “First Command Room.” Afterward, when the situation turned about, it fell back on the contrary northward, to Yangliuqing near Tianjin.

A man who does not commonly heed geographical position may perhaps not know how great or small the distance is from Yangliuqing to Dezhou; but if one take a national junior-middle-school “Provincial Atlas of the Republic of China” at a scale of one part in 3,500,000, and turn to the page for Hebei Province, and measure it off with a whole pencil, it is no more than half a pencil’s length—yet in fact, going along the railway line, it is already more than two hundred kilometers.

This is as much as to say, “not to advance is to retreat; battle upon battle, defeat upon defeat.” And in fact so it truly was: the “Second Command Room” and the “directly subordinate assault team” fought none but winning fights, and yet the working district shifted ever northward—which showed that pacification and the suppression of rebellion had made no progress in substance. The personnel of the “Second Command Room” changed much: in a year and more, four commanding officers came and went one after another. The first was Colonel Li Changqing, who had been an adjutant of brigade headquarters, and had served too as a deputy commander in the “Lizhi Class” at Nanjing. He was of long standing, and rather conservative in his ways.

The second was Jiang Tian, a fellow-student of the fourth term of the Military Academy who came by recommendation; in his time, for the sake of working the case of Lin Biao and Tao Zhu (see the third chapter above), he could not attend to both, and was transferred away. To speak fairly, brother Jiang Tian did not do well during his term at the “Second Command Room.” The third commanding officer was Mr. Liu Yuanshen, the reviser of this book; and because I had need of him to help me carry out a special case (the “stay-behind work”), he had to be recalled to Beiping, and it was for this that my fellow-student Chang Shaozeng, commander of the “directly subordinate assault team,” was promoted and transferred to be the fourth commanding officer of the “Second Command Room.”

I remember that it was during the commanding officer Liu Yuanshen’s term that I once went to Jinghai county, and met Commander Zhu Zhankui near the station.

The commanding officer Chang Shaozeng has furnished, in brief and plain form, a short passage on the “Work of the Second Command Room”; what he sets down is the general state of things during his own term, and I here copy out the original as follows: “The working district of the Second Room was on the northern reach of the Jin-Pu railway, from the edge of the city of Tianjin south to Cang county. But because the Communist Party made its disorders and broke the lines of traffic from time to time, the ground over which the common people could move freely grew ever narrower, while our line of defense and the tracts where the Communist army came and went were interlocked like the teeth of a dog—so that in truth no clear boundary could be drawn between them.

“Our command room was set at Jinghai, and was divided into three groups, stationed severally at three places along the railway line. The First Group: group leader Liu Chunxi, deputy group leader Zhang Linge, stationed at Yangliuqing. The Second Group: group leader Ma Zhonglin, deputy group leader Sun Shouyi, stationed at Duliu. The Third Group: group leader Liu Chufeng, deputy group leader unnamed, stationed at Tangguantun. After I took up the charge in the Xth month of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic, the first instruction brigade headquarters gave me was this: ‘Work in close concert with Zhu Zhankui’s force’; and so, in all that touched important intelligence and the doings of our own force, I gave word and report as far as I could, that the understanding between us might be bettered and the efficiency of the work raised.

“One evening, a little after seven, I went with the staff officer Gu Shoulin to call upon Commander Zhu Zhankui; in the courtyard and before the inner gate there were five or six armed guards more than on ordinary days, and after we were announced, the answer came back that Commander Zhu was in the midst of trying a case. We waited a long while, and nothing came of it, and had no choice but to return.

“A case such as this fell out two or three times over, and there was much amiss in it; yet we could point to no concrete fact, and dared not lightly report it upward. On yet another day, going again with the staff officer Gu Shoulin to call on Commander Zhu, we did meet him, and talked of the great state of affairs of the time; and Zhu Zhankui said: ‘If the whole situation should change—we are men of this place, and cannot, of course, leave our native villages; but you are a force of the Central Government—what will you do then?’ We answered him very frankly, saying: ‘We are all soldiers, and must of course obey the orders of our superiors; at the very worst, we can still take to guerrilla war!’ The staff officer Gu Shoulin, with a stronger stress upon his words, said to Zhu besides: ‘We are a force of the nation, disciples of the Three Principles of the People; we do our duty, and look on death as a homecoming; should the case change, we will fight absolutely to the very end!’ Zhu Zhankui, hearing these ardent and impassioned words of Gu Shoulin, changed countenance at them, and rising heavily to his feet, said: ‘Ah, you are well worthy of the name of a staunch political force …’ By the Xth month of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic, Commander Zhu Zhankui called a briefing and there announced: ‘By order, to meet the present situation and to strengthen our fighting gains, the several commissioner-districts are to begin carrying out on all fronts the widening of the guerrilla front; all the forces are to gather together and open a guerrilla war in the Beiping-Tianjin-Baoding triangle—the men and their equipment all to be borne along, the military stores loaded on great carts, and the families every one to go with them.’ At the same time Commander Zhu made a point of telling us that he had already met the deputy unit commander Li at Beiping, and that Li agreed to all of us carrying out together this action of widening the guerrilla front.

“Our command room at once telegraphed brigade headquarters, and received the instruction: ‘You may work in concert with Zhu’s force.’

“And sure enough, the widened guerrilla campaign began. At first it was no more than going round in circles nearby: setting out from Xiaguantun, the first day to this village, the second day to that; and along the way there were local forces that joined the ranks, so that the men grew more and more and the road went farther and farther—but the strange thing was that we never once met the enemy.

“We could not see Commander Zhu himself; it was always his orderly who came with a message, saying: ‘Commander Zhu is now at Xx village; pray hurry up and join him’—and we had no choice but to hurry up.

“At that time those taking part in the action were: over a hundred men of the ‘Second Command Room’; the ‘assault team’ together with the ‘cavalry squad,’ about two hundred; and the self-defense corps of a certain village, more than 120—four hundred and more in all; with, besides, three baggage carts and six pack-mules. Wherever they went, men looked askance at them. The force marched on to a village, and it was already noon, when suddenly an orderly came, saying: ‘Commander Zhu is two li ahead; he asks you to come to “Tang’erli” and go forward together.’ So we hurried on as fast as we could—first, because we had long not seen Commander Zhu and were eager to meet him and ask into the matter; and second, because our breasts were full of doubts, and we must needs seek an answer to them.

“When we had come to about a li outside Tang’erli village, the sky was growing dark, and the soil of the road was full of sand, so that at every step the foot sank in and the going was hard; and so the order was given to halt. Just then there came from the west two of Zhu’s orderlies, dressed in blue and riding bicycles, whom we asked what the true state of things was; and they answered: ‘Commander Zhu went off westward in the afternoon, to what place we know not; there are no forces left within Tang’erli village, and westward on the road it is all sandy ground.’ Thereupon we sent the cavalry squad into the village to look, and there was indeed no force of Zhu’s there.

“On taking counsel with Commander Wu Chunxiang and the staff officer Gu Shoulin, each held to his own reasons. Staff officer Gu thought it a great pity not to go now and join forces with the working unit at Baoding; but upon the ground of our own task, of the principles of tactics, and of the lie of the land, it was in the end decided to turn the rearguard into the vanguard and return through the night to our base.

“Early the next morning, our room’s intelligence group leader Zhao Zixia and deputy group leader Wang Weining came running to report: ‘Zhu Zhankui has led his force off to the bandits; the brothers of the self-defense corps who just now escaped back all say so with one voice—it is, of course, an accomplished fact.’”—Writer’s note: that the commanding officer Chang, going with staff officer Gu several times to call on Zhu Zhankui, was each time refused an audience, showed plainly enough, by the look of things at the time, that there was already much amiss. Judging it out, it may be that Zhu was even then plotting in secret with men come from the Communist side—was perhaps in the very act of talking terms.

Zhu Zhankui himself, and those behind him, were rather minded to persuade the commanding officer Chang and the rest to run with him in the same current; but the upshot of the sounding-out was that they despaired of it.

The so-called “widening of the guerrilla front” was plainly a snare. Think of it—where under heaven is there guerrilla war carried on with one’s household and family in tow?

To be sure, they meant to bag these few hundred of us without bloodying a blade; but they were still less than clever, and in the end let the horse’s hoof show through after all.

The happening of this thing cannot be laid to the “Second Command Room” as a failure to guard against the trouble before it took shape; for brigade headquarters at Beiping had again and again instructed them “to work in close concert with Zhu’s force,” so that they kept no wariness in their hearts. On a reckoning of it, the fault lay in the want of thoroughness in our own headquarters’ direction of the work.

Besides this, my fellow-student Chang Shaozeng’s mention of the “staff officer Gu Shoulin” in his text cannot but call up a most painful memory. In the fortieth year of the Republic, when I was carrying on mainland work in Hong Kong, I asked my fellow-student Gu Shoulin over from Taiwan to lend me a hand. Gu was inward by nature and given to deep thinking; I set him first to learn the state of the bandits, meaning that in time, when he had the power, he might handle intelligence on his own. I feared that, a stranger to the place and its people, he might lose his way and not get back, and I was anxious besides to keep his identity from being laid open; and so I warned him to “go out but little”—and thereupon he would often sit there alone, letting his mind run wild, glum and out of sorts. At times, too, he would mutter to himself some sleep-talking words that none could clearly hear. Now and then he would give a long, wild cry and say: “Do not hold me back—let me go! I mean to fight my way back to the mainland!” And it was then that I found his mind was unhinged.

I took him to the outpatient clinic, to no effect. For a long stay in hospital, the public ones would not take him, and the private ones were beyond my means. And so it dragged on and grew worse and worse, until it was a raving madness, his speech all without order or sense.

One day, through a single carelessness, taking the moment when the man who watched over him was away, he went off, once and for all, never to return, and what became of him none can tell.

Hong Kong is a small place; I inquired everywhere, and there was no word; I had people go to the chaiguan (the police station) to look, and there was no trace; day after day I pored over the social news in the papers, and there was not a single item that touched on it. Where, then, had Gu Shoulin gone? To this day it remains a mystery.

### 4. For All the Waverings, Magnanimous Still

The “assault team’s” working in concert with Zhu Zhankui’s force was but one of its chief tasks. Besides this, there was another use, unlooked-for at the outset, and that was “beating out intelligence.”

Through the assault team’s unceasing experience, “beating out intelligence” had become the most effective of the many means of “gathering intelligence.” Whenever the assault team stormed a bandit-army strongpoint, the bandit army, with no time to gather up its things before it fled, would always leave many things behind; and of these, what we prized as rare was not the guns and shot and shell, but certain little slips of paper, long and short, of unequal sizes. From these slips we could refine out many precious things—which is to say, the most trustworthy of intelligence material.

To be sure, the confessions of prisoners would disclose something too; but besides the need to sift them with care, there was needed a further labor of judgment, so that they were never so valuable as a document ready to hand. After the assault team was set up, from its first commander Wang Hongzhu to its third, Wu Chunxiang, they had in this respect many an achievement to their credit.

In what follows, still with the Zhu Zhankui affair for its chief matter, I continue.—Part the Third: my fellow-student Wu Chunxiang’s account, “The March and the Withdrawal between Yangliuqing, Wangqingtuo, and Tang’erli.”—“In the Xth month of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic, Chunxiang was ordered to leave the post of second group leader of the ‘Fourth Command Room’ (Shunyi) and take over the post of commander of the ‘assault team.’ At that time the force was stationed at Qingwangzhuang in Tianjin county, and in concert with our ‘Second Command Room’ undertook the chief tasks—about the outskirts of Tianjin—of mustering and drilling the people, gathering intelligence, and turning the enemy and making assaults.

“The former commander, my fellow-student Chang Shaozeng, was transferred to take charge as commanding officer of the ‘Second Command Room.’ On the very day I took up the post, Chang Shaozeng, that I might learn as soon as might be the state of our force, of the surroundings of our station, of the movements of the Communist army, and the like, made me a special half-hour briefing. At that time the Communist army’s local forces were rampant in their doings about the outskirts of Tianjin, and our force would often take the way of lying hid by day and coming out by night to make its rounds with the Communist army. Bearing this in mind, we took the chance besides to strengthen our training in night marching and our schooling in marksmanship. By one unofficial record, our force’s marching power could go a hundred li by night without being over-wearied. All the Communist army’s local militia nearby stood in some dread, one and all, of the ‘0760’ force.

“After I took over, the ordering of the assault team was a little changed: besides a slight lessening of the numbers, it had been cut down from four district companies to three, with one cavalry squad besides. The first commander, my fellow-student Wang Hongzhu, had been most earnest in the training—one may say the groundwork was solid—and joined to a generally very high quality, the discipline and bearing of the force were all good; and herein too lay the thing most beloved of the local people.

“It was because there was a bond of feeling with the people that there were so many volunteer couriers who would ever bring word and report, so that our eyes and ears were indeed kept quick and nimble.

“As for equipment, the whole team had in all twelve light machine guns and twenty-eight automatic rifles, with, besides, semi-automatic rifles, carbines, pistols, and the rest. Its firepower was strong, and after a few encounters it could indeed make the bandit army lose heart at the very sound of it.

“On the Xth day of the ninth month of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic, our force was ordered to move into the town of Wangqingtuo, fifteen li to the west of the outskirts of Yangliuqing. The garrison stationed in that town was the security corps under the security commander Zhu Zhankui, about a regiment in strength. On a day in the last third of the tenth month, the “Second Command Room” received word from Zhu Zhankui, asking the command room to pass on to the assault team a request to join in a sortie against the bandit-army militia near the town of Tang’erli, so as to hold down its now-and-again incursions. Tang’erli was about thirty-odd li from Wangqingtuo; our force mustered at seven that morning and set out together with the security corps, and passing through Wangjiazhuangzi and other places, came to about two li outside the village of Zhaojiazhaizi. When we were still three li and more from the target ground of Tang’erli, our force’s volunteer intelligence men reported: ‘There are more than two hundred bandits at Zhaojiazhaizi, now at their meal, and seemingly about to set out.’ We judged that these might be a covering force of the bandit army, and were about to get into touch with Zhu Zhankui’s security force, when, all unlooked-for, his force had already moved on its own, and had given us no word of it beforehand.

“When our force had marched to but a few hundred paces outside Zhaojiazhaizi village, we found the case was other than it seemed, and at once deployed, first taking two isolated dwellings at the northern end of the village, and sending the cavalry squad in haste to scout four or five dwellings close by, and dispatching besides the district-company commander Zhang Tongfu to lead his company in a fire-search; and after some twenty minutes of fighting the bandit army drew off without a battle. Because our force could get no coordination with Zhu’s force, we made no pursuit.

“The search over, our force gathered a little short of one li north of Tang’erli village and kept a state of watch. By this time it was about forty minutes past five in the afternoon, the sun pressing down upon the western hills, and dusk already come. We decided at once to send the cavalry squad first into Tang’erli village to reconnoiter and search, hoping, besides learning the bandits’ state, to get into touch with Zhu’s force. By the report of the cavalry-squad leader Wu Yulin, the whole village of Tang’erli was about five hundred meters in depth, and on the road not a single one of the common people was to be found, nor any trace of Zhu Zhankui’s force. At this we were in a most awkward case, and of course saw that in Zhu Zhankui there was some trick. Taking counsel with the “Second Command Room’s” commanding officer Chang Shaozeng and the staff officer Gu Shoulin and the rest, we came to the judgment following: ‘(1) Zhu Zhankui’s force moved in a strange fashion; both inviting us to join the action and playing a double game, it was in very truth to be suspected.

“‘(2) The bandit army at Zhaojiazhaizi, drawing off without a battle, plainly had some other design—perhaps to draw us in deep and form a stance of pincer-attack and encirclement. (3) Our force, having marched from seven that morning the whole day long, had neither eaten nor rested, and though it held on by main force, already showed signs of weariness; should we meet with some case, there was fear that both spirit and strength would fail.

“‘(4) The case was as yet nowhere clear, and we might not lightly take action, lest we suffer a loss then and there.

“‘Upon these four points, our force ought at once to withdraw with speed from the present ground and turn back to Wangqingtuo.’ But unlooked-for, the command room’s staff officer, my fellow-student Gu Shoulin, was of another mind: he held that to do so gave a sense of a tiger’s head and a snake’s tail, and could not but be a dispiriting thing. The commanding officer Chang ruled that we should, all the same, return to Wangqingtuo and reckon the matter afresh there.

“Thereupon Zhang Tongfu’s district company was named the vanguard, the whole team in a single file, each man a meter apart, rounds chambered and careful against a stray shot. The cavalry squad brought up the rear and kept watch along the way, the marching pace set at ten kilometers the hour, the faster the better; and it was strictly enjoined to avoid making any noise on the march. Beyond all common measure, the twenty-kilometer road was done, and we reached Wangqingtuo, in an hour and thirty-odd minutes.

“The whole team, after a little setting-in-order, entered the town of Wangqingtuo in a marching column, and by this time it was twenty minutes past nine at night. By the time the cooking was done and the meal eaten, it was near to midnight.

“At ten minutes past five the next morning, the commanding officer, my fellow-student Chang Shaozeng, came at a run to the team’s post; and because it was not yet six, the hour for rising, Chunxiang and the rest were still in the land of dreams. Commander Chang woke me, saying: ‘There is intelligence—let me tell you: that fellow Zhu Zhankui has turned again! He has turned traitor! Lucky it was that we drew back in time to Wangqingtuo, or the thing does not bear thinking of.’ I asked: ‘From what quarter did this intelligence come?’ The commanding officer Chang said: ‘Four cadres of Zhu’s security regiment, unwilling to go over to the bandits, have fled back, and came together to the command room to give word of what was afoot.’ At that, without a thought, I blurted out: ‘We must think of shifting our station with all speed; and at the same time, whether the words of those four men have some hidden purpose in them, of that too we must quickly seek proof.’ Even as we were speaking, a volunteer courier came with word: ‘Zhu Zhankui himself, and more than a thousand of the bandit army, are now gathering at Tang’erli, ready to set out.’ This would be a fact past all doubting.

“The commanding officer Chang and I opened the map and looked, and after a little counsel decided at once to give up Wangqingtuo, and to halt first at Yangliuqing, there to await brigade headquarters’ instructions before settling our course thereafter.

“After a little inward setting-in-order, we set out at nine in the morning: the whole team left the town in four parties, to muster at a certain village five kilometers north of Wangqingtuo. It is a pity the name of this village cannot be called to mind. As a second step we moved on to about a kilometer east of Yangliuqing, for a temporary quartering-place of the assault team.

“The commanding officer Chang, together with the officers and men of the ‘Second Command Room,’ moved back at the same time to their former quarters within the town of Yangliuqing.

“The next day we got intelligence that on the very night our force left Wangqingtuo, Zhu Zhankui, leading in person about two regiments of the bandit army, moved in and took the town of Wangqingtuo; the other garrison forces of the place, and the village corps, the police, and the rest, were all disarmed, and Wangqingtuo thereupon fell.”—So much for the course of Zhu Zhankui’s turning traitor as set down by the third assault-team commander, my fellow-student Wu Chunxiang, which may be read against the foregoing.

Writer’s note: before Zhu Zhankui turned traitor, his standing was that of the highest civil and military authority of this district (the ——th Special District of Hebei Province). In the civil line, he was administrative inspector-commissioner, having charge of a dozen and more counties; in the military line, he was the district’s security commander, his rank being, as a civil official, that of the Selected Grade, and as a military officer, that of major general.

The area under Zhu’s charge lay within the Beiping-Tianjin-Baoding triangle, close to the northern reach of the Jin-Pu railway, the northern reach of the Ping-Han railway, the Beiping-Tianjin section of the Bei-Ning railway, and the not-yet-opened Cang-Shi railway (from Cang county to Shijiazhuang); its importance needs no telling. His superiors had entrusted him with a weighty civil and military office, and of course set high hopes upon him. The guiding principle the Ministry of National Defense gave us was only “to work in earnest concert with him,” and nothing else besides—which is to say, our superiors had never instructed us that we must, with Zhu Zhankui, keep one hand in guard against him; openly it could not, to be sure, be said, but neither, in any other manner, was there ever any hint of it. After Zhu Zhankui came with our brigade to the working district, he cooperated seamlessly throughout: first in Anci county, and afterward in concert with the “Second Command Room” and the “directly subordinate assault team”; and from first to last he dealt with us in good faith. In the spoken instructions our brigade’s deputy commander Mr. Li Yulin gave to Chang Shaozeng, he too laid stress on “working in close concert with Commander Zhu”; and we looked on him with no strange or altered eye—which is to say we held him in no discrimination, and still less, of course, would we bear him any envy for his high rank. The reason we honored him was that he was of the greatest importance to our work; and this had nothing to do with the so-called “realism,” for this was the doing of work, and not the making of friends.

Zhu Zhankui too would come from time to time from his post to brigade headquarters at Beiping to confer upon the work; and though we made him no special entertainment, neither were we ever cold to him: whatever he had need of, we gave him all the support we could; and our bearing was of a piece, and never changed. If it be said that, because he was a man come over to us, we ought always to have kept one hand in guard against him, then this is a thing well worth grave debate. Let me first set forth my own opinion. Since the government had accepted his coming-over first, and had entrusted him with a heavy charge after, it cannot be denied that there was in this the effect of calling other Communist cadres to come over too; and yet it was still meet to keep the magnanimity of “employ a man and doubt him not, doubt a man and employ him not,” for only so would it not fall short of being a political measure to win back the hearts of the people. Even though he turned, why, let him turn—our eyes were set upon a broader field yet.

I myself know well enough that an opinion such as this is fit only for the classroom, the council chamber, or the church, and is by no means to be allowed in an actual military force.

And to go on: at the beginning of the thirty-eighth year of the Republic, when the forces of Lin Biao and Nie Rongzhen laid siege to the city of Beiping, Zhu Zhankui too led his troops to Nanyuan (outside the Yongdingmen of the city of Beiping), and using a loudspeaker called out toward the city; and the writer, though he was in Beiping at the time, did not hear what it was he was calling.

Ever after, in these several tens of years, the writer has never once, in the materials of the Communist Party, come upon this name “Zhu Zhankui.”

I think it likely that either he has vanished from among the living, or he was struggled down and broken; and that the best end of it would be that he was demobilized and went home to his old village, and took up his old trade again—the blowing of the horn.
