## Commentary — Adapting Burn, O Sword for the Screen

Harada Masato

My grandfather on my mother's side was the son of a samurai named Andō Tatewaki, who had served as a secretary to the Numazu domain, and he used to talk about the last days of the shogunate as though he had seen them with his own eyes. His stories, I feel, are what first set me reading historical fiction. It was the heyday of Kurama Tengu, but I liked Katsura Kogorō, who turned up in my grandfather's tales, better than the invented figure of Kurama Tengu; even in our boyhood games of samurai swordplay I would dream myself into Katsura, lying low in disguise on the Sanjō riverbed.

I hunted for a novel with Katsura Kogorō as its hero, but in those days there was none. What I happened to take down in the bookshop just then was Shiba Ryōtarō's book of short stories <i>Bakumatsu</i>, and in the table of contents was one titled "Kogorō the Runaway." I bought it on the spot and devoured it. For me, who had heard from my grandfather the love story of Katsura and the beautiful Gion geisha Ikumatsu, its contents were something of a shock: in struggle and in love alike, this Kogorō was a man whose one move was always to run. The other stories I found more to my taste. In "The Incident Outside the Sakurada Gate" I wept over the fate of Arimura Jizaemon, and Shiba's reading of history, which pronounces of Ii Naosuke — the man who set off the "mad repression" of the Ansei Purge — that "here is a man of nothing but ignorance and obstinacy, and he holds absolute power; it is no different from a madman wielding a blade," opened my eyes. "A Curious Man, Hachirō" became a private craze of mine, title and all; to this day, when I hear the name Kiyokawa Hachirō, the phrase "a curious man" passes without fail through my head. Later the director Shinoda Masahiro filmed it under the title <i>Assassination</i>, and I remember being indignant: why had he not kept "A Curious Man, Hachirō"? I was captivated by the whole crowd of human figures caught up in the assassinations of the late shogunate that Shiba brought so vividly to life. A Shiba fan was born.

I first met <i>Burn, O Sword!</i> in my high-school years. Hijikata Toshizō, whom until then I had only ever seen — in novels, in manga, in films — as a villain, suddenly came close to me; but it did not at once become a work I loved. The night-crawling scene in the opening struck me as something unclean (laughs). As Shinsengumi stories go, I was drawn rather to <i>Shinsengumi Keppūroku</i>, which I read later. It was only when I reread <i>Burn, O Sword!</i> in my fifties that I truly came to love it. If I were to carry on the dignity of Shiba's work in my own way and put one of his novels on the screen, I thought, this was where to begin.

The <i>Burn, O Sword!</i> that was made into a television series was well thought of in some quarters, it seems, but I have not seen it. I have heard, though, that it was after seeing a filmed <i>Burn, O Sword!</i> and being appalled that Shiba stopped granting permission to adapt his full-length novels for the screen. Probably it means that none of the works filmed by Tōei, Shōchiku, and Daiei in the 1960s reflected the author's vision of the world. At the least, the ones I saw as a film-mad boy and a Shiba fan — <i>Castle of the Owl</i> (1963), <i>Warrior of the Wind</i> (1964), <i>Burn, O Sword!</i> (1966), <i>Magoichi the Bold</i> (1969) — were program-pictures without a particle of the original's dignity, its fragrance, its large-hearted hymn to humankind.

As it turned out, I came to the filming of <i>Burn, O Sword!</i> by way of <i>Sekigahara</i> (2017), and it was thanks to that detour that I met Okada Junichi; the finest roundabout road I could have taken, I think. On the set, Okada in the costume of Ishida Mitsunari and I would say to each other, "Burn, O Sword! next, then." To have brought off <i>Sekigahara</i>, which in my own mind I had thought impossible to film, with a wonderful cast and crew gave me great confidence besides.

In adapting it, I thought at first of a two-part film that would fold in <i>Shinsengumi Keppūroku</i> and Shimozawa Kan's "Shinsengumi trilogy" as well; but Tōhō, the producers, wanted a running time of about two hours and fifteen minutes, tracing Toshizō's footsteps from his birthplace at Hino in Tama district to his death in battle at Goryōkaku. Constraints of this kind are part and parcel of making films. To put it the other way round, so long as you hold to that, you have room to be free. <i>Burn, O Sword!</i> by itself is of considerable bulk, so met head-on it will not fit into a single film. A shift of conception is needed.

I read my way through books on Toshizō and the Shinsengumi from every possible angle. Out of that came the idea of carrying the story forward through the recollections of Toshizō after he has entered Hakodate (the person to whom he tells it is a pleasure I shall leave to the film). In this way Shiba's own "narration" in the novel could be kept alive, as Toshizō's words. Further, since the Shinsengumi could only have been born out of its entanglement with the Aizu domain, I worked in elements of Shiba's novella <i>The Guardian of the Royal Citadel</i>, whose hero is the Aizu lord Matsudaira Katamori. I should have liked to bring in Matsumoto Ryōjun as well, the doctor who struck up a friendship with Toshizō in <i>Dream of the Butterfly</i>, but I could not widen the frame that far. In the end it became a film of two hours and twenty-eight minutes.

The thing I researched exhaustively was the Ikedaya Incident of 1864. The novel spends a good many pages on this passage, describing the people involved in fine detail. There are points that differ from facts later established, but the line the novel takes — that there were twenty-seven or twenty-eight men at the Ikedaya, and that Yamazaki Susumu had gotten inside as a spy — I followed in the film as well. Yet from novel and historical fact alone the true shape of the Ikedaya Incident will not come into view. In the novel, at the same moment as Yamazaki's intelligence, word comes from a spy of the town magistrate's office that the "extremists" under Miyabe Teizō are gathering at the Shikokuya, and the corps divides into two parties. This is the "popular account" that a great many Shinsengumi films have taken up. The fact is that Kondō's party of ten made for the Ikedaya while the main body of several dozen, Toshizō among them, was searching the inns of Nawate-dōri one after another. Then why did they split into two? The answer I found was "Where is bin Laden?" The Shinsengumi's bin Laden was the Higo loyalist activist Miyabe Teizō. The arrest of Furutaka Shuntarō that touched off the Ikedaya Incident, too, came of a raid on Furutaka's shop, the Masuki, in the hunt for Miyabe. That night the extremist activists under Miyabe gathered at the Ikedaya to plot whether or not to rescue Furutaka. The film depicts this stretch in a documentary manner. For the Ikedaya and its surroundings we built a full-scale open set. The desperate fight of Kondō, Okita, Nagakura, and Tōdō — the forty minutes it took Toshizō's party to come to their aid — we reconstructed with painstaking rehearsal. For one of the charms of Shiba's work is the sheer conviction of its swordplay. Shichiri Kennosuke, Toshizō's worthy rival, who in the novel dies in the first half, fights in the film as the twenty-seventh man of the Ikedaya. The Ikedaya Incident, mixing the false and the true, we have made into one of the great set-pieces of the film.

Toshizō was a cold-hearted vice-commander of the Shinsengumi. He did cruel and merciless things. But there was a proper code in it. The comradely love with which he stood by Inoue Genzaburō, who was no great swordsman, right to the very end. Then the light banter with Okita. The deep friendship with Kondō. This is the first charm of <i>Burn, O Sword!</i> On these four I laid the foursome of Howard Hawks's <i>Rio Bravo</i> (1959).

For Kondō Isami, in point of physique and acting power, I had pictured Suzuki Ryōhei from early on. It was just after he had played Saigō in <i>Segodon</i>, so he came to the part having shed twenty-seven kilograms. He is John Wayne-ish, if you care to call him so. That said, if you ask whether Toshizō is therefore Dean Martin, he is not quite that, which makes it a delicate matter; but Okita and Gen-san are, entirely, Ricky Nelson and Walter Brennan.

Yamada Ryōsuke, whatever film he appears in, throws himself in with everything he has. He is quick and nimble, and his eyes are clear. That he is Okada's junior was a great plus, too. By training at Okada's dōjō he made himself into a perfect Okita Sōji. On set there was almost no need to direct his acting, for the breath he let out and the breath he drew in were Okita's. His wasting away in the latter half was superb as well. Since we did not shoot in sequence, the vigorous Okita and the failing Okita were set alternately in the schedule, so it must have been a punishing shoot for him.

For Gen-san we aged him beyond his real years, and had him played by the fine stage actor Takao Taka. An homage to old Walter Brennan.

Okada is a man born to play Toshizō. All the sword-choreography for Toshizō, the swordsmanship of a street brawler, I had him design as well. There is a sense in which the <i>Burn, O Sword!</i> project only got moving once Okada had reached the right age to play Toshizō. Toshizō's "cat-walk" before he goes up to Kyoto, too, he plays a little comically, in the manner of a Tama country bumpkin.

What must not be forgotten in <i>Burn, O Sword!</i> is the presence of Oyuki, the heroine and the woman Toshizō loves. She is a created character, but there are signs that Shiba overlaid on her the story of how he and his own wife first came together. One might well call her Shiba's own beloved. To the Oyuki played by Shibasaki Kō I gave a strength able to hold its own against the drama of the men.

The novel's premise — that she came from Edo to Kyoto in her husband's train so as to study the painting of the Shijō-Maruyama school — I have treated from the angle of what became of her after her husband's death: how she turned to "cruelty pictures," how her painting changed when she met Toshizō, and how she drew the current of the age toward herself. The painter's life that interests me most, for my part, is Goya's. He went to the battlefield, and as a war artist painted the very scenes of slaughter. Oyuki, I fancy, had something of that same spirit. It becomes "I want to paint Toshizō," and at the very last she goes all the way to Hakodate to see him. Shibasaki's bearing — the cool, upright strength of an independent woman, the keen and aching nuance of her presence — will be praised the world over, of that I am certain.

We took pains, too, over the home cooking with which Oyuki captivates Toshizō. I had the master of Kurita, the noted Kyoto-cuisine house I frequent, devise the dishes. The New Year's Day Kyoto fare at the Shimabara pleasure quarter, too, is Kurita's cooking, set out on the table. Feed the actors good things and get soulful playing out of them: that is the motto of the Harada company, after all (laughs).

The locations, centered on Kyoto, spread out to Shiga, Osaka, Nara, Wakayama, Hyōgo, and Okayama; but the hard thing was "the place where Oyuki is." Oyuki's house, the setting of her meeting with Toshizō, we were able to find at the Fukiya Furusato-mura in Takahashi City, Okayama. Saishō-an, where the two are first joined, being a space charged with deep feeling for Shiba, set the bar higher still. Even in using an old temple, I felt I wanted to avoid the places that turn up in film after film. What we chose was the Raikyū-ji, in that same Takahashi City. A garden made by Kobori Enshū makes a sumptuous backdrop.

Where does the root of Toshizō's thought lie? Not once did he utter the word "expel the barbarian." From whom, then, did he learn his reforming ideas? In the diary Toshizō left behind, a figure named Honda Kakuan comes up again and again: a physician, said to have taught Toshizō his reading and writing. He had ties to Watanabe Kazan and belonged to the party that favored opening the country. It is possible that Toshizō learned the ideas of opening the country at Kakuan's school. Under his elder brothers' influence Toshizō took to haiku, and left a collection of them. His verses are said to be second-rate, but one like "The white peony — I would have the moonlit nights, night upon moonlit night, dye it" I like enormously.

What I was conscious of, in the look of the thing, was not the late shogunate my grandfather taught me but the late shogunate seen through "the stranger's gaze." I took as reference the sketches left by the painter Heine, who came over with Commodore Perry, and by Wirgman, and by the French army officer Brunet, and the photographs of Beato, and worked them into a number of shots. My using Bizet for the music was in the spirit of Kurosawa Akira, who arranged and used Ravel's <i>Boléro</i> in <i>Rashomon</i>. Toshizō and Bizet were of one generation; both died in their thirties, their purposes half-accomplished.

According to the Japanese-history classroom at Harvard University, the three great turning points of Japanese history are the year 1600 (the Battle of Sekigahara), 1868 (the Meiji Restoration), and 1945 (the end of the war). That struck me as apt. What draws me is the "aesthetics of the loser" at these turning points. In order of period, they are Ishida Mitsunari, Hijikata Toshizō, and War Minister Anami Korechika. To have made <i>Japan's Longest Day</i> (2015), then <i>Sekigahara</i>, and at the last to have been able to take up <i>Burn, O Sword!</i>, which I had long kept warm, I count myself extraordinarily fortunate. I should like to hold a screening of the three together at Harvard (laughs).

I spoke with Shiba just once, by telephone. It was after I had shot my directorial debut, <i>Farewell to the Movie Friends</i>, so around 1980. I wanted so badly to film <i>Magoichi the Bold</i> that I called his home. And then the man himself suddenly answered. Flustered though I was, I went on pouring out all that I felt. "A lively fellow, aren't you," he said, but permission to film it he would not, in the end, give me. That I am able to tell a story like this at the close of <i>Burn, O Sword!</i> fills my breast with feelings past counting.

If I were next to film something of Shiba's, I should like to do a ninja story. Something that combined short stories like "The Last of the Iga Men" and "The Subcontract Ninja." Shiba once wrote that the work of the "ninja," transposed into our own age, is the work he himself had done, that of the "newspaper reporter": a member of a class-bound society and yet a man of action, independent and alone. An ultra-realistic ninja story, as a piece of social drama.

Nor am I done yet with the late shogunate. There is a great deal I still want to film. For it is an age in which each single person shone, an age that holds the Japanese I love best.

"Assassination alone I hate," Shiba writes in the afterword to his story collection <i>Bakumatsu</i>. He goes on: "And saying so, saying so, I have gone and written, for a whole year, over several hundred manuscript pages. […] History, at times, has thirsted for blood. Unwelcome though it is, both the assassin and the corpse that fell to his murdering hand are alike a part of our historical inheritance."

The Toshizō of <i>Burn, O Sword!</i> carries out assassination in the manner of a brawler, and raises the pitch of the carnage higher and higher. He was, indeed, a towering piece of our historical inheritance.

(Film director)

(The film <i>Burn, O Sword!</i> opens on 22 May 2020.)
