## The Sea Fight at Miyako Bay

The three ships, the Kaiten, the Banryū, and the Takao, were running south in a single file, in that order.

Toshizō had been all the while on the bridge of the flagship Kaiten.

On the twenty-second they put into a nameless port next to Kuji in Nanbu territory, which the local people called "Same."

All three ships had lowered the shogunal war-flag, the Rising Sun, and hoisted at the masthead the government's flag, the chrysanthemum-crest ensign. It was for fear of being reported by fishermen or passing ships.

"Hijikata, shall we put a scout of the land troops ashore?"

{j} asked the captain, Kōga Gengo. The scout was to feel out the movements of the government fleet in Miyako Bay.

"I will go."

{j} Saying so, Toshizō took only his page, Ichimura Tetsunosuke, and got into a cutter.

They landed at a fishing village called Same-mura and asked the local fishermen about the government's movements.

None of them knew a thing.

Disappointed, Toshizō became once more a man aboard ship.

"Kōga, there is no making out the government's doings."

If the search for the enemy did not go well, this surprise attack would fail.

"True — from Same-mura it may well be too far from Miyako Bay to learn anything. Hijikata, we weigh anchor. We set sail. Dawdle in a place like this and the enemy will get wind of us,"

{j} said Captain Kōga.

Just so, Toshizō nodded, and peered at the land-chart under Kōga's hand.

Between here and Miyako Bay there seemed to be no more fishing ports handy for reconnaissance.

Only, if one ran past Miyako Bay, there was a fishing port called Yamada, five ri south of that bay.

"Here will do. It is somewhat too close, granted; but a man of this Yamada village would surely know the state of Miyako Bay, five ri off."

"A fine plan. As you say, it carries the danger of being discovered, but a fight needs its gamble."

Kōga Gengo at once passed word to the captains of the other two ships, hove up the anchor, and presently began to steam out at low power.

Once outside the port he stopped his engines and shifted to sail.

Fortunately, the wind was fair astern.

The bridge was quiet.

Toshizō was a man of few words, and Kōga Gengo, too, was of a make that scarcely spoke but for what was needed.

(This man may be the finest talent in all Hakodate.)

{j} and Toshizō watched Kōga with a most kindly eye.

He was somewhat younger than Toshizō in years — thirty-one. He had ears as if pared down, and small eyes. In his small-made body there was nothing wasted; he was a man built as if his vital force had been pressed hard together.

(In build he is like Tōdō Heisuke, or Nagakura Shinpachi. In nature he may be like me.)

Kōga Gengo was, of course, a shogunal retainer. But, like almost all the officers of the Hakodate government, he was no hereditary hatamoto.

He was born the fourth son of Kōga Magodayū, a samurai of the Enshū Kakegawa domain. The distant forebear of this line came out of Kōga district in Ōmi province, famous for its ninja.

In Edo he studied navigation under the shogunal retainer Yatabori Keizō (later Kō, navy president at the end of the shogunate); afterward, under Arai Ikunosuke (navy commissioner of the Hakodate government), he translated Dutch books on higher mathematics and fleet maneuvers; and further, at Nagasaki, he served an apprenticeship in navigation in practice, and by this skill was raised into the shogunal service, holding such posts as instructor at the warship-drill institute and navy chief.

Kōga Gengo, too, seemed to bear Toshizō good will.

Toshizō's way of fighting set store by the search for the enemy. But since it meant dropping anchor at the coast with a warship each time to hunt for the foe in a fishing village, the movement grew ponderous and troublesome, and, for a navy, it was no easy preparation for battle.

Even so, Kōga assented meekly and lent his aid to this land-soldier's notion of scouting.

"Hijikata, at the Ikedaya, too, did you scout enough?"

{j} Kōga was eager to ask about that famous affair of the sixth month of Genji 1.

It was a fight in which a few of the Shinsengumi cut their way in and won a marvelous success.

"That was Kondō's exploit. I had charge of the house of Shikokuya Jūbee in Kiyamachi, and when I hurried to the Ikedaya afterward it was all but settled. But before that, the Ikedaya was searched thoroughly. For the searching we had, as the vice-commander's jokin in the reconnaissance line, Yamazaki Susumu—"

{j} just as he said it, the ship began to pitch heavily.

Toshizō looked out the window. The swell of the waves was rising. Whether it was from coming out onto the open sea.

"This Yamazaki,"

Toshizō kept looking out the window.

"was a master of searching-out. Disguised as a druggist, he lodged himself in the Ikedaya, drew close to the enemy and won their trust, and even carried in the trays at their drinking-party. As the room was cramped for the number gathered, the druggist Yamazaki said, 'I will keep your side-arms for you,' gathered up the long swords, and put them away in the closet of the next room. That the first blow of Kondō, who cut his way in with a mere five men, told so well was owing to this. To win, you need a stratagem; to lay a stratagem, your reconnaissance must be thorough. It is the common rule of a fight."

The ship's pitching grew violent.

The wind rose. Rain did not fall, but the clouds began to hang heavy out to sea, and even an untrained eye could see the weather was turning to something not to be taken lightly.

(On land this would be weather made for a night raid.)

Toshizō came down from the bridge, went to the side, and threw up in one heave all that he had eaten a while before.

As night came on the barometer began to fall fast, and the wind and sea grew fierce.

Up to then the Kaiten had been taking in her sails, stripping them one by one, but at last she shifted to steam.

She was running, throwing up black smoke.

At midnight the officer of the watch raised the alarm. The side-lamps of the following Banryū and Takao had gone from sight.

They woke Captain Kōga, who was catching a nap on the bridge.

Kōga did not fret.

"They are giving themselves up to the waves."

Their engine-power was not the Kaiten's.

Both ships, poor in engine-power, would find it, if anything, the more dangerous to run under their own steam in this wind and sea.

The Banryū and the Takao had likely stopped their boilers, dropped anchor, and taken the course of simply floating, bent only on sparing the ships from harm.

But even floating, in such wind and sea, so long as they did not err at the helm they ought to drift naturally on southward of themselves.

That night the Kaiten had the housing of her paddle-wheel on the side stove in by a beam sea.

With the dawn the wind fell.

"Gone,"

{j} Toshizō looked out the window and gave a little laugh. There was nothing for it but to laugh. The Banryū and the Takao, which ought to have been following, were nowhere in all that wide sea as far as the eye could reach.

(What an awkward thing a warship is.)

Presently the ship began to run toward land.

Under the morning-glow of the sky the scenery of Yamada Bay opened out before them.

There was a thing to astonish. At the mouth of the bay lay a warship throwing up dense black smoke, and coming closer, it was the Takao.

Being carried on the current had proved the quicker way. Of the Banryū there was no knowing the whereabouts.

The Kaiten and the Takao went into Yamada Bay.

Today the Kaiten flew an American flag at her masthead, the Takao a Russian.

"Hijikata──"

{j} Kōga Gengo on the bridge turned to Toshizō as if he had made some grave discovery, and pointed to a hill onshore.

"Rape-blossom fields."

The hills and fields had taken on a yellow so vivid it hurt the eyes.

Though in Hokkaidō the snow still lingered, here in the Nanbu territory of Ōshū there was already the feel of early summer.

Toshizō too narrowed his eyes. It brought a pang of longing. It was the feeling of returning to one's home country after long absence.

The ship, guarding against an enemy raid however unlikely, did not cast anchor, but lowered a cutter as she was.

The scouts were Frenchmen. As interpreters, two Japanese were sent with them. Calling themselves a foreign ship, it would not do for Toshizō and the other Japanese to go scouting.

This time the reconnaissance bore fruit.

Just as foreseen, the government fleet, they said, had put into Miyako Bay.

The Kōtetsu they were after was there too, it was said.

By the talk in Yamada village, the fishing villages along Miyako Bay were in high bustle over the untimely arrival of a fleet.

At once a council of war was held aboard the Kaiten, and it was resolved to attack before dawn the next day.

They would go at it with the two ships, the Kaiten and the Takao.

To wait for the Banryū to arrive would be to lose the chance of battle.

At two in the afternoon the two ships set out. But soon after they sailed, the Takao's engine, damaged by last night's wind and sea, fell off badly in speed.

She began repairs out on the water, but it was no use, and she was forced to drop out.

In the end the attack fell to the one ship, the Kaiten.

In Miyako Bay, meanwhile, the government's ironclad Kōtetsu, the Kasuga, the Yōshun, the Teibō No. 1, and the transports Hiryū, Hōan, Boshin, and Shinpū — eight ships — lay at anchor.

The land troops had gone ashore and were billeted about the fishing villages.

The ironclad, in the shape of an armored warrior crouching, sat still in the lee of an island. She was two-masted, her funnel shorter than an ordinary warship's. None of the warships was giving off smoke. Their boilers were not lit. At a moment's need one would have to begin by lighting the boilers, and a good deal of time would pass before she could move.

This day was the twenty-fourth of the third month. Before sunset almost all the naval officers had gone ashore. Night fell. At just about that time the raiding ship Kaiten put out her lights and floated at a single point on the water outside Miyako Bay, in the posture of an assassin holding his breath in the dark, making ready to burst in at first light next morning. The dark painted sea and ship alike pitch-black, and so the government fleet within the harbor did not notice.

No — there was a man among the government forces who had eyes as well.

He was not a naval officer. It was the staff officer commanding the army units, Kuroda Ryōsuke (a Satsuma samurai, the later Kuroda Kiyotaka; but for his drunkenness, a man rarely so able in politics and war as he was then).

Kuroda was quartered, with a headman's house in a coastal fishing village for his post, when that evening he caught a rumor drifting in from the Same-mura direction.

"What — they were flying the chrysanthemum-crest flag?"

{j} Kuroda pressed his subordinate.

"Yes, so the fishermen say. There were three warships, they say. Might they be government warships?"

"Fool — government warships, though the Heaven above and the earth below and the five continents be wide, are only those four ships within this harbor. Those others must surely be rebel ships."

They could not be left alone.

Kuroda Ryōsuke at once thrust his two swords into his belt, had a fishing boat put out, and called on the ironclad Kōtetsu floating in the harbor.

Aboard the Kōtetsu almost no officers remained.

The captain was not there either.

"Then Ishii will be here. Is he not?"

{j} Kuroda bellowed, seizing on a young third-class officer.

Ishii was the Hizen samurai Ishii Tominosuke. He held the post of fleet staff officer.

"Ashore."

"Is he off bedding some woman?"

"I would not know."

The Kōtetsu's crew and captain — the captain was the Chōshū samurai Nakajima Shirō, and the ship's officers mostly Hizen-Saga samurai, with men of other domains such as Uwajima mixed in — were, so to speak, a motley force, and in that respect without discipline. The martial temper, too, was slack. That, from the first, had grated on the army man Kuroda.

"Ishii — go and call Nakajima."

"Is an army staff officer giving orders?"

{j} the young third-class officer, in his Hizen accent, took offense.

The high-handedness of the Satsuma staff officer had, no doubt, stuck in his craw.

"You there — what is your name?"

"Kagaya Daizaburō, a Hizen-Saga samurai. I serve as a third-class officer of this ironclad Kōtetsu."

"I am Kuroda."

"I am aware of it."

"Then I ask you. Here a house is burning. Bring water, I say. Is that an order? It is no order, surely. Quick — run ashore and call Ishii and Nakajima."

(These Saga fellows are altogether too full of argument.)

Kuroda went into the captain's cabin.

Peering from the porthole, Kuroda knew that of them all only Satsuma's warship Kasuga, right before his eyes, had forbidden its men to go ashore.

(Ho.)

{j} and Kuroda, looking about the cabin, discovered a great flask holding two shō set upon a shelf.

Kuroda reached out, hugged the flask up, tipped it, and began to pour it into his mouth. Drink brought Kuroda several a misfortune in his life, and this time too, if one chose to count it a misfortune, it might, perhaps, be counted one.

There was a shō in it.

In a twinkling the drink passed from the great flask into Kuroda's belly.

About the time he finished drinking, footsteps sounded on the deck and presently stopped before the captain's cabin.

The door opened.

The navy staff officer Ishii and the captain Nakajima stared in amazement at Kuroda, who had trespassed without leave. Kuroda was drunk. Turning to face them, he

{j} "Does the navy not send out scouts?"

{j} he said.

This way of putting it was ill. From the first there had been a rift of feeling. It was not only the army-navy quarrel: Nakajima was a Chōshū man, and on that count too there was, since the end of the shogunate, a hatred toward Satsuma samurai past uprooting.

"I do not take your meaning."

"My meaning is plain enough. I ask whether the navy does not send out scouts."

"At times it does. You said, it seems, to this ship's Kagaya Daizaburō a while ago that there was a fire — where is this fire?"

"A fire, is it? Do you even know that enemy ships have come as far as Same?"

"Kuroda, this is Nanbu territory. The Nanbu domain, until only the other day, was in the league of Ōshū, and is a domain that carries the rebel taint still. The false report must have come drifting from that quarter."

"A false report?"

"Scouting may be important, but to judge good report from bad is the work of a good general."

"What?"

Kuroda kicked back his chair and stood up.

"Come, let us leave it,"

{j} said Ishii.

"You are not sober. You have been drinking. And that, moreover, is my drink."

The impromptu army-navy conference broke down at this. Kuroda had the weakness of having drunk off the other man's nightcap, and, unable to pound the table further, quit the ship.

The Kaiten lay hidden out on the dark water, like an assassin.

That night the captain, Kōga Gengo, had every one of the ship's guns loaded with shell.

After that Toshizō gathered the land troops and the crew on the pitch-dark after-deck and explained once more, over the method of the boarding-attack he had gone over so many times.

"Onto the enemy's deck we leap all together. Spring over piecemeal, and you will only be cut down."

The disposition was divided into five squads.

The one that displayed the finest point of the attack was the hatch squad.

This squad was to shut every hatch on the deck and guard them, sealing the crew asleep in the cabins below like fish in a can, so that they could not come up on deck. If it went well, by this alone the ship would be ours entire.

There would be a few enemy on deck. Those two squads would deal with.

The remaining squad's task was to seize the most fearful firearm the Kōtetsu carried on her deck.

Besides the ship's guns that fired on an enemy vessel, there was mounted on a carriage a new weapon for sweeping the enemy's deck — a field quick-firing gun, the Gatling gun.

This was a gun with six muzzles; work the mechanism at the breech, and small shells just twice the size of a Niel rifle-ball flew out at 180 rounds a minute.

"Hold this down, and the victory is ours,"

{j} said Toshizō.

After that there was a drinking-party for all hands in the cabin.

A whole sky of stars shone with a fierce light, and the sea was still as death.
