## Chapter 56

For a moment she did not know me. I said:

"Ah Mei, how come you to be here?"

Ah Mei buried her face on my arm and wept.

Helen came out from within, in a dress of blue-figured crêpe silk, a patent-leather belt at her waist, her long hair tied with a purple knot — just as I remembered her from the last time I saw her, without a trace of paint or powder. Seeing me, she was blank a moment, then let a smile show and came drifting over. I saw she wore today a pair of cork-soled high shoes, so that she seemed much taller. She put out her hand and shook mine, but at once helped me hold Ah Mei up. I saw the smile on her face had long since drawn in, and she would no longer look me in the eye.

We helped Ah Mei to the drawing room, where she sat and for a while could not check her sobbing. Helen told me Ah Mei had come that morning.

"So they've let you go?"

"Yes," said Helen.

"Did they ask you anything?"

"I said I knew nothing to it all," Ah Mei faltered.

"Did they ask about me too?"

"Yes, but I said you'd only come to our place, and that gentlemen callers came often, so how should I know anything about you." Ah Mei wiped her tears as she spoke.

"And so they let you go?"

"First they took me to the police station, and last night moved me to the Hongkou command headquarters; they pressed me, threatened me, beat me, but I said nothing all through. This morning they sent me back to the police station and let me go."

Then she told me slowly how the Japanese military had come to search and how she had been seized. She said it was about eleven in the morning, but that they had searched out nothing.

"Ah — those two cases in the inner room?…" I thought suddenly of the box-room upstairs in the tailor's shop.

"Yes — someone came and took those away a few days before," said Ah Mei. "Could it be that in them?…"

"I don't know either," I put in quickly. "And nothing was searched out of the drawers?"

"Only a few pieces of jewelry from the cabinet."

I nodded, and for a while was silent; Helen too sat mournfully silent. Just then I suddenly saw the cat under the chair — it was Jimi, its eyes wide, gazing at me as if for the moment it could not make me out. I called it:

"Jimi."

Jimi came quickly over, mewing, twining its soft body snake-like about my legs, and then sprang onto my knees.

Ah Mei suddenly wept out again, and asked:

"Is Miss Bai Ping truly dead?"

Grief stopped up my breast, a sourness rose in my nose, my eye-sockets felt heavy; I could not say a word, and nodded. I saw Helen's face already buried in her hands, and Ah Mei wept till she could not get her breath.

Stillness, and in the stillness only sobbing and sighing. When the air had softened a little, I stroked Jimi on my knees and began to think — since Ah Mei had come out of the police station, how had the cat come here? So I asked:

"When was Jimi brought over?"

"That was," Ah Mei faltered, wiping her tears with her handkerchief, "when you were leaving — Miss Bai Ping charged me that if she was not back by six o'clock I should bring a few things straight over here."

"Jimi?…"

"And that diamond ring," said Ah Mei.

"And her diary," said Helen.

"She said Jimi was to go to Mrs. Manfield, the diamond ring to Miss Helen, the diary to be left to Miss Mei Yingzi…" said Ah Mei.

"And," said Helen, rising and going to the table, and taking from a drawer an envelope, "a portrait — that's for you."

"A portrait." I pushed Jimi off and went and snatched it: quite right, inside was a portrait — the one I had drawn of her on the car back from Hangzhou, as she dozed. So she had kept this drawing all along. I gazed at it a long while, hoping for a few words on the back, but there were none.

Then Helen took a ring from the drawer and handed it to me, saying:

"This is the one she gave me."

My heart leapt up as if it boiled: this diamond ring was the very one I had first given her — no, the one we had exchanged. Could Bai Ping have had some further meaning in it? I turned it over a long while, and at last handed it back to Helen, and saw her put it straightaway on her finger; but my mind was still on the portrait in my hand, and I thought: did Bai Ping foreknow her own death? No — this was perhaps of the same meaning as when, before I went to work at Umetake's residence, she had me write a will; and now, indeed, she had put everything to use! None of us had a word; my heart ached in waves, and at last I set the portrait on the music-stand and asked:

"And the diary?"

"Mei Yingzi has taken it," said Helen mournfully.

"She's been here?"

"At half past eight," she said. "She told me everything, and told me too of your present plight; we've already got the room ready for you."

"Which is to say, I may not so much as read her diary."

"It was meant for Mei Yingzi alone," said Helen.

There was nothing more to be said between us; the heavy air pressed on our heavy hearts! I was like a ghost that had lost everything; I could think of nothing that still bound the world and me!

"Go and rest a while," said Helen. Then she set Bai Ping's portrait in her own photograph frame on the piano. And said:

"Go and rest a while in there." She went first, carrying the frame, and I followed her out, Jimi behind me. It turned out Helen had moved in with her mother and given her own room over to me. She went in first, set the frame by my bed, and drew the curtains for me.

"Rest well a while," she said, and went out, softly drawing the door to. In the room now were only Jimi and I — and Bai Ping's portrait in the frame by the bed. The portrait was very small, tucked in above Helen's own photograph, so that Bai Ping seemed to lie in Helen's arms, and Helen's smile seemed to soothe Bai Ping's sleep.

I threw myself on the bed and wept without restraint, until all the grief and strain and pain and sorrow of these two days had turned to weariness, and only then did I sink faintly into sleep.

When I woke, Mrs. Manfield had come home. She had met Mei Yingzi that morning, and so was not surprised at my coming; she waited on me kindly, comforted me, and charged me to go out but little, saying she could buy for me whatever I needed.

And so I stayed on at their house. Mrs. Manfield went out early and came home late, while I was all day with Helen and Ah Mei; save that we sighed together over Bai Ping, and that I sometimes longed keenly for Mei Yingzi to come and see me, life was calm and sweet.

On the one hand I was already getting my outfit ready for the journey; many things I entrusted to Mrs. Manfield to buy, and now and then I went out myself. I had to buy some cloth and go to a tailor's to have some Chinese clothes made; later I had the tailor come to my place for the cloth. On the other I was still having my inoculations, and when the clothes were made and the shots done, I could get a travel permit and set out.

But one afternoon the tailor delivered clothes, and I saw they were two sets of women's underthings and three cheongsams. I was much puzzled, but Helen put in quickly: "I'm a Chinese girl now."

This was a cheongsam of brown check on a yellow ground, very harmonious with her golden hair, and very well made too; Ah Mei, beside us, said: "Splendid!"

I too kept praising it, till the tailor there was quite delighted; and as he left, Helen gave him several more lengths of cloth.

From that day Helen wore a Chinese cheongsam every day. Her mother too was very pleased about it.

But two or three days on, it was a Saturday night. That day Mrs. Manfield came home rather early, and had prepared a very good dinner for us all to enjoy; after it everyone was merry, Ah Mei included. We had coffee and fruit, and chatted, listening to the lovely music on the wireless, until at eleven o'clock we all went off to sleep. It is my habit to sleep very late, and having gone to bed early, I was reading in bed as usual, when, at about twelve o'clock, I suddenly heard a faint sound of weeping. It came from Mrs. Manfield's room; at first it seemed to be Helen's voice, and now and then Mrs. Manfield's, and then Mrs. Manfield too wept. I first thought to get up and call Ah Mei, who slept in their drawing room; then I felt it would not do to disturb them, and so only lay uneasily in bed, until at two o'clock I heard them grow quiet.

Next day mother and daughter both wore a somewhat unnatural air; ordinarily Sunday was their happiest day, and early on they would go to church, but that day they rose very late, and no one said much. I did all I could to break the mood, but with no effect at all, and before nine o'clock at night they went to sleep.

But at eleven o'clock someone suddenly came and knocked at my door.

"Who is it?" I asked.

"It's me." It was Mrs. Manfield's voice.

"Please wait a moment." So saying, I threw on the dressing gown I had worn when Bai Ping shot and wounded me, got up, and opened the door for her.

Mrs. Manfield came in, shut the door behind her, and said softly:

"Forgive me! May I talk with you a while?"

"Of course," I said.

So she sat down on the single sofa, and, looking at me with a grave air, said gently:

"Young man, I have always been very fond of you, and thought very highly of you. And in our dealings, you have many times given us the noblest help." These words seemed long prepared, so that she spoke them like a speech: "And I understand a young person's feelings very well." She paused a while, and suddenly her voice grew very frail: "And I quite believe you and Helen would do well together; but I have only the one daughter now, and — setting aside the hopes I have of her in music — to have her cast me off and go to another world, this, this…" She suddenly broke off.

"Where does this come from?" I thought perhaps she meant Helen's plan of going to Beijing, and so comforted her: "If she goes, isn't it only for music? There's a good setting there, good teachers, and travel between the two places is easy enough; and the day she finds you a position there, won't you be together again?"

"But now you're not going with her."

"That can't be helped," I said. "Here I can't even show my face, can I?"

"And you mean to take her to the interior."

"Where did this come from?"

"Then it's she who means to go with you."

"I've heard nothing of it."

"But she's had Chinese clothes made, and had her inoculations too."

"I didn't know she'd had the shots; the Chinese clothes I always took to be a fancy of hers."

"Then you truly don't know she means to go with you?"

"I truly don't."

"You had no plan to take her?"

"I didn't so much as know of it."

"But now you know."

"But I will never take her, rest easy."

"And if she truly loves you so?"

"Rest easy, Mrs. Manfield," I said. "You're afraid I love her even more deeply than she loves me."

"You've not deceived me?" she said suddenly, in a voice holding back tears. "If you love each other, then when you come back you may marry her — I'll by no means object. Victory, I think, is not far off now."

"I don't deceive you — of course I'd not deceive you. I too have a mother; how should I take your daughter away behind your back?" I said. "And besides, I am one who holds to the single life."

"Then will you persuade her for me?"

"Of course, I must certainly persuade her. I'll persuade her to go to Beiping first, alone, and send for you after — not only for your sake, but for her gift and her music."

"Truly?"

"Of course."

"Then how wonderful!" She came over with tears, and lightly kissed my brow, saying: "Thank you."

"It is I who should thank you for everything," I said, an unspeakable heaviness wringing me. Mrs. Manfield made ready to go, and I said:

"Good night."

"Good night." At the door she smiled sweetly through her tears, and softly drew the door to. A gentle, loving mother's face vanished on the door — an impression that stays with me to this day, and will stay forever in my heart. It stands for the holy love of all the mothers of the whole world, of all mankind.

Next day, when Mrs. Manfield had gone out and Ah Mei was away, I began to say to Helen:

"Now, I'm nearly ready. But I hope you'll leave before I do."

"I?"

"Beiping! The sky there is blue, the air is still, the people are simple, the flowers everlasting — a pity I've not the luck to go."

"You mean I should go to Beiping by myself?"

"Of course, Helen. There's the setting you love there, and the teacher who hopes for you. You can study composition. You can awaken the gifts of many pupils, can create voices in them, and those voices will be the trumpet of freedom and peace for the whole world, the prelude to our victory."

"But you're not going with me."

"Of course, Helen; all the turns of things are beyond what you or I can imagine," I said. "Not until victory comes shall I have this chance again."

"And so, I won't hide it from you," said Helen: "I'm not going to Beiping — I've decided to go to the interior with you."

"But how can that be?" I said. "Your music, your mother, your brilliant future."

"Because," she said, bowing her head, "I — I need you at my side."

"It cannot be, Helen," I said. "That would only be the ruin of your future."

"My future?" Helen grew angry; she tossed her long golden hair, and in a voice sharp beyond all telling said: "My future is love, my life is love. I love music, but do not make music my career, because it is that I am loving; I love philosophy, but do not mean to study philosophy, because too it is that I am loving; even if I love vanity, it is only because I am loving — this 'love' is my purpose, is my future, my life."

"But love is an offering," I said. "What waits for your offering is music."

"All that I have and may have, I offer only to my own love."

"Then what a selfish philosophy is this?"

"Perhaps; but I can explain it only so!"

"But hadn't you already decided to go to Beiping?"

"And so had you."

"Yes, but I cannot now — you know that."

"My cannot is no different from yours."

"But is it only because I cannot go?"

"For me," said Helen, suddenly cast down, "to have no you to bid me live is like having no piano to bid me learn the piano."

"I am worth nothing," I said. "If I am so important to you, that is my glory; but in the interior I shall have no quiet setting to study philosophy, and you of course no setting to study music. We shall be rushing about and running risks, doing all the work I can do."

"This is all empty talk," she said. "The only question is whether you love me."

"Yes!" I said firmly. "But the love of one who holds to the single life — is that the love you mean?… It is forever of the spirit, and forever not to be fixed on one alone."

"That is the plainest word yet," she said. "But you mistake me if you think I want to marry you — that's wrong. What I want now is life, soul, music, the world, and so I need a love like yours. If I wanted marriage, that would be to bury myself, to want no life, no soul, no music, no world — I'd want only a husband, to live in a better house, eat better food, lead a more lavish life. And that, then, is not you."

It was long since Helen and I had talked so deeply; her view of life and the world was wholly beyond what I had looked for, and I had nothing to say. After a while I said:

"But the one who loves you most is your mother."

"But my life is my own."

"And there is your gift."

"And the gift belongs to me — not I to it." Just as I was at the end of my words and wit, with nothing to answer, I saw the morning paper come in through the slit of the letterbox. I went out to pick it up, opened it without thinking, and, reading as I walked to the sofa, was shaken to the core!

Here was that day's news:

MIYAMA YOSHIKO POISONED — CAUSE UNKNOWN — MURDERER UNDER INVESTIGATION. Special to this paper: The Japanese gentlewoman Miyama Yoshiko, niece of the head of the military's press bureau, having lately come here from Tokyo, had been much engaged in society; last night she attended a banquet at the Palace Hotel, and, on returning, died of poisoning. The steward, the cook, and the waiters of the Palace Hotel have all been summoned for questioning. Rumor for the moment runs wild, some saying it has to do with the Youheng Road bloodshed.
