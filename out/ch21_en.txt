Chapter 21. Cast into Prison Together
On December 2, 1942, I gave birth to a boy. In former years, because the Nanling range shields northern Guangdong from the cold air of the north, the climate there was warm even in winter; but that winter, for some reason, it was especially cold, and Qujiang even had a rare fall of snow. The house we lived in—the office of the Central Relief Commission’s special commissioner—had no brick walls built around it; it was a makeshift structure screened only with thin planks. The doors and windows all had cracks, through which the cold wind blew unceasingly into the rooms, and there was no fire lit within, so that to live there was much like living in an ice-cellar. Having lived a long time in Hong Kong, I was no longer used to such cold indoor air, and, weakened after childbirth, I ran a high fever of 40°C. Zhigao consulted with the hospital and engaged a special nurse, Miss Lu, to give home care and look after me, a new mother in illness.
On the twelfth, as usual, Zhigao went out early to his office and by nightfall had still not returned; I was in the bedroom chatting with Miss Lu. Suddenly three uninvited guests burst in; I heard them only shouting loudly outside the bedroom door that they wanted to see Madame Chen. Miss Lu hurried out to the sitting room and said to them: “Madame Chen is running a high fever and cannot receive visitors; please excuse her!” One of them then said in a loud voice: “We are from the Garrison Command, and we are here on orders to invite Madame Chen to go and talk.” And even as he spoke he pushed his way into the bedroom.
The moment I heard them arguing in the sitting room, I knew in my heart that something had changed, and at once I picked up the telephone at the head of the bed, meaning to call Wu Jufang, wife of Li Hanhun, chairman of the Guangdong provincial government. Only when I lifted the receiver did I find that the telephone line had already been cut, and I knew that trouble had indeed come. Once they had rushed in, they shouted loudly: “Our commander requests Madame Chen to go and talk!” I said: “Come now! Your commander only came this morning to visit me; what urgent, pressing business is there now that I must go and see him at once? Please excuse me—I am a sick woman running a high fever and cannot go. If there really is urgent business to discuss, when Commissioner Chen comes back I will tell him and will certainly have him go; how is that?” They said: “No need. Mr. Chen is already with us, waiting to have you brought over!” I said: “Very well; let us speak plainly and not in riddles—has Commissioner Chen already been arrested?” They said with a sinister grin: “Exactly so. We are acting under orders, and if we don’t bring you in we can’t report the job done. Be sensible—better come along with us!” I said: “If that is how it is, then send one of your men to go first with this Miss Lu, to hand over in person the relief commission’s official seal and its bank passbook for the public funds to Mr. Zhang Yilin, head of the cashier’s section at the office; when the handover of official business is done, I will go with you.” They felt this was proper attention to official duty, that the handover ought to be carried out, and could hardly object, and so they agreed. When Zhigao later learned of this, he greatly admired my calm, unflinching composure in the face of danger, and praised me as having something of the open, magnanimous bearing of Guan Yunchang taking his leave of Cao Cao, sealing up the gold and hanging up his seal.
The nurse Miss Lu at once volunteered: “Madame Chen has just given birth and is running a high fever. If there is no one to nurse her along the way, her life could be in danger. I must go with her to look after her at all times. Once her fever has broken and she has recovered enough to get out of bed and walk, I will go back to the hospital. This is the duty of a special nurse!” At first the men would not agree to her request, but then I heard the three of them discussing it quietly: “Those above say we’re to take her alive; if anything goes wrong on the road we couldn’t bear such a heavy responsibility—better agree to let her go along; in any case, on the way we’ll all be careful and keep a strict watch.” And so at last they signified their consent. This was no case of robbers turning kind-hearted; rather, I owe thanks to Miss Lu’s lofty spirit—faithful to her duty, brave in a just cause, standing by another in adversity—which let me, ill as I was, receive from her such devoted, responsible care and rescue, and so keep my life. Without more ado the men wrapped my body in an eiderdown quilt from the bed, bound it with rope on the outside, laid a towel over my head to keep off the wind, and carried me out of the house and down the hill on a bamboo bed. Miss Lu, hastily gathering a few necessities for a new mother and an invalid, followed close behind the whole way.
This carrying took me straight to the Qujiang railway station. Opening my eyes and looking, I saw Zhigao already sitting there in silence. The two of us looked at each other and said nothing, knowing in our hearts that things had come to this and that talk was of no use. That evening we were put aboard a special carriage coupled onto the train, bound for Hengyang in Hunan. Although Hengyang is separated from Qujiang only by the single barrier of the Nanling range, its climate is much colder. When we got off the train, great snow was whirling down all across the sky. They hired two sedan chairs, seating me and Zhigao separately, while Miss Lu and the escorting soldiers all followed on foot. Whether it was that I enjoyed the reflected glory of Zhigao, His Honor the “Commissioner,” or that he enjoyed the reflected glory of me, a sick woman just out of childbed, at any rate to let prisoners under escort ride in sedan chairs was truly a rare thing. Passersby on the road even took it for the welcoming of some high official.
The party went perhaps forty or fifty li, and delivered us into a secret detention house that the Military Statistics agents had set up outside the city of Hengyang. At the gate they told me to get down from the chair, and I shouted loudly: “Sitting in the chair my feet have frozen stiff; I can’t even stand up, so how am I to walk?” Having no choice, they had to carry me into the prison on a rattan chair. The head of the detention house was a man surnamed Xu, short in stature, who was very courteous to us. The moment I went in I cried out: “I’m freezing to death—bring a fire, quick!” And he really did send in a brazier for warmth very quickly.
Why did we receive such favorable treatment in prison? As the saying goes, there is no story without a coincidence: at that time the military police and secret agents of the Hengyang region came under the “Loyal and Patriotic National Salvation Army,” and the commander of the “Loyal Salvation Army,” Zhou Kuilong, was a graduate of the first class of the Whampoa Military Academy, senior in standing even to Dai Li (who was of the sixth Whampoa class, and had dropped out partway through besides). He was blind in one eye, and people called him “One-Eyed Dragon.” In the early part of the war he had once led men secretly into the Shanghai concessions to buy up munitions, and had been caught and locked up by the concession police. His attendants and subordinates, hearing the concession police say that the great lawyer Chen Zhigao was very willing to help those engaged in anti-Japanese work, came to Zhigao for aid. Zhigao not only found a way to bail him out but also gave him a refugee identity card that would let him pass freely all along the road, and three hundred dollars for travel, so that he was able to leave Shanghai safely and return to his unit. From then on Zhou regarded Zhigao as a great benefactor. When he saw the order for the secret arrest of Zhigao and me, he at once wired Dai Li to say that he would take it on himself to guarantee us and keep us under guard on the spot at Hengyang—might we not be sent under escort to Chongqing? Dai Li wired back that the Chen case was a matter of great weight and that the “higher authorities” would not approve Zhou’s request. Having no choice, Zhou then showed Zhigao all the telegrams that had passed back and forth, indicating that he had done his utmost but that the orders from above were hard to defy and there was nothing he could do; only, in the matter of our living conditions there, he naturally had the authority to give us every favorable care he could.
In this way, though the two of us were held apart in the Hengyang prison, at mealtimes we could eat together, and the food provided was very good, so that we suffered no real hardship. Because we could meet at all three meals every day, we also had the chance to “coordinate our testimony” and unify our story. I reckoned that the great uproar over Yang Huimin’s affair, the sensation of the hour, was very likely a smokescreen deliberately laid by the Military Statistics Bureau; their main aim was still to investigate my connections, to make clear my true identity, to see just what relations I had with the Communist Party, and whether I was a secret underground worker of the Chinese Communists. Because, when I had gone to Zengjiayan in Chongqing to have an audience with Comrade Zhou Enlai and report on the situation, I had ridden in a car of the Eighth Route Army’s Chongqing Office, and around Zengjiayan Kuomintang agents kept close watch day and night on those going in and out, I had very likely thereby fallen onto the agents’ blacklist of “suspicious elements.”
I quietly said to Zhigao: “As for my having gone to Zengjiayan, I can say that, longing for the orphan of the Wan family, I went specially to Zengjiayan to ask the Chinese Communist side about the whereabouts and recent circumstances of Wan Changjie, the elder son I bore to my former husband Wan Xiyan. You need only say that you did not know I had gone to the ‘Eighth Route Office’ at Zengjiayan, letting them think that I had kept even this from you—that will do. As for the anti-Japanese salvation work carried on in Shanghai and Hong Kong, I did it together with He Xiangning, a left-wing elder of the Kuomintang; patriotism is no crime, and they can do nothing to me.” As for the work at the Qujiang relief commission, when I conveyed Enlai’s instructions and the Central Committee’s commendation to Comrade Zhang Yilin, my eldest younger brother Bopei, and the others, I had made a point of charging them that on no account must they reveal to outsiders any relation between us and the Chinese Communist side; they could only say that Zhigao had sent me to Chongqing because he wished to resign and go to the Bank of Communications. Chairmen Xu Shiying and Qu Yingguang of the Central Relief Commission had greatly praised our work at Qujiang, and so both pressed Zhigao to stay and would not agree to his resigning.
So it was that after our arrest, when the Military Statistics agents went to interrogate Zhang Yilin (head of the cashier’s section) and my eldest younger brother’s wife Zhang Haoran (head of the accounting section) and to examine Zhigao’s accounts, their story was uniform: they all said that every item of expenditure in the accounts of the relief commission’s Qujiang office had been strictly in accordance with the rules and regulations, approved and disbursed by the special commissioner in person—to whomever the special commissioner approved payment, they paid it—and that they had never known anything of the special commissioner using the relief commission’s money to aid the Communists’ East River guerrillas…. Because they kept their mouths shut as tight as a sealed bottle, the agents questioned them for a long time but could not turn up any handle of “communicating with the Communists.”
After being held in especially favorable confinement at Hengyang for six weeks—during which the prison sent someone each week to go into the city with Miss Lu to buy medicine for a new mother and nourishing foods such as eggs and brown sugar—thanks to Miss Lu’s careful nursing my body recovered quickly, so that I could already get out of bed and walk and see to myself. Then we were sent on again under escort, taking the train to Guilin, there to change to a plane and be taken to Chongqing for trial.
On the train I happened to meet Zhang Fusheng, the young man who had once been a steward with my father on the Zhuzhou–Pingxiang Railway and who had now risen to be a conductor. At first sight we each recognized the other, but I signaled him with my eyes and pretended we did not know each other, so as not to draw the notice of the escorting soldiers. In the evening, when he brought in tea, I took advantage of a moment when the soldiers were not beside me to signal with my hand and ask him for paper and pencil. He at once brought a sheet of white paper and a pencil. Writing as if drafting a telegram, I put down a few short phrases: “Sixth Brother and I, taken suddenly ill, will reach Chongqing in a few days; hoping you and Ninth Brother and Boss Cai will together arrange for a doctor to treat us.” Below I signed it “Sixth Sister-in-law Shuyi.” On the paper I also wrote out a request that Zhang Fusheng help by adding an envelope and sending it by airmail to Mr. Li Huanzhi at Qingmuguan, Chongqing, Sichuan, at the Ministry of Education (Zhigao’s seventh sister’s husband). And so at last the Military Statistics agents’ blackout on news of our secret arrest was broken through. Before long Comrade Tian Han, too, published an article in a Guilin newspaper openly demanding of the Kuomintang authorities: the patriots Chen Zhigao and Huang Dinghui have suddenly vanished—where on earth have they gone? Dai Li was the one most enraged at having the base doings of the Military Statistics agents—secretly arresting and imprisoning public figures—exposed. Learning that our arrest had been openly laid bare and had become a political storm that many in society were watching, he was of course furious; but the fire could no longer be wrapped in paper, and there was no more hiding or covering it up.
Miss Lu accompanied us all the way to Guilin, and only at the airfield, when she had seen us onto the plane, did she reluctantly bid us farewell and return to Qujiang. With hot tears brimming in my eyes I gripped both her hands and said that I would never forget her lofty medical virtue in acting bravely for a just cause, that she had after all snatched my life back from the hand of the god of death—such great benevolence, great courage, great kindness, and great virtue, beyond what an ordinary person could do, how could a single word of “thanks” ever express it? I also wrote a note asking her to hand to Comrade Zhang Yilin and my eldest younger brother and his wife, telling them to pay Miss Lu forty-two days’ double wages; and I said to her: “Your kindness cannot be repaid by this trifling wage. Your sense of justice and your noble friendship I will forever hold in grateful memory. I only hope there may yet be a day when we meet again.” It is only a pity that from that parting to this day, I have never had the chance to see her again.
After we were taken under escort to Chongqing, we were held in secret at the Military Statistics agents’ Wanglongmen detention house. During our confinement at Wanglongmen, Zhigao had the good fortune to be a fellow prisoner with General Ye Ting. General Ye Ting had once, on the battlefield, carried the wounded Chiang Kai-shek down a mountain on his back, and Chiang had someone hint that he hoped Ye Ting would come over to his side. Ye told the man that if he were spared and released, the moment he stepped out of the prison gate he would rush straight back to the “Eighth Route Office” at Zengjiayan to report for duty. General Ye Ting’s firm revolutionary will and his brave, unyielding fighting spirit deeply instructed Zhigao.
Cai Shuhou was a man of boundless resource, and was on good terms with Wang Xinheng, a backbone member of the Military Statistics Bureau. At Cai Shuhou’s request, Wang once came to the Wanglongmen detention house to visit me, and gave me a copy of the Diamond Sutra and ten notebooks of yellow paper, together with a writing brush, cinnabar ink, a small inkstone, and other stationery, charging me to copy the sutra with a calm mind and by no means to be impatient, and saying that friends outside were working to rescue us. He also gave instructions to a Shandong man named Wang Yukun in the detention house, arranging specially for him to bring us our meals, to pass us novels (prisoners were allowed to read non-political fiction), and to carry our correspondence outside. The letters he took to Luo Jianbing’s house, whence they were passed on to the persons concerned. On novels such as The Romance of Qin and Han, Zhigao would mark certain words and phrases with a pencil, in circles or crosses, and pass them to me, whereupon, by stringing the marked words and phrases together, they combined into a message—this was our secret method of correspondence in prison.
The content of the testimony that Zhigao and I secretly coordinated in prison, besides the reason we had already agreed on at Hengyang for going to the “Eighth Route Office” at Zengjiayan in Chongqing, also anticipated that the enemy might press us about such matters as my relations in Shanghai with the “Wind and Rain Bookroom” and the Daily Translation Herald. The Daily Translation Herald was openly published and circulated, and my having funded it in my capacity as managing director and deputy general manager of the Tongyi Company was a thing known to all and impossible to deny; I could only answer in its defense that it was supporting anti-Japanese salvation propaganda, and that patriotism is no crime. But the “Wind and Rain Bookroom” had never been made public, and if asked about it I could only firmly answer, “I don’t know.” As for the loss of the Hu Die couple’s “luggage,” that was the fuse of our arrest, and could not but be defended against; out of a strategy of not making too many enemies, one might answer: we consider that Hu Die herself was indeed a victim in this affair, but that it was an accidental loss for which no one could bear responsibility. We were staff of the relief commission, taking on the task of receiving and escorting people of every circle in their evacuation from Hong Kong to the great rear; we were not a fee-charging insurance company—whence should come any responsibility to make good the loss?
As for my true identity: from the very first, when I was charged with white-area work, the Party organization had given instructions that, if I should ever be arrested, I was to insist that I had joined the Chinese Communists during the first Kuomintang–Communist cooperation of the Northern Expedition and the National Revolution, but had since left the Party and lost all contact. This would cut off the enemy’s inquiries into the leadership I answered to, above and below and on every side, and into my organizational relations. And the Chen family’s younger brothers and sisters and relatives and friends truly did not know that I was an underground Communist. Zhigao thought this analysis and defense of mine reasonable and sound. He was a star pupil of the great lawyer Shen Junru, and in coping with the enemy’s interrogation naturally had more assurance than I; and so I too firmly believed that he could surely withstand the test of the enemy’s questioning.
At the interrogation, our coordinated testimony did indeed prove very effective. The agents first spoke out: “We have long known that on that day you went to the ‘Eighth Route Office’ at Zengjiayan; we have noted down the number of the ‘Eighth Route Office’ car you rode in, and we reckon you won’t deny it. You must confess frankly: why did you go to Zengjiayan? What instructions did Zhou Enlai give you, what new task did he assign you?” Calmly I answered according to the testimony I had long since prepared, saying that it was to inquire after the circumstances of the orphan of my late husband Wan Xiyan, and that I had gone without telling Chen Zhigao. Of course they did not believe it could be so simple, but there was no help for it: I set my teeth and firmly would not tell the truth, and after questioning me half the day they could get nothing else out of me. So they suddenly shifted the drift of their talk and said: “In the editorial offices of the Shanghai Daily Translation Herald that you supported, quite a few Chinese Communists were harbored, which is why that paper was forever printing the war reports of the Eighth Route Army and the New Fourth Army—that is propaganda for the ‘alien party.’ Is that not a crime?” I answered: “A newspaper is the mouthpiece of the people. Patriotism is no crime, and of the state of the fighting at the anti-Japanese front it is only proper to record all one hears and tell the broad mass of readers, to lift the fighting spirit of the resistance. Of General Xie Jinyuan leading the Eight Hundred Heroes in a bloody, isolated stand against Japan at the Sihang Warehouse in Shanghai, and of the great victory at Tai’erzhuang on the Shandong battlefield, every newspaper reported and publicized at great length! Why then, when the Eighth Route Army dealt the Japanese invaders a heavy blow at Pingxingguan, should that not be reported and publicized? Is this not the period of the second Kuomintang–Communist cooperation and united resistance—what crime is there in our doing so? As for your saying there were Chinese Communists in the paper’s editorial office, they wore no Communist badge on their faces, so how was I to know?” With another sinister grin they changed the subject: “Very well—you really are capable, actually managing to trick Mr. Du Yuesheng’s money into investing to support you.” I said: “Mr. Du is the president of the Local Association, and that money was the Local Association’s, not Mr. Du’s own. To mobilize him to invest in support of anti-Japanese propaganda work is a patriotic act—what crime is there in it? Not only is Mr. Du guiltless; we too are guiltless. That the Daily Translation Herald carried news of victories won at China’s anti-Japanese front not only heartened people at home; even our British, American, and French foreign friends, who together with us were resisting the fascist war of aggression, were glad of it—the only ones displeased were the Japanese invaders and the traitors who sold out the country. How can you say I am guilty?” In shame turned to anger they upbraided me: “You still dare to quibble! Japanese intelligence says that you are Chinese Communist agents disguised as patriots to steal intelligence.” I could not help laughing and retorting: “Is this not a joke? You are not traitors who sell out the country—how can you so believe the Japanese when they wrong patriots?” Without a change of color I spoke on with ease and fluency, refuting them until they were struck dumb and could scarcely find a way to climb down, and at the last could only roar at me: “Don’t be so stubborn-tongued—we will investigate this thoroughly….” And with that the first interrogation came to a hasty close.
I was confined in a room on the second floor of the secret Wanglongmen detention house, from which I could gaze out at the scenery on both banks of the Jialing River—though at such a time I naturally had no such leisurely, refined mood to enjoy the view. In my idle time I copied the Diamond Sutra in the yellow-paper notebooks Wang Xinheng had given me, and doggedly copied out all ten of them. Here the Military Statistics agents trumped up charges, and, killing without batting an eye, secretly arrested and murdered many patriots and even innocent common people; in the dead of night one would often hear the gunshots of their killings, and there is no knowing how many underground workers met their deaths here. Placed in such a den of demons, one set aside all thought of one’s own life or death, safety or danger.
During my time in the Wanglongmen detention house there is another small matter that I may as well mention in passing. Once, that Shandong man Wang Yukun brought me a packet of pickled kohlrabi, with a little note written in pencil enclosed inside, signed “the elder brother of Ge Xiuluan.” The note said only: “Once a prisoner at the foot of your dais, I cannot forget the kindness of my release; take good care of yourself—good news before many days!” I thought for a while before I recalled that Ge Xiuluan had been a schoolmate of my second younger sister when she studied at No. 2 Middle School in Hankou. After the Northern Expedition army took Wuhan, the people’s tribunal publicly tried the war criminals who had colluded with the Northern Warlords, and members of the Ge family, being related by marriage to the warlord Wang Zhanyuan, were arrested and tried. At the time, Liu Bochui was the presiding chief justice, and I had been sent by Zhang Yun to sit as an assessor representing the Women’s Department. Big Sister Zhang had charged me that, in accordance with Party policy, we were to be severe with the ringleaders but not to call the coerced followers to account, not to widen the net, and not to implicate the innocent. So in the questioning, for those who had merely been connected by marriage and had taken no part in the crimes committed in the war, once the true circumstances were made clear, we all found them not guilty and released them on bail. Only later did I learn that this “elder brother of Ge Xiuluan,” whom we had that year found not guilty and released, was by then already an inside-duty worker of the Military Statistics Bureau, and, hearing that we were about to be transferred under escort to the military prison and so would escape the calamity of secret murder, had written the note specially to tip me off out of gratitude. Truly, in life there is no place where one may not meet again! My second sister and her husband were then far away in America, and afterward often regretted that they had been unable to take part in the work of rescuing us. I mention this matter not to win glory for my second sister, but to show how far-reaching are the political effects of correctly carrying out the Party’s policy.
Later my Ninth Brother, Chen Shijun, told me that when he learned we had fallen into Dai Li’s hands—an ill omen with little hope of good—he at once made use of his special relationship as a family doctor also serving in the household of Bai Chongxi, and went to beg Bai to reach out a hand and save us. Bai said coldly: “I can’t help you in this. Your sister-in-law is a famous Communist—did you know that?” Shijun said: “We know only that she is ardent for anti-Japanese salvation work; no one in the whole family knows she is a Communist. I beg you, do save my brother and sister-in-law.” And in his desperation he knelt down. Bai, displeased, flung his sleeve and made to leave. Shijun stubbornly refused to rise, and knelt a whole day through. Perhaps this spirit of Shen Baoxu weeping at the Qin court moved Bai; and besides, there had all along been friction between Bai and Dai, and Bai agreed to find a way to have us transferred from the hands of the Military Statistics Bureau to the military side. On the other hand, because the news had already leaked out, Chiang Kai-shek, cowed by the pressure of public opinion, had no choice but to give written instructions that we political prisoners, held in secret, be transferred to the Ministry of National Defense’s military prison for open trial. Only thus were we able to turn danger into safety.
When we were transferred from the Wanglongmen detention house to the Ministry of National Defense’s military prison, because not one of us “prisoners” had bowed to the enemy, and the enemy, awed by our air of upright integrity, was worried besides that something might go wrong on the road, there were military-police vehicles guarding us front and rear, under strict watch. I sat between Wang Yukun and the warden Li, both my hands cuffed by two pairs of handcuffs to theirs; yet in my heart I breathed a sigh of relief at escaping this dark den of secret murder, and grew all the calmer and steadier.
In order to rescue the patriots and revolutionary comrades held in secret confinement, General Ye Ting among them, the Party Central Committee, on the one hand, through Dong Biwu and Deng Yingchao and others, launched a motion at the National Political Council opposing the secret imprisonment of patriots by the agents; and on the other hand ordered Liu Shaowen, together with Cai Shuhou, Li Nanxiang, and others, to work jointly to carry out the rescue. Our two families, the Chens and the Huangs, and their younger brothers and sisters in Chongqing, also took the home of Luo Jianbing, my comrade-in-arms from the Northern Expedition, as a liaison point, and each through various channels sought to rescue us. Zhigao’s brother Chen Shijun was the medical officer of the Guangxi-clique generals Li Zongren and Bai Chongxi, with the rank of major general; when he came to the military prison to visit, I said to him in a loud voice: “Ninth Brother, my health is still good, you needn’t worry about me. In this military prison there are no single cells for women prisoners, but for the high officials among the men there are single favored-treatment cells; you need only find a way to have your brother moved into a favored-treatment cell, and that will do!” Chen Shijun, much grieved, said only one sentence: “Sister-in-law, set your mind at rest, and take care of yourself!” In less than a week Zhigao was indeed transferred into a favored-treatment cell.
I was shut up in an ordinary women’s cell. This was a large room made of two interconnecting chambers, holding sixteen women prisoners in all; apart from one who was a public functionary of some office, most were illiterate, all serving on wrongful charges, with no political prisoners among them. At night the cell was lit by a kerosene lamp, smoky and choking. What was especially hard to bear was that two very large communal chamber pots were set in the room, one for stool, with a lid, and one for urine, without even a lid—an evil stench hard to endure. Among the women prisoners was an old, sick woman of fifty-odd who coughed the whole night without cease, which kept me the more from sleeping the night through. After my Ninth Brother Chen Shijun came to visit, he asked our third-branch cousin Chen Youdan, who lived nearby, to bring me something once a week—among it sweetened fried rice-flour and raw eggs and such foods—and to take away my dirty changed clothes, have them washed clean, and bring them back the next time for me to change into. It was hard on him, lame in one foot as he was, to bustle back and forth without complaint, tending to me warmly, with deep feeling and kindness—it moved one. The eggs he brought I gave to that old sick woman to eat; as the saying goes, medicine mends less well than food, and, her nourishment improved and her resistance strengthened, her night cough actually eased greatly, so that everyone could thereby sleep in peace.
On inquiry, I learned that the old sick woman and her son, the two of them, had made their living by rowing goods on a boat; once, meeting a storm while transporting military grain, their boat had capsized and sunk in the river, and the military grain had all gone to the river bottom—though mother and son were saved and survived, being unable to pay for the lost military grain they had been seized and imprisoned, already for five long years. Besides writing a defense for myself, I also helped that old sick woman write a plea, pointing out that the capsizing of the boat and the sinking of the grain were wholly an irresistible natural disaster that no human power could have prevented, and stressing the bitterness of her plight—poor and sick at once, with no means to pay compensation. Learning too that she had a cousin who kept a grain shop in Chongqing, I sent out a letter to inform him. After the cousin came to visit, he found a guarantor, and had mother and son released from prison. After she got out, she even had someone bring me a packet of fried rice-flour as a token of her thanks.
After Zhigao (of a rank equivalent to major general) was transferred to a favored-treatment cell, his food was very ample, and at every meal he divided out half of his rice and dishes and had them sent to me, to show that we shared sweet and bitter alike. Because of my stomach trouble I could not eat much, and often shared out the dishes among the sick fellow-sufferers in my cell to give them a little “feast” and some added nourishment. I not only wrote pleas for that old sick woman to cry out against injustice; for the other women prisoners too, after questioning them clearly about the circumstances of their arrest, I lodged appeals on their behalf, so that each was tried openly and in accordance with the law and at last had her day of deliverance, to everyone’s great joy. Knowing that I was weak after childbirth, they took it on themselves, one after another, to do the rotating sanitation duties for me—sweeping the floor, emptying the chamber pots, and the rest. I had no thought of doing them a kindness in hope of return; it was only out of a sense of justice and a nature that grieves at others’ griefs, is anxious at others’ anxieties, and loves to right a wrong—yet unexpectedly I won my fellow-sufferers’ spontaneous care, mutual help in adversity indeed.
In the Tuqiao military prison, the court interrogated us only once, in a place set on the opera-stage of a Guandi temple two or three li from the prison. Zhigao and I were each escorted to the trial under guard by four soldiers with loaded rifles, kept a certain distance apart and unable to speak to each other, but with no instruments of torture on us—which counted as fairly courteous, favored treatment. The interrogators were three young men, mild in manner; after they had looked through the great heap of file materials compiled by the Military Statistics agents, they proceeded to question us. I answered fluently according to the testimony I had long prepared, without the least discrepancy from my earlier statements. After the interrogators had heard my testimony, they raised no new questions.
This Tuqiao interrogation was basically a copy of the Wanglongmen one. Feeling that their record could not fully express my meaning, I wrote out a defense openly and lawfully in prison. It first recounted my own taking part in those years in the Northern Expedition and the National Revolution and my having joined the Chinese Communists, whereby I came to be joined in love with the martyr Wan Xiyan, who soon after unhappily lost his life, leaving an orphan yet living today; and that I had already left the Party and had long since lost contact with the Chinese Communists. My going to the “Eighth Route Office” at Zengjiayan in Chongqing the year before had been solely to inquire after the whereabouts and recent circumstances of the orphan, with no other intent. It passed over entirely that marriage bond with the martyr He Chang, and dwelt instead on the family background of Chen Zhigao’s mother—deeply understanding of right principle, skilled in raising her sons, generous in making friends, and a supporter of the resistance. It set down the grand occasion of her fiftieth birthday, when the celebrated figures of every circle in Shanghai, headed by the Kuomintang elder Hu Nanhu (styled Egong), presented her with a screen in celebration; and how she later died of illness in Hong Kong, honored to the utmost after death, with not a few Kuomintang notables coming to offer condolences, and how I had written a lament mourning Old Madame Chen, published in the Hong Kong National Daily, and so on. In the defense I did not denounce how the Military Statistics agents perversely acted against the Communists; I only made clear that, situated now in the period of the second Kuomintang–Communist cooperation and united resistance, my own standpoint was that, setting aside the differences of view between the parties, I firmly supported and took part in anti-Japanese salvation work, and that therefore I would never oppose the Communists. To this day I remember well that in the defense I wrote an epigram: “If I were to oppose the Communists, then in years to come, when the orphan is grown, with what face should I meet him? If to ally with the Communists be a crime, then I beg the extreme penalty, that I may follow my late husband below the earth—dead, yet living still. For the three armies may be robbed of their commander, but a common man cannot be robbed of his will….” What I set forth in circumstances and in attitude tallied entirely with my testimony.
During our confinement in the military prison, Luo Jianbing asked to come and visit, but did not obtain the authorities’ approval; she could only have someone bring in ten dollars and a good number of raw eggs, with a message charging me to await good news in peace and to look forward to our reunion after release. She also had Chen Shijun copy out the defense I had written under interrogation in prison, to publicize it in society and win the broad sympathy and support of every circle. Her comrade’s warmth and care for me in adversity was an unforgettable friendship.
After my release Zhigao told me that the defense I had written in prison, setting forth Old Madame’s patriotism and the great virtue of the whole family, had spread beyond the prison walls, and that the Chen family’s younger brothers and sisters had all been deeply moved on reading it; that Chen Xunshe, the Hong Kong head of the Central Daily News, had even cut out the article I had written mourning Old Madame Chen in the Hong Kong National Daily and sent it to his cousin, Chiang Kai-shek’s trusted aide and counselor Mr. Chen Bulei, advising him that to kill people of this sort would be bad for the political effect. He said too: “How many upright patriots did your impassioned words and just defense in prison win the sympathy and respect of! The Chinese Communist councillors Dong Biwu and Deng Yingchao launched at the National Political Council a motion opposing the secret imprisonment and persecution of patriots, which won not a few seconders besides, putting Chiang Kai-shek and the Kuomintang authorities in a sorry plight; cowed by the pressure of public opinion, the danger of secret murder was removed, and political inducement to surrender did not work either. Your brave spirit in willing to bear the ‘guilt’ alone and to face death as a homecoming won people’s admiration. Even the Du Yuesheng couple and the Wang Xinheng couple and others went up the mountain in person to see Dai Li face to face and explain that we were not members of the secret societies, but that their Shanghai Local Association and we husband and wife cooperated in the resistance and got on extremely well; and that we, in the name of the Central Relief Commission at Qujiang, had sent people to Hong Kong to rescue in secret and escort many well-known patriots back to the great rear to take part in anti-Japanese work, and had helped Shanghai banks rush goods back into the country to support the resistance, and other great contributions. Kuomintang notables such as Chairman Xu Shiying of the Central Relief Commission and Li Hanhun, chairman of the Guangdong provincial government, also came forward to testify that we had merit and no guilt. Later I learned that Ye Nan, descendant of Lin Gengbai’s lifelong friend Ye Chucang, then head of the Second Bureau of the Ministry of National Defense, together with his wife Yuan Xiaoyuan, had also done much to rescue us. So the favored treatment we received in the Ministry of National Defense’s military prison was the result of the joint efforts of all the various parties named above.” At first the Military Statistics side had meant to sentence Zhigao to seven years’ imprisonment on the groundless, trumped-up “charge” of “misappropriating public funds.” My brother-in-law Shijun, fearing that in prison the nights were long and dreams many and the outcome hard to foresee, had once meant to signify agreement, so as to secure our safety; but once we had been transferred to the Ministry of National Defense’s military prison, knowing that we had escaped the Military Statistics Bureau’s demon grip and our lives could be saved, he of course no longer agreed to the Military Statistics Bureau’s sentence. And so we sat in prison only two years before Chen Shijun came forward to bail us out. I never saw a written verdict.
Only after my release did I learn that the infant son I had borne at Qujiang had long since died, alas, in infancy. Li Nanxiang hurried to Qujiang and brought our two daughters to Chongqing. My second daughter, Chen Dazhong, riding in the front cab of the truck from Guiyang to Chongqing, was thrown out of the truck at a turn because the door had not been shut fast, and one leg was hurt and she fainted away; people found her in the grass at the edge of a paddy field. Within Guizhou the mountains are high and the cliffs sheer, one side of the road a sheer wall and the other a stony ground; luckily Dazhong fell on the hillside side, or it would truly have been too dreadful to imagine. After reaching Qingmuguan in Chongqing, she was first taken by Chen Shijun to live at his house, and later went to my mother’s, where she lived for a time together with Guo Fajin, my second sister’s daughter. My elder daughter, Yunzhong, was lodged at the home of Zhigao’s sister Chen Baozhang. While Zhigao was in prison, he was allowed to read history books. He wrote the wrongful cases of past and present into a book manuscript titled A Record of Wind and Rain in the Southern Cap, which he later brought back to our Shanghai home—it is a pity it was never printed and published. During the “Cultural Revolution,” our home was ransacked three times, and all that was stored in the strongrooms—whether the paintings and calligraphy of past and present, or the rare and precious old editions of the laws of successive dynasties that he had collected—together with this manuscript, were all confiscated and swept clean away, and to this day there is no trace of any of it. In 1998 comrades of the Shanghai Museum came to call, saying they wished me to go and look, as one or two pieces of calligraphy and painting by recent hands, inscribed and presented to Zhigao, might be preserved in their museum. I thought that if his own handwritten manuscript could be found it would have some commemorative value, but as for the famous calligraphy and painting he had collected, those were things external to the person—why fret over them?—and so I did not go to look. Would it not be still better for these works, as cultural relics, to be permanently held by the museum and so handed down to the world?
In January 1945, the Chongqing National Government issued a notice ordering the two of us released on bail. After Chen Shijun took us out of prison, he also sent us to Nanwenquan for a few days’ stay. Then, taking our second daughter, Dazhong, we went to the home of the seventh sister, Chen Baozhang, to fetch our elder daughter, Yunzhong, and the family of four settled at Qingmuguan. Chen Shijun was warmhearted and glad to help others; in working to rescue us he had run about in every direction and worn out several pairs of leather shoes—truly a man of feeling, filial and friendly both. After my wrongful case was reversed in the 1980s, Comrade Lin Lin, former vice-chairman of the China-Japan Friendship Association, then retired, told me in Beijing that Comrade Enlai had once said of us: “Huang Mulan is a good comrade who can stand the test; in the prison of the Military Statistics agents, the most dangerous of enemies, she was able to keep up the struggle with loyalty, wit, and courage, without exposing her true identity or the Party’s secrets.” To receive such an appraisal of me from a leading comrade of the Central Committee—even had I suffered hardships still greater and endured wrongs still more, I would feel deeply comforted. By the time of our release it was near the lunar New Year, and we stayed for a time at the Qingmuguan lodging of Chen Xinzhu, Zhigao’s cousin, and her husband. Sister Xinzhu was two years older than I; her parents had died early, and she had been raised by Zhigao’s parents. She was of course very warm toward us in our coming to lodge with her, treating us attentively, so that we felt all the more the closeness of kin. When Sister Xinzhu was in her early twenties and still unmarried at home, Luo Jianbing urged her to leave home and make her own way, and the two went together to Chongqing, where later, through Cai Shuhou’s matchmaking, she was joined in marriage to Zhang Ruichu.
The cousin’s husband, Zhang Ruichu, was an old-style scholar well read in the Four Books and Five Classics, honest and sincere, of much learning and cultivation. After marriage the couple were very fond of each other, and had had one son, named Huiqing, clever by nature, who at three could recite Tang poems and had the repute of a prodigy. Cai Shuhou’s brother-in-law Jiang Zhicheng was then head of the General Affairs Department of the Ministry of Education, and so introduced Zhang Ruichu to be section chief of general affairs in the department; the whole family lived on this official post. They had a dwelling of three simple single-story rooms; the cousin and her husband lived in the east room, and the west room, which had been the study, was cleared out for us to live in, while the central hall between was shared by the two families. At that time Zhigao’s two younger sisters, Bingzhang and Baoqi, were both studying in Chongqing, and often went to Luo Jianbing’s house to inquire for news; the moment they heard the happy tidings of our release on bail, they arranged for us to lodge with Sister Xinzhu, so that we might have a temporary refuge. In wartime Chongqing housing within the city was very tight; Sister Xinzhu’s home in the suburbs was relatively more spacious, and sometimes when the sisters Bingzhang and Baoqi came to visit us, they would push two square tables together for a bed and sleep in the central hall. Sister Xinzhu kept house frugally; I did not know how to cook on a coal stove, and she taught me hand to hand, and went with me to market to buy food. We both loved the rice wine made of glutinous rice; she was good at brewing wine, and I learned the art of brewing from her too. Her back courtyard was quite spacious, with two great trees standing tall as canopies, in whose shade one could take the cool in summer and shelter from the wind in winter, and washing and drying clothes and quilts were both convenient. We lived here in peace nearly a year, until we returned to Shanghai after the victory in the war.
Because in my testimony in prison I had all along insisted that I was a person who had long since left the Communist Party organization, now that I was released on bail I naturally could not, the moment I came out, go openly and straight to the “Eighth Route Office” at Zengjiayan to report for duty—this in order to protect the safety of the three families joined to me by marriage, the Chens, the Huangs, and the Lis, and of my many friends. So the leadership charged me to live for a while first in the countryside, keeping my light hidden and biding my time, nursing my health and quietly awaiting further orders.
Because we had been released on bail, we could not resume public office (only after the victory in the war, when Zhigao returned to Shanghai, did he take up, between 1946 and 1948, a concurrent post as professor at the College of Law and Government). During our time at Qingmuguan, empty-handed, without a scrap of savings about us, our life was fairly hard. Our living expenses then had only two sources: one, the support of a bosom friend, the righteous gentleman of Jiangnan, Mr. Wang Genzhong; the other, my mother’s selling of her jewelry, together with timely aid—help in the snow—from my third brother, Zhangren, and his wife, who sent me from Yibin a subsidy worth about the price of ten taels of gold at the time. I used this money as sparingly as I could, and out of it saved up the travel money to return to Shanghai after the victory in the war.
Chen Shijun was extremely well liked. Once Bai Chongxi told him that the Kuomintang Central Committee had arranged in advance that, after the victory in the war, Li Zongren of the Guangxi clique would take up the post of director of the Beiping Field Headquarters; that Li greatly valued Zhigao’s abilities and meant to engage him to go along to Beiping as a senior staff officer of the field headquarters, with the treatment of a lieutenant general; and Bai invited Zhigao and me to dine at the Bai home. Feeling this was a weighty matter, we dared not answer on our own. So, through secret liaison, we met Liu Shaowen at the Sanliujiu tangyuan shop in the city of Chongqing. He conveyed to me the concern of the Central Committee and of the leading comrades Zhou Enlai, Dong Biwu, and others: “So long as the green hills remain, one need not fear a want of firewood; first nurse your health well, and later we will arrange a task for you.” As for Zhigao’s future course, he said he would have to report and ask instructions of the higher level before giving an answer; and as for Bai Chongxi’s dinner invitation, he said it could be gracefully declined on the ground that I was pregnant and unwell. To avoid the enemy agents’ surveillance, in that period I went into the city only once, and all day long, like a housewife, was busy with the household chores of cooking and washing. During this time Zhigao, idle at Qingmuguan, could not but find it lonely and dull, but he at least had no need to fear being tailed and watched by enemy agents, and so often went into the city to meet and talk with Cai Shuhou, Wang Genzhong, Li Nanxiang, and the others, looking forward to the counter-offensive at the front and the early coming of victory in the war of resistance. Not many days later Liu Shaowen conveyed the Central Committee’s instructions concerning us: “We are now in the final counter-offensive phase of the war of resistance, and victory is already to be looked for any day; please let Zhigao not rashly agree to go to Beiping with Li Zongren. You already have a very good working foundation in Shanghai; better to wait a little longer, and after the victory follow the Central Committee’s arrangements.”
And indeed, before long, the Japanese invaders proclaimed their unconditional surrender to the Allied powers, China among them. The Japanese still looked down on the Chinese, and so did not proclaim their surrender directly to the Chinese government, but only to the American-led Allied command. The moment this news was announced over the radio, the Chinese people, who had suffered so bitterly under the Japanese invasion, rejoiced to hear that the eight-year war of resistance they had upheld had at last won final victory, and all across the country, high and low, at once burst into jubilant cheering. The residents of Chongqing, within the city and without, all set off firecrackers in wild joy and drank deep of the fine wine of victory. Especially we “down-river folk,” who had fled from the middle and lower Yangtze to the great rear, were gladder than any. Tossed and drifting in a strange land for eight years, we could now at last, and soon, go back to Jiangnan to rebuild our homes—how could it not drive one wild with joy!
Not long after the Japanese surrender, Liu Shaowen conveyed to us the Central Committee’s instructions, telling us to return to Shanghai as soon as possible, revive the business of the Tongyi Trust Company, focus on maintaining ties with financial and cultural circles, and continue to serve as an outward liaison point for upper-level united-front work in the Kuomintang-controlled areas; and at the same time to restore at once the liaison work with the members of the “Bankers’ League” and the “Cultural League” from the days of the Solitary Island.
At that time I was carrying our little son in my womb and was anxious to get back to Shanghai; so Zhigao had someone find a way to book a cabin on the Changxing, of the Chongqing–Shanghai line, letting me take the two girls and Chen Bingzhang along to return to Shanghai by boat. Pan Xiao’e, deputy secretary-general of the Chinese Red Cross (whose wife was a schoolmate of Song Meiling), had been ordered to Shanghai to take part in the work of taking over, and had a special plane; he said to Zhigao: “The mines have not all been swept from the Yangtze shipping lane, and to go back to Shanghai on the very first steamer is very dangerous—let your good lady come along with us by plane!” Though this was indeed kindly meant, we felt it not quite right to accept so heavy a favor from another for no reason, the more so as it was not I alone who was traveling, and moreover the cabin on the steamer had already been booked and the money paid—why go and cancel the ticket and trouble others again? And so we gracefully declined Pan’s kind and earnest invitation. After the special plane Pan Xiao’e rode in took off from Chongqing, it lost its bearings in a heavy fog and crashed on the Yunnan border, and Pan Xiao’e and his whole family were unhappily killed. The Yangtze steamer we rode went down through the Three Gorges, and at Wuhan the steamer broke down and could not move; we waited a good many days, then changed to another boat, and, after alarms but no real peril, reached Shanghai safely.
By this time I had left only enough money about me for a rickshaw ride; so I called three rickshaws and went to the home of Zhigao’s youngest brother, Tingxiang, and his wife, Liying. Before Shanghai fell, Tingxiang was not yet married and had lived together with us in a small Spanish-style garden villa on Fuxing Middle Road, where, on our account, he too often kept company with A Ying, Yu Ling, Cai Shuhou, and the colleagues of the World and China Society. When A Ying withdrew from Shanghai, there were a good many books he could not take with him, and he entrusted them to Tingxiang to keep. After the liberation of the whole country, when A Ying was director of the Bureau of Culture in Tianjin, Tingxiang personally took these books to Tianjin and handed them to A Ying in person. Liying was a niece of Old Madame Chen, a childhood sweetheart of Tingxiang; after the two married (when we were no longer in Shanghai), they subleased the Fuxing Middle Road house and exchanged it for two small lane villas, living apart from brother Yingu. After the Japanese invaders occupied the Shanghai concessions, Tingxiang and his wife left Shanghai for the New Fourth Army’s territory, where Liying even took part for a short time in women’s work; being too weak in constitution and often ill, she returned to Shanghai. Seeing us come home safe and sound, the two of them were overwhelmed with surprise and joy, and together we celebrated our reunion after the disaster. Though the house was a little small, by squeezing in we could just manage to live there. The Chen brothers and sisters were deeply bound in kinship, and the couple were of course all the warmer toward us. Tingxiang set to work at once helping me to solve the housing problem and to plan the revival of the Tongyi Company’s business.
