### The Eight Trigrams on the Roof

The Shanghai man is a great one for superstition: with him everything has its taboo and every last thing its fengshui, so that he will even nail a plaque of the Eight Trigrams up on his rooftop, and some nail a little square mirror up along with it. Look into the thing with any care, and in a good half of every ten households you will find this game being played. They will tell you the Trigrams are nailed up because the house across the way, jutting against one's own, is most unlucky: leave it unnailed, and depend upon it the star of ill-fortune will come in at your door, and all the year round, through the four seasons, your home will know no peace and neither your fowls nor your dogs any quiet; but once it is up, it drives off the evil and calls down blessing, and everything goes as you would have it. Some, besides, stand a few empty wine-bottles up beside the Trigrams, and what that is meant to do is more than anyone can say — one man will have it that the empty bottles are set up to serve as cannon-shells, by way of quelling the baleful influences. The foreigner, who holds by neither fengshui nor taboo, not only never comes to grief but has his country rich and his people strong; while we, a shallow-minded set of Chinese, forever minding our taboos and doting on our superstitions, have sunk instead to being the pitiful vermin of a sub-colony.

Yet the Shanghai man I speak of is no true, dyed-in-the-wool Shanghainese; for of genuine Shanghai natives the place has in truth very few, and it is the false Shanghainese, come to lodge here from every province and every port, who on the contrary are the multitude.
