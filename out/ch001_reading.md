### The New Year Rush

It is twenty-four years now, in the blink of an eye, since the national calendar was brought in and the old lunar one done away with. And it is a good seven or eight years more since the National Government set up its capital at Nanjing and decreed all over again that everyone was to keep the national calendar and be finished, once and for all, with the lunar. Yet the common people, wedded to a habit handed down over several thousand years, seem in no hurry to reform, nor can they all be brought into line, which shows plainly enough that changing a custom is no easy business.

On the surface the national calendar may be in force, but in practice it is the old one that most people still keep. When the year turns by the national calendar, not a soul stirs; when it turns by the old, everyone treats it as a great event and throws himself into it. Habit being what it is, the authorities can only shrug and let it pass. Here, then, set down one by one, are the various doings of the year's end and the year's beginning.

*Cleaning.* The lazy streak in the Chinese is nowhere plainer to see. All year round they let the house and the furniture sit under their gathering dust and never give them a wash; then, when the end of the year comes, they fall to in a wild scramble, beating off the dust, scrubbing the floors, wiping down the window-rails, scouring the vessels, working themselves into a fine muddle. This they call the "great sweep."

*Thanking the Year.* Through all four seasons a man lives by the grace of Heaven; so as the year runs down, out come the big fish and the big cuts of meat, the red candles blaze, the incense-smoke curls, and the rite of Thanking the Year is performed to repay the gods for their protection. To finish, there are resounding kowtows and a great volley of firecrackers. Apart from the modern households and the Christian ones, there is hardly a family that does not go through it.

*The Ancestral Rites.* To sacrifice is to make offering to the ancestors, the descendants' way of honouring those long gone. Offerings fall due at all four seasons and the eight festivals of the year, but the year's-end sacrifice is conducted with a special gravity. In this one matter, but for a handful of Christian households, every family performs the rite in earnest devotion.

*Settling Accounts.* Every debt run up in the dealings between shop and customer must, when this season comes, be squared and paid off in full; no arrears may be carried over. If a man cannot pay, or means to dodge, he may slip through this once, but his credit is bankrupt ever after. And so a friend with any regard for his face, however tight he is pressed, will strain every nerve to raise the cash and clear what he owes, sooner than lay himself open to ridicule and lose his good name. It is the same between a firm and its native bank: any money drawn on credit must, when the year-gate comes, be repaid to the last coin before dealings can carry on into the new year. Let the sum go unpaid, and the shop's good name is picked at; the word passes from one mouth to ten and from ten to a hundred, that So-and-so is plainly hard up and none too sound.

*Burning Incense.* The Chinese are famous idol-worshippers, and so their temples and convents and shrines lie thick on the ground everywhere. On New Year's Day the pious men and devout women, one and all, take their baths and change into fresh clothes and set off in troop after troop, to the City God Temple in the Nanshi, to the Hong Temple on Nanjing Road, and the like, to burn their incense, shoulder against shoulder and hub against hub, jammed past all bearing. Then there is the sport of the "first incense." What is the first incense? It is to be the very first man through the temple gate; and to burn the first incense is counted a great honour, for the Bodhisattva, seeing your devotion, will single you out for a whole year of protection, of every wish granted, of wealth and good fortune. But if you would burn the first incense, so would the next man; you come early, he comes earlier, and the thing has by now been pushed back so far that around ten o'clock on New Year's Eve they are already off to burn their "New Year's Day" incense. To watch them at it is enough to raise a laugh.

*Paying New Year Calls.* Through the fortnight from the first of the month to the fifteenth, the young keep the forms of the New Year call upon their elders with the utmost ceremony, either the full obeisance, that is, the kowtow, or a bow, as each man's standing dictates. Equals meeting for the first time must at least make the clasped-hands salute and call out some lucky phrase, a "May you prosper!" or a "Joy to you in the new year!" There are, among the modern set, those who neither clasp hands nor say the lucky words; but they are, after all, the few.

*Amusements.* Amusements are of two kinds, the wholesome and the low. Custom being hard to shift, most people incline to the low sort; they are steeped in it the year round, without limit or restraint, and at New Year, the merchant shut of his shop and the workman off his work, they go junketing together, playing their fill, squandering time and money with never a thought for the reckoning. To do no more than stroll the amusement halls, take in a film, or hear a stretch of opera would be modest indeed; the greater number fling themselves like men possessed into whoring and gambling, and a sorry thing it is (gambling above all being the commonest of the New Year's low pleasures).

*The Tea Packet.* Whenever the New Year comes round and people go calling on family and friends, the servant brews a covered bowl of tea with two green olives set on the lid and announces, "Pray take the Ingot Tea." As the guest leaves, custom requires that he be given a "tea packet" wrapped in red paper. For roughly the first fortnight, each time a guest crosses the threshold for the first time and is served the Ingot Tea, the packet must be handed over. The sums run to about three grades. In the better households, where the coming and going is lavish, each packet runs from one dollar up to five; in the middling sort, from four *mao* in small silver up to a dollar; in the humblest, two *mao* at the least, the common run being from four *mao* to a dollar. Your true grandee, your great magnate, will make it ten dollars, or twenty, or fifty; but that is a tea packet of an altogether exceptional order.

*Lucky Money.* Elders must all hand their juniors "money to weigh down the year." The amount is not fixed, a dollar at the least, or five, or ten, or several tens; there is no settled rule to it.

*Red Candles Burning High.* The superstitious sort, over and above going to the temples to burn incense and knock their heads, must light great candles at home besides and, in all reverence, make a single sounding kowtow, in a rite they call "honouring Heaven and Earth." In such houses, through the first days of the New Year, every home has its red candles burning high and its incense-smoke curling, and it does not let up until the Lantern Festival is past.

*New Year Gongs and Drums.* From the middle of the twelfth month the ear already catches the surge of the year-end gongs and drums, and they will not fall silent until the Lantern Festival is over. Walk the streets in this season and you are forever hearing the beat of gong and drum, without rhythm or measure; wearisome as we find it, they hammer away with a will, and shun it you cannot. As for what they are about, the idea is simply that the household (meaning the one doing the drumming) should have itself a bit of fun.

*The Street Pedlars.* The small traders who carry their goods on a shoulder-pole, though they have paid for their licences, are by the regulations of the Settlement forbidden on ordinary days to halt and sell at the roadside; break the rule and be spotted by a plain-clothes constable, and you are hauled off to the police station and fined. Only on the eve of the New Year is the ban lifted for a single day, by way of leniency. And so on that day the pavements swarm with pedlars, their wares laid out in a jumble, fruit and toys, hair ornaments, shoes and stockings, flowers and plants and all the rest, touting the passers-by for custom.

*New Clothes.* Through the first days of the New Year, men and women, young and old alike, must each put on a suit of newly made clothes; the notion is that, a fresh year being under way, one may as well change into something new to show one's good cheer. So even the man who wears his rags without a second thought the rest of the year must, at this season, change into something better; and not the clothes alone, his shoes, his cap, his stockings must all be renewed. There are, to be sure, those who go on in their old clothes as usual, but they are the few.

*Welcoming the God of Wealth.* The "welcoming of the road-head" goes also by the name of "welcoming the God of Wealth." On the night of the fourth day he must be met with every reverence, the thought being that, so honoured, the God of Wealth will mark your devotion and send down blessings and riches, that trade will thrive, that great profit will follow, and that every wish will be fulfilled to the letter. Ridiculous as the performance is, the old-style shops for the most part keep it up, and there are private households that observe it too. By old custom, whether or not a shop's clerks are to be kept on for the coming year is also settled on this night. Before the welcoming, the offerings are laid out and the incense and candles lit; the manager makes his kowtow first, and when he has done he draws from inside his coat a sheet of red paper, prepared beforehand, on which the clerks' names are set down, and each clerk kowtows in his turn. But should your worthy name not be on the red paper, you may spare yourself the bow, for your post for the coming year is not renewed, and you had best roll up your bedding tomorrow and be on your way.

*Firecrackers.* Close the shop and you let off a "closing" volley; open it and you let off an "opening" one; Thanking the Year and Welcoming the God of Wealth each call for their crackers too, and among the strings are the "skyrockets," whose report is loud enough to leave the eardrum deaf. As the year turns, this banging and cracking and rattling can be heard on every side; tiresome as it is to the bystander, the people themselves are in the highest feather, pleased as anything. Were someone to reckon up all this money gone off in smoke, the total would be a sight to behold.

*Beggars on the Cadge.* On ordinary days a beggar who works the street for money is driven off the moment a constable spots him, or run in to the police station for correction, or turned out beyond the Settlement bounds; but from New Year's Eve through to the fourth day they are let beg as they please, and no one interferes. So for these few days the roads are thick with beggars, men and women in bands and troops, dunning the passers-by and not letting up till they are paid. The lane-mouths, too, and the ground before the gated house-doors, are the great gathering-places of the tribe. Once the fourth day is past, though, they may no longer beg out in the open. There is besides a sort of low fellow who bands together on the spot, five or six or seven or eight of them: the leader takes up a long-handled lantern and the rest each a noise-maker, gong or drum or cymbals and the like, and the moment they catch a household at its Thanking the Year or its Welcoming the God of Wealth they come swarming up, singing and banging away; refuse them silver and they only bang the louder and sing the harder, and set up such an uproar besides that, till they are paid, they will not stop. Give them a few small silver coins at the least, and off they whoop. They go by the name of "soliciting the lucky-market money"; the gong-and-drum they beat keeps no rhythm, and the songs they sing no tune, only doggerel. There are rules, they say, even to their cadging: where a household keeps its Thanking the Year or its Welcoming the God of Wealth but hangs no lantern at the door, they will not come to ask; but hang out a lantern, and they march up without ceremony. Such people are not quite common beggars, and yet, when all is said, they are only beggars in cap and gown.

*Fish and Meat in Plenty.* Every year, as the end draws near, households great and small alike lay in fish and meat and vegetables against the New Year. Only, the great house has its big fish and big meat, the small house its small fish and small meat; and there are the New Year feasts and the spring feasts to which guests are asked, all held at this same season. The idea, as people have it, is that with the old year going and the new just come, a little eating and drinking is a way of showing one's good cheer. But in the writer's own view, to buy a bit of fish and meat at the year's end and eat it is reasonable enough; the trouble is that the fish and meat cooked back at the turn of the year go on being eaten, with some to spare, well past the Lantern Festival, and by then, cold as the weather may be, so much time has gone by that the vegetables have turned, and to swallow them down shows a sad want of care for one's health. To gratify the belly by eating tainted food instead, what is the good of it? Yet so it is that custom carries all before it, and reform it though one would, there is just no reforming it.
