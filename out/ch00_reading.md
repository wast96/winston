## Preface: Probing the Secret

Seeking out secrets seems to be a part of human nature.

A secret is never sealed away without a reason: behind it lies a prize! The prize might be a hoard of buried gold, or a martial-arts master's secret manual, or a scientific cipher, or some earth-shaking conspiracy.

And if the secret touches national security, then it is more secret still, classified absolutely. A secret either way!

People are greedy. To seek a secret out is not satisfaction enough; inevitably they want to possess it too.

And to possess a secret takes professional skill.

At the level of national security, the organizations that ferret secrets out are called intelligence agencies, and those that keep secrets are called security organs. This work, intelligence and security both, the profession of secret struggle, becomes in itself the deepest secret in the world.

A secret within the secret!

The most hidden of all the world's intelligence and security systems is not the American CIA, nor the Soviet KGB, but China's. The people in this line of work in China keep faithfully to a tradition of secrecy: "While you live, let it rot in your belly; when you die, carry it to the coffin."

Who knew that modern China's first secret-service organization was created not by the Nationalists but by the Communist Party? The CCP, famous for beating the strong from a position of weakness, was in fact always ahead of the Nationalists in secret work.

Who imagined that Zhou Enlai, the world-renowned diplomat, was at the same time the leader of a vast secret apparatus? At a time when the secret agents of every country and every faction stank to high heaven, the CCP's own spymaster kept a fine moral name.

Who guessed that Mao Zedong was also a master analyst of intelligence? At the very moment when Stalin, the Soviet supreme commander, and Roosevelt, the American president, were both misreading the intelligence and bringing their nations to the edge of ruin, Mao had already seized the strategic initiative. At a time when every trade in China lagged behind, its intelligence and security work sprinted into the front rank of the world during the Second World War.

Who foresaw that, in the largest civil war of all, the People's Liberation Army's headquarters could get almost any intelligence it wanted, for the asking? Before the Volunteers ever went into Korea, Mao had already read America's hand.

These astonishing secrets appear in no published text and rest in no classified file. They are sealed away in the deep memory of old intelligence and security hands.

Secret work is hard, and writing about the secret front is naturally hard too. Yet the thing most worth doing in this world is precisely the thing that is hard to do. The rich seams that were easy to work are long since mined out; to find the treasure now, you must labor for it.

No trade in this world is as mysterious and as perilous as the hidden front, and none is as full of fascination. A single piece of intelligence can turn the whole outcome of a campaign; a single agent can save a vast organization from ruin. Here a small player can rewrite great history!

We small players can seldom take a hand in rewriting history. But there is another choice open to us, and that is to write it down: I have taken the history that others rewrote and set it down as a book.

This book is the historical record of China's hidden front.

I interviewed many an "old spook" across every branch of the Chinese Party, government, and army: ministers and bureau chiefs, and men who once wore the label "traitor" as well. I drew too on a great mass of written material, combing it over and over. Only then did I understand: the fabled secret manual was never set down as a finished text, left lying there for you to leaf through. It is the assembled skill of countless masters, waiting for you to dig it out and put it in order.

Since it was published, this book has been fortunate to win the affection of a wide readership and of specialists alike. One reader asked me: how can a true record be better reading than an invented story? My answer is that the people who made these true events were every one of them artists, and the cases they designed had to fool everybody. When we spin a tale badly or act it out unconvincingly, the worst we suffer is to make fools of ourselves; but the instant they slipped, they forfeited their lives!

The many events set down in this book are all stories created by people of the first rank, with the whole of their lives and their wits. How could such stories fail to move us?

This book has been added to again and again and printed many times over, and still I dare not feel the least bit satisfied. The water is too deep!

China's hidden front holds countless secrets yet, buried out of sight.

The water is deep; you cannot touch the bottom of the pool. But wade in we must, and try, for only by testing the water do you learn how deep it runs. This book is my wading in together with the reader, to search out treasure and prospect the vein; and once the vein is found, one day the whole hoard will come to light!

I am especially grateful to the readers who have pointed out the book's gaps and handed me leads to follow, guiding me to keep filling in the blanks. And the pointers of specialists in the field have done still more to lift my understanding of the subject.

The newly upgraded illustrated edition I now bring out can be called, in a way, an expanded and revised one.

If the first edition merely set the treasures out on display, this new one goes a step further, and tries to search out the cultural value of the hoard.

The most precious treasure in this world is not gold or diamonds but cultural heritage. Culture too has life; the growth of a civilization, it turns out, has its own cultural genes.

Reader and author together, shall we take up the engineering of that cultural gene?
