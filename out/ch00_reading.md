## Preface: History Must Not Be Made a Monster

On February 20, 2021, in a keynote address to the mobilization meeting for the study of Party history, General Secretary Xi Jinping said: "From the very day of its birth, our Party has been bound up with the future and fate of the Chinese people and the Chinese nation. After the Opium War, China sank step by step into a semi-colonial, semi-feudal society, into the tragic condition of foreign invasion, of ceaseless war, of a broken land and a people ground into the mire, and the Chinese people lived in fire and flood. From that time on, the rejuvenation of the nation became the greatest dream of the Chinese people. To change a fate of enslavement and humiliation, countless people of high purpose pressed forward rank after rank, striving to find a way to save the nation from ruin."

In the modern age the Chinese nation has passed through every calamity and lived on, has drunk its fill of hardship and only hardened its will, has kept its vigor and its power to hold together. The deepest reason is that batch after batch of people of high purpose have carried a boundless loyalty to their country and their people, have shouldered the responsibilities that history and the age laid on them, and have given everything for the good of country and people.

Loyalty above all: that is the eternal theme of our Party's struggle on the hidden front.

Devotion as one's honor: that is the clear, true color of the nameless heroes who pass on the flame though the fuel burns away.

On September 10, 2009, the results were announced of the "Double Hundred" selection — one hundred heroes and models who had made outstanding contributions to the founding of New China, and one hundred figures who had moved the nation in the years since. Among those put forward by the state security system were Qian Zhuangfei and Li Bai. The first stood on the roll of the "Three Heroes of Longtan"; the second was one of the models for the classic film and the acclaimed ballet *The Unfailing Radio Waves*. On the afternoon of September 14, at around three o'clock, Qian Zhuangfei's grandson Qian Hong attended the forum of "Double Hundred" honorees and their kin as a family representative.

I had once written, with loyalty for warp and devotion for weft, two series of newspaper features — for the sixtieth anniversary of the founding of New China, for our Party's struggle on the hidden front, for the nameless heroes who had gone forward wave after wave. Two of the articles concerned Qian Zhuangfei: "Qian Hong: Onscreen My Grandfather Played the Swordsman, His Real Work Was to Lurk" (*Labor Daily*, Taste Weekly, September 6, 2009) and "The Three Heroes of Longtan" (*Liberation Daily*, September 7, 2009). I took them to Beijing and gave them to Qian Hong. He shared his own gladness with me, showing me the group photograph from the honorees' forum and the certificate, the plaque, and the commemorative medal the organizing committee had conferred. He also gave me a book, *Across the Happy Time-Lag: Stories Told by the Old Man of Yuetan* (2009), which included an article of his, "Remembering My Grandfather Qian Zhuangfei," where he mentioned a schoolgirl who had given one cent toward the repair of his grandfather's grave. Qian Hong said: "At the place where my grandfather was killed, the ordinary people raised money on their own and built a 'Red Army grave,' and among the donors was a schoolgirl who gave one cent. When I heard of it I was very moved, and I badly wanted to meet the child. Many years later she wrote to me, saying she was by then a student at Guiyang Normal University. I invited her to Beijing as my guest and received her at my home. I thanked her to her face, and she said she revered heroes like my grandfather. I asked what she meant to do after she graduated. She said she would go back to Jinsha County to teach, so that the children of her home district might get a better education and grow up straight and strong into pillars of the state, and so make her due contribution to fulfilling the last wishes of the revolutionary martyrs."

Qian Hong's account opened my thinking. I told him I was helping to plan a film drawn from the story of the "Three Heroes of Longtan." A single cent, a single grave, a single schoolgirl — out of these you could build a scene of aching, tearful beauty.

Qian Hong asked how the film had come about. I told him that, to mark the ninetieth anniversary of the founding of the Chinese Communist Party, the State Administration of Radio, Film and Television meant to fund a film on a "Special Branch" subject. After *A Nanjing Night* (released in 1985, directed by Qian Hong's father — Qian Zhuangfei's son — Qian Jiang, whose credits include *Daughters of China*, *The White-Haired Girl*, *New Year Sacrifice*, *The Lin Family Shop*, and *Hai Xia*), the imperishable deeds of Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di would be brought to the screen once more; the assignment had come down, and a certain up-and-coming director was to take the helm. The State Administration thought highly of his gifts, judging the tribute film he had directed for the sixtieth National Day very well done. Hearing that I had a hand in the new "Three Heroes of Longtan," Qian Hong urged me, again and again, that we could do it however we liked, only it must not be too vulgar, too commercial. I told him that was exactly what I feared most. Because before *A Nanjing Night* went into production, his father had first written *Seven Top-Secret Telegrams*, and to write it well his father had not only, as he says in the afterword to that documentary work, "gathered a great many historical archives of the period from every quarter," but had also, "following his own boyhood memories," striven "for the truth" in everything, from "the events, the people, the incidents" down to "the very places where things happened." When I went to Gansi East Road (today Jiashan Road in Shanghai) to verify the exact address of his grandfather's lodging in those years, the local residents all told me how conscientious and painstaking his father had been when he came to scout the location. And yet nowadays certain people, under the banner of "market operations," want nothing more than to turn revolutionary history into a purely commercial blockbuster. If the names of Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di were all changed, if Gu Shunzhang too were transformed with a shrug — from the man who escorted Zhang Guotao, Chen Changhao, Shen Zemin, and the others to the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Soviet into an imperial commissioner cracking a case, sent by order of the Center to Hankou to get to the bottom of why "the Communists' underground organizations in Hubei were being smashed one after another" — the way it is all so lovingly told in *Chinese Communist Agents* (1996), where one moment he "sweeps his mistress Cuihua up around the waist," "like a lion gone mad," and "rips her bodice open with a single tug," and the next is "grappling" with an agent, "pulling a dagger from his waistband" and running through, on the spot, a bull-necked, tiger-backed "big fellow" — then it would be a wretched thing indeed.

Qian Hong's face turned grave.

In our years of acquaintance I had come to know him as an easygoing man. But just then his expression went unusually cold and hard.

Qian Hong said he asked for nothing more than this: that the good name of the martyrs not be defiled.

His hope is my hope too.

In *Unsolved Cases of the Republic* (2008) I once wrote the following passage:

This is not a book that chases the sensational. Just the opposite: it is a book against the sensational. Every mystery in it has its robberies and killings, its graft and betrayals; its flashing blades and bloody rains, its lechery and knavery, its cheating and double-dealing. But all this is only a surface, a shallow appearance. No one here means simply to reconstruct the darkness of a wicked society. The further disclosures, the further checking of the record, the further digging in the archives, the further reading of the historical facts, the further surfacing of those who lived through it, the further speaking-out of those who were there — all of it serves to give history back its true face, and to keep this generation and the next from loose talk, from taking words at their surface, from hearsay, from the plausible-but-false. I know very well that I am by no means the first to tell these great and notorious cases. Nearly every story, every chapter, has been told before, written before. The trouble is that the more familiar a thing is, the more error breeds error; the more it is told and told again, written and written again, the less truth is left in it, and the more it turns the already-clouded doings of petty thieves and lawbreakers into something still more tangled, still more riddled with doubt. And just for that reason the single word "doubt" takes on many meanings. That one word "doubt" becomes the very heart of this book, its very key. That a case is in itself doubtful is only natural. What truly wrings the heart is the "doubt" manufactured by so-called documentary, so-called faithful accounts — the "doubt" of the stirred-up rumor, the "doubt" of the guess passed off as fact, the "doubt" of the outright fabrication, the "doubt" of the cheap knockoff. To trace things back to their source is what I set out to do; to sift the false from the true is what I have done. I dare not say I have done all I wished to do. I dare only say that I do not take rumor for true history. I am drawing nearer and nearer to the truth I mean to reach. I will not lightly draw conclusions or pass judgment. Least of all will I, on the strength of evidence not yet exhausted, wildly guess at great events still unsettled or speak carelessly of right and wrong. I can do without a settled verdict; I cannot do without rigor. History is not a little girl to be dressed up as one pleases. History must not be made a monster.

That history must not be made a monster was the starting point for my writing of *Unsolved Cases of the Republic*, and it is the starting point for my writing of *The Sword Roars in the West Wind: A Chronicle of the Central Special Branch*.

A hot topic, considered coolly: that, I suspect, is what can best carry people today — whether the old who look back with longing, or the young who hunger to understand the living tissue of history — step by step toward that treacherous, shifting past, toward those years thick with the shadows of spies, toward all those nameless heroes who, for the cause of the Chinese people's liberation, went through fire and water amid the bloody rains and the gilded dissipation of old Shanghai, with great wisdom and great courage. All those underground vanguards who, on the hidden front under the leadership of the Chinese Communist Party, pressed on and never turned back are shining examples of the man whom "riches and honor cannot corrupt, poverty and low station cannot move, force and arms cannot bend." They are the pioneers of their age; they are the monuments of history; they are the elite of the nation; they are the backbone of the people. With their hot blood and loyal souls they founded imperishable deeds; with their shining achievements they composed a song of triumph that will be sung for a hundred generations. In that earlier time, when the five martyrs of the League of Left-Wing Writers were killed, Lu Xun wrote gravely at the close of "In Memory of the Forgotten": the night is still long, and the road too is still long; the time will come, all the same, when they are remembered and spoken of again.

On February 20, 2021, in that same keynote address to the mobilization meeting for the study of Party history, General Secretary Xi Jinping said: "Over a hundred years of extraordinary struggle, generation upon generation of Chinese Communists have fought tenaciously and striven without cease; there has arisen a great host of revolutionary martyrs who faced death unflinching, a great host of heroic figures who struggled on, a great host of advanced models who gave themselves without a thought for self, and from them has taken shape a series of great spirits, a genealogy of the spirit of the Chinese Communists, which has given rich nourishment to the founding, the raising, and the strengthening of our Party."

At the same time, the better to trace things to their source and shore up the root, and to oppose historical nihilism under a clear banner, General Secretary Xi Jinping stressed: "If one's view of history is mistaken, not only will the aims of study and education go unmet; the effort will run in the wrong direction, into blind alleys. At present certain mistaken tendencies must put us on our guard: some magnify the errors and setbacks in Party history, willfully blackening and distorting the Party's history and attacking the Party's leadership; some deliberately link events in Party history to present-day problems and stir up malicious hype; some trust unofficial history over the true record, cheapening Party history into vulgarity and entertainment, delighting in the spread of gossip and anecdote, doting on illegal publications from abroad, and so on. We must hold to our Party's two resolutions on questions of history and to the relevant guidance of the Party Center, grasp accurately the theme and main line, the mainstream and the essence, of the Party's historical development, and understand rightly and appraise scientifically the major events, the important meetings, and the important figures of Party history."

A nation with hope cannot be without heroes; a country with a future cannot be without pioneers. The deeds and the spirit of the Central Special Branch and its fallen heroes are the very force that spurs us on. We must take history as our mirror and win people by reason; we must strike back at the frivolous wind blowing through the world of the historians, the sensation-chasing wind through the literary world, the wind of travesty through film and television, and not let historical nihilism run wild and spread like a plague. We will never allow the historical nihilists to blacken the faces of the Central Special Branch's fallen heroes. The nihilist history of the so-called "post-truth age" cannot and must not become a cheap consumer good for the spiritual life of people today.

The good name of the Central Special Branch's fallen heroes endures with the rivers and the mountains.

The spirit of the Central Special Branch's fallen heroes shines with the sun and the moon!

Ye Xiaoshen

April 15, 2021

the Sixth National Security Education Day for All
