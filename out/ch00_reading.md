## Preface

Zhou Enlai was a great Marxist-Leninist and an outstanding leader of the Party and the state. He was one of the founders of the Chinese Communist Party and its earliest military leader. After Chiang Kai-shek and Wang Jingwei each in turn betrayed the revolution, Zhou led the Nanchang Uprising on 1 August 1927 and served as secretary of the Party's Front Committee. In 1928, at the First Plenary Session of the Sixth Central Committee, he was elected to the Standing Committee of the Political Bureau, and he went on to head the Party's Central Organization Department and to serve as secretary of the Central Military Commission. In those years the Party's Central Committee worked in secret in Shanghai. Zhou did much to keep it safe, to link and guide the armed struggles that Communists led across the country, and to build up the underground revolutionary work in the territory the Kuomintang ruled. For most of this period he was, in practice, the chief steward of the Central Committee's work. For decades he labored to master and apply the fundamentals of Marxism-Leninism and Mao Zedong Thought; for the liberation of the Chinese people and the cause of communism he spent himself to the last, and built a record of great achievement for the Party and the people. The wisdom and daring, the command of both pen and sword, that he showed across a lifetime of revolution won him the love of the people of China and of the world.

As the founder and outstanding leader of intelligence and security work for the Chinese Communist Party and its armed forces, Zhou Enlai was the commander of the hidden front, and he poured his heart and soul into that work for half a century. After the counter-revolutionary coup of 12 April 1927, the Central Committee moved to Wuhan in the spring and returned to Shanghai that autumn. Its leading organs now worked under the eyes of enemy agents, exposed to attack at any hour. Without a covert struggle they could not protect themselves. Reading the needs of the moment, Zhou drew together the lessons of the Party's past work, and above all the bitter lesson of the April 12 coup, and proposed a dedicated apparatus for intelligence and security, to guard against sabotage by enemy agents and keep the Central Committee safe. Under his direct leadership, the building of the Central Special Section began in 1927. In November 1928 the Standing Committee of the Political Bureau resolved to entrust the work to a Special Committee of three: Xiang Zhongfa, Zhou Enlai, and Gu Shunzhang. Drawing on the Soviet experience of the Cheka, Zhou set the Central Special Section three tasks and one rule: to penetrate the enemy and gather intelligence, to raise funds, and to eliminate traitors; but the special methods of secret-service work were never to be turned inward, against the Party itself. Through practice he married the successes of the Russian underground to the realities of the Chinese revolution, while discarding certain Russian practices, such as placing intelligence work above the Party and setting it apart. He kept the Central Special Section always under the direct leadership of the Central Committee, so that it became a fighting bastion of the Party.

Under Zhou Enlai's own direction, the Central Special Section recruited its first counter-espionage source from inside the Kuomintang's professional anti-Communist apparatus, uncovered the enemy's plots against the Party, and warned the Party's secret organizations in time. It planted its own people deep in the enemy's heart, in the highest secret-police organs, and laid hold of the enemy's core secrets. It rescued arrested comrades. It eliminated traitors. Under the bloody White Terror, at the moments when the Party's survival hung in the balance, it kept the Central Committee and the Party's organizations at every level safe.

Zhou Enlai was also the founder of radio work for our Party and army. Under his direct guidance Li Qiang built the Party's first transmitter-receiver, trained its first radio operators, and set up its first wireless station. Zhou himself compiled the first codebook and named it the "Hao Code," after his own alias, "Wu Hao." In March 1931 Ren Bishi carried the Hao Code from Shanghai to the Central Soviet. Once the two ends were in contact, Zhou Enlai and Deng Yingchao in Shanghai, and Ren Bishi in the Soviet area, did the enciphering and deciphering themselves. Wireless work then spread through the Red Army across the country and into the great cities, lending real weight to the revolutionary war and joining the Central Committee to Party organizations everywhere. This was a turning point in the history of the Party's communications: from then on its wireless network grew by the day, and an international station set up in Shanghai even opened a channel to the Comintern.

"Because the Party's Central Committee did its secret work in a Shanghai the enemy ruled, its surroundings were extraordinarily dangerous. Zhou Enlai was a Communist leader known to all, a prime target the enemy hunted by every means. Having held open leading posts for a long stretch during the Great Revolution, he was known by sight to many inside the Kuomintang, which made his position more perilous still. Yet in so tangled a setting Zhou, cool and quick-witted, took things in stride and built up an ever richer store of underground craft. He changed his name and his lodgings constantly. He would stay somewhere a month, sometimes only two weeks, and take a new name with every move; only two or three people ever knew where he was. Because so many knew his face, he held his outings to the hours between five and seven in the morning and after seven at night, and unless there was special need he did not go out at all. He had studied the layout of Shanghai's streets with care, kept off the main roads where he could, threaded the small lanes, and neither rode the trams nor went to public places. He usually dressed as a merchant, and later grew a full beard, so that many in the Party called him "Old Hu." Hard as the enemy pressed him, though he was one of its chief quarries, it never once picked up his trail."

The chapter "On the Use of Spies" in Sun Tzu's *Art of War* speaks of how much intelligence work matters and of the qualities the man who leads it must have: "Of the whole army, none should be closer to the commander than his spies, none more richly rewarded, none held in deeper secrecy. Without the wisdom of a sage one cannot use spies; without humanity and justice one cannot direct them; without a subtle mind one cannot get the truth from them." With his uncommon intellect, his alertness and resourcefulness, Zhou Enlai founded our Party's intelligence work; in his years directing the Central Special Section he lived through many heart-stopping struggles and built an imperishable record in the fight against the enemy on the hidden front.
