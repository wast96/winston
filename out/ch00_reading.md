### Foreword to the Series
Zhang Baijia
The New Democratic Revolution led by the Chinese Communist Party was contested on two fields at once: the open battlefield and the hidden front. Open and hidden worked in concert, each supporting and strengthening the other, and together they sounded the triumphal notes of the Chinese revolution. As Mao Zedong observed, to defeat the enemy one must wage two kinds of war, the open and the hidden. Hidden war has its strategic offensive, planting agents at the enemy's very heart, and its strategic defense, the guarding of one's own. Since an enemy can be beaten only by attack from without and within, the two count for equally much.
The Party's hidden front grew as the Party grew and strengthened as it strengthened. At the height of the White Terror, Zhou Enlai founded the Party's first dedicated organ of intelligence and security, the Central Special Branch, and trained a body of outstanding leaders and working cadres for the hidden front, rendering signal service to the safety of the Party Center and its leaders. In the years of the Central Soviet, the political-security work established in the Central Soviet and the other main soviet areas made its own particular contribution: it helped the Workers' and Peasants' Red Army smash the enemy's "encirclement and suppression" campaigns, develop and consolidate the soviet power, and keep the ranks of the Red Army pure. On the Long March the Red Army's intelligence and reconnaissance arm intercepted the enemy's telegraph traffic in good time, so that the central command could follow his military dispositions, strike where he was weak, spring one surprise after another, break free of pursuit and blockade, and carry the great strategic transfer through to success. After the Red Army reached northern Shaanxi, the hidden front played a decisive part in winning the peaceful settlement of the Xi'an Incident and bringing the anti-Japanese national united front into being. During the War of Resistance the Center set up in turn the Central Department of Social Affairs and the Central Intelligence Department, and the hidden-front struggle came of age as a profession. Our intelligence workers reached inside the Japanese and puppet Party and government organs and obtained a great volume of the enemy's military and political intelligence, together with intelligence on the Kuomintang's policy of resisting Japan feebly and the Communists fiercely, and this counted for much in the victory of the War of Resistance. In the War of Liberation the intelligence and security work of our Party and army developed further and more widely still. Our intelligence workers penetrated the Kuomintang's most secret organs of decision and command and supplied the Party Center, the Military Commission, and the regional commands with timely, accurate strategic intelligence of the first importance, giving the Center a firm footing for its strategy in the War of Liberation; our army knew the enemy's main dispositions as if they lay in the palm of its hand, and held the initiative from first to last. Mao Zedong judged this the period when "our intelligence work was at its most successful."
That bitter, unyielding struggle on the hidden front produced a great company of nameless heroes. Some had the wit and daring to plan a campaign from within the tent and settle the issue a thousand li away; some went into the tiger's den, fenced with the enemy, and came away with his secrets; some laid down their lives for a principle, met torture, and faced death as calmly as going home; some lay hidden in the enemy's camp for years and in the end brought their units over in revolt; some, steady and dependable, made the careful arrangements that kept the Party Center safe; some, quick and resourceful, broke the blockade and held the lines of communication open. Generation after generation of these nameless heroes gave their whole loyal hearts and went through fire and water; out of loyalty and faith they raised an unmarked monument to the Chinese nation. Communist after Communist held to his first purpose and kept his mission in mind, tempering in his own blood and life a red inheritance handed on like a torch from one hand to the next. In the struggle they showed a firm revolutionary faith, a fearless revolutionary heroism, the mettle to dare to fight and dare to win, a supple mastery of the art of struggle against the enemy, a hard and exacting habit of work, and the high temper of those content to be nameless and content to give. All of it settled into the character of the hidden front: victory without conceit, defeat without loss of heart; wronged, they did not cry out; suffering, they did not complain; they saved others at cost to themselves, braved danger, and bore down at the work in silence.
Mao Zedong once said that when the revolution was won a great medal ought to be struck for the nameless heroes of our intelligence front. Many of them left us early, before the medal they had hoped for could come. Their names are known to no one, but their deeds will last forever. Xu Xiangqian pointed to a serious gap in our Party and army histories: "they do not give intelligence work its due." Recent years have brought a number of published sources and biographies on the hidden front, but on the whole the record is still thin, out of all proportion to what the hidden front achieved. This series, The Hidden Front Chronicles, is divided into a Biography Volume, a Memoir Volume, and a Historical-Account Volume, and gives a fairly full picture of the history of the hidden front and the spirit of its nameless heroes. It is at once a heartfelt remembrance of and tribute to the older generation of hidden-front heroes and a book from which the state-security officers of a new age can learn, one that lets a new generation understand that history and carry the inheritance forward. In that it has real value.
(The author is deputy chairman of the Chinese Society for the Study of Figures in Chinese Communist Party History.)
