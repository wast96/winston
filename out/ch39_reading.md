## 9

When Tang Ya saw Yang Qun's body in the mortuary of the Central Hospital, she had not yet had time to change out of her uniform. Standing in the drone of the generator, her face white as paper, she was dazed, as if just waking from a dream.

The current head of the security bureau was a man Yang Qun had promoted with his own hand. With a grave face he took an arrest warrant from an aide and put it into Tang Ya's hands. "Rest assured, Miss Tang. The minister has already pressed the army to seal off the whole city. The killer cannot possibly get away."

On the warrant, staring out, was Jiang Yongnan's official army portrait.

Late that night, as the head of the security bureau drove Tang Ya home himself, soldiers and police manned checkpoints everywhere. At the door of the apartment building he hesitated. "A telegram just came. Mrs. Yang has reached Fuzhou. Early tomorrow she takes the mail plane to Chongqing."

Tang Ya said nothing and pushed the door open, got out numbly. But when she came into the flat and switched on the light, she found the whole place in wreckage—even many of the floorboards had been pried up, baring the dust-choked cavities beneath. Tang Ya took it in with a single glance, then turned and went into the washroom, filled the basin with cold water, and plunged her head in, as though to drown herself, until a figure appeared in the mirror above. Tang Ya jerked upright, flinging up a sheet of water.

Jiang Yongnan was in filthy coarse-cloth work clothes. He took off his cap, opened his mouth, and yet no sound would come.

Tang Ya looked at him once, then pulled a towel from the rack and pressed it to her face, left the washroom, and stood in the sitting room where there was no longer anywhere to set a foot. Jiang Yongnan, behind her, said, "They must be looking for the original of a list."

"So that's what you came for too." Tang Ya's face, wiped dry, looked unusually severe, and colder still was the gaze she turned on Jiang Yongnan.

Jiang Yongnan shook his head. "He wanted me to take you away, out of this muddy water. Those were his last words."

Tang Ya stood stunned a long while, then gave a cold laugh. She flung the towel down to the floor and turned into the bedroom.

Jiang Yongnan stood alone a while in the dim yellow light, took the Browning pistol from his pocket, and set it on the table; and just as he turned toward the door, Tang Ya's voice came from behind him: "Don't go. I want to know the truth."

Toward dawn a police car went by on the road outside, its siren wailing. Jiang Yongnan sat on the floor, his head against the edge of the bed. "He knew he wouldn't live. To his last breath he meant to pull you back off this road."

"My road, I'll walk to its end myself." Tang Ya lay on the bed in her clothes, and said it as if on a sigh, then closed her eyes. A long time passed before she spoke, all at once, of the bar called the White Night, destroyed in a Japanese air raid; buried with it was the blind Black musician. Rebuilt under a new owner, it was now renamed the Memory Café, but it still sold foreign liquor of every kind and still served the men and women who could not sleep at night. In the end, Tang Ya said, they really had come to call that homemade cocktail potassium cyanide—only the barman had gone home for good, and no one now could mix that fiery taste again.

When she had said it, both of them fell silent. In the dark they waited quietly for the light to come up, for voices to rise on the road and swell, little by little, into noise.

When Jiang Yongnan rose to leave, Tang Ya found a set of Yang Qun's civilian clothes in the wardrobe, laid them on the dressing table, and without a word withdrew from the bedroom, going to stand at the window where Yang Qun had so often stood in life, gazing through the curtain at the road below. After a good while Jiang Yongnan came out of the bedroom, gripping tight in his hand the silver dollar that had never once left him.

Tang Ya, her back to him, said, "You ought to have a plan ready. What if you're caught?"

"Death, too, is a way of going home." Jiang Yongnan crossed to her as he spoke, took her hand from behind, drew her around until she faced him, and pressed into her palm the silver dollar that had been clenched in his other hand.

Tang Ya asked, with those eyes of hers round as a cat's, "What is this?"

"Potassium cyanide. The last gift a killer keeps for himself." When Jiang Yongnan had said it, he let go of her hand, and the two of them, close enough to touch, were all at once parted as if by mountains and rivers without number. Jiang Yongnan looked at her lash-veiled eyes and, of a sudden, gave a bleak smile. "If not for this, my life would not have been like this. Nor would yours."

But Tang Ya thought suddenly of the moment they had parted on the Hankou docks. She waited until long after Jiang Yongnan had gone before she slowly turned, swept the curtain open, pushed the window wide, and, hands on the sill, gazed down without moving at the clamoring street. Tang Ya remembered, too, that day—how he had turned to look back from the crowd on the shore, in a brand-new Japanese medical officer's uniform.

A few hours later the airliner carrying members of the Korean Provisional Government took off on time, but Jiang Yongnan was not aboard. On the road to Jiulongpo airfield he was seized by a squad of soldiers and police at a spot check.

That night, when the hard overnight interrogation had reached its second round, the relief examiner came in bearing a case file, and before he had asked two questions he took out several photographs, walked up to Jiang Yongnan, and said, "Look closely. Think it through, and make a clean confession."

The photographs had plainly just been developed—a touch left a fingerprint on them. They showed Jiang Yongzhu walking in line out the gate of Hongqiao airport, and in the crowd his wife and daughter, watching for him with lifted faces. Jiang Yongnan let out a long breath. "So your Nationalist government goes in for guilt by kin now, does it?"

The examiner shook his head. "When they leave, and how they leave, depends on your confession."

Two months later the Chongqing district court passed sentence in open session: for the crime of murder, the retired officer Jiang Yongnan was condemned to death, the execution to be carried out on a day to be fixed.

For the coming Spring Festival, the Memory Café had hung many red lanterns from its ceiling—neither Chinese nor Western, yet giving off a festivity all their own. But the night was not yet deep enough, and the hall stood thin of guests; only a young pianist played a single piece over and over.

Tang Ya sat on a high stool at the bar, pouring vodka and Fuling rice wine into a shaker with grave concentration, shaking them hard into a milky liquid. Then she tipped it down her own throat, glass after glass, so that by the time Old Jin sat down beside her, her eyes had already begun to glaze.

"What are you doing to yourself?" The look in Old Jin's eyes was as pained as ever. "Whatever it is, couldn't you say it at the office?"

"Taste it. Somehow I can't get the old flavor anymore." Tang Ya poured a glass as she spoke and pushed it in front of Old Jin.

Old Jin took a small sip. "That's your own taste that's changed."

Tang Ya paused, tilted her face up to the red lanterns on the ceiling. "I remember you once said someone had saved a condemned man on the execution ground."

Old Jin paused too and waved a quick hand. "That was just spinning yarns. Nonsense."

Tang Ya shook her head and drank the glass down in one. "Not nonsense. I believe it's true."

"Even if it's true, that was another time." Old Jin said, "You know the identity's always verified before the shooting, and so many eyes watching there." "I'll pay double." Tang Ya poured another glass from the shaker and drank it down, and a mist came over her eyes. The money was all what Yang Qun had left her, in installments, by degrees, deposited in her account at the Bank of China. He had known all along that this day would come. He had prepared everything for her.

"No amount of money could bring it off." Old Jin gently pushed the glass away. "Now there are no Japanese planes overhead, and the days are at peace—who would take money over his own life?"

Tang Ya clamped a hand over his and with the other took the shaker and filled his glass to the brim.

Old Jin narrowed his eyes. "Now what are you after?"

The next day Tang Ya woke in a bed at the inn with a splitting head. Old Jin still slept on, snoring. Over Chongqing, most rarely, snow had begun to fall. Naked, she went to stand at the window, gazing without a motion at the snowflakes stuck to the glass, until they blurred to a smear in her eyes and Tang Ya was as cold, all through, as the air itself.

Two days later the whole mountain city lay under a thin snow. A prison van came out through the slowly opening iron gate and wound its way along the muddy mountain road.

All the way, as the van swayed, the only sound in the compartment was the clash of chains. Tang Ya watched, without blinking, the condemned man seated across from her. Jiang Yongnan had plainly just shaved; he looked so clean and so pale, and at the corner of his mouth there seemed to hang a faint smile that only she could see.

Never before had they gazed at each other so long. In the dim, swaying prison van they called to mind every meeting of their lives.

When the prison van came to rest at the execution ground below Mount Gele, in the moment the door opened and they stepped down, Tang Ya spoke at last, saying firmly at Jiang Yongnan's ear, "Remember. When you hear the shot, fall."

After the presiding judge had once more verified his identity, Jiang Yongnan was marched to a patch of ground already swept clear of snow. A bailiff crouched and locked his leg irons to a wooden stake. Now Old Jin came up to Tang Ya, took the rifle from her hands, drew the bolt, checked the chamber, loaded a blank round, worked the bolt shut with a clack, and handed it back to her.

"Ready..." As the officer of the command raised the signal flag high, Tang Ya slowly lifted the rifle. Across the sight, for the first time, she found that Jiang Yongnan's face was so blurred. Then the officer slashed the flag down. "Fire!"

The gun fired. But Jiang Yongnan pitched to the ground before he could hear it. A bullet from the opposite slope had struck him in the forehead, and blood and brains splashed across the ground.

Tang Ya froze, staring from afar at the blood soaking into the yellow earth, and it was a long while before she understood. She threw down the rifle in her hands and, like a madwoman, wheeled and tore up the slope behind her, scrambling on hands and feet, stumbling, her face streaming with tears, until she plunged into the little wood.

But search the little wood as she might, she could not find the shell she had imagined. Panting hard, all she found in the thin snow was a line of bootprints. Following them, she soon came out of the wood and saw, at the roadside, two tire tracks running away into the distance.

When Adjutant Yan came to report, the spent shell in hand, snow had begun to fall again. Guo Bingyan stood a long while in the courtyard, and in the chanting of sutras from the temple next door the snowflakes settling on his face melted, little by little, as though it were wet with tears. He lifted his face to the snow-bright sky and murmured, all at once, "When I knew him, he was still an army doctor. I led him onto this road, and I sent him to his grave."

Adjutant Yan, somewhat unnerved, stood behind Guo Bingyan, and it was a long while before there came back to him a line he could not place: the best end for an agent is to be struck in the head by a bullet from nowhere anyone knows.

That night Tang Ya went, as always, to the executioners' gathering, scouring the smell of blood from herself with the fiercest liquor, until without a word she had drunk herself blind. But on the way to the inn, on Old Jin's arm, she sobered all at once. Leaning into Old Jin's chest, she pressed the Browning pistol to his belly—like a pair of lovers whispering in a snow-banked corner—until he told her about the car that had come into the execution ground.

"I was thinking of you, and I had my brothers to think of too." Old Jin still watched Tang Ya with that pained look of his. "Storming an execution ground—that's the stuff of operas."

Tang Ya let go, weakly, of the hand that had gripped his coat, leaned against the wall, and said, spent, "I should have known long ago. You're one of them too."

"We're all our own people here." As he spoke, Old Jin, wavering, made to bring his face close once more.

Tang Ya gently squeezed the trigger, and after the muffled report Old Jin slid down her body to the ground.

Just then, far off, a string of fireworks rose and lit the silent night sky in a spray of colors. Tang Ya remembered, all at once, that tomorrow was New Year's Eve, the last day of the year.
