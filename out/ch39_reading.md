## The Sword's Fate

Toshizō went by palanquin as far as Kashō-chō, and as he passed under the gate of the barracks,

{j} "My, but it's coming down hard."

Kondō, with the chief among the corps men, had come out to the gate to meet him.

"Toshi."

Kondō clapped him hard on the shoulder. He seemed glad to see him.

"Toshi, when you come into Kyoto even the heavens feel it and send down rain, it seems."

It was a clumsy joke, just like Kondō, but there was something hollow in the way he said it.

(Strange.)

Toshizō was sensitive to such things. Was it not that, with the return of power to the throne, some change had begun to work in Kondō's heart?

(That must be it.)

As they walked the corridor shoulder to shoulder, Kondō's tone shifted again. He spoke now as if to humor Toshizō.

"The road must have tired you."

"Hm."

Toshizō knew the man Kondō well.

Even if he felt such a thing in his heart, Kondō was not the soft sort of man to say it aloud.

"I'm not tired. Looking at the state of you, though, it seems staying in Kyoto is the wearier work."

"You think so."

"Your face is dull. It looks as though you've something in your belly that won't sit still."

"Toshi, there's a thing you don't know."

"Never mind. We'll talk of this later."

That evening, at Kondō's mansion, the corps officers gathered and opened a drinking-party to welcome Toshizō back.

"Hijikata-san, how was Edo?"

{j} said Okita Sōji.

"Ah — I saw your sister, too. I'll tell it in full later."

What struck him as odd was that the mood of the company was not what it had been when he left Kyoto. There was a heaviness somewhere in it.

By nature these were not men to sink into brooding, look them over as you might. Harada Sanosuke was the first of the optimists. Nagakura Shinpachi, too, was a man who had made his peace with death. Then there was the mild, unlettered Inoue Genzaburō; and Okita Sōji, a young man for whom the one sure thing was that he would share life and death with Kondō and Hijikata, and who left all his other cares in the keeping of the gods and buddhas.

Toshizō talked of Edo.

Old Shūsai's illness.

How Satō Hikogorō was faring.

And the reputation of the Shinsengumi in Edo.

"There were no fireworks at Ryōgoku this year. Edo's changed, too. Walking through the town, you'd see no end of samurai going about under those bat-wing umbrellas. It caught on among the hatamoto first, but little by little the townsfolk seem to be taking it up as well."

"Has it changed so much?"

{j} said Nagakura.

Nagakura Shinpachi had left the Matsumae han, and being the son of a low-ranking retainer permanently stationed in Edo, he was Edo-bred through and through. For that reason his homesickness ran deeper than the rest's.

"How I'd like to go back to Edo."

He said it with a look of utter weariness.

"What's brought this on?"

Toshizō stayed the cup at his lips and smiled. This man's smile was a troublesome thing.

"No, there's no reason to it. It's only that you've carried the smell of Edo back with you after so long, Hijikata-san, and so I said it."

"All the same, Shinpachi, I'll not let you go back to Edo."

Toshizō set down his cup.

"Kyoto is the Shinsengumi's battlefield — that is how I understand it."

"But, Toshi —"

{j} came a low voice from the side.

It was Kondō. He was muttering.

"It does well enough for you, keeping to a single tune, but —"

"But?"

"While you were away, Kyoto too has changed."

He must mean the return of power to the throne. In the face of that sudden reversal, Kondō did not know how to conduct himself.

"The shogun has handed the government back to the Imperial Court."

"That talk can wait."

{j} said Toshizō, but Kondō pressed on over him,

"Toshi, hear me out. Three hundred years — no, ever since Lord Minamoto no Yoritomo, Japan's government has been held by the head of a warrior house. There have been risings and fallings of that power, but this is Japan's way from of old. And now, when the Western barbarians have their eyes on the country — now, when we ought to raise up the Barbarian-Subduing Great General and defend the land — can Japan be defended by handing the government over to the court nobles?"

"Just so."

From the lowest seat Harada Sanosuke clapped his hands fit to split them. He was a simple man.

"Sanosuke, hold your tongue."

Kondō checked him.

"And yet — one cannot draw a bow against the Court. Toshi."

"What is it."

Toshizō set down his cup.

"Have you a view on it?"

"A view — sure I have one. But it's nothing so difficult. The Shinsengumi's chief is you. There's no call for you to fret with a pale face like some Minamoto no Kurō Yoshitsune. A chief is a man who does not fret. To show his men always a face without fret, so that they look up to him and feel him steady as Mount Tai — that is a chief. Because you fret, look, the air in the corps has gone strangely hollow."

"This is a thing to talk over."

"Either way, it's needless."

He spat the words out. If it was a thing to talk over, then let Kondō do it quietly with him alone; that was Toshizō's view. For a commander to lay bare his troubles before his men was to see the Shinsengumi crumble away, not tomorrow, but this very day.

***

"Kondō-san."

{j} he said afterward, at Kondō's mansion. None of the other corps men were there.

"Let us go by one thing only — fidelity. Once you begin on the tides of the age, on the Court, on what Satsuma and Chōshū and Tosa are up to, on what the noble Iwakura is doing, the talk goes strange. Kondō-san, scrape the grime off your body."

"Grime?"

"Politics, is what. Since you came to Kyoto you've learned the taste of it. Politics is a thing that shifts by the day. Let yourself be swept up in every turn of it, and there's no telling how many times the Shinsengumi will have to change its colors from here on. A man has his fidelity. That is a thing unaltered from of old. And we —"

{j} Toshizō drained his cooled tea, and then,

"When we first came to Kyoto, there was no thought in our heads of shogunate or Court. It was only to be the vanguard of expelling the barbarians. But as things fell out, our ties to the Aizu han and to the shogunate grew deep. Without knowing it, we drew to that side; and yet to cast it off now would be the ruin of a man's honor. Kondō-san, you're a devoted reader of the Nihon Gaishi, but history is a thing that turns and changes. And within it, the one thing unchanging through the ages is the name of the man who kept faith in his own time. What I want, at this pass, is for the Shinsengumi to be a band of fidelity. Though the branch houses, the related han, the hereditary daimyō, the eighty thousand hatamoto turn their backs on the House of Tokugawa and draw the bow against it, the Shinsengumi will not betray it. Down to the last man, it will not betray it."

"Toshi — Lord Kusunoki was the same."

"You're quite the scholar."

Toshizō gave a small laugh. It was because he had remembered that Itō Kashitarō, who had broken the oath and left, had also been a devotee of Lord Kusunoki.

"But there's no need to borrow the name of some dead Kusunoki. We go by the way of Kondō Isami and Hijikata Toshizō, and that alone is enough."

"But the corps is unsettled. We ought to give out some notice, surely."

"No, words will not do. To make the corps know fidelity is to cut down the man who has none. By that one act, all will grow quiet. First, Itō Settsu, who broke the oath and ran to the Satsuma side."

That Itō, in his Edo days, went by the name Suzuki Daizō has been told already.

In the year he joined the Shinsengumi he changed his name to Kashitarō to mark it, and now, having run to the Satsuma han and become captain of the Goryō-eji, he had altered it again to Settsu.

In those days it was common enough for han-deserters and their like to change their names, but to change it with each turning of his coat was a thing perhaps only Itō Kashitarō did.

It was on the night of the eighteenth of the eleventh month, in the third year of Keiō, that this Itō Kashitarō was assassinated.

He had been invited to Kondō's private residence and had drunk himself blind. It was past ten at night when he took his leave.

There was no wind, but the road was frozen. The moon, already high at the zenith, lit the Kitakōji avenue. Itō walked east. He meant to return to the barracks at Kōdai-ji in Higashiyama.

He had put no light in his lantern. He had brought no attendant. Itō must have trusted too far in his own gift of tongue.

At Kondō's residence Itō had held forth on the affairs of the hour and heaped abuse on the shogunate; it had been almost a one-man stage.

All were moved.

Kondō, for his part, took his hand and,

{j} "Itō-sensei. Let us both press on. Is it not the true wish of a stout man to fall in the service of the nation?"

{j} and there were even tears in his eyes. What was in Kondō's heart, that he wept?

Even a man like Harada Sanosuke was charmed by Itō's eloquence, and would raise a cry of admiration and pour out more wine.

(Dull fellows that they are, once a thing gets through to them the feeling runs all the greater.)

Itō was in a fine humor.

(But Hijikata was not at that gathering.)

At first it had struck him as odd, but cup after cup, it ceased to trouble him.

(As the world changes, a stubborn dolt like that will have no choice but to vanish from the Shinsengumi. I should guess he was ashamed to sit at the same table as I, who had foretold the very crisis of this day.)

That "stubborn dolt" stood in Itō's path, half a chō or so ahead, in the shadow of the gate of the town temple Sōtoku-ji, motionless, his eyes glittering.

Across the way, too, was a temple.

The road before it was narrow, barely wide enough for three men to walk abreast.

The board fences there, the eaves of the townhouses, behind the stacked rain-barrels — every last patch of shadow was breathing in secret.

Itō set his drunken steps onto a bridge. As he crossed the little bridge, he began to chant low a passage from Chikubushima, a Noh song he had learned in his Edo days.

He finished crossing.

The road beyond the bridge ran straight east, and at its far end a black mountain of tiled roofs cut it off — the great halls of the Higashi Hongan-ji.

Itō's chanting went on.

Then it broke off.

A single spear had run him through the root of the neck, from the right.

Itō went on standing where he was.

The windpipe having been missed, he could breathe faintly, but he could not stir. The spear did not move, and Itō Kashitarō did not move.

Just then a man named Mutō Katsuzō, who had stolen round behind him, swung his sword up and struck at Itō.

Itō, quicker still, drew and cut Katsuzō down where he stood, they say; so in an ordinary case there is no knowing what work Itō might have done.

When he made that drawing-cut, the spear that had skewered Itō's neck pulled free.

At the same instant the blood spurted out. It comes to this: that being pinned by the spear was the one thing that had barely kept Itō alive.

Five or six paces he walked, with a step astonishingly sure, and then, with a sound like a squared timber being rolled, he toppled sideways to the ground.

He was dead.

"The battle begins now."

{j} and Toshizō began to walk.

He was like a demon.

(This is what becomes of the man who loses his fidelity.)

Itō's corpse he left cast down in the very middle of the crossroads at Shichijō Aburanokōji, as a lure. Before long, by the report of the town officials, word of it would reach the Goryō-eji barracks in Higashiyama.

In all likelihood they would come rushing, every man of them armed.

To lie in wait for them and annihilate the oath-breakers' band at a single stroke: that was Toshizō's tactic. A man who would hit upon so cruel and pitiless a stratagem as to bait a trap with the corpse of the enemy's own commander must be rare in all history.

He did not treat Itō as a human being.

So deeply did Toshizō hate the prime villain who had split the Shinsengumi, his own handiwork, to the very brink of ruin.

And it was the same toward the rest of that faction.

"Soon the lot of them will come. Let not one man escape the sword."

{j} he ordered sternly, to the forty-odd corps men turned out for the action.

***

Toshizō took over the whole of an udon shop, the Yoshiji, on the east side three doors north of the crossroads at Aburanokōji and Shichijō, and quartered the main body of the action party there.

The rest he set in groups of three, hidden here and there about the crossroads.

The moon began to sink at last, but still they did not come.

"Hijikata-san, will they come?"

Harada Sanosuke put the question from the earthen floor to Toshizō, who sat on the raised sill of the Yoshiji.

"They'll come."

He was certain of it. In truth the men of Itō's party were skilled hands, and many of them fierce of temper. To save their leader's corpse from dishonor, they would forget life and death.

Meanwhile, at the Goryō-eji barracks in the Gettsuin at Kōdai-ji, misfortunes had piled up that night. There were only a small number of men within.

The corps officers Arai Tadao and Kiyohara Kiyoshi had gone down to the Kantō to recruit.

Utsumi Jirō and Abe Jūrō, Itō's live-in disciples, had gone off the day before deep into Mount Inari to hunt with guns, and had not yet returned to quarters.

With Itō gone, it fell naturally to Shinohara Tainoshin — his counselor, and the eldest among them — to take the position of command.

After sending back the town official who had brought the urgent word, Shinohara quieted his clamoring comrades and,

{j} "The thing is to recover the body. In all likelihood they lie waiting for us. But recover the body we must, come what may; on anything beyond that we should waste no needless thought."

"Shinohara-san."

{j} It was Itō's own younger brother, Suzuki Mikisaburō, who said it. He was trembling.

"They are men we've long known. We are all acquainted. If we go to take him up with every courtesy, surely nothing need come of it?"

"With every courtesy?"

Shinohara laughed. Men who understood a samurai's courtesy would not have murdered Itō by treachery.

"There is nothing for it but to fight."

{j} said Hattori Takeo. He was a man who had once been called a swordsman without equal even among the Shinsengumi.

"Shinohara-san, let us go in armor."

"That will not do."

Shinohara ordered them all into ordinary dress. His state of mind at this moment is set down thus in a memoir Shinohara Tainoshin wrote after the Restoration:

— If we come to fight the villains, the foe are many and we are few. Yet were we to don armor and die in the roadway, later ages would laugh at our cowardice.

The men turned out for the sortie were seven.

Shinohara Tainoshin, Suzuki Mikisaburō, Kanō Michinosuke, Tomiyama Yahei, Tōdō Heisuke, Hattori Takeo, Mōnai Kenmotsu. All rode in palanquins.

With them, two porters to carry off Itō's remains, and one manservant.

By the time they came down the Higashiyama slope it was past one in the morning.

— We hurried to the Aburanokōji.

Looking about on every side, all was desolate, as though not a soul were there. So we came at once to the place of Itō's death, and seeing how he had been struck down we all raised a cry of grief, and were making haste to lift the bloodied corpse into a palanquin when the villains' soldiers sprang out from three sides, every one in chain mail, and fell upon us slashing without mercy. Their number was some forty-odd men.

Toshizō watched the fight from beneath the eaves of the Yoshiji, his arms folded.

The moon lit the melee in the road.

At the ferocity with which Tōdō Heisuke and Hattori Takeo fought, even Toshizō felt a shudder run through his body. They would not give a single step to flight.

Leaping past to cut, leaping in to cut, they cut with never a wasted stroke.

"Hijikata-san, let me go out."

{j} said Nagakura Shinpachi, held in reserve.

"No. Leave it to the new men."

"With respect, it only adds to the dead."

Nagakura sprang out.

As Toshizō watched, Nagakura drove into the press like a bullet and came out in front of Tōdō, an old friend since the binding of the oath in Edo.

"Heisuke, it's Nagakura."

{j} he said, and drawing his sword, he drew close to the eaves and opened the way south for him, as much as to say: run.

Tōdō caught Nagakura's kindness and made to run. It must have been the moment of relief that undid him; his guard went slack at his back. Onto that back a rank-and-file trooper, a certain Miura, brought down a stroke.

Tōdō had taken by then a dozen and more wounds on his body.

Even so he did not go down; he cut Miura, but at last his strength gave out, and he dropped his sword and pitched headfirst into the gutter beneath the eaves and died.

Hattori Takeo was more terrible still. The men he merely wounded must have numbered twenty. Even the corps' most consummate hands, Harada Sanosuke and Shimada Kai, could not ward off Hattori's blade and took wounds of it. In time he, too, fell fighting.

Mōnai Kenmotsu, too, fell fighting.

Shinohara, Suzuki, Kanō, and Tomiyama had slipped away swiftly in the early part of the fray.

The dead, strangely enough, were every one first-rate swordsmen. Even had they tried to break away, their swords would not permit it. The sword moved of itself, striking down enemy after enemy, driving its owner on into the place of death, deeper and deeper.

(He who lives by the sword dies, in the end, by the sword.)

So Toshizō thought, of a sudden.

By the time he stepped out from under the eaves, the moon had gone down. Toshizō set off alone down the pitch-black Shichijō avenue.

The stars were out.
