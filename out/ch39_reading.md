## Chapter 7. Looking Before and After, Providing Against the Storm

Would that history should not repeat itself—by which I mean that we have had our fill of bitter and painful lessons; and if only the good times of this world would come round again and again, how much better that would be.

After every large-scale slaughter there must needs be dead and wounded past counting, and so the phrases run: corpses strewn across the field, blood flowing in channels; of set expressions of this kind there may be several score. But what was to be done with those bodies covering the ground? Of that the records tell very little. Why? One reason is that they could not bear to look; men were unwilling even to spare them a second glance, let alone to fix them in memory and afterward set them down.

This chapter has just such a scene in it: to say nothing else, the coffins requisitioned alone came to several thousand.

The text also speaks of the saying, “fleeing calamity, they met their calamity instead.” The country folk, at the first crack of gunfire, were already out of their wits: some, at their wit’s end, could only resign themselves to fate; others still tried to struggle on, and so, following the crowd, ran this way and that, until without knowing it they blundered across the firing line, and in their confusion stumbled into the City of the Wrongfully Dead.

This was much like fleeing the air-raid alarms at Chongqing in 1939: he who did not flee might well have come to no harm, while he who fled ran straight into it. The writer does not accept that talk of “what is numbered among one’s fates cannot be escaped,” that notion that such was one’s appointed lot; the blame ought to be laid upon those responsible for making the war and the turmoil.

Fu Zuoyi, now commander-in-chief of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters, had in 1932 once been commander of the Thirty-fifth Army; after his promotion the post changed hands several times over. By 1948 the commander of the Thirty-fifth Army, with its fine record of battle, was General Lu Yingqing.

It was in the Laishui campaign that, having lost men and lost officers, and following the division commander Li Mingding, who had raised his pistol and taken his own life, Commander Lu too in his anguish took his own life.

Thereafter the command of the Thirty-fifth Army passed to General Guo Jingyun. In the winter of 1948 the army, by order of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters, raced to Zhangyuan to lift the siege of the Eleventh Army Group. The troops arrived, the siege was raised; but unexpectedly, on the way back to their posts, they fell into an ambush, and fighting as they withdrew were at last surrounded at the town of Xinbao’an on the Ping-Sui line.

They could not break out, relief came to nothing, the airdrops could not be kept up, and in the end their shot and their provisions alike were spent; yet still they fought on from house to house, striving valiantly to the last man. Commander Guo too took his own life and died for his country, a martyr in glory.

Only because it was a battle lost, and because Fu Zuoyi afterward went over to the Communists, deeds so brave and so worthy of honor came to be little spoken of.

From the latter half of 1948, the military campaign to suppress the rebellion met with reverses on many fronts, and the situation of the whole country was in turmoil and unrest. The Corps headquarters convened a conference at Nanjing, and Mr. Zheng Jiemin gave word, outside the sessions, that the planning of “stay-behind work” was to be set in hand at once. Taking this as my cue, I formed, with a view to the whole unit’s future, the following intent: that those whose conditions of survival were suitable and who volunteered to stay behind and work should, after a short technical briefing, lie low and unstirring, cultivating meanwhile a footing and a cover identity; and that all the rest should be transferred south. And so I asked the deputy brigade commander, Brother Li Yulin, to go up to the capital and first secure the Corps headquarters’ support.

Such a move, coming from a military unit, and one that had moreover been assigned a fixed work area, was no ordinary thing.

### 1. What I Saw and Did While Clearing the Battlefield

This body of material was provided by Commanding Officer Wang Zhaofen of our unit’s “First Command Room.” Fellow student Wang Zhaofen was himself a graduate of the Military Academy; while training in the “Lizhi Class” he served as district-company commander of the First Company, and often took charge of my company in my stead.

The fellow students now in Taiwan all look upon him as their “senior elder brother,” which is of course also a mark of the respect they bear him.

Fellow student Wang Zhaofen had, in the matter of the “suppression of rebellion and pacification,” a theory of his own built up out of practice—neither rigid nor halting, thoroughgoing and well-joined; and had he but pursued it more deeply, it would have grown into a system.

Below is the original text of what fellow student Wang Zhaofen set down under the title “The Unfolding of the First Command Room’s Work.” 1. The Selection of the Work Base. By the thirty-sixth year of the Republic, the work of suppressing the rebellion had, owing to military reverses and the political unrest of the recovered areas, gradually lost its control of the “surfaces,” so that we were confined by the enemy to a posture of holding “points and lines.” The early aim of the pacification work was precisely to join point to point into lines, and by degrees to develop the lines into surfaces. Thus, when the first class of the Lizhi Class completed its training, the personnel allotted to the “First Brigade” were rather few, and we could at first establish only two “Command Rooms.” In keeping with the central authorities’ hope that the throwing open of the two railway lines, the Ping-Han and the Jin-Pu, should be our chief task, we first established the “First Command Room” on the northern section of the Ping-Han line and the “Second Command Room” on the northern section of the Jin-Pu line. At that time the Ping-Han line from Anyang in Henan to Shijiazhuang in Hebei had already been cut, and the reach of the military and political power of the “Beiping Field Headquarters” and of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters extended only from Beiping as far as Shijiazhuang. Hence as to where the “First Command Room” had best be sited, there was much to ponder. For a central position, it should have been chosen at Baoding, which would have been convenient both for command and control and for coordinating with the Party and government of Hebei province; but measured against the pacification work’s first concerns—the gray zones and the political front line of the newly recovered areas—that seemed less than fitting; and so, after much thought, it was decided to place it at Zhuoxian, and thereafter, as circumstances allowed, to distribute its subordinate units in balanced fashion.

At first, hampered by the shortage of personnel, we could only group the grassroots work units in mixed formation, and could not form up the four great categories of work—pacification, intelligence, assault, and action—each independently to carry out its own task: what the “First Command Room” formed in the earliest period were the “Shijiazhuang Mixed Group” and the “Zhuoxian Mixed Group”; and only after the second class of the “Lizhi Class” had been assigned to the unit did we then form two further district work groups, at “Baoding” and at “Wanping.”

2. Entering the Work Areas. Once the work units were formed up, they had each to enter its own work area separately. This looks a plain and easy matter, but in fact it took no end of trouble. Such things as bringing the personnel to the assigned place and lodging them there—how to arrange board and bed, to whom to report, with whom to keep in touch—on all this the higher authorities gave the several commanding officers no outline of work guidance, whether spoken or written, still less handed them any official letter with which to coordinate and make contact with the units concerned; and so, just as the epigraph of the second chapter said, it had to depend on the comrades’ own initiative and self-motivation, their inventiveness and their drive.

After the “First Command Room” had entered its work area, we were rather fortunate—in a way one may chance upon but cannot seek out—for the following several reasons: (1) The magistrate of Zhuoxian, Mr. Wang Yousheng, was a fellow student of Zhaofen’s from the same year and the same company at the Military Academy.

(2) The commissioner of the local Administrative Inspectorate Commissioner’s Office, Professor Li Zhongyong, had been a colleague of Zhaofen’s when he was engaged in the training of press personnel at Chongqing.

(3) The peace-preservation commander of the northern section of the Ping-Han Railway, Mr. Zhang Yinwu, stood in the relation of teacher to pupil with Zhaofen, and was moreover the witness at Zhaofen’s wedding.

(4) The chief of staff of the Peace-Preservation Command, General Zhao Boheng, was likewise an old acquaintance of Zhaofen’s.

(5) The principal staff officers of the headquarters of the Ninety-fourth Army stationed there, and the cadres at division and regiment level under it, were for the most part Zhaofen’s fellow students of the same year at the Military Academy.

Because there were so many personal connections of this kind, dealings became a great deal easier. And so the “First Command Room” not only met with no difficulty in matters of business, but found the ground favorable for the work that was to unfold thereafter.

3. Some of the More Effective Results After the Work Got Under Way. On first reaching Zhuoxian, all was a blank; we did not even know which way to turn. Though the broad premise was to assist the administrative organs, to reorganize or rebuild the administrative system, to comfort and gather in the displaced and settle the people’s hearts, and to collect military and political intelligence for relay to our superiors or for the reference of the local military and civil organs—yet where lay the concrete direction of action, the object of the work? It was truly like a dog biting a hedgehog: there was no place to get one’s teeth in. As luck would have it, Commissioner Li Zhongyong knew something of our tasks, and in that time of training press cadres at Chongqing the “revolutionary technique” of the teaching materials had been near akin and closely related to the pacification work; and so he made a special request that our “First Command Room” send men to help train the administrative cadres of the districts under his commissioner’s office. Thus was formed a relationship of cooperation that both sides had need of.

A number of the townships and villages belonging to the counties neighboring Zhuoxian had long been held by the Japanese army, the puppet Imperial Auxiliary forces, and the Communist army; and the countrymen of these districts, hearing with joy of the central government’s benevolent intentions and benevolent governance toward the people of the recovered areas, one and all raised their hands to their brows in celebration and rejoiced beyond measure. This power of allegiance, welling up from the people’s own hearts, worked upon the localities a settling effect worth who knows how many “Peace-Preservation Regiments” or “Peace-Preservation Brigades”—every county of Hebei province had its own county peace-preservation force, that is, a “Peace-Preservation Battalion.” Every commissioner’s district had one “Peace-Preservation Regiment,” which was then enlarged in turn into a “Peace-Preservation Brigade.”

Seen in this light, the propagation of central policy, and indeed the conduct of local administration, ought to be counted a chief part of the pacification work; and so, applying for registration in a private capacity, under my own name (Wang Zhaofen), and having obtained approval, I founded the Zhenmin Herald at Zhuoxian, and engaged as editor-in-chief Mr. Zhao Baichuan (his original name Zhao Mingshan; his friends jestingly called him “the Star Shell”), who to this day is still engaged in press work in Taiwan, running the Taoyuan Weekly. This publication was much needed by the grassroots administrative organs, and so its circulation grew day by day, and with it the renown of the unit whose code-name was “0760” came little by little to be spoken of. At that time another commissioner’s district lying between Beiping, Tianjin, and Baoding (probably the “Third-District Administrative Inspectorate Commissioner’s Office”) had as its commissioner Wang Fenggang, a man who had graduated from a simplified normal school and worked his way up from primary-school teacher; he understood the temper of the people in his district very thoroughly, and his methods of carrying out the anti-Communist policy were correspondingly severe—which is to say, he was fond of killing. Hence the Communist Party found it very hard to operate within his jurisdiction, and this district looked to be somewhat more settled, on which account it was praised as the “iron triangle of Beiping-Tianjin-Baoding”; but the Communist Party too racked its brains to bring him down and do away with him. On one occasion, seizing the chance while he had left his post on official business elsewhere, they suddenly threw against his commissioner’s office a force several times his own; and it was thanks to Commissioner Wang Fenggang’s staunch and resolute bound-footed wife that the place held—she, heartening all the civil and military officers, held out, of all things, for three whole days and nights, and going without sleep or rest moved along the stockade wall comforting the fighters—until at the last, the odds too uneven, it was given up. Yet this bout of fighting, for all its casualties, yielded one precious lesson: that in the struggle against the Communists one cannot rely on force alone, and that whether or not the people cooperate is in truth the decisive factor.

It was probably because Commissioner Li Zhongyong had advised him that in the pacification work he should keep in closer touch with the “0760” unit that Commissioner Wang Fenggang once came specially to Zhuoxian to seek out Zhaofen; but it happened that Zhaofen had gone to Baoding, so Commissioner Wang made a special trip to Baoding after him, only to draw a blank once more.

Little did one think Commissioner Wang had such a stubborn, ox-like streak in him: he turned back from Baoding to Zhuoxian. Again he missed his man, and hurried on once more to Beiping. This time, at last, we met—at the Far East Hotel. When the pleasantries were done and he had made his errand plain, he borrowed a line from the opera and said with a touch of drollery: “General! What a weary chase you have led me!” We talked the night through at the hotel, and were much of one mind. Because he needed our help, he insisted on posting a liaison man to be stationed permanently at the Zhuoxian “First Command Room”; this Zhaofen gently declined; but in the end Commissioner Wang changed his tack and introduced his cousin Sun Zuyi to Zhaofen, nominally as an aide-de-camp, though in truth he still meant to learn a trick or two, his object being to carry our methods back with him. (Whether he had some further design, one could not determine.)

(Wang Fenggang afterward died of illness in a Taiwan prison, in connection with some case.) There was one thing more: a “performance” put on by us, a demonstration for the local corps, its theme the use of our own armed force to control the “surface” of a district by roving movement—a dozen men and more of this Command Room’s assault group, together with one company of the Zhuoxian Peace-Preservation Battalion, struck at the village of Xiaodaocun on the border of Zhuo and Lai (Laishui county). At that time the local forces knew only how to hold fast and dared not stir abroad; and hearing that they were to go so deep into enemy country, they were fairly struck pale at the very name of the tiger. But Zhaofen, on the strength of four years of regular-army guerrilla fighting in the Taihang Mountains and of the nerve tempered in him thereby, went in and came out in perfect safety, as though through a land without men. This action was indeed a coming and going at our own will, and so it stiffened the courage of the local corps, and its influence was such that thereafter they all grew lively and bold.

In this region there had been a saying of old: “By day it belongs to the Nationalists; by night it belongs to the Communists.” Ever since the “0760” unit had taken the lead in activity, the situation had greatly changed: the sabotage by which the Communist army’s militia, under cover of night, tore up the rails and blew the bridges had been greatly checked; and the locomotives running from Beiping to Baoding no longer had to push ahead of them a train of four or five loaded freight cars as a roller to set off the mines the enemy had buried.

Before the Laishui campaign we had given the units concerned an early warning. The “Baoding Group” made use of a gray figure named Cui Laoxuan, a man of Jiannan village in Wangdu county; being a “man of two faces,” he had his conveniences in going in and out of enemy territory. In the Laishui campaign, when the enemy army in front was massing toward the hill country of Lai and Yi (Yixian) and its coming movements were unclear, we sent Cui Laoxuan to follow at the enemy’s heels and report to us from time to time. Cui, fearing he could not win our full trust, had his youngest son Cui Wanxing take Zhaofen as his godfather, to show his sincerity. And so, throughout this period, the workers of ours whom Cui Laoxuan led into bandit territory were all quite safe, and the intelligence he sent back was very sound. But from that time on Cui Laoxuan never came back again: had he died among the disordered troops? or been found out and put to death? To this there was never any answer. His son Cui Wanxing, Zhaofen could only treat as his own; and carrying him some thousands of li through all his wanderings, he brought him to Taiwan, where the boy afterward took a wife and had children, and is now full grown—so that Cui Laoxuan may close his eyes in peace at the Nine Springs.

The Heroism of the Laishui Campaign and Its Aftermath. The main body of our forces in the Laishui campaign was the division of Li Mingding, under Fu Zuoyi’s Thirty-fifth Army and its commander Lu Yingqing. When the battle opened, though both sides took casualties, Li Mingding’s division held the upper hand, and a great number of the bandit army surrendered on the field; but Li’s division was obliged to use no small part of its strength to guard the prisoners, and being thus hampered, it met with defeat. When the hard fighting was over, corpses truly lay strewn across the field, a sight too dreadful to look upon. The division commander Li Mingding, seeing how heavy were the casualties among his men, raised his pistol and took his own life; and the army commander Lu Yingqing, on hearing the news, hastened to the “Zhangfeidian” railway station, and beholding the sight wept aloud; without a word he strode into the blockhouse at the western end of the station and there too raised his pistol and made an end of himself. In this one battle, besides the countless dead and wounded among the rank and file, two great commanders were lost at once—a spirit that may well move men to reverence!

When Fu Zuoyi heard the news—these being troops of his own line—he felt a grief that bit to the very bone; and besides resolving to give Commander Lu and Division Commander Li a rich encoffining, he ordered that every one of the dead be buried in a coffin. He therefore commanded that coffins be requisitioned in great quantity from every quarter of Hebei, Chahar, and Suiyuan, and sent on to Zhuoxian without cease, by day and by night. For a time there were coffins of every shape and kind laid out within a radius of two or three li around the Zhuoxian station.

The magistrate of Zhuoxian, my fellow student Wang Yousheng, was at his wit’s end: how was one small county government to cope? And so he asked that our “0760” unit step forward to coordinate every quarter—Party, government, and army—and, widening the area drawn upon, appeal for gifts of money and shrouds to encoffin the dead, that they might be distributed among them and their loyal souls comforted.

This special task, too, showed how much our “0760” unit was respected in the locality. Afterward Fu sent the commander of the Provisional Third Army, An Chunshan, expressly to the “First Command Room” to give thanks.” So ends the passage on “the opening-up of the work” set down by Commanding Officer Wang Zhaofen of the “First Command Room.” It is not long, but it holds much instruction.

The writer’s note: To speak of “opening up the work” was no easy thing—even these four words are not often met with. Without living through it oneself, one can hardly grasp the flavor of it. To put it in a single short sentence: unless one could master beforehand, one by one, the unlooked-for difficulties, there was no setting one’s hand to the carrying-out of the task.

In the account fellow student Wang Zhaofen has furnished above, mention is also made of the matter of “work guidance.” For several tens of years I have on the whole said little of these things; but now there is no harm in speaking plainly, and there may even be some small point in it—at that time the brigade headquarters was in Beiping, and sent out its many work units in radiating fashion; and by rights—for it was, indeed, a thing needful—each unit before it set out ought to have had a “work guidance.” Its content, broadly, would have taken in: the assignment of the basic task, the current requirements of the work, and a brief on the general state of each such district; besides which, such things as the pointing-out of particular tasks or the flagging of special circumstances would have had to be given case by case, according to the differences of the working environment. In fact, the brigade headquarters did not do all this: for one thing, it could not—the brigade headquarters itself did not fully understand how things actually stood on the ground; and for another, it always supposed that everyone already knew perfectly well, so that there was no need to say it over again. This was, to be sure, less than careful. Yet the field units were many, and not every one of them was so.

In the foregoing pages I have mentioned, one after another, three administrative inspector-commissioners of Hebei province: Zhu Zhankui in the sixth chapter, and Li Zhongyong and Wang Fenggang in this one. They belonged to three commissioner’s districts, each with its own office, and not to one and the same; only, because I cannot clearly recall the designations of these three commissioner’s offices, I have left them all out of the text. Roughly, say, of Zhu Zhankui and Wang Fenggang, the one was probably the Third and the other the Fourth; while as for Li Zhongyong, his may have been the Second district.

Moreover, as to the administrative divisions of Hebei province and of its several districts—that is, how many counties in all Hebei province had, a hundred and some score? into how many commissioner’s districts it was divided? which counties each district governed?—of this I am for the moment even less clear, and so in some places I dared not set pen to paper. In truth, a glance at the records would make it plain; the fault is only that I was a little lazy.

Let us leave aside how Fu Zuoyi afterward went over to the Communists: in the “Laishui campaign” of the thirty-seventh year, the heroic deaths of the Thirty-fifth Army’s commander Lu Yingqing and its division commander Li Mingding were of course a sacrifice for the suppression of the rebellion, a dying “for one’s country” in the line of duty.

Below, a few more sorrowful words. “Corpses strewn across the field”—this set phrase is common enough in the storybooks; seen too often, it loses its sting, and one takes it for mere figure of speech; but if it be the plain truth, who has ever seen it with his own eyes? Fellow student Wang Zhaofen has said: “Within a radius of two or three li around the Zhuoxian station, coffins were laid out everywhere.” Close your eyes and picture it: into every one of those coffins a corpse was to be laid—what a scene is that! In those days, that is to say forty years ago, the battlefields where the two sides met and clashed across the whole country, great and small, were past all reckoning; and one may be sure that at every hour and every moment there were dead and wounded. The newspapers carried their reports, but dwelt only on who won and who lost, and on the wastage of equipment and the casualties of the troops. Never once did one see a description of the handling of the bodies. That Fu Zuoyi should order the bestowal of a coffin apiece—that already counted as a mercy.

Writing to this point, the writer cannot but be put in mind that not all the bodies on the battlefield were necessarily of one side only; and if there were among them bodies of the other side as well, then what was to be done?

As it happened, only the other day at a wedding feast I met fellow student Wang Zhaofen. That at another man’s happy occasion we should talk of such things—was it not a sad spoiling of the mood? And so fellow student Zhaofen took up his pen and wrote out this further passage, “Supplementary Material on the Settling of the Aftermath of the Laishui Campaign,” which the writer has put into a little order as follows: “The thorny problems that arose in dealing with the dead and the wounded of the battlefield: the wounded, sent without distinction of side to the hospital, were a simpler matter, of which I shall not speak here; but the gathering up and encoffining of the dead was troublesome indeed. (1) First arose the problem of identifying who they were. Our own officers and men could perhaps be told from their clothing and their kit.

(2) The common folk who on the spot suffered as the innocent bystander—among them some who died in fleeing the calamity and some caught in the midst of the firing line, struck down by shot and shell that have no eyes—these could not be told apart.

(3) Whether a man was a civilian laborer press-ganged along by the Communist army, or one of the Communist army’s own officers and men, was likewise hard to tell, for the Communist army did not necessarily wear uniform.

Then again, how were the Communist army’s officers and men to be dealt with?

(1) Bury them where they lay?

(2) Hand them over to the local civilian bodies?

(3) Or encoffin them likewise and bury them apart, to show the state’s compassion for the people?

In the end the principle was decided: all alike were to be put into coffins and buried separately, each with a marker set up. At the encoffining, the bodies were of grotesque and contorted shapes, blurred with blood and flesh, a sight one truly could not bear to look upon. I remember that on the first day we gathered up and encoffined seven hundred and sixty-nine; after that I lost count; in all, those handed over to the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters came to over three thousand and some hundreds.” Here fellow student Zhaofen’s supplement ends; the writer has again left out a few sentences.

What follows is another activity at Zhuoxian. Fellow student Meng Guangdi, leader of the “Baoding Group” under the “First Command Room,” while he was still at Zhuoxian—where the “First Command Room” had its seat—once took part in a “Cleanliness and Hygiene Movement” jointly got up by the various local bodies; of the matter of this movement Meng Guangdi has made a brief record, here set down word for word as follows: “In the spring of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic, the director of the political department of the Provisional Third Army stationed at Zhuoxian, the administrative inspector-commissioner, and the major who represented our headquarters’ ‘First Command Room’ jointly formed a ‘Cleanliness and Hygiene Guidance Small Group,’ and under the cover-name of ‘Cleanliness and Hygiene’ launched a ‘Cleanse-the-Source, Root-Out-Traitors’ movement, aimed above all at the Communist spies lying hidden in the outskirts of Zhuoxian.

“This movement, because it had the support of the garrison troops, could be carried through thoroughly and without hindrance; and with the administrative commissioner taking part, communication with the local grassroots bodies was made easy. But the one who really had charge of the business was our ‘0760’ unit.

“Because we had beforehand got hold of the materials concerning the Communist Party’s hidden ‘intelligence work-station’ at Zhuoxian, at the very outset of the movement we arrested, with a suddenness that gave no time to cover the ears against the thunderclap, the head of that ‘work-station,’ Chen Fengtong, together with his confederates.

“After Chen Fengtong was taken, we went further and put him into the uniform of the Nationalist army, with two officers in uniform to accompany him, so as to keep clear of passers-by’s eyes and ears; and, Chen Fengtong picking them out one after another, we seized at the Zhuoxian railway station and in the warehouses near the station a dozen and more of the party’s adherents. When we tried to follow the trail further, they had all caught wind of it and fled into hiding.

“To speak of how we came to have the materials on the Communist Party’s ‘work-station’ in the first place—that too took some effort. At that time our tasks were: to organize and train the people in support of the Nationalist army, to gather intelligence and understand the enemy’s dispositions, in ordinary times to help the local corps defend their native soil, and, when there was fighting, to clear the battlefield besides. It was once, while clearing a battlefield, that we came upon a good many suspicious persons, and taking them back questioned them a while, but got nothing out of it. We could only lodge them separately in the lockup of the Zhuoxian police bureau, and keeping each one apart so that they could pass no word to one another nor rehearse a common story, we neither beat them nor cursed them nor even questioned them; they ate white rice and white steamed buns, with cabbage, bean-thread noodles, and pork stewed with bean curd, two full meals a day; and in no more than a few days they had eaten themselves plump and fair. Then, with a word of ‘we have wronged you,’ we let them go home.

“The homes of these men all lay in the middle ground where the Nationalist and bandit armies stood facing each other, and the Communist bandits often came out by night to work in these places. The moment they opened their mouths they pressed these men about how they had been tormented after the Nationalist army seized them; and these men told the plain truth—‘no beating, no cursing, white rice and white flour to eat one’s fill’—which roused the Communist bandits’ suspicion that they were hiding something, so that they were abused instead. In this way, back and forth, and with our sending men besides to talk them round, these men in the end became our eyes and ears, and by every means they could contrive brought us word of matters great and small: that the men the Communist bandits sent to work at Zhuoxian all had to pass through those men’s villages, and would sometimes even stop there a night. Little by little all this became a thread; and unraveling it strand by strand, we ran Chen Fengtong to earth at last.” The writer’s note: To break up one Communist-spy organ was, at that time, but a small matter; what is worth remarking on, rather, is the importance of the people’s allegiance. After the victory in the War of Resistance, of the many measures taken, had they not lost the people’s hearts, how should things ever have come to such utter ruin?

### 2. A Strategy Revised Again and Again, Still Without Direction

Our unit’s “Fifth Command Room” was set up at Zhangyuan (Zhangjiakou in Chahar), its commanding officer Brother Zhang Luying. Had not Brother Luying furnished this account, the writer would in truth remember very little of the affairs of the “Fifth Command Room.” For this I owe an apology to the working comrades of the “Fifth Command Room,” and at the same time all the more thanks to Brother Luying for his timely help, without which I could not have written it at all.

Zhang Luying was a graduate of the Army War College; during the War of Resistance he served in the staff office of the First War Zone commander-in-chief’s headquarters at Xi’an, in its Second Section (intelligence), as lieutenant-colonel staff officer and section head and the like. After Japan’s surrender in 1945, the First War Zone posted him to Beiping under the name of setting up a Beiping liaison office, charged with the collection of intelligence on the Chinese Communists.

Between September and October of 1947, in the student brigade’s First Company of the “Lizhi Training Class of the Central Training Corps” at Nanjing, he served as lieutenant-colonel deputy company commander, taking charge of leading and training the trainees of the First Company of the second class; and at the end of October, after the second class had completed its training, he led the company from Nanjing by way of Shanghai, taking ship to Tianjin and thence to the brigade headquarters at Beiping, to rejoin the unit.

In November 1947 he was appointed lieutenant-colonel brigade-adjutant of the “First Brigade.” Presently the brigade was authorized to establish a “Fifth Command Room,” and Brother Zhang Luying was appointed its commanding officer, and went to Zhangjiakou to set up the work; after about half a year, when the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters moved from Zhangyuan to Beiping, he was recalled to the brigade headquarters as brigade-adjutant, and besides taking charge of staff business, dealt exclusively with liaison and coordination with the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters.

What follows is the original text furnished by Brother Zhang Luying under the title “A General Account of the Fifth Command Room’s Work at Zhangyuan.”—“In early December of the thirty-sixth year, in the very season of cold weather, I led the several comrades and fellow students to Zhangjiakou, found and fixed on office premises, and set up the Command Room. We began to make contact with the various units concerned.

“Planning the disposition of the work, we completed the assignment of tasks. The disposition was, broadly, as follows: The ‘Zhangyuan Intelligence Group,’ its basic task the collection of intelligence on the bandit situation in the environs of the provincial capital, Zhangyuan.

“The ‘Cha-Sui Pacification Intelligence Group,’ its basic task the intelligence on the bandit situation of western Chahar and of the Suiyuan region.

“The ‘Duolun Intelligence Group,’ staffed by fellow students of Mongol race, with fellow student Bai Dezhao as its leader, and with Gongchuge Ceden (younger brother of the Kanjurwa Khutukhtu), Wu Ruishan, Renqin Daorji, and others as its members.

“The ‘Xuanhua Pacification Group,’ operating in the eastern Chahar region; the ‘Huai’an Pacification Group,’ operating in the southern Chahar region.

“The ‘Assault Group,’ stationed at Shacheng, its early work centered on the eastern Chahar region, with Sun Wenliang as its leader.

“In the above disposition, though there was a division of emphasis among intelligence, pacification, and assault, the actual work still had its flexibility: thus the intelligence group was chiefly given to intelligence activity; the pacification group, besides putting its emphasis on organizing and training the people’s armed force and pacifying the locality, also carried on intelligence and assault; and the assault group, besides carrying out mobile assaults, also, as circumstances allowed, undertook other related work.

“The work of the several groups unfolded quickly, and already bore fruit: (1) Because our attitude to the work was active and earnest, and our coordination with the various units concerned was good, we won no little regard and support from the responsible organs of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters, its Second and Third Sections (intelligence and operations).

“(2) We came to understand the basic conditions of the Chahar-Suiyuan and Inner Mongolian regions, and collected intelligence on the movements of the Communist bandits, which, besides being reported to the brigade headquarters, was also furnished near at hand for the reference of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters.

“(3) In the eastern and southern Chahar regions we organized and trained the people’s armed force, assaulted the Communist bandits’ ‘people’s armed forces,’ carried out the pacification of the locality, strengthened the civil defense, and roused the people’s spirit, and had won the affection of the local people.

“(4) Between April and May of the thirty-seventh year, when the bandit army’s ‘military-district troops’ struck at the county town of Huai’an, our Huai’an ‘Pacification Group’ mobilized the county’s local armed force and its people to rise up in resistance, and dealt the bandit army a very heavy blow. In this action our ‘Pacification Group’ had comrades wounded, among them Zhong Ningshou.” So much for Brother Zhang Luying’s brief record.

The writer’s note: Before the “Fifth Command Room” was established, the brigade headquarters had already dispatched fellow student Wang Zhiyi to Duolun county to open up the work. Fellow student Wang Zhiyi has recalled: “After I came back north from Nanjing (the first class of the ‘Lizhi Class’), I was straightway sent to Duolun county in Chahar province, where I stayed two months and more, charged with the collection of military intelligence.” Communication between Duolun and Beiping was difficult, and even when important intelligence could be got, by the time it reached Beiping it had mostly lost its timeliness. Just as we were considering the need to set up a radio station there, Duolun had already fallen; and to set up a station then, there was no longer any footing for it. Having had at the outset no long-term plan of “coming to ground and striking root,” but seeking only quick results and a lucky snatch—that was in the end a thing not to be relied on.

Brother Zhang Luying mentions that at Zhangyuan his coordination with the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters was good. This, one would say, was no more than a duty that ought to have been discharged; but in fact it was already far from easy. I have said above that Fu Zuoyi, commander-in-chief of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters, had a “sectarian bias,” which, besides narrowness, may also be construed as a rather strong “parochial outlook.” The more so because they—all the troops and staff whom Fu Zuoyi looked on as his own sons and brothers—had for so long endured too many hard days, so that even their way of life was not altogether like that of the general run of men. All this has, to be sure, its background of formation and existence, and though there is that in it which may be questioned, what I mean to point out here bears only on that one trait of theirs: the difficulty of their “blending in” with the outside. That my work unit could set up a work organ at Zhangjiakou, which they regarded as their own “sphere of influence,” and could operate there to the full, was no small thing; and that it reached the degree of “good coordination” was really very well indeed. Such being the fact, I cannot but append the explanation above.

And just because Brother Zhang Luying got on so very amicably with the several working organs of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters, when the Headquarters moved from Zhangjiakou to Beiping, Brother Luying was recalled from the “Fifth Command Room” to deal exclusively with liaison to the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters—right up until after the bandit army laid siege to Beiping.

Since the foregoing has spoken of the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters” and of its commander-in-chief Fu Zuoyi, let me then, on the evidence, without the least prejudice, and speaking to the matter as it stands, discuss the whole string of ways in which Fu Zuoyi, before the “battle for the Beiping-Tianjin region” of late in 1948 and early in 1949, let slip the chances of war. This one battle not only lost us half our rivers and hills, but bore upon the bandit army’s step-by-step southward incursions in the time to come.

On December 3, 1947, Chairman Chiang by his own hand ordered the establishment of the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters,” with Fu Zuoyi as deputy director of the “Beiping Field Headquarters” and concurrently commander-in-chief of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters; Shanxi, Hebei, Chahar, and Suiyuan all came under the command of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters, though still subject to the oversight of the “Beiping Field Headquarters.” The director of the “Beiping Field Headquarters,” Li Zongren, in fact concerned himself very little with affairs, and moreover in March 1948 flew from Beiping to the capital, and thereupon went and did not return.

On December 11, 1947, the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters moved from Zhangyuan into Fengtai in the southern suburbs of Beiping, and presently shifted again to the western suburbs.

In the middle of January 1948, Fu Zuoyi flew from Beiping to Shenyang to attend a “military conference” presided over by Chairman Chiang. At the conference some decision must have been reached on the strategy for the Beiping-Tianjin region.

On January 15, 1948, the director of the news bureau of the Ministry of National Defense, Deng Wenyi, issued a report on the week’s fighting, to the following effect: “The bandit army of the Northeast, in a surprise attack on Shenyang, met with defeat; of the five columns under Lin Biao—the First, Second, Fourth, Sixth, and Tenth—the Fourth Column suffered the heaviest hurt.

“On the North China side, after the Nationalist armies joined forces along the Ping-Bao line, on the ninth of January they routed the harassing incursion of the Third and Sixth Columns under Nie Rongzhen, taking some five thousand and more bandit prisoners. On the tenth, near Dingxing, they wiped out the whole of the Thirty-third Regiment of the Eleventh Brigade of the bandit Nie’s Third Column. On the eleventh, southeast of Mancheng, they broke the Second, Fourth, and Sixth Columns under the bandit Nie until they were an army no longer.” Battle reports such as this were, from that time on, not often seen.

Now when Fu Zuoyi moved the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters from Zhangyuan to Beiping, he took as his backbone the forces of his own sons-and-brothers troops—the Thirty-fifth Army under Lu Yingqing and the Provisional Third Army under An Chunshan—which, together with all the other troops of the Beiping-Tianjin region, made a total strength of about 300,000 men. In their disposition, they radiated out with Beiping as the center: along the Ping-Cheng line by way of Gubeikou to Chengde in Rehe; along the Ping-Sui line to Zhangjiakou; along the Ping-Han line to Baoding; along the Ping-Jin line to Tianjin; along the Jin-Pu line onward from Tianjin to Jinghai; and along the Bei-Ning line onward from Tianjin to Shanhaiguan.

Six key points extending in radiating fashion. Yet by the beginning of 1948 these several lines could no longer be passed along freely and unhindered. In the general state of things at that time, one could barely keep in contact along them, no more.

As for the ways out to the world beyond, they depended only on the seaport of Tanggu and on the several air-force bases in the two places, Beiping and Tianjin.

Surveying the whole board, there is no denying it: the Beiping-Tianjin region had fallen into a state of isolation.

What must be pointed out here in particular is that, if one wished to move the 300,000-strong host of the Beiping-Tianjin region southward (not counting those who had withdrawn from the Northeast in through the passes)—that is, to draw them off the Beiping-Tianjin battlefield—there was, apart from the single seaport of Tanggu, nothing to be used; and air transport was very limited.

The common run of ordinary folk, of course, knew nothing of all this; but Fu Zuoyi, being the commander, must have had it at his fingertips.

Again, after Fu Zuoyi had brought the Thirty-fifth Army and the Provisional Third Army into Hebei, on the Zhangjiakou side he still left Sun Lanfeng’s Eleventh Army Group to hold fast. For the two cities within Suiyuan, Guisui and Baotou, there remained only the Independent Seventh Division, the Eleventh Cavalry Brigade, and a number of local corps to hold them.

Before long, of the two provinces Chahar and Suiyuan, save for the four strongpoints of Guisui, Baotou, Jining, and Zhangjiakou, all was seized and held by the bandit army.

After this, what the records afford for examination is that the man Fu’s conduct was all topsy-turvy, hesitant and irresolute, and marked besides by many mistaken judgments. Whether there was in this some other hidden story—such as Communist Party members lying concealed at his side and working mischief from the sidelines—cannot be affirmed for certain; but that his capacity fell short, that he could not shake off a small and mean-spirited streak, and above all that he was hemmed in by narrow frames of mind, will be a fact beyond all doubt.

Next, let me briefly relate how Fu Zuoyi threw his own ranks into disorder in the face of the great battle to come. In July and August 1948, the North China bandit army under Nie Rongzhen assailed the Ping-Bao line and East Hebei and other places; and the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters, to ensure the safety of the Beiping-Tianjin region, used more than half the strength it could call upon—about six or seven armies—to strike back at various points. Though it drove off the invading forces of the bandit Nie, its area of defense grew ever smaller; which is to say, it lost some more of the outlying towns besides.

In October 1948, the situation in the Northeast turned against us and the storm over the Xuzhou-Bengbu region grew tenser by the day; and Fu Zuoyi of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters, seeing the whole state of things grown yet more unfavorable, regrouped all the troops under his command, hoping thereby to adapt. Yet his strategy of attack and defense remained as undecided as a hand hovering over the chessboard.

On November 1, 1948, bad news came: Shenyang, the strategic center of the Northeast, had fallen to the bandits, and in all the Northeast, save for the more than a hundred thousand Nationalist troops packed together at Huludao awaiting transport, the coastal military works had already been demolished. This meant that the whole territory of the Northeast was lost.

Besides this, though Fu Zuoyi knew full well how hard-pressed and perilous his situation was, it may be that he lost his measure and, of all things, lost his resolve. Had he followed the supreme strategic guidance, he ought at once to have abandoned Zhangyuan in Chahar, Chengde in Rehe, and even Baoding, and to have shifted all his heavy forces to Tianjin and Tanggu, so that he might defend for the long term, and might tie down Lin Biao and Nie Rongzhen and keep them from turning their strength too early upon South China—which would surely have helped the two battlefields of East China and Central China as well. But Fu Zuoyi, alas, clung fondly to Chahar and Suiyuan, so that he could hardly bring himself to a decision, and let slip a good many opportunities. It is understood that the supreme command too gave the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters instructions, to the general effect that, apart from keeping part of its strength to go on garrisoning Beiping, all its main force should at once be concentrated toward Tianjin and Tanggu and there made secure; and that against the forces of the bandit Nie entrenched in East Hebei it should take the initiative in attack, so as to weaken the bandit Lin’s offensive strength once he came in through the passes.

In military action, speed is prized; and to this Fu Zuoyi was still hesitant and undecided.

After several revisions, Fu Zuoyi at first accepted the following plan: that all the troops of Beiping and Tianjin be at once concentrated toward the Tianjin-Tanggu region, and, racing to get ahead of the freezing-over of the Dagu estuary (roughly between the eleventh and twelfth months of the lunar year), be carried by sea to land at Qingdao, there to join the “Xuzhou Bandit-Suppression Headquarters” in a pincer attack on the bandit forces of Chen Yi and Liu Bocheng. Thereafter, joining with the main force of the Xuzhou battlefield, they would turn and strike back northward, and seek once more a decisive battle with the two bandits Lin and Nie, to recover North China.

But this plan, in the staff work as first framed, was less than thorough, and fell far short of the actual carrying-capacity of the shipping. Only think: a host of several hundred thousand, together with its equipment—how many ships would it take, and in how many days could it all be carried across?

To change and change again is a great taboo of the soldier’s art; yet one had to bend to the facts, and the final decision was this—that all the troops of the Beiping-Tianjin region, before the bandit Lin’s forces had yet come in through the passes, be swiftly concentrated toward Tianjin and Tanggu, with defensive warfare as the supreme principle: first to check and blunt the enemy’s momentum, and then, biding the moment, to shift to the offensive as occasion served; while at the same time Guisui, Baotou, Datong, and Taiyuan were each required to hold fast with all their might, so as to draw upon themselves the assaults of the bandit forces of Xu Xiangqian and He Long.

But when it came to carrying out the above decision, Fu Zuoyi held back considerably.

From the affair at Zhangyuan one may see all the more plainly Fu Zuoyi’s divided mind. At the end of November 1948, the bandit Nie, with the strength of three columns, took in succession Wanquan, Chaigoubu, and Huai’an on the Ping-Sui line, and Zhangyuan was in peril. The garrison commander Sun Lanfeng made urgent preparations for battle, and at the same time wired the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters for reinforcement. At that time the staff of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters held that, since it had already been decided to move east to Tianjin and Tanggu, it would not do to send troops west again to relieve Zhangyuan, and urged that Sun Lanfeng break out on his own to the east and abandon Zhangyuan of his own accord. But Fu Zuoyi wavered and could not decide whether or not to give up Zhangyuan; and in this one moment of hesitation, the bandit Nie had already ringed Zhangyuan round tight and fast, so that to break out now had become a matter of the utmost difficulty.

Fu Zuoyi went against his earlier decision and, of all things, sent the Thirty-fifth Army, concentrated at Tongxian, racing off to relieve Zhangyuan. Sure enough, in two or three strokes the siege of Zhangyuan was lifted—which then gave Fu Zuoyi a false impression, so that he fondly supposed the bandit army could not withstand a single blow, that Zhangyuan might yet be held, and that if it could tie down a part of the bandit Nie’s strength, would that not be to the advantage of the fighting for Beiping and Tianjin?

The matter had many a twist in it, and one may as well call it Fu Zuoyi’s inconstancy. The siege of Zhangyuan once raised, the plan was to give the place up of one’s own accord the very next day, and the relieving Thirty-fifth Army, together with the Eleventh Army Group in garrison there, some seventy thousand men in all, were to return in a body to Beiping. But who could have known that, owing to the bitter entreaties of the local inhabitants, the matter was put off again and delayed a day. And in that one day’s space a change came about: the bandit army that had been driven off now came rolling back once more, so that the two sides fought fiercely again for two days and nights, until at last the bandit army could hold out no longer and slipped away.

Just as Guo Jingyun’s Thirty-fifth Army and Sun Lanfeng’s Eleventh Army Group were making ready to set out according to the original plan, Fu Zuoyi, against all expectation, changed his resolve once more, and suddenly ordered that Sun Lanfeng’s Eleventh Army Group should go on garrisoning Zhangyuan to tie down the bandit army, and that if need be (that is, when it could no longer be held) it should shift toward Guisui to draw the bandit army westward. As for the Thirty-fifth Army, however, he sternly ordered it to return north within the day by forced marches.

And what came as the greatest surprise of all—the Thirty-fifth Army, some twenty thousand men, thereupon never got back to Beiping!

The Eleventh Army Group, some fifty thousand men, not only could not hold Zhangyuan, but could not reach Suiyuan either!

Now the Thirty-fifth Army, having received Fu Zuoyi’s wired order, set out near midday on December 6, 1948, marching eastward along the Ping-Sui railway line. Its whole number was some twenty thousand, among whom a small part were soldiers’ dependents. All along the way it went in a mighty stream, with vehicles great and small of every kind, about four hundred of them.

At sundown the vanguard reached Xiahuayuan, to the southeast of Xuanhua county, and was suddenly set upon by troops of the “Ji-Re-Cha Military District”; the leading regiment got down to give battle, while the rest of the force fought as it went. As night fell, they pushed on to the town of Xinbao’an, and were there hemmed in.

The bandit army grew more and more numerous: besides the ——th Column of Nie Rongzhen’s “North China People’s Liberation Army,” there were found besides troops of the Fourth Column under Lin Biao’s Northeast People’s Liberation Army (which had come in through the various passes of the Great Wall and lain hidden about Badaling). Under the siege of these heavy forces, though our men assaulted again and again, they could not so much as open a single breach.

The North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters ordered the Provisional Third Army and the Sixteenth Army to go to the rescue by separate routes, but still to no avail. Fearing to sink deeper and deeper in, it withdrew them again; but the Provisional Third Army fell into an ambush on the way, and suffered a heavy loss.

The Thirty-fifth Army, hemmed in at Xinbao’an, held the lone town in bitter straits for more than a week; all its supplies were used up to the last, and its daily needs depended wholly on airdrops. The nearest airfield was Nanyuan at Beiping; beyond that there was only distant Qingdao. But within the town of Xinbao’an there was no ground fit for the drops, and much of what was dropped either missed the mark or drifted down outside the town. From this the wretchedness of their plight may be imagined. Moreover it fell just at the coldest of the deep-winter days, a world of ice and snow, the temperature morning and evening commonly about twenty degrees below zero. A single old sheepskin coat, serving both as bed and as cover, had long since “matted into felt”; and with empty, hollow bellies besides, they were truly beset at once by hunger and by cold—enough to be the death of a man.

By the morning of December 24, their shot and their provisions at last spent, the bandit army poured in in force through the eastern gate; and the defenders, though sunk in hunger, cold, and exhaustion, still summoned up the last of their courage and joined battle in the streets—fighting from house to house, hand to hand with naked blade, from dawn until dusk, on into the night—until Xinbao’an fell to the enemy at the last.

The commander of the Thirty-fifth Army, General Guo Jingyun, took his own life and died for his country! He was the second army commander of the Thirty-fifth Army to die by his own hand for his country (the first being General Lu Yingqing, who took his life at the Zhangfeidian station in the Laishui campaign). More heroic still—of the whole army, officers and men, not one came back alive! How should this not move men to solemn reverence!

Here indeed were crack troops and fine commanders; the pity of it was only that this Fu Zuoyi, for all that he was commander-in-chief, was not commander enough.

To speak again of the Eleventh Army Group under Sun Lanfeng, garrisoning Zhangyuan.

When the news of the utter destruction of the Thirty-fifth Army reached Zhangyuan, Sun Lanfeng felt his position the more solitary and weak, and all at once saw that to hold on in this way was by no means a lasting plan; and so, dividing his troops into two columns, he resolved to break out in two directions at once.

This action, against all hope, ended in failure too, so that of the fifty thousand and more officers and men, those who broke through the encircling lines and reached Guisui came to but a few thousand.

This story, which can never be told to the bottom, comes here to a first pause; the rest of what bears upon it I shall leave until the time of the “siege of Beiping,” and what follows now is but odds and ends. Of these, too, the greater part are the writer’s own opinions.

Talk keeps to its own trade: let me begin with “intelligence.”

In the foregoing—I wonder whether it was noticed?—there is this: that whenever the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters made a military move, the other side seemed to have a good deal of the “prophet” about it; whether it were “interception,” “flank attack,” or “meeting them head-on,” it was either near enough or just by chance exactly right, and the one to come off the worse was always our side. One cannot but think that the secrets had been leaked beforehand; else how could it be? What was hard to affirm was only that no proof could be produced. And when afterward it all turned into a tangled mess, the thread was harder still to find.

Besides the “leaking of secrets”—that is, the passing of word to the other side so as to give them guidance—there was also the confusing of the false with the true, the misleading of judgment, so as to bring about improper decisions. Things of this kind, too, were of no rare occurrence. In the language of intelligence, this is what is called “the counter-agent.” In fact, the chief condition for it must be that within the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters there be a high staff officer—yes, and Fu Zuoyi’s deputy chief of staff was one such, and a known one.

To turn to another angle, and speak of our own side: in the war to suppress the bandits, had we got hold of the Communist bandits’ intelligence? We had—of the strategic kind and of the tactical (the movements) kind alike; its greatest defect was only that its “timeliness” was too poor, so that it was often either a shot fired after the horse had bolted or a flower a day too late; failing that, one could grasp only the half of a tail-end. Such an inability to master the enemy beforehand naturally cost us a great deal. And why was the timeliness lost? Because the process of relay was too involved—or say, there were too many turns in it: to take our own intelligence work, the intelligence gathered by the work units of the various places was first reported to the brigade headquarters, went through a first handling by the staff, and, if judged worth relaying, was then sent on to the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters, where it went through another round of staff work before it was decided how it should be used. At this rate, a day or two at the quickest—so what “timeliness” could there be to speak of?

Then again, the “handling and use of intelligence” was much amiss too. To say nothing of the rest, and speaking only of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters: our pacification brigade’s intelligence was, as a rule, first sent to the Second Section of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters; and the charge of that Second Section, besides having authority over the whole of intelligence administration, was of course also to do the “handling of intelligence.” After handling it, whatever was judged to have “value for use” would be countersigned with comments and submitted specially to the commander-in-chief (the chief of staff) for his ruling, to be duly obeyed.

But the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters did not do it so: they laid it down that the intelligence, once handled by the Second Section, must be passed to the Third Section, which had charge of operations, to be examined over again—and this not as the customary “joint minute” between units of equal standing. To look at it, the Second Section had actually become the staff of the Third. Why? To put it plainly: the head of the Second Section was one sent by the central authorities, whereas the head of the Third Section was one of “their own people.” In saying this I may seem to be laboring the “line of division,” but in truth I go not one whit too far. All this the head of the Second Section of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters, Brother Shi Hong, told me himself, to my face, and there is not the least falsehood in it.

From this one may believe that what I said above, of Fu Zuoyi’s having a “sectarian bias,” had its grounds.

Lost is lost; the thing is now several tens of years gone by, and nothing one says can mend it; yet one cannot but take the lesson to heart: against the Communist Party, even to be “of one mind” was hardly enough—how much less could “division” be borne!

In all the above I have, from a “note,” turned aside and rambled in broad strokes into Fu Zuoyi’s divided mind and his hesitancy and irresolution. But by Fu’s divided mind I mean that in the decision between “moving east to Tianjin and Tanggu” and “going west to Baotou and Suiyuan” he was in a conflict of mind; to insist that he had long harbored an intent to go over to the Communists would be to show something less than fairness.

Mark, please, that in Fu Zuoyi’s instruction to Sun Lanfeng, garrisoning Zhangyuan, it is said: “if need be, it should also shift toward Guisui”—and this again betrays his “secret longing for Chahar and Suiyuan” and his “heart set on the Northwest.”

The success or failure, the gain or loss of any affair rests never on a single cause; and of the battle for the Beiping-Tianjin region, and how at last it fell into the enemy’s hands, there is yet more to come.

### 3. A Loyal Heart Providing for the Whole Unit’s Future

On the twentieth of May, the thirty-seventh year, the President was inaugurated at Nanjing.

Gu Zhutong succeeded Chen Cheng as chief of the general staff.

The “Beiping Field Headquarters,” by order, was abolished, its powers handed over to the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters.

At the end of May of the same year, Chu Xichun took office as peace-preservation commander of Hebei province.

On the twenty-second of June of the same year, the mayor of Beiping, He Siyuan, resigned, and Liu Yaozhang succeeded him.

In the “First Brigade of the Pacification Corps of the Ministry of National Defense” there was no change of personnel, and the work went on as usual.

From the middle of June of the thirty-seventh year, the beacon-fires of East Hebei blazed ever fiercer: there was fierce fighting on the outskirts of Tangshan, and Fengrun fell.

On the sixteenth of June of the same year, at Beiping and Tianjin, Communist-spy organizations were broken up in each, and fifty-seven persons, men and women, were arrested and handed over for prosecution. It was said that these were but “a small handful” of the whole.

On the twenty-first of June, the thirty-seventh year, fighting broke out again on the Ping-Gu line, the chief commander Fan Hanjie disposing the defenses. (Note: Chairman Chiang, at Shenyang, decided to set up a guiding organ for the Hebei-Liaoning-Rehe border region, to be headed by General Fan Hanjie.) On the twenty-third of June, the thirty-seventh year, the bandit army under Liu Bocheng took Kaifeng, the provincial capital of Henan. Three days later, our forces under Qiu Qingquan and Sun Yuanliang counterattacked and recovered it.

On the twenty-fifth of June, the thirty-seventh year, Changli was attacked, and at Qinhuangdao the sound of the guns could already be heard.

On the seventeenth of July, the thirty-seventh year, the fighting on the northern section of the Ping-Han line suddenly grew tense, with fierce battles in the outskirts of the county towns of Fangshan and Dingxing.

On the tenth of August, the thirty-seventh year, Changchun in the Northeast, besieged for half a year, was in dire famine, with those starving to death reaching several hundred a day.

In the middle of September, the thirty-seventh year, the battle of attack and defense at Jinan reached its height, with savage fighting in the southeastern outskirts.

In the same year, between June and August, prices in North China soared, changing several times in a day.

The above is excerpted from the Dagong Bao, then published at Tianjin, and gives some glimpse of the state of things. The writer’s note: the Dagong Bao ceased publication after the fall of Tianjin on January 15, 1949. When afterward it resumed, it had already been seized by the Chinese Communists, and though on the surface it was said to be run by the “democratic parties,” in fact it had become a mouthpiece of the Chinese Communists.

We who bore so heavy a charge, placed in such an environment, our hearts grew heavier by the day, as may well be imagined.

Now in the summer of 1948, the headquarters of the Pacification Corps convened a work conference at Nanjing, and the several brigade commanders were ordered by wire to attend. To say nothing else, merely to get from the various brigades’ stations to Nanjing already took no end of trouble.

The writer and the commander of the Second Brigade, fellow student Chen Zhenshan, who came from the Northeast to join me at Beiping, then took a China National Aviation flight together to Shanghai. Had it not been for the help of Brother Wang Yunsun (director of the Beiping office of China National Aviation, and formerly secretary of the “Beiping Station”), a ticket would truly have been hard to come by; and had one waited one’s turn on the register, one might have waited ten days or half a month and still not got off. After reaching Shanghai, we took the Nanjing-Shanghai express and so came to Nanjing at last. As for the other brigades—Shandong, Shanxi, and the rest—it was harder still for them.

The matter of the conference was trifling and involved, more than a few words could tell; from the standpoint of the Pacification Corps itself there was much that called for reckoning-up, which here it would be hard to make clear; and set beside the whole great situation, it was less than worth the telling. Of this, then, I can only be brief.

Afterward, the Corps Commander and the several brigade commanders below him were each granted a separate audience by His Excellency Chiang at his official residence at Xianglin Temple.

As for this place-name “Xianglin Temple,” few even of the people of Nanjing know it, and how it came by its name the writer too does not well understand. I remember that sixteen years before, in 1932, when the writer was training in the “Military Academy Special Research Class” (see the first book of my writings), his dormitory was not far from Xianglin Temple, no more than two or three hundred meters, with nothing between to bar the way and a flower-bordered path leading across. In the garden were several hollows of water, and in the ponds were grass carp; dropping a line one could get five or six of them, and boiled up on a “gasoline stove,” they were fresh and tender and toothsome, a delicacy, unforgotten to this day.

This time, going there, I still went along Huangpu Road, entered the main gate of the “Central Military Academy,” turned right and went several hundred feet; at the road’s end, where in those days had stood several rows of dormitories, they were now no more to be seen. Turning left again, not far off, was the “official residence.”

Being received by His Excellency President Chiang, I had the honor of his inquiring into the actual state of the work, and of his asking, besides, for my views. Whether the others offered any views, I do not know; I had no views to offer, because I did not know what would be well to say. His Excellency Chiang at the last exhorted me with the words: “Be a nameless hero.”

It comes back to me: that day military uniform was required, but I had not brought any, and had to borrow a set on the spot from my colleagues. The stuff was heavy khaki, the cut a grass-green long-sleeved service dress, bearing a colonel’s rank, worn with black leather shoes. And it happened to be a sultry, close day, so that swathed tight from head to foot, I broke into a sweat at every move. With the audience close at hand, and fearing besides to fail in bearing, I was in no small anxiety; and when I had taken my leave and come out, it was truly as though a weight had been lifted.

The conference over, Mr. Zheng Jiemin, at his own residence, informally and earnestly enjoined upon me: “When you go back (to Beiping), you should at once set about preparing the ‘stay-behind work’; best of all, put forward a concrete plan as soon as may be, and send a man specially to bring it to Nanjing, so that support may be given as circumstances allow, and it may be entered as a special case for the record. But in carrying it out, you must be most careful, lest it affect the morale and the hearts of the men.” By rights this was a “work directive,” like an “order”; why then call it “informal”? Because Mr. Zheng Jiemin, being precisely the superior in charge, ought, when he had a directive, to have it handed down through the Corps headquarters—otherwise it was an overstepping; yet were it to appear in an official document, black on white, there was the fear of some ill effect, which would after all be less than proper.

Put so, it may still not be plain enough; better to begin from the nature of the “stay-behind work.”

The so-called “stay-behind work,” to explain it simply, is this: “after the troops withdraw, a body of men is still to be left behind, to lie hidden and carry on the work.” In this there is an “intimation,” pointing to the likelihood that our unit, as the situation turned against us, would be greatly moved about. Only think: we were a special-operations unit charged with a task in a fixed area, and once we withdrew from the work area assigned to us, what did that signify? Were it plainly proclaimed, it would of course have an ill effect.

This point, above, is precisely the one reason why the order was not formally handed down.

Again, this time I came to Nanjing much livelier than before, not so stiff as of old, forever holding myself in check as though I were not fit to be seen. In the spare moments between the sessions, I went of my own accord to call on some old friends with whom I stood on good terms, all of them fellow students of the Military Academy: some from the “Special Research Class,” some from the “Honggong Shrine Special Police Class,” and some comrades of the former “Lixingshe” and the “Military Association” besides. Because we did not all serve in one place, there was no great caution in our talk; and with one or two “big-gun” sorts among us speaking without the least restraint, it was truly a ranging over sea and sky, and a right good, hearty talk we had.

Every one of them was a mainstay, every one a pillar, and what they came into contact with was all too real. And so their demand, with one voice, was for “remaking” and “renewal”—but to whom was one to carry it? The greatest knot of all was that the time was not right, or say it was already too late. Suppose one put it forward openly: if one were not taken for out of one’s mind, one would be mocked as not knowing the times, or even charged with some ulterior design.

What exactly we talked of, it is a pity that not a scrap of paper, not a single word, was kept in record; put it this way—it would have been historical material of the greatest value for reference.

Forty years have gone by; and counting carefully, of these old friends only two or three are still in the land of the living. On the way back to our post I still traveled with Chen Zhenshan, commander of the Second Brigade, and along the road we had time to talk deeply. We two had a spoken compact: that if the Northeast could not be held, Brother Zhenshan was resolved to take those who were willing to carry on with him (including the fellow students of the “Lizhi Class” and the cadres recruited in the Northeast) and stay in the Northeast to wage guerrilla war and keep up the struggle with the Communist bandits; while the others, those able to withdraw in through the passes (meaning generally the various passes of the Great Wall) (in principle only the fellow students of the “Lizhi Class,” because of matters such as pay and supply), might report to the “First Brigade headquarters” at Beiping or to the several units under the First Brigade, and the First Brigade would take charge of settling them and, as circumstances allowed, assign them work case by case.

This compact, once we had returned to our stations, we would enter with our superiors for the record, and would besides request leave for the “First Brigade” and the “Second Brigade” to “signal” each other directly, for the convenience of contact.

Brother Chen Zhenshan, staying a day at Beiping, once brought his betrothed to dine with the writer at an eating-house, and we talked again of the unforeseeable future; and this one parting, against all thought, proved a parting forever! To this day I cannot cease to think of him.

Of the battle record and the doings of the Northeast Brigade under Brother Chen Zhenshan the writer cannot write, knowing only that they went about it with color and spirit; and merely from the fact that, in a short space of time, they could at one call rally a host of tens of thousands, one may see that there was in it something out of the common, both in deed and in meaning—the pity being only that it came a little late, and the lost opportunity could no longer be turned back.

I understand that at present there are still several tens of the Second Brigade’s fellow students in Taiwan; I recall that twenty years ago there was still contact, but now there is little coming and going. I hear, too, that the deputy commander of the Second Brigade, Brother Yang Yu, is also in Taiwan; let me inquire and make sure, meet with him once, and ask a little of how things went—I should dearly wish to set it down in an essay of its own.

To tell this later matter first: when the situation in the Northeast grew tense, the Second Brigade’s several work units all fell into bitter fighting, and the whereabouts of the brigade commander, Brother Chen Zhenshan, became unknown; it was rumored that he had died in battle, and some said he had been taken prisoner, but neither has ever been confirmed. Only this—Brother Zhenshan never appeared again.

As for the men of the Second Brigade—that is, the fellow students of the “Lizhi Class” assigned to the Northeast—after the fall of the Northeast a part of them, just as had been agreed, came to Beiping. How many in all I no longer clearly recall, some several tens or so; and the highest in rank among them was Colonel Wei Ning and the like. This was a matter of after November 1948.

To turn back again, and speak of after the writer returned to Beiping from the conference at Nanjing—the time was about July or August 1948.

First I had an earnest talk with the deputy brigade commander, Brother Li Yulin, half private and half official in form, its aim to reach an understanding; for from now on there would be a whole string of unusual measures, and only by building a common mind could they be carried through.

What we spoke of at the outset was still the resolutions of the Nanjing conference, all of which were to be carried out at once. Yet in my own heart I held them to be no longer of much importance.

Brother Yulin too asked a few questions, no more than how our superiors rated us, and the like—which was in truth of even less importance; and talking on and on, we widened the scope, ranged far from the “Pacification Corps,” and came to the great affairs of the state. In those days, being simple, I thought such talk fit only for two men to speak softly in the open wilds—how could one discuss it seated in an office? Yet our hearts were warm and our minds were upright, and our starting point was of course concern for the future of the whole unit’s several thousand men; and since no man is without his own self, quite naturally we took ourselves in too.

As to the “stay-behind work,” I told Brother Yulin the whole of what Mr. Jiemin had charged me with. Brother Yulin felt it really too hard: no long-term reckoning had been made at the outset, and now it was wanted at once, to be done at once, so that he feared the result would fall short of the ideal; the more so as this was not a thing that any man picked at random could carry through. Brother Yulin was quite right; but we must, after all, do our utmost. And so Brother Yulin and I talked it over and made a brief division of labor within: the ordinary business of the brigade headquarters, and the work guidance of the field units, and the like, Brother Yulin would take the larger charge of; while the “stay-behind work” about to be set in hand I myself would first plan, and, once it was approved, would appoint a special man to carry on in secret.

There was one point that Brother Yulin and I were little willing to touch upon: that between me and the Corps Commander, Mr. Liu Peichu, there were certain things that would not fit together. This time we were minded to propose that the Ministry of National Defense order the “First Pacification Brigade” transferred to the south and given a fresh assignment—for which the Corps headquarters’ assent could not be dispensed with. Otherwise it would be an awkward business.

Though our superiors already had this inclination, it needed active pushing; and so I meant, at the earliest possible time, to ask Brother Yulin to make a trip to Nanjing. This journey would, for one thing, let him present the “Outline of the Stay-Behind Work Plan” to Mr. Zheng Jiemin in person; and at the same time it was needful to have Brother Yulin verbally lodge a case for the record with the Corps Commander, Mr. Liu Peichu, lest resistance arise. More important still was to have Brother Yulin ask Mr. Peichu, on my behalf, to support the transferring of the whole “First Brigade” to the south, and the sooner the better.

This was originally a thing very hard to put into words, still less to be allowed as a request in writing; for in fact it had never happened that, the work barely begun and the situation seen to be going ill, one should dodge the brunt, change the place, and set up the stove anew. Not only would this not do to be argued—it was simply past all reason.

Yet this may be understood; to say it plainly, the influence I came under on this trip to Nanjing was from two quarters: first, the intimation given in Mr. Zheng’s charge to set about the “stay-behind work”; and second, the whole understanding gained from my repeated contact with the fellow students of the Military Academy. Reasoning from these, the entire situation showed that not only could the Northeast not be held, but even North China would be hard to hold. The plan for the present, then, was rather to gather all one’s strength in South China, first to steady the line, and then to plan the deed of counterattack.

Our little unit, though it could not be said to stand for any great force, had after all several thousand patriotic young men; left in North China, they were of little use, but if they could be preserved and cultivated besides, would they not be a small hope for the future? Brother Yulin, being in complete agreement, went off gladly. We reckoned beforehand that the Ministry of National Defense would not give separate consideration to the single brigade that was the “First Pacification Brigade”; if it were to be done at all, it must surely be as part of some whole, or on a larger scale.

Reckoning the time, Brother Yulin’s going to Nanjing was about September 1948.

Since we were preparing to do this, we naturally made our arrangements without letting a sign of it show. And so, in keeping with the development of the wider situation, we began to draw in the disposition of the field units; the small units or individuals that were in remote areas, of no effect, and hard to keep in touch with, we recalled to the brigade headquarters for reorganization and retraining.

Our work had now entered another stage, and the goal of our efforts was to make a long-term reckoning. The first step was the hope of winning this: that a “special-operations unit” must not be used as a “combat unit,” and that its several thousand fine cadres should be put into fitting posts of work—best of all, each in his right place, in ranks where his latent powers could be brought fully into play.
