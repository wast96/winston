## Chapter 39

"Dear Xu,

"Mother writes that on Christmas Eve you accompanied her to an evening party, but that after coming home you suddenly fell ill and are now in hospital. Her letter said nothing of your condition, which leaves me very anxious. But she says Mei Yingzi thinks that if only you had been allowed to go on to the nightclub you would not have fallen ill—this must be a joke; someone as clever as Mei Yingzi would not, I think, misread the feeling between us. Mother is forever taking other people's jokes for their true meaning; this is of course a mark of her guilelessness, but it seems too a little wanting in humour—don't you think I'm right?

"Everyone comes to Qingdao to escape the summer heat, thinking it a resort for passing the hot months; and now I have come here to escape the winter (or say, to escape Christmas and New Year's Day), and find it has a savour all its own. In years past, when people came to escape the heat, the beaches were crowded with ugly throngs—those idle rich of Shanghai, the drunken sailors off the Western warships, and the dancing-girls and prostitutes who spring up to meet the demand—by day baring their ugly flesh on show along the sands, by night draping that show in clothes to flaunt it on the roads and in the bars, staining the beautiful colours of sea and hill with their filth. But now all of it has been given back its purity, and often I can walk alone on the beach, listening to the sea water's long, drawn-out sighing, watching the white clouds change at their leisure; under the sunlight the shadows of the hills and the hues of the islands, the gulls like gold; sometimes a rainbow spans half the sky, and the coloured reflections in the sea are like a ring—at this I feel body and soul made new, as though I had drawn much nearer to God. At dawn and at dusk, the red sun like a ball, dappled golden waves rising on the sea, I let down my hair and climb alone to the top of a crag and sing out at the full pitch of my voice, as if my song reached straight to the courts of heaven and I had been chosen for a fairy before the throne of God. I repent, and am ashamed, that in the past I took the applause of men for my joy and forgot the glory of drawing near the Creator. I find that I now have God's endowment of genius. For here I have come to know Mr. Stoyevsky, a musician of Russian nationality, his beard already white but his bearing full of spirit; hearing my song, he prized it and invited me to his home. He plays the piano for me, guides me, encourages me. My progress and my gains in singing are not owed to him but truly, as I have said above, to the bounty of Nature. Still, I have formally become Stoyevsky's pupil; I am learning the piano and composition with him, and I believe I shall make good progress, for I learn with great interest and so am very willing to work hard.

"I am forever grateful for what you hoped of me; your hopes for me were purer than anyone's, and this I keep especially in mind. Now I tell you all my doings; I trust it will not seem too trivial to you in your sickness. As for my leaving Shanghai, I decided it that very day we came out from the cemetery; and though the bringing-forward of the date had to do with your inviting me to the evening party, besides you was there not a whole crowd of far more tiresome people about to invite me too? In Shanghai, through my profession and my social ties, I had got myself into a plight there was no shaking off; here, I wear plain clothes, let my hair hang loose and tangled, wipe off the powder and rouge, and wear flat shoes. I refuse all society, and people all believe me still a child not yet out of the nest; I have recovered the life of those early days when I first knew you, and I begin to treasure this life.

"Stoyevsky is a music teacher living in retirement in Beiping, where he teaches a group of pupils; he has told me to go to Beiping, to live in his house and help him teach a little music—he is willing to teach me piano and theory without pay, and I could even have a small income. Beiping is a place everyone says is fine and one I have never been to; I think this a very good chance. He came to Qingdao on a little private business, and once that is settled he means to go back; I plan, when he returns, to make a trip back to Shanghai, and then to go straight on to Beiping to find him. After that my life can be wholly bound up with music. I am sure you will be glad; Mother may not approve of my leaving her, but I think that if, after I reach Beiping, I can find her a place in a shop there, might she not come at any time too?

"In Shanghai there is nothing to hold me; what there is to hold me is two or three friends, and you above all. Yet I do not wish you to be in Shanghai—I have said as much to you before. Wherever you go I would want to follow; only if you are in Shanghai shall I too want to be in Shanghai. And I have a kind of fear besides: that if you do not change the life you now live, you will surely lose your own self, and the distance between us will grow ever greater.

"The life of the hospital gives you more chance for reflection, so perhaps your little illness is good for you. Have you not long grown estranged from the subject of your studies? Can you still recall at what point your book broke off? These are the things Bai Ping worries over on your account, and now I join her in worrying over you.

"Bai Ping really is remarkable; I feel she understands you more deeply than anyone. She says that of other people's affairs you are clearer than anyone, and of your own more muddled than anyone. She says you understand neither your own powers nor your own feelings; neither your own life nor your own living. These words I feel to be quite right. Whether it is harder, in the end, to understand others or to understand oneself is hard to say; but I believe everyone has his bias—some are made for understanding others, some for understanding themselves, and the gradations by which the ingredients are apportioned are of course without limit. Ever since that one talk with Bai Ping—the day we came out of the tiger's mouth, after the night I stayed at her place—I have felt she is truly worthy of respect and love: knowing, yet not proud; with little vanity, and sincere; and these are all points beyond Mei Yingzi's reach. In the past I often set the two of them down as one type; now I find that they are fundamentally different. Mei Yingzi came to such a life of her own accord, Bai Ping was brought to such a life against her will; the one uses such a life to unfold her brilliance, the other wears her brilliance away by force within it. So Mei Yingzi's life widens amid glittering vanity, while Bai Ping's deepens amid the noise and splendour—I wonder whether you think my words are right?

"In your sickness I imagine they often come to see you, and often send you fresh flowers; this scene I can picture—the white sickbed, the white air, the quiet world, the beautiful universe—and so I both envy and am jealous, and feel that the prayer I said just now for you was after all needless.

"Just now the moonlight was very fine, and I was by the sea; the hills over there were solemn as Bach's sentiment, the sea grand as Beethoven's imagining, and the moonlight gentle and tender as Mendelssohn's; I felt that the soul of every musician is a pulse-beat of Nature. With both hands thrust in my coat, I sang up at the sky on the sands; and before the song was done my hand touched Mother's letter in my pocket, and so I thought of you, and stood still on the beach, bowed my head and closed my eyes and prayed for your health. I hope this prayer has already passed to the courts of heaven on the ripples of the lovely moonlight, and has flowed down again on that lovely moonlight into your heart. Today is the fourth of January; I hope you will seek out this day in your memory—this night—and whether, in the streaming-down of the moonlight, you felt a little watering of the soul.

"Then the wind rose on the sea, the sea-floor began to heave, and the spray of the waves flew up onto my hair and skin; I turned my back and wrapped my coat close, but my hair was thrown into disorder by it, and I had to set the band that bound it to rights again—when a gust of sea wind came all at once and whirled the band away.

"Clouds rolled the moon away, the sky grew heavy as lead, the wind cold as a knife, and I hurried back. Now the stove-fire is like spring and my body has grown warm; sitting quietly under the lamp, with the surging sea calling in the distance and the moonlight beyond the window now hidden, now showing, I feel a kind of need, and begin to write to you. What, I wonder, should I use to bless you? The dreamlike moonlight, or the genial stove-fire? No, my friend—I shall ask Mother to buy pure white roses and water-lilies regularly to bless you, and set them at the window of your sickroom, to glow beside your stove-fire, to receive the moonlight that comes in at the window night after night, and to give off their faint cool fragrance to soothe your peaceful dreams.

"Your friend, Helen Manfield. January the fourth."

I read it quietly through twice, and felt there was much in it that gave me matter for thought. That she did not understand the cause of my illness was of course because her mother, fearing the Japanese censorship, had not told her of it in her letter; and this was just as well, or she could surely not have written so serene and deep a letter. From her tone and her wording I could picture the openness and brightness of her mind. How important, after all, this journey had proved for her! The change in a person's life can be settled by any trifling thing, and now this journey had perhaps become Helen's turning-point. Had Helen been here, and gone with me that day to the dance, then what would her life have been? It—perhaps she would have been drawn from then on into the social round; perhaps, perhaps she would have been wounded in some other set of circumstances, and even have lost her life, there is no telling. Had this journey been put off to now, or to some more distant future, it is possible she might have come to feel the opposite about Nature; she might not have met Stoyevsky, or might have met some other person, and so given up music and... and gone into trade, and been made use of by someone, and married.

Life gropes its way among ten thousand possible roads; the one she has now groped her way to may not be the best, but it is certainly the best that lay within my expectation, and I ought of course to rejoice for her, and ought to encourage her to keep walking down this road.

I called to mind the night of the fourth of January, and found it was the very eve of my third operation; I remembered I had lain sleepless on the bed, while the moon poured in through the window frame and spread over my bedclothes, as if to soothe me, and had truly watered my desolate soul. In a mood of faith and thankfulness, I let the tears fall.

The tears wetted my pillowcase, and I fell asleep upon their cool dampness; when I woke it was suppertime, and the hospital supplied me an ample and tasty meal. In the night, wearing that dressing gown of grey ground, black stripes and red trim, at the edge of the sofa, with a stiff-backed book for a rest, I began to write to Helen.

In the letter I urged her with all my strength to go to Beiping, hoping she would not fail the talent and the chance that Nature had bestowed on her; I hoped I might have the luck, after the war was over, to attend her first concert.

Of my illness I explained nothing; I only said that I was now nearly healed, and that the reflection I had done in my sickness was a hollow thing—but rather than say I did not understand myself, it would be truer to say I understood too well my own contradictions.

My letter was written very long; but in the hushed sickroom my feeling drifted little by little into a keen, sensitive melancholy, and, thinking that such reflections would have a great effect on Helen's soul, I laid down my pen at this point.

It was a cold winter's night, and the heat of the radiator seemed hardly enough; I lit one of the cigarettes Philip had given me, and, gazing at the tangled coils of smoke, my own thoughts grew all the more tangled.

Perhaps it was that the easing of my body's pain had added to the burden on my spirit; perhaps it was that Helen's letter had raised in me many conflicts of reason and feeling; perhaps it was that the letter I had just written had stirred up the many cares I had put out of mind—for a while I did not know how to order myself.

Mei Yingzi was to come and see me tomorrow, a thing I both keenly hoped for and keenly dreaded. Ever since I had fallen ill, at first there had not been a day I did not fret over the unfinished matters of the work and the questions that must arise; later I gradually forgot them, and then I strove hard not to call them to mind; and now all the reality was about to come flooding in, and I ought to make ready a strong frame of mind to meet it—but I could not settle my heart to a cool, rational analysis, to arrange, among these vexing and tangled questions, an order of first and last, of heavy and light.

Whether I understood myself too little, or understood too well my own contradictions—these topics of my letter to Helen had become the very refuge into which I fled from reality; I had, at bottom, a contradictory cast of mind and a habit of philosophical speculation that met and repelled at once Philip's words, "I permit Mei Yingzi to come and see you tomorrow." But still I went on smoking the cigarette he had given me.

I threw away the cigarette-end and put out the light and went to bed. The moonlight beyond the window flowed in like water; the red roses flashed the colour of blood, the white roses flashed with the light of tears, and my white bedclothes were dyed to silver.

I thought of the night of Bai Ping's illness, of the melancholy of that silver room. This child could be a spy, and yet there was counter-proof that she was not a spy. And that counter-proof lay in my own body: before my eyes I saw her hand, saw the gun trembling in her hand, and so I felt again the wounds on my shoulder and my arm.

But as I lay on the bed and looked round the room steeped in moonlight, there rose before my eyes the shadow of Stephen: his iron-grey cheeks, his deep-purple lips, his tightly clenched jaws, his slightly parted eyes...!

I longed for this friend, and my tears fell. By the moonlight I thought of going to his graveside, but I did not stir; I lay stiff and still as a corpse, mimicking the sleep of Stephen in death.

Had I never come to know him at all, in what kind of world would my life have grown? Had he not died, what kind of change would there have been in my world?

And at his graveside—had not both Helen's life and my own life been set into their waves and turmoil because of it?

And so I thought again of Helen, on the beach. Her golden hair hanging loose, facing the lovely moonlight, she sings, she prays for me—and of course still prays for her family scattered in their several places, and perhaps prays too for Stephen underground.

I lay on my side, but quite naturally settled into the posture of one kneeling with bent knees in worship, and, closing my eyes, began to pray without a sound.

And in this silent prayer I fell asleep.
