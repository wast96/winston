Chapter 9. Head of the Rescue Department
By January 1931, the Party's underground work in Shanghai, under the influence of the "Left" error, had suffered great damage; many comrades had been arrested, the underground Party was in extreme peril, and the task of rescuing the arrested comrades was very arduous and heavy. The Mutual Aid General Association, the Party's peripheral organization specially responsible for rescue work, had just held a delegates' congress in the winter of 1930, adjusting its leading body, with Dai Xiaoyun as the new secretary. The Mutual Aid General Association had opened a shop near Xujiahui as a secret liaison point with the comrades held in Longhua Prison. Because I had once been imprisoned in Longhua, the Party organization decided in January 1931 to transfer me to serve as head of the rescue department of the Mutual Aid General Association. This was jointly proposed by Comrade Chen Geng, then chief of the Second Section of the Special Branch, after he had sought He Chang's opinion, and approved by Comrade Zhou Enlai.
Besides recommending me to the Party Central, He Chang also recommended Comrade Pan Hannian. During the Great Revolution, in Wuhan, Pan had worked in the propaganda section of the General Political Department of the Northern Expedition army, and we had had a working relationship. Pan had not taken part in the Nanchang Uprising, but when He Chang and other comrades withdrew from Nanchang to Shanghai, they had not so much as a copper of travel money on them and were in great straits; halfway along they ran into Pan Hannian. Quick-witted and brave, he went single-handed into the home of a local despot and, at gunpoint, forced the despot to "lend" them travel money. That despot, not knowing how many men and horses Pan had brought, was so frightened that he meekly produced a sum of money. Thus He Chang and the others were relieved from their plight and were able to reach Shanghai safely. From then on He Chang felt that Pan Hannian was quick-witted and brave and suited to staying on in the White areas to do underground work, and so he recommended Pan Hannian (then head of the propaganda department of the CCP Jiangsu Provincial Committee) together with me to the Central Committee, to stay in Shanghai and engage in underground work centered on rescuing fellow sufferers. After studying it, the Central Committee adopted He Chang's suggestion and decided that our organizational relationship should be single-line contact, and determined that I should be in charge of external liaison and Pan Hannian of internal contact. By ordinary custom it should have been the man for the outside and the woman for the inside. Why did the Central Committee determine that I should handle the outside? This had to do with my family and with my social connections in Wuhan.
When I worked in Wuhan, I came to know many upper-class figures of various circles, especially well-known figures of women's circles such as He Xiangning and Song Qingling, with whom I had had a working relationship; Zhang Duhe, Zhao Wanhua, and others all had some social connections that could be used and could serve as cover. As for my family, it could serve as excellent cover for my carrying out work: when my father was chief instructor at the Yuelu Academy in Changsha, many upper-class figures of military and political circles, and the sons and daughters of the new and old warlords, had all been my father's students—for example the orphans of Tan Sitong and Tang Caichang, and the sons of Huang Xing and Cai E. The one who then concurrently held the post of head of the Yuelu Academy was Tan Yankai, military governor and governor of Hunan Province; by the time of the oath-taking ceremony for the Northern Expedition at Guangdong in 1926 he was already provisional chairman of the National Government. After the March Eighth International Women's Congress of 1927, I presented to him the resolution of our congress, putting forward the general demand for women's liberation, and Tan accepted it smilingly. He said to his secretary that he had never imagined Huang Yingchu had such a capable, fine daughter. My father was upright and incorruptible and had not gone to seek an audience with Tan, but among Tan's entourage there were still not a few old friends who knew my father well. On 17 April 1927, the order issued by the Wuhan provisional Central expelling Chiang Kai-shek from the Party and revoking all his posts was signed by Tan Yankai, and so the Central Committee considered that Tan seemed rather more reliable than Wang Jingwei. When Nanjing and Wuhan merged and the right wing supported Chiang Kai-shek's restoration to office and power, Tan could only be president of the Executive Yuan. The reason he could still keep his place on the political stage was that he still had latent strength within the Hunan Army. The Party organization thought that if by some mischance I were arrested, my father's connections could be used to run about and rescue me. This was cherishing the cadre, a consideration for safety. My experience of social activity during the Northern Expedition helped in opening up upper-level liaison outside the Party, and, together with the several years of tempering in underground work that had improved my adaptability, made me qualified to serve as head of the rescue department. This was the encouragement and trust that the Central Committee and Comrade He Chang placed in me.
During his period of reflection, He Chang worked hard at studying revolutionary theory, reading many Marxist-Leninist philosophy books and Party Central documents. Because I had just begun new work, I was very busy, going out early and coming back late, running about outside all day, only now and then coming home to eat a midday meal. He Chang cooked at home himself, and sometimes we would just buy a little pickled vegetable and eat some rice gruel. Though life was very hard, we felt very happy. At that time the matter of his applying to the Central Committee, behind my back, to go to the Soviet Area had not yet been approved, so I knew nothing at all of it and only felt that he was more considerate and caring toward me. At the Southern Bureau and the Northern Bureau, because he was busy with work, although I am not very good at looking after people it was still mostly I who looked after him; now it was just the reverse—I was busy outside, and he, while studying and reflecting at home, also took the buying of vegetables and cooking wholly on himself. Though I felt his extraordinary consideration and care for me, I always thought it was only because our working conditions had temporarily changed, and did not know that he had already made up his mind to go to the Soviet Area. It was not until the Central Committee had approved his request, shortly before he left, that he explained it to me.
I firmly refused to agree to his going alone, and demanded to go with him to the Jiangxi Soviet Area. I said: "Don't think I can't bear hardship. I've been in prison, I can bear hardship. If I stay behind to do rescue work, there can only be single-line contact, and if the line breaks, what then? Think back—when Wan Xiyan went to work in southwest Jiangxi, because I had just borne Jie'er I couldn't go with him, and the result was that once he went he could never come back again. Of course I'm not saying that you, He Chang, will also be killed on this trip; I only mean that whatever the circumstances, I want to be together with you." And so I cried for three whole days, and he could not force me any further, and could only ask Wu Defeng and Qi Yuande, husband and wife, to do the work of talking me round. Wu Defeng had worked in Wuhan and at the Jiangxi Provincial Committee, and had gone with Xiyan to work at the Southwest Jiangxi Special Committee—they were old comrades-in-arms. After Gu Shunzhang's betrayal, many of the Shanghai underground Party's courier liaison points had been wrecked. The Central Committee had transferred Wu Defeng to Shanghai to take charge of withdrawing all the courier points and stations that Gu Shunzhang knew of and rebuilding the Party's underground courier liaison network. Qi Yuande was very good at persuasion and education work; she invited me to her home and said to me: "We know you want to go to the Soviet Area with He Chang—that is only human feeling. We also know you've been a worker and been in prison, that you don't fear hardship, and that you're fully prepared in your thinking for the hard struggle of life in the Soviet Area. But your skin is too fair; going to the Soviet Area means traveling long distances through mountain regions and country villages, and to wear city women's clothes would arouse the enemy's suspicion, which of course won't do. If you dress up as a peasant woman, you won't look the part at all, which would arouse the enemy's suspicion even more. This would be very unfavorable to the safety of He Chang traveling with you, of the underground couriers escorting you, and of the underground courier stations set up in peasants' homes all along the way. Besides, your health has stayed very weak since you got out of prison and gave birth, and you couldn't stand such a hard, long journey; if you were to fall ill on the road, you'd only make things hard for He Chang. So the Central Committee thinks it's better for you to stay in Shanghai for now, and when a better and safer underground courier route to the Soviet Area has been opened up, we can then come and fetch you to the Soviet Area. This present decision was a request He Chang himself put forward, approved by Comrade Zhou Enlai. He Chang has said that Comrade Enlai has great prestige in the whole Party and the whole army, and that a decision he has considered and approved is very thorough and proper. He Chang's recommending you as head of the rescue department was also his considering over and over that you are very well suited to this work, because you have many social connections that can be used and serve as cover, so that you can find ways to rescue the arrested comrades. Now Comrade Guan Xiangying, secretary of the League Central, has been arrested, and if you can find a way to rescue him, that will be snatching a great general back from the enemy's prison, and will be rendering a signal service to the Party and the people. You've always styled yourself an extraordinary woman in the manner of Hua Mulan—I believe you can surely hold a post single-handed and brilliantly accomplish the heavy charge the Party has given you. Of course this work is also very dangerous; if by some chance the situation turns bad, you'll just have to wait—you mustn't be rash or act blindly and expose your true identity. If it really won't do, you can naturally still use your maiden family's social connections as cover, withdraw for the time being, and lie low for the long haul. This will mainly depend on your own quick wits in coping with things."
Guan Xiangying was born in 1902 in Jin County, Liaoning; he joined the Communist Youth League in 1925 and was the first League member of the city of Dalian. Afterward he joined the Party, was elected a member of the Central Committee at the Sixth Congress, and was always in charge of leading the work of the Communist Youth League. In 1930 he served, together with Zhou Enlai and other comrades, as a member of the Standing Committee of the Military Commission. When Zhou left Shanghai, Guan was secretary of the Military Commission. In 1931, when Gu Shunzhang defected, in the haste there was no time to notify Guan Xiangying, and Guan was unfortunately arrested, but his identity was not exposed. On learning of this, Zhou Enlai was greatly anxious and at once ordered Chen Geng to quickly find out the relevant situation and rescue Guan Xiangying as soon as possible.
Guan Xiangying and He Chang had fought shoulder to shoulder ever since the founding of the Communist Youth League, and their relationship was very close. When He Chang heard the news of Guan Xiangying's arrest, he was greatly anxious; at home for three days and three nights he spoke very little and had no smile, only sighing long and heavily, and said to me again and again: "Don't come with me to the Soviet Area—you're head of the rescue department now, this task is very heavy, and Guan Xiangying and the others are still waiting for you to find a way to rescue them from prison!" Having also heard Qi Yuande's reasonable and heartfelt persuasion, I came to recognize too that I could not attend only to the tenderness of a couple's love, and that I really ought to put first the safety of He Chang, of the couriers escorting him, and of the underground courier stations along the way. And so I could only agree, through my tears, to let He Chang go first to the Soviet Area alone.
He Chang also told me that Comrade Enlai had said: "Mulan must trust the Party organization; the organization will send people to protect her in secret. Should Pan Hannian be transferred or any other situation arise, the organization will send someone else to make contact with her, and will be sure to send someone who worked with Mulan during the Northern Expedition and whom Mulan trusts to contact her." He wanted both He Chang and me to set our minds at ease.
In May 1931, He Chang was sent to a secret reception station in the suburbs to await the moment to set out for the Soviet Area. Wu Defeng and his wife warned me that I must keep myself very carefully concealed—I could neither go to the organs, for fear of dragging in a "tail" and betraying my whereabouts, nor take part in organizational life. They arranged for me to live at a single-line contact point of the underground Party. It was a White Russian apartment building, opened by White Russian aristocrats who had fled into exile in China after the October Revolution; the rent was thirty dollars a month, and with board included one had to pay more than eighty dollars a month. This was very dear at the time, more than an ordinary person could afford to live in, but because of the needs of the cover work, all the expenses were paid by the Party organization. The rooms in the apartment had telephones, convenient for liaison; the suites of furniture and fittings in the rooms were very grand. This was because, as I was to be in charge of external liaison, I had to have a place fitting my status where outsiders could be received, so as not to arouse people's suspicion. The greatest advantage was that, like a hotel, if some emergency arose and one needed to move at once, one could pick up a small trunk, needing to take nothing else, and get up and go on a moment's word—very convenient for action. The room we chose to rent had its window facing straight onto the mouth of the lane, so that from the window one could see the people coming in from the street. To dress up as an upper-class woman, I had my hair permed and wore mid-heeled shoes, and Qi Yuande also made me a georgette qipao—that one qipao alone cost several dozen dollars. All this was handled by Qi Yuande.
Once all this had been arranged, I formally began the rescue work. The staff officer of the rescue department was Huang Hao (Jingwen), who in Wuhan had taken part in the women's corps of the Whampoa Military Academy. The Mutual Aid General Association also had a secretary, Zhu Bochen, who joined the Party in 1930 and was the son of Zhu Jianfan, headmaster of the Zhounan Girls' School.
The first important task the Party gave me was to rescue Comrade Guan Xiangying. The organization had already made arrangements and had sought out a lawyer to defend Guan Xiangying: a young progressive lawyer named Chen Zhigao. Who was this Chen Zhigao, and what were his family circumstances? Because he was to have an extraordinary bearing and importance for my later work and life, it is necessary here to give a fairly detailed account of his family background and personal circumstances.
Chen Zhigao was a native of Haining in Zhejiang, of a renowned old family of standing—the descendants of Chen Yuanlong, the famous "Grand Secretary Chen" of the Kangxi and Yongzheng years of the Qing dynasty. Rumor among the common folk had it that the Qianlong Emperor was in fact a Han Chinese, born of this Chen clan, a tale much embroidered in many literary and artistic works, though it is not true. But this clan did in several centuries produce not a few high officials and not a few men of letters; only, the family fortune was not rich, and a children's rhyme was current in the countryside: "The Chen mansion's grand academician / has legs but no trousers; / pumpkin for his proper meal, / two meals in three days." A gibe, and praise as well, perhaps! Chen Zhigao's father was named Chen Qishou, styled Jieqing, and had once been a rank-two high official of the Qing court. The old gentleman had served eighteen years as presiding judge of the criminal court of the Mixed Court in the Shanghai French Concession, a very enlightened judge, and by this time had retired in old age. In those years, when he was prefect of Suzhou, the Guangxu reform movement had already failed, and Guangxu's teacher Weng Tonghe had been demoted and sent back to his native place, with Cixi ordering the local officials of Suzhou to have Weng submit each month a self-examination of his own words and deeds.
Chen's predecessor had of course complied with the order, which made Weng Tonghe very unhappy. But when the old gentleman Chen held the post, out of sympathy for the reform movement and respect for Weng, he changed it so that each month he himself invited Weng to a banquet, discussing literature and poetry with him, host and guest thoroughly enjoying themselves in great harmony. To fob off the court, he memorialized upward every month, saying that Weng kept to himself and was law-abiding, with no words or deeds out of line. The court believed it could thereby learn of Weng's movements every month; Weng Tonghe too kept his dignity of character; and both approved of the old gentleman Chen's handling of it. When he held office in the Shanghai French Concession, he upheld justice and released old members of the Revolutionary Alliance who held fast to democratic revolution. For this, Mr. Sun Yat-sen once wrote him a letter of thanks in his own hand. Before the Revolution of 1911, when Huang Jinrong was involved in a homicide in Suzhou and should have been sentenced to death, the old gentleman Chen was serving as an official in Suzhou, and, holding that Huang had taken the blame for another out of the "code of brotherhood" within the secret societies, did not sentence Huang to death. When the Revolution of 1911 broke out, the prisoners in the jail scattered in a rush at the chance. Before long, Huang Jinrong went to work at the police station of the Shanghai French Concession, first as an ordinary detective, later rising to chief inspector, and became a "magnate" of the Shanghai secret societies—Chiang Kai-shek too once became his disciple. As it happened, the old gentleman Chen was also transferred to the Shanghai French Concession as presiding judge of the criminal court, in effect governing the people of the police station, and so was in a sense Huang Jinrong's superior. Because of this double relationship, Huang Jinrong called the old gentleman Chen "benefactor" at every turn, and went to the Chen home every year to pay his New Year respects; even when Chen Zhigao the lawyer went to the police station of the French Concession to handle a case, on seeing him Huang would very courteously call him "Second Young Master." From this one can see that the Chen family was very influential in Shanghai.
When Chen Zhigao was studying at Aurora University in Shanghai, he already had leftist thinking and had taken part in class-boycott activities; later he transferred to the Shanghai College of Law to study. The teachers and schoolmates of the Shanghai College of Law—Chu Fucheng, Shen Junru, Li Jianhua, Song Yuanru, and others—were all famous patriotic democrats. As the saying goes, "he who stays near vermilion is dyed red"; being often close to them there, Chen Zhigao was influenced by them; Mr. Shen Junru especially, who was both his teacher and his elder cousin, had a great influence on Chen. Before he graduated, he had once taken the initiative to ask his father to bail out arrested schoolmates and Communist Youth League members Shi Zhecun, Dai Wangshu, and Du Heng. These three later all became famous writers. In those days people in Shanghai were all much given to show and face; when Zhigao, after graduating in 1930, put out his shingle as a lawyer, the old gentleman Chen, to build up his prestige, deliberately gave a great banquet at the Hongyun Restaurant, widely inviting relatives and friends. Many famous figures of political-legal, financial, and other social circles came to lend their support, some being sworn brothers of the old gentleman Chen—such as Bei Songsheng, manager of the Bank of China, and the owners of the *Shen Bao* and the *News Daily*—and some institutions, for the sake of face, vied to bestow on him the title of "legal adviser." With such a background and standing, in going to law he was naturally very "well connected." So no sooner had Chen Zhigao put out his shingle as a lawyer than many people flattered him, calling him a "great lawyer." In those days ordinary lawyers in Shanghai had only private "little rickshaws"—the rather ornate hand-drawn rickshaws with a puller one hired oneself; only a great lawyer had his own motorcar. Chen went to and from his cases in a motorcar, entirely in the style of a great lawyer.
Not long after Chen opened his practice, the workers of the French Tramway Company went on strike, and in the struggle several fellow workers were injured by policemen of the French Concession; the union wanted to sue the police station, but what ordinary lawyer would dare take on such a case and go to law for the workers? Chen Zhigao, a newborn calf that does not fear the tiger, boldly served as pro bono defense lawyer for the workers, spoke up for justice in court, argued the case on its merits, and actually won. Because he refused to take any fee, the tramway union gave him a silver shield engraved with "the Light of Justice" as thanks and a memento, and with that he became more famous still.
A very important point is that Chen Zhigao was also a member of the progressive body "the World and China Society." In the early 1930s, a cadre of the CCP Central Propaganda Department named Zhou Kangwen organized a study group of progressive non-Party figures, ten members in all: Zhou Kangwen, Chen Zhigao, Sun Xiaocun, Wu Gengmei, Chen Gaoyong, Li Nanxiang, Cao Liang, Dai Wangshu, Liang Yuandong, and Wu Zhendong, of whom Chen Zhigao was the youngest. Li Nanxiang, Chen Gaoyong, Wu Gengmei, and others were all intellectuals returned from study in Japan; before going to Japan they had all taken part in the Northern Expedition and the Great Revolution, some having directly joined the Communist Party, others working outside the Party under its influence, and after the Great Revolution failed they had gone to Japan to study, and on returning still accepted the Communist Party's leadership and did some work outside the Party. These people formed a progressive academic body, "the World and China Society," which accepted the leadership of the CCP Central Propaganda Department, and also put out a ten-day periodical, The World and China, later changed to a monthly, which brought out a special enlarged issue at the time of the "September Eighteenth" Incident and was a very influential publication of the day; its editorial office was set up on the third floor of the Chen family home. The old gentleman Chen and the old lady lived on the first floor, the eldest brother and sister-in-law and the younger sisters on the second, and the several other unmarried brothers all on the third, Chen Zhigao's study being the editorial office of The World and China. Qian Nashui, head of the propaganda department of the Mutual Aid General Association, had had work contact with Zhou Kangwen and Pan Hannian, and it was through Qian Nashui that I came to know Zhou Kangwen, who introduced me to Sun Xiaocun, who in turn introduced me to Chen Zhigao. Sun had joined the Communist Party during the Northern Expedition and later left it, and the Party organization deliberately had him play a core role among the democrats in the capacity of a non-party figure (after the "Cultural Revolution" he served as vice-chairman of the National Committee of the CPPCC and vice-chairman of the China Democratic National Construction Association Central). This looks like a social process of friends and acquaintances introducing one another in turn, but in fact it was all a planned, step-by-step arrangement by the Party organization after careful investigation and study.
It was precisely because of Chen Zhigao's family background and status, and especially his own ideological leanings, that he was very well suited to the needs of carrying out rescue work, and so the organization had me go out to invite him to serve as Guan Xiangying's defense lawyer. Dressed as an upper-class woman, I went to find Chen Zhigao and told him that I was someone who had broken away from the Communist Party organization, that my husband was the famous Communist Wan Xiyan, who had already given his life and had left behind an orphan to be raised by the family, and that I had come to Shanghai to look for a livelihood. Declaring my identity this way was precisely to let the lawyer I was engaging know that I was a revolutionary woman keeping nothing from him, so that the necessary trust could be built up between us. I also told him that my father, Huang Yingchu, was a well-known figure of Hunan, who in those years had helped Tan Sitong compile and edit the *Study of Benevolence* (Renxue) and had supported the reform movement; that my family were petty landlords (this was a pretense—in fact they were not), and that the man to be rescued was a good friend of my dead husband Wan Xiyan and also my elder cousin on my paternal aunt's side. He had come to Shanghai to do a little business and, who knew why, had been arrested as a Communist. But he really was not a Communist, so the relatives and friends of both families all wanted me to think of some way, at all costs, to save him. In the end I said: "I've long heard that the old gentleman Chen and the great lawyer Chen are both very enlightened and progressive, full of a sense of justice and sympathy—please, at all costs, take the trouble to help release this wronged and innocent man." After hearing my statement, Chen Zhigao took on the case, and set a time for me to visit his father at his home, so as to win the old gentleman's sympathy and support and thereby use his influence to resolve the matter quickly.
The first time I went to Chen Zhigao's home, Sun Xiaocun accompanied me. Sun Xiaocun, Chen Gaoyong, and Li Nanxiang were all university professors and often went to the Chen home; Chen Zhigao had introduced these good friends of his to the old gentleman and the old lady. Sun and the others were courteous and well-bred in their speech, and so made a very good impression on the old gentleman. The old gentleman was ill and resting at home, and as for his sons' and daughters' social dealings, so long as those who came and went were decent people, he never inquired into or interfered with them. Of course, the old gentleman did not know that these people would have any relation to the Communist Party. Chen Zhigao said to his father: Sun Xiaocun wanted to introduce a Miss Huang to come and pay her respects; it was said that Miss Huang's father had in those years helped Tan Sitong compile the *Study of Benevolence* and had supported the reform movement. In the late Qing, enlightened men among the scholar-officials all admired Tan Sitong greatly, holding that in compiling the *Study of Benevolence*, upholding the Guangxu Emperor's reforms, advocating study of the experience of Japan's Meiji Restoration, and instituting a constitutional monarchy—all to seek China's wealth and strength—and then giving his own life for his country without even implicating his parents at home, he was truly loyal and filial both, most rare and admirable. The old gentleman had always revered Tan Sitong and his fellows, and so, before we had even met, he already had a good impression of me. Chen Zhigao also said that Miss Huang's cousin was a petty merchant who had been arrested by the authorities in the mistaken belief that he was a "Communist suspect," and that it was for this that she had come to seek help. Hearing this, the old gentleman said very open-mindedly: "In times like these, when the fruits of the Revolution of 1911 have all been thrown away, warlords fight year after year, and everywhere there are exorbitant taxes and levies—the Communist Party, the Communist Party! How could there be so many people willing to risk losing their heads to be Communists? Isn't it all forced on them by those new warlords and corrupt officials? Where officials press, the people rebel!" And so Sun Xiaocun took me to be received.
When we reached the Chen home, the old gentleman took one look and saw that I was dignified and poised in bearing and speech, an upper-class woman of good breeding. He did not stand on ceremony, but at our first meeting asked me very earnestly and minutely: "Why is it that you are a Hunanese, yet your cousin is a man from beyond the Pass?" (In those days the Northeast was called "beyond the Pass.") I was well prepared, and answered at once, unhurried and calm: "My paternal aunt married far away, beyond the Pass, in those years, and so my cousin is a man from beyond the Pass. He came to Shanghai this time to do business, and, who knows why, was fitted with a 'red cap' and arrested as a 'Communist suspect'—it is truly a wrong!"
Having finished the business at this first call, I took my leave. Afterward the old gentleman said to the old lady: "No wonder Run'er [Chen Zhigao was born in a leap year and leap month, and his pet name was Run'er, 'Leap Boy'] is always mentioning Miss Huang in front of us. This Miss Huang has a strong scholarly air, very refined, very elegant in speech and bearing—she really does look like a young lady from a house of scholars…" And so, at that very first meeting, I left the old gentleman Chen with a very good impression. This created very favorable conditions for Chen Zhigao's taking on the case. He then, in his father's name and using his connections, began the work of smoothing things over with the judge.
The judge trying Guan Xiangying's case was named Zhao Yunyi, a young man who had taken part in the Northern Expedition war and later transferred out of the army to Longhua, where he became a judge. His family too were probably old family friends of the old gentleman Chen's, so a single telephone call from the old gentleman brought him over. The old gentleman Chen had Chen Zhigao tell me to come to his home to have midday dinner together. The house the Chen family lived in was a great house of three bays with front and rear wing-rooms in two courtyards; besides the several sons and daughters still at school, there were also some nephews of the clan lodging there, the family kept a chef to do the cooking and several serving-women, the front and rear wing-rooms were all full of people, and it was a large household that had to set out three tables at every meal. The old gentleman had always eaten in his own room downstairs, waited on specially, together with the old lady, by Sister Qian (we all called her Mother Qian), who had been brought over from the old lady's own family. Only at the New Year and festivals would the sons and daughters come in to drink a cup with the old gentleman; ordinarily he seldom had anyone dine with him. That day was an exception: the old gentleman had me, Chen Zhigao, Judge Zhao, Sun Xiaocun, and a few others dine together. During the meal, the old gentleman himself came straight to the point and said to Judge Zhao: "This Miss Huang is my goddaughter. Her father, Huang Yingchu, was in those years a private secretary on Tan Sitong's staff and edited the *Study of Benevolence* for him. Her cousin is a businessman, and, who knows why, has been arrested in Shanghai as a 'Communist suspect.' Perhaps you've got the wrong man? What evidence is there? How was his deposition?" The judge said: "There's no evidence to speak of; he says himself he's a merchant." The old gentleman then said: "Just so! With no evidence, why arrest people wildly? This case is one Zhigao has taken on—for my sake, then, let him go!" The judge said quickly: "Uncle Chen, I'll look over the case file again when I get back. Whatever you instruct, I'll be sure to do my utmost to carry out."
After the meal, I hurriedly reported all this to the organization, and hearing it everyone felt there was great hope and was very pleased. At the same time, I also reported to the organization that the old gentleman Chen had suddenly declared me his goddaughter. The organization considered that accepting this adoptive kinship would be favorable to covering my work, and so it prepared a generous gift for me and chose the 28th of the seventh lunar month, the old gentleman's birthday, for me to go to the Chen home and formally perform the great ceremony of paying respects to my adoptive father and adoptive mother. At the ceremony I said this was my parents' wish—that it was not easy for me, alone in Shanghai, to make a living and find work, and that if I had the care of such an old gentleman and old lady of standing and status, they too would be at ease. The old gentleman was delighted, and instructed the eldest brother and sister-in-law, in his and the old lady's name, to set out three tables of a banquet at the Hongyun Restaurant, feasting the various branches of the Chen family's relatives. During the banquet, Chen Zhigao and his eldest brother and sister-in-law introduced me to everyone and formally announced that I was the old gentleman and old lady's goddaughter. The Chen family's relatives in Shanghai—such as the Seventh Aunt's husband (surnamed Han) and the Eighth Aunt's husband (surnamed Li), both judges of the International Settlement like the old gentleman, and many relatives and friends of Zhigao's generation—all came to offer congratulations. Later, in my rescue work and during the War of Resistance against Japan, the many relatives and friends of this large family all helped us a great deal and played an important part.
