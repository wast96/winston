## 6

The fire in the reed-marsh burned three days and three nights, and Zhu Qi died in it, turned to ash together with the reeds that ran on out of sight. That day Zhu Qi had thought the Japanese soldiers would come after them without let-up; he had gripped Yanzhi's hand and run for his life, but the bullets shrieking past and the flames spreading on every side left them nowhere to hide. He threw the fowling-piece away, and when a shoe came off he could spare no thought to pick it up, but only held tight to Yanzhi's hand and all but dragged her along. Yanzhi truly could run no more; she wrenched her hand from Zhu Qi's, dropped to the ground, and said she couldn't, she couldn't run another step. Zhu Qi gasped the harder and said, you'll burn to death.

Yanzhi shook her head hard. “Better that than run myself to death,” she said.

If you die, who am I to marry? Zhu Qi gave a laugh and said, I'll carry you.

So saying, he put out his hand, but his body swayed, and slowly he toppled onto Yanzhi. Her hand came away full of blood, and only then did Yanzhi find that Zhu Qi's black satin gown was long since soaked through with it. A bullet, at some moment she never knew, had bored a hole below his ribs.

And so Zhu Qi died on top of Yanzhi. Before he died he put out a hand to point a direction and told Yanzhi to run; the boat, he said, was moored just ahead. But Yanzhi did not move; her hands were smeared with the warm, living blood, and she had no strength at all to push this man off her. A dying man is so very heavy. Yanzhi had never thought she would die together with a man like this, that this great fire would fold their ashes into the earth as one. Then Zhu Qi put his mouth to her ear and said his money was all buried under the bed where they had slept; he told Yanzhi to dig it up, go home, and live well. That said, Zhu Qi buried his head in Yanzhi's breast, and only after a long while lifted his face to look at the sky dyed red by the firelight. Zhu Qi said, last of all, a pity I never had the luck to be your man.

These were the last words Zhu Qi left in the world. Afterward it was the water bandits, catching up, who carried Yanzhi on their backs and found that boat, and even when the boat had run far out Yanzhi was still looking back at the firelight reddening the edge of the sky. In her ears one voice alone went round and round: a pity I never had the luck to be your man.

Five days later, when the thick smoke over the shoal had not yet cleared and the scorched earth still burned the soles of the feet, this boatload of men came back. The moment they stepped onto the shoal they were turning over the ruins for their kin and friends, but every ash was the same as the next, all carrying the hot reek of burning, blown this way and that in the wind. Grief and despair left the men helpless; when they had wept they questioned one another with their eyes, and in the end every gaze came to rest on Yanzhi's face.

Yanzhi's face was pale, and Zhu Qi's blood was clotted on her still, so that her look was strange and savage. Take me home, Yanzhi said. The men were silent; no one knew who was to make the decision. So Yanzhi urged them to go home together, all of them, back to their wives and children. But the men squatted one by one on the ruins and only clutched their heads. Old Mo said suddenly, where is there to go back to? They had taken to this road, he said, because they could not live otherwise. He told Yanzhi to look at these men: if they went home, could they still farm, could they still fish? Old Mo shook his head. Apart from robbing and smoking opium, he said, there's nothing we can do.

Yanzhi said nothing, and turned to look at the far mist-blurred reach where water met sky. After a long while she asked Old Mo of a sudden, which household on this marsh is the richest? Old Mo said, it used to be us; now... that would be Pockmarked Liu.

At dusk Yanzhi had the men dig Zhu Qi's fortune out of the ruins: two urns filled with silver coin and gold bars. The men's eyes lit up all at once, but Yanzhi said, this is not for you to go and smoke away. She told Old Mo to find someone to buy guns — she wanted the kind the Japanese had, the kind that put a hole through a man with a single shot.

Old Mo looked at the two urns. “This is the chief's savings of a whole lifetime,” he said.

For all his money, didn't he die anyway? Yanzhi looked at them all and said slowly, only with guns can you keep your lives.

Old Mo said, at a loss, but where are we to buy guns like the Japanese have?

“No guns,” Yanzhi said, “then buy hoes, and go home and farm, every one of you.”

A few days later Old Mo brought back a bundle of chopping-knives, long and short, by boat. He told Yanzhi he had tried every channel worth trying; no one dared trade in arms anymore, for the Japanese killed at the sight of a gun.

Yanzhi looked at the bundle of knives on the ground, raised her eyes slowly, and asked them all, have you made up your minds?

Someone said, Pockmarked Liu was the chief's sworn brother, and he has twenty or thirty men under him.

Sworn brother? Yanzhi said with a curl of her lip; then when we were in trouble, why did we never see him lift a hand to help?

But that would be brother turning on brother — a great taboo.

Whose taboo? Yanzhi's voice turned all at once shrill; looking at the men standing off to one side, she said, tell me — do we wait here to starve? Or wait for the Japanese to come and finish us off all over again?

Then let's just go over and throw in with Pockmarked Liu.

Yanzhi gave a cold laugh. “A homeless cur,” she said, “gets killed sooner or later.”

The men fell silent, and the wind scraped their faces like a knife. But Yanzhi decided all at once to marry down to Pockmarked Liu. Among the fishermen of the Xiangfu marsh this was a rule handed down a thousand years and more: when an elder brother died, all that was his passed to the younger brother to inherit, his woman included. Yanzhi sent Old Mo across to say that her dowry was these twenty-odd brothers, and to beg Pockmarked Liu for a bowl of rice. Hearing it, Pockmarked Liu laughed aloud and said, a fine fat slice of white meat delivered to your mouth — not to have a taste would be to do Zhu Qi a great wrong.

To Yanzhi this was a boundless humiliation, yet she agreed in a word. That night Pockmarked Liu's boat dropped anchor in the middle of the Xiangfu marsh, and he sent a little skiff to carry Yanzhi out to it. Yanzhi drank with him in the cabin, then waited on him into bed, her every act and gesture those of a low and wanton whore. Never had Yanzhi smiled at a man so. In the small hours, when everyone on the boat was sunk in sleep, Yanzhi slipped out from under the quilt, listened quietly a good while, then slowly drew the short knife that hung at the head of Pockmarked Liu's bed and drove it hard into his chest. This was the trick Old Mo had taught her before she boarded: to kill a man at a stroke, it is either the throat or the heart. But Yanzhi was not sure of it; she shut her eyes and stabbed, stroke upon stroke, as though pounding rice-cake in a stone mortar. Only when the knife was sunk in the corpse's chest and she had no strength left to pull it out did she let out a breath and slump down at the foot of the bed. So killing was as simple as that. Yanzhi drew a deep breath, stood, and put on her clothes. She went out of the cabin, lowered the fishing-lamp that hung high on the mast, blew it out with one breath, then came back into the cabin, shut the door, and sat down again on the floor against the foot of the bed, holding herself tight.

Before long Old Mo brought the brothers climbing up along the hull like water-ghosts, and swinging their knives they had the situation in hand in short order. Before the day had fully broken they steered the boat straight for Pockmarked Liu's lair. The fight was over before it began. Yanzhi had not slept all night; wrapped in a man's fur coat, her two eyes red and swollen, her face pale, she sat in Pockmarked Liu's carved armchair and stared at her own two hands.

There was not a sound in the room; the men watched her one and all, holding their breath. Old Mo suddenly raised a hand and said loudly, come, let us pay our respects to the Big Sister-in-law.

In the water bandits' dictionary the two words Big Sister-in-law were not merely a form of address but an office, just as they called Zhu Qi Big Brother. Its other name was this: the one who runs the house.

After the autumn of the second year had passed, not the least trace of the great fire's burning could be seen anywhere on the shoal. The wind came off the face of the lake and lifted the reed-down whirling like snow across the sky. Yanzhi gave birth to a baby girl.
