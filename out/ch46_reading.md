## Toshizō at Osaka

A rout, in a word.

There was no other word for it. Toshizō sent the unhurt south on foot along the Yodo River, and put the wounded aboard thirty-koku riverboats to go down to Osaka.

(A beaten army is a sorry thing to look on.)

{j} so he thought, for the very look of his men's faces, the set of their shoulders, had changed. By any measure they were the soldiers of a beaten army. Even a man as full of spirit as Harada Sanosuke, captain of the Tenth Unit, walked leaning on his spear as on a staff.

Toshizō got down from his horse,

{j} "Sanosuke, put some heart into it. Don't forget the men are watching,"

{j} he said.

Sanosuke was tired, too. But being a man of high spirits at ordinary times, a losing fight, no doubt, left him utterly limp; —he gave Toshizō a hard sidelong look,

{j} "I can't be like you, and that's a fact,"

{j} and, saying only that, walked on with the look of a man spent to his last thread.

"We still have Osaka Castle, all of us,"

{j} Toshizō, back in the saddle, heartened them. At Osaka Castle was the Shogun. There were tens of thousands of unhurt shogunal officers and men. There were weapons.

"The castle is a fortress of metal walls and boiling moats. So long as we hold it and fight with the Shogun at our head, every clan in the land that is against Satsuma and Chōshū will rise as one,"

By any reckoning it was a battle to be won. True, at Toba-Fushimi those who actually fought were about the Aizu clan, the Shinsengumi, and the Mimawarigumi, and no more; the Tōdō clan, though charged with the gun-position at Yamazaki, had turned its coat in fine style. The shogunate's own Western-style infantry had been busier running than fighting.

But the main strength was at Osaka. And the castle, though Hideyoshi had built it, was a great fortress that, ever since Ieyasu's day, had been strengthened and strengthened again against any rising by the western daimyo—the Mōri and the Shimazu above all.

(Never could the Satsuma-Chōshū strength bring it down.)

Not Toshizō alone: any military expert, of any age or country, would have taken an easy view of it here.

"At Osaka we begin the war over again,"

Toshizō put fresh spirit into them all.

Toshizō was not wrong.

Here, too, lay the headache of the Imperial army in Kyoto. The Imperial army had not even the power to pursue. To pursue and widen a victory is the common sense of war, but they had not the numbers.

At the time, the Chōshū clansman Inoue Monta—later Kaoru, and a marquis—who was taking part in the Satsuma-Chōshū allied army's planning in Kyoto, for one,

{j} "The shogunal troops are certain to hold Osaka Castle. As I judge it, they will make Osaka their base, stretch their forces out on every side, seize the open port of Hyōgo—Kobe—to bring in weapons from abroad, block the landing of reinforcements coming up from the Satsuma and Chōshū home provinces, and, with their superior fleet, blockade the Inland Sea. Do that, and we here in Kyoto are rats in a bag. And the shogunate's hereditary daimyo, the Wakasa Obama clan and the like, will close off the Ōtsu approach. The townsfolk of Kyoto get their rice mainly from Ōmi, so they will be driven to starve. Once it comes to that, we few troops in the capital are beaten. Therefore I will hurry back to my home province at once, raise all its troops, and come up into the home provinces by the San'in and the San'yō roads. I would have Satsuma do the same. There is no other way but that,"

{j} so he argued; the Satsuma side agreed, and it was settled that until the home-province troops came up, they would take a holding posture, merely setting gun-batteries on the hill-country south of Kyoto, at Yawata and Yamazaki.

Toshizō's optimism about the state of the war was, then, only natural.

When they had come down as far as Moriguchi, the five-tiered keep of Osaka Castle showed in the sky to the southwest.

"Look,"

{j} said Toshizō, raising his whip.

{j} "So long as that castle stands, the realm will not pass so easily into Satsuma-Chōshū hands."

In feeling, it must have been much like that of Sanada Yukimura at the Winter and Summer sieges of Osaka. Though in Yukimura's case, to be sure, the enemy was the Tokugawa house, the other way about.

But the whole force was grimly silent. All of them had taken a fearful fire at the Fushimi approach. The Satsuma-Chōshū breech-loaders could fire ten rounds for every one that an Aizu muzzle-loader fired. While an Aizu matchlock was loading a single round, the other side would pour in twenty.

(The world, it seems, has changed.)

That was the truth the corpsmen came to know in their bodies, under the actual fire. It was not mere defeat; the shock in the mind, of that realization, was the greater thing.

(Bah—guns like that, we've only to buy them.)

Toshizō alone made light of it.

But the shogunal army's wounded were beyond counting, and every man who came down the river, one after another, had his bandages soaked red. The wounds were mostly not of sword and spear but of shell and rifle-ball: men with an arm or a leg torn off, men whose jaws a splinter had bitten away, men with as many as three balls driven into their bodies—it was a sight of the utmost horror.

"Oh, that war"—the Battle of Toba-Fushimi—"was a terrible commotion. When we fled off toward Kyōbashi in Osaka, ever so many blood-soaked samurai of the shogunal side came down the Yodo River by boat," so a witness of those days recalled, one who long outlived them: Inaba Yukie, of Konohana-chō 1 in the Kita ward of the city of Osaka. When, at a hundred and one years of age, she was honored by the city as one of its oldest people, her very first words to the newspaperman who came to see her were, "At the time of the war—." She said simply "the war." The reporter took it for the Greater East Asia War, but on close asking it turned out to be the Battle of Toba-Fushimi.

Toshizō entered the Osaka intendant's residence, assigned as the Shinsengumi's quarters. It stood on the east side, at the south end of the Tenma Bridge—a stately mansion.

"Is Kondō here?"

{j} he asked the people of the intendant's residence, and was told he was at the castle-warden's lower mansion in the third block of Uchihonmachi, resting his wound.

"And Okita Sōji?"

{j} he asked, but the official did not know so much as that.

"Nagakura-kun, I leave the wounded to you,"

{j} and with that he settled into the saddle.

Going straight south down the narrow Tanimachi-suji, he entered the castle-warden's lower mansion, and as he was tying up his horse, a drop of rain fell.

Cold.

This commonplace sensation, which for some days past had not so much as crossed his mind, came back to Toshizō's skin for the first time.

The rain came pattering down. Toshizō began walking slowly toward the entrance. He was tired. Tired to the bone. Never in all his life had he felt his legs so heavy.

Of a sudden,

{j} (What has become of Oyuki?)

{j} —the thought came. It was a wildly unexpected fancy, yet beyond the pine at the entrance he seemed to see, vividly, the figure of Oyuki.

An illusion, of course.

He was tired.

"Where is Kondō's room?"

{j} he asked, walking down the corridor, of a man in a brand-new woolen uniform who looked to be a shogunal infantry director. A man who had not gone out to the fighting, no doubt.

"Kondō? Which Lord Kondō would that be?"

{j} the woolen uniform asked back, reasonably enough.

"You don't know? Kondō means Kondō of the Shinsengumi, and no other,"

{j} Toshizō bellowed. His nerves, of course, were not in an ordinary state.

Toshizō slid open, with a clatter, the paper screen of the room he was shown to.

Kondō lay there alone.

"It's Toshi,"

{j} and Toshizō edged close and laid his sword down by the pillow.

"We've come back beaten,"

"I've heard,"

{j} and Kondō looked up at Toshizō with eyes gone terribly weak.

"You've had a hard time of it,"

"How is the wound?"

{j} Toshizō said, turning the talk aside.

"The shoulder won't move at all. Dr. Ryōjun—Matsumoto—tells me it will soon heal, but its not moving is the nuisance of it. No—he does say it will be as good as new in another month or so,"

"Then in a month you can fight,"

"I should be able to,"

Toshizō nodded, and after telling him briefly of the state of the fighting, the men's work, the losses,

{j} "And how is Sōji?"

{j} he asked.

***

When he called at Okita Sōji's room, the Tokugawa house physician Matsumoto Ryōjun was just then sitting at the bedside.

"Ah, so you're Hijikata-san. Every day I hear your name from Kondō-san and from this Okita-san here, so I feel already as though we were friends of a hundred years,"

{j} he said, all at once and without ceremony. He was of an age a little above Kondō's, thirty-seven or thirty-eight, with large features and a bold, stalwart face one would hardly take for a physician's. Since this was a man who would go on to campaign in the northeast, and after the Restoration, by a special pardon, rise at last to be Surgeon-General, he seems to have had a taste for war.

"Bah, Toba-Fushimi's no lost battle. Come, let me hear about it,"

"Let that wait a little,"

{j} and he peered into Okita's face.

Okita was smiling. It was that bright smile peculiar to him, as though the sun shone on that one spot alone.

(But how wasted he's grown.)

"Okita-kun will be all right,"

"Is that so,"

{j} Toshizō looked at Ryōjun doubtfully. The smile had gone from Ryōjun's face.

(So it is hopeless, after all.)

"Hijikata-san,"

{j} Okita opened his mouth to speak.

"Don't talk. With this illness it's bad to tire yourself,"

{j} and Toshizō made to take Okita's hand.

But Okita, as if abashed, slipped his hand down into the coverlet.

He was thin. There was no flesh at all on his arm. That, no doubt, was what Okita was ashamed of.

"I've corps business left to do. I'll go. But Sōji, I'll come every day,"

"Hijikata-san,"

{j} and Okita, letting his eyes run to a spray of plum by his pillow, as though pointing to it with them,

{j} "Oyuki-san arranged that for me."

"What?"

{j} Toshizō, half risen to his feet, asked it back.

"Oyuki—which Oyuki is that?"

"Come now,"

{j} Okita looked into Toshizō's eyes and, only smiling, nodded.

"She is here in Osaka. Every day she comes to look in on me,"

"Now that you say it, there was the same spray of plum in Kondō's room,"

"So there would be. And yet, even when she comes here, Oyuki-san does not say one word about you, Hijikata-san,"

(That is the kind of woman she is.)

Toshizō's eyes went far away for a moment, but he was already on his feet. And, for a sign of how flustered he was, he forgot to take his leave of Matsumoto Ryōjun.

Ryōjun seemed to say something teasing, but Toshizō was already out in the corridor.

(Oyuki, is it.)

{j} and as he slid the screen shut behind him with one hand, he saw the thin rain falling into the inner court-garden.

(I want to see her . . .)

Toshizō walked softly down the corridor, and when he came to himself he was crouched on the open veranda, gazing at a small shrub of winter daphne.

(Oyuki—won't you listen again to the old tales of my home country?)

The rain filled Toshizō's eyes, yet the pupils of them seemed to see nothing at all. His face was like an idiot's. Perhaps because the damp of the rain had begun to settle in, the smell of powder still clinging to his shoulders drifted faintly to his nose.

"It was a worthless fight,"

{j} Toshizō was murmuring aloud, half to Oyuki.

"But we'll fight one battle at Osaka,"

"Hijikata-san,"

{j} came a voice behind him.

With a start, he turned.

There stood Matsumoto Ryōjun of a moment before. Toshizō remembered long afterward the look on Ryōjun's face at this time—an expression, how shall one say, hard to put a name to.

"Did you not know?"

{j} said Ryōjun.

"Yoshinobu, and the Aizu Lieutenant-General too, are no longer in the castle,"

"What—"

"They have fled,"

"And—and did Kondō and Okita know of it?"

"They know. To you, who had come through such bitter fighting, it must have been hard to tell,"

(Left behind.—)

{j} —that feeling was not Toshizō's alone. It must have been the feeling of every warrior who had fought at Toba-Fushimi, the dead not least of all.

"Tell me the whole of it,"

{j} and for Toshizō there was no phantom of Oyuki now.

Yoshinobu had fled from his own side as well.

In fact, Yoshinobu fled from his own side as well. When the ill turn of the fighting toward Toba-Fushimi was reported in haste within Osaka Castle, the castle was in a ferment, and the war party, by way of natural tactical counsel,

{j} "Let Your Highness leave the castle and take the field with all speed. If you but set at your head the horse-standard borne since Lord Ieyasu's day, the hatamoto and the vassals of the hereditary daimyo will fight, one and all, resolved to die before your horse. The numbers are on our side. Victory is beyond all doubt. And on the Settsu sea our navy already has its warships afloat, awaiting your command,"

{j} so they urged him earnestly. Yoshinobu's whole entourage joined in this, and at last Yoshinobu rose to his feet,

{j} "Very well. From this moment I take the field. Let every man make ready,"

{j} he said. The Aizu clansmen above all raised a shout, and in high glad spirits all went back each to his post.

In that interval, Yoshinobu made his escape. It was toward ten at night on the sixth of the First Month. He took only a handful of men with him. And the chief of that handful was—of all people—the Aizu Lieutenant-General Matsudaira Katamori, who had once wielded such power in Kyoto as Protector of the city. The Aizu clansmen were cast off by their own Aizu lord as well. Of this man Katamori most speak with the word "composed." But, as the saying has it that "the highborn know nothing of feeling," a lord born to his rank seems in the end to have, at the last extremity, a sense of things different from the common run. Toshizō and the Shinsengumi were cast off by two masters: the lord of Aizu, and Yoshinobu.

The Kuwana troops too had fought savagely toward Toba, but their lord, Matsudaira Echū-no-kami—Katamori's own younger brother—was among this handful of fugitive nobles. As with Yoshinobu, so with these two daimyo: they did not tell even their own close attendants of the "flight."

By night they went secretly out of Osaka Castle's rear gate. As they passed the rear gate a sentry challenged them,

{j} "Who goes there?"

{j} he demanded; but the senior councilor Itakura Iga-no-kami, who was with Yoshinobu,

{j} "It is the changing of the pages,"

{j} lied, and so they got out of the castle without trouble. After that they hurried through the night streets of Osaka, took a small boat from Hachikenya, and rowed down the river out to sea. Off Tenpōzan, four shogunal warships should have lain at anchor.

But the sea's surface was dark.

The warships of various foreign countries lay at anchor there too. Yoshinobu and his party, worn out with searching for where the shogunal warships were, went at last to the American warship lying nearest at hand and begged a night's lodging. The American captain received the party into his cabin. In the small hours, when the state of the harbor could be made out, they moved to the shogunal warship Kaiyō-maru.

It was not until the next day that the castle knew Yoshinobu and the rest were gone. Within the castle, all were stunned.

The Meiji journalist Fukuchi Gen'ichirō—Ōchi—was at this time within Osaka Castle as a translator under the shogunal commissioner of foreign affairs. He too was a proper hatamoto. He has left a written account of it. (What follows is the sense of it.)

On the night of the sixth I was in the translators' room in the castle, smoking and speaking ill of my superiors with my colleagues; in the end I tired even of idle talk, took out a blanket, and lay down to sleep in my usual way. But in the dead of night my friend Matsudaira Tarō came in, armed in the Western fashion.

"What are you all so calm about?"

{j} he said, and, raising his thumb,

{j} "His Highness took his leave long since,"

{j} so he said. I chid him—"Tarō, in a place like this, don't say such ill-omened things even in jest"—and,

{j} "If you doubt it, go and look in the audience chamber,"

{j} and Tarō went away.

Matsudaira Tarō was, directly after the Shogun's withdrawal, appointed an infantry captain. So this "I," Fukuchi Gen'ichirō, who heard it from Tarō, must have been among the very first in the castle to catch the news.

Toshizō, his doubt still not cleared, rode his horse in through the great gate and, catching hold of men who looked to be high officers, put the question to them.

"It is true,"

{j} they all said.

In proof of it, the smoke of burning secret papers was already beginning to rise in thick clouds.

"Sir,"

{j} said a hatamoto of some rank.

{j} "We did not know of it either. But off Tenpōzan many shogunal warships lie at anchor, under the command of Enomoto Izumi-no-kami—Takeaki,"

"Then it means we fight on, does it,"

"No—it means our persons will be carried safely away by warship,"

"Fool,"

{j} and Toshizō knocked the man down. He must have been struck very hard, for he lay still.

(There now.)

{j} Toshizō got back on his horse. His hot rage at Yoshinobu and Katamori had made him, in spite of himself, lay hands on the man. Feeling he had done him a wrong, no doubt,

{j} "I am Hijikata Toshizō of the Shinsengumi. If you bear me a grudge, come and have it out with me,"

Wheeling his horse's head about, he broke into a run toward the great gate.

(I'll fight, by God.)

Let Yoshinobu flee, let Katamori flee: Hijikata Toshizō, at least, would fight on.

Yoshinobu and Katamori had their own reasons, such as they were.

But Toshizō had nothing but the brawler's instinct.
