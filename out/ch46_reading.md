## Chapter 46

Back at my lodgings, I suddenly could not sleep. Like a man on his way to the execution ground, I found myself wanting, before I died, to call on a few relatives and friends and see them one last time. I resolved that once I had slept and woken I would go to see several of the people who had a special bond with my life, and one of them was Helen. This resolve made me anxious to fall asleep, but that was just what I could not do; I tossed and turned, my thoughts running this way and that, and it was nine o'clock before I finally dropped off.

I woke at four in the afternoon. Meaning to go and see those few people as I had planned in the night, I decided to carry my evening clothes in the car and change into them at seven, when I went as arranged to Honsa Jiro's place, and to set out with him once I had changed. So what I had on now was ordinary dress. I wound my scarf about me and put on my overcoat, and in the instant of drawing on my gloves I took out a cigarette from habit to smoke; and just as I struck the match and drew the first mouthful of smoke, a sensation like a flash of lightning made me waver over the whole business of calling on my relatives and friends. So I sat down on the sofa and began to turn a good many things over. First: the way I had said goodbye to Bai Ping the night before made it certain that I would let the same air of a parting-before-death show through in front of others, which would be an omen of my failure, or else I would let others' questioning shake my first resolve. Second: everyone's pity and sympathy, or their groundless melancholy, would only sap the courage I needed for my work. Third: I ought to hold within myself a faith that I would surely win. That being so, I ought not to indulge in such weak, soft, half-hearted conduct; and if for a moment I could not master my feelings, above all in front of Helen, I might let slip the secret of my work, and how shameful that would be. With these several considerations, I decided in the end to give up the plan. It was still too early now to go to Honsa Jiro's; they would not be at home, and yet to stay in was too stifling. My mind, of course, was in no state to read or to work; I thought too of all the places of amusement, but did not want to go to any of them. Just as I was at a loss how to pass the time, the servant came up to say there was a telephone call.

"Who is it?" I went down, picked up the receiver, and asked.

"Bai Ping."

"Bai Ping."

"Yes," she said. "I want you to come."

"No."

"You must come, Xu!"

"All right," I said. "But you are not to bring up last night's question again."

"Very well," she said, after a moment's hesitation. But it struck me all at once that the atmosphere at her place really would not suit my present mood, and I made my tone very light and said:

"Bai Ping, let's go out and enjoy ourselves, shall we?"

"But at half past six I have to go to dinner with someone."

I knew this was Arita's dinner engagement, before going on to the masked ball afterward. I said:

"Of course. Shall we say the Xiangong?"

"Good." Her voice was very cheerful. "I'll come right now; see you there."

"But," I put in quickly, "no mention of last night's question."

"Of course," she said crisply. "Today is for pleasure, pure and simple; we need to forget reality."

When I had hung up I went off to keep the appointment; Bai Ping arrived later than I. Although we were able to find some pleasure in the music, and although she did not say a single word about last night's question or tonight's work, there seemed to be a strange uneasiness in both our hearts, so that even with lively talk and a cheerful air, Bai Ping seemed forever to be trying to break this loneliness and gloom, and I too was consciously making the effort; but every burst of laughter was forced, every conversation dry, and our wits could not dilute our feelings. The time slipped by in one piece of music after another. Under the pressure of an unbearable silence I had no recourse but to keep asking her to dance; and this heavy mood governed not only our talk and laughter but our every movement, so that our dance steps could never fall into the old harmony.

In this uncomfortable situation I came slowly to feel that today's amusement was rather a kind of torment. Two or three times I thought of escaping from Bai Ping, but still I stuck it out, and I supposed Bai Ping was the same. So I began to regret having come to this tea dance where there were no dance hostesses, and I said:

"Let's move on to somewhere else."

Bai Ping did not answer; she looked at me and said slowly:

"It's nearly time, too." That word "too," plainly enough, meant that she had already given up hope of today's atmosphere.

I looked at my watch: it was already eight minutes past six. So I said nothing, nothing at all, and let the time slip by in the music. But this whole silence was not because we were pondering the night's work, nor because there was any fear in our hearts; I believe that beneath consciousness we both had the night's business buried in us, but had not thought it through in detail. My mind was extraordinarily blank, with nothing to reflect on, nothing to observe or experience, only the sense that Bai Ping held some indescribable menace for me. Several times I feared she would bring up last night's question; every smile seemed as though it might lead to last night's question; but she did not, she only sat there in silence, her eyes gazing at a world without reason, neither questioning nor curious, only looking emptily and forlornly into space. At last, with a disappointed smile breaking through, she said:

"Let's go."

I saw her out, and at the door she said:

"Will you see me home?"

"You're going home first?"

"Of course," she said. "I have to change."

So I opened the car door and let her get in; she sat beside me, and I drove, and neither of us said another word, all the way to the front of her lodgings, where she got out. As though to stop me from getting out too, she said:

"See you tonight."

"Right," I said. "See you tonight."

But she suddenly turned back again to shake my hand, her eyes on me, and said again:

"I wish you victory."

"Thank you."

She shut the car door, I started the car, and I saw that she was still waving to me.

Being with Bai Ping brought no sense of gaiety, and yet the moment I left her I felt an indescribable loneliness. As if in flight, I put on full speed and hurried off to find Honsa Jiro.

Honsa Jiro had in fact invited me to dinner at his house and then to go on together to the masked ball, but I had not thought that he had also invited the other people who were to go with us. Only after I came in did I discover there were so many guests. Of the men there were four, mostly Japanese merchants I had met before; of the women there were five, all of them Japanese except for one dance hostess from the Xiangong, Sophie, not one of whom I knew, and they said Sophie had been asked expressly for me. Among the women I did not know was one called Miyama Yoshiko, said to be a young lady who had come from Tokyo two months before, exceedingly quiet and demure, and speaking very little.

Not long before, Honsa Jiro had begun living with a Japanese woman, whom we all called Mrs. Honsa; I had met her three or four times. She received us very courteously, but showed Miyama Yoshiko a quite unexpected deference, which made us feel that we too could not but hold Miyama Yoshiko in a special respect.

I have no Japanese, and from the time I went in until we sat down to table I spoke very little with those few Japanese women guests, and least of all with Miyama Yoshiko.

Honsa Jiro had been many years in China and was thoroughly versed in both the language and the food; the dishes that night were Beiping food from the Minghu Chun, very rich and sumptuous. Once we were seated, I realized that Honsa Jiro was giving this dinner tonight especially in honor of Miyama Yoshiko. So Miyama Yoshiko sat in the seat of the guest of honor, and I sat at Miyama Yoshiko's left hand.

When the wine had been poured, Honsa Jiro rose, raised his cup, and said:

"Let us all drink a cup to Miss Miyama Yoshiko."

We all rose and raised our cups, but Miyama Yoshiko sat where she was and, with a serene air, lifted her own cup.

When we had drained our cups and sat down, Honsa Jiro suddenly said to me:

"You may speak English to Miss Miyama."

Since the outbreak of the Pacific war, English had in Japanese eyes been the language of an enemy country; but when Honsa said this so abruptly, I thought Honsa must be on very familiar terms with Miyama Yoshiko.

I began to exchange a few words with Miss Miyama, but her English was not good; she answered my questions throughout with a word or two, so I did not talk much. In fact Miyama's silence seemed to be her nature: she spoke little Japanese too, her voice was very low, she ate little, and her manner was refined and delicate; she seemed a young lady of a noble family. After Honsa introduced us I had sat far from her the whole time and had not looked at her directly; now, seated beside her, I began to catch the faint elegant fragrance of her powder, and so I looked rather more closely at her profile.

Of the women present, three had already changed into evening gowns; Sophie was still in a soft-yellow cheongsam, Mrs. Honsa was in kimono as before, and Miss Miyama too was in kimono.

I can tell the splendor of a kimono, but of what a kimono signifies of rank I understand very little. Miss Miyama was not short; sitting there she was not much lower than I. Looking up from her collar I felt she was just such a Japanese beauty as one sees in pictures; yet her face was altogether of the lively, childlike type, with no strong air of the classical. Such a face ought to have carried the charm of a ready, laughing grace; yet she was quiet and grave throughout. When she reached for the dish at her left I watched her eyes: the lashes were very long, but the eyes were always cast down as though gazing at the ground, an impression just like the one given me by photographs in which many a photographer has retouched away the light in the pupils: a kind of solemnity, or one might say a touch of mystery.

I waited for her to smile, but she did not so much as smile; only while she was eating did there faintly show the ripple one so often sees on a child's face. I had taken her at first for twenty-three or twenty-four; after I noticed that ripple, I could truly have taken her for not yet twenty.

After dinner the several girls were all taken upstairs by Mrs. Honsa, while I went to the back room downstairs to change into evening clothes, taking great care to put the poison Bai Ping had given me into my waistcoat pocket. When I had changed and come out, Honsa and the others were dividing up the order of travel. This Honsa seemed to have thought out well beforehand: it was settled that Mr. and Mrs. Honsa should go with Miyama Yoshiko and another short, plump Japanese merchant called Kiya, while I, who had to escort Sophie to change into her gown, should take only Sophie along. The rest would ride in another car and could apparently start first, since those women guests had all already changed. To this arrangement, of course, no one objected. But the first to come down from upstairs was Sophie; afterward, according to what Sophie told me, this was because Mrs. Honsa, knowing she had to go back and change, had told her to come down first and go.

Once she had come down, Honsa told me to escort her home to change first, so that we could reach the ball at the same time as they did.

So I took my leave and came out, and thus I never did learn whether their two cars set off together or one after the other. In any case, when I reached the ball they had all arrived before me.

The Xiangong tea dance had no dance hostesses. I seldom went to the evening dances later on, but before I discovered Bai Ping, Stephen and I too had once gone often; it was then, and because there were Japanese patrons, that Sophie caught Stephen's notice, and I too came to know her then. But after I discovered Bai Ping I personally had no more dealings with her. Only lately, knocking about with Honsa and the rest, had I had a few dealings with her again, and learned that she and Honsa were very close.

After I had decided not to bring the Manfields, mother and daughter, I had asked Honsa to find me, on the spur of the moment, any companion he liked; I had not expected the one he found to be Sophie. I like going to such an affair with someone I know very well, Bai Ping or Helen, say; nor am I afraid of a complete stranger; but someone half-known, half-strange I find very awkward. I can neither be at ease nor keep too distant, can treat her neither as a friend nor as a passer-by. And now it was just in this predicament that I found myself: treat her as a friend, and much of the conduct and talk was impossible; treat her as a strange dance hostess, and yet, going to such a gathering, I could hardly not speak to her, or not put on a show of being cheerful.

In the car, her sitting beside me put me out of countenance. Following her directions, I drove to the mouth of the lane where her lodgings were, and she said:

"No need to drive in."

I stopped the car.

"Will you come in and sit a while?"

"No," I said. "I'll just wait here."

Sophie did not press me much, and got out. She said:

"But don't you fret."

"Will it take long?" I said.

"Twenty minutes."

"I hope you'll be a little quicker." As I said this I really wanted to go up to her flat and wait, but she did not, after all, ask me in; she only smiled and nodded and went quickly off into the lane.

I kept watch over the car, watch over my watch, smoking cigarette after cigarette as I waited for her, waiting minute by minute. At first I still had the ceiling light of the car on, but afterward, seeing that the passers-by all turned their attention on me, I switched it off and began to watch what went on outside, though I could not fix my mind on it at all.

Half of it, naturally, was still that the work was on my mind; I waited with great impatience, somewhat on edge. Had it been a close friend, I could have gone in to hurry her; had it been a strange dance hostess, I could really have left her and driven off; but as it was, she was neither known nor strange, could be called a warm friend of Honsa's, and I neither knew her door number nor could I fail to wait. I bitterly regretted not having gone in with her just now, and several times I thought of leaving her to herself, but always felt that this would not only wrong her but put Honsa too much out of countenance. So I could only wait it out to the death. Yet twenty minutes passed and still she did not come out. I went down into the lane two or three times; the lane was very dark, winding and complicated, and of course I could not find even her shadow, and had to go back to the car to smoke. It was only by my third cigarette, when I thought a full half hour must surely have gone by, that I saw Sophie come out in an evening gown, a coat of sealskin plush over her shoulders.

When we reached the Umetake residence the masked ball had long since begun. We checked our coats and hats and were shown into a drawing room, where a masked woman sat; she had us sign our names, gave out masks, and very courteously asked us to put them on at once and join the ball. We of course complied, put on our masks properly, and went into the ballroom.

At this my heart began to pound. I do not know why, but I now bitterly resented having come late, feeling that if I had come early I could surely have made rather fuller preparation. Beyond my eagerness to make out Bai Ping, Mei Yingzi, and Miko, I felt an indescribable urgency to make out Mr. and Mrs. Honsa and Miyama Yoshiko, who I believed must have arrived before us.

The ballroom lights just then were violet, very dim. Sophie set her handbag down on a nearby seat, and I led her out to dance among the crowd. I was eager to find Bai Ping or Mei Yingzi and tell them I had arrived, but there were so many people, jostling this way and that, that I could not search. Not until the music stopped, and Sophie and many others had taken seats all around, and the great lamp overhead came on, did I think I could surely find them; but I could only look about me, unable even to move around too freely, and I was extraordinarily anxious, at a loss what to do. In an instant the music struck up again, the light overhead went out, and I danced with a girl nearby, but I did not say a word, my mind only fretting over how to find them. I stole glances at every woman's hand to see whether it bore the ring I hoped for. At last, on our left, two couples away, I saw a glittering ring. I pressed through with my partner; the ring seemed very like Bai Ping's, but that girl was really too short, so short that I could be sure she was not Bai Ping, and at once I saw too that the ring was not like Bai Ping's after all.

Before long the music stopped and the lights came on, and still I could not find them. My heart by now was in the extremity of anxious unrest, but there was no help for it; I could only endure, suppress it, hold myself in. When the music struck up again I invited another woman to dance. This time I made an effort to entertain no other thought or consideration, but looked near and gazed far, watching every woman, every woman's hand. At last, at a turn, I saw on a girl's hand not far behind me a ring with a white cross in a red square, and at that I was instantly beside myself with excitement, my heart pounding. I checked my steps to let the people behind me pass, and after a good deal of maneuvering I finally saw that ring appear on my left. I pressed close, putting myself behind to follow them; I hoped the music would soon end so that I could note her seat and, at the next number, ask her to dance. But the music was uncommonly long, and in the crowd it cost me great effort and my whole attention to keep after her. Just then, in the turning steps, I trod on my partner's skirt, and I said:

"I beg your pardon, miss."

"No," the young lady said, "it was my skirt."

This voice and intonation were somewhat like Bai Ping's, and I gave a start!

She wore a silver mask, and her figure was very like, though her hair was plainly different; but this could well be that Bai Ping had had it done again after going home. For an instant I almost cried out; but I at once realized my own folly: how was it that at this moment I was, on the contrary, overlooking her ring? So I felt for her ring; this ringed hand was in mine, but I had no way to look at it closely. I saw that it was a white diamond, and beyond that I could only feel it by touch, and in this too I had no experience, so of course I could not prove anything, and in fact it seemed I must wait until the music stopped to know for certain. So I went on dancing with her, and began to think of the ring with the white cross in a red square that I had just been following; but it was no longer before me. First I watched to left and right, before and behind, then looked all around: nowhere. I could no longer find it, and just in this disappointment the music stopped. I escorted my partner to her seat, and in the bright light I noticed her ring: it was a claw-set mounting, plainly not Bai Ping's, beyond doubt. In the extremity of my disappointment I hurriedly thanked her and moved off. I bitterly regretted the carelessness in the dance just now, which had let Miko, whom I had already found, slip hurriedly away.

The air in the room was very hot, and I was a little damp with sweat, deeply ashamed at heart and deeply anxious. Two more pieces of music went by; I did not dance, but only sat to one side looking closely, and still I did not find her. Not until the third piece stopped and the lights came on, and many people went out to the back veranda, did I, watching every woman who went out, at last follow along too. The back veranda had been arranged somewhat today, with several round tables one could move about among, and servants there supplying drinks. Today the door of the veranda stood open, giving straight onto the garden, and some people went out too to breathe the fresh air. Seeing that they were not there, I went back inside, where a servant too was pushing a trolley and supplying drinks, with many people crowding round to take them. Just as I too was reaching for a glass of wine from the tray, I saw a girl raise her cup and say, first in Japanese and then in Chinese:

"A blessing on you, gentlemen, ladies, misses."

All at once I realized with a start that the hand raising the cup bore Bai Ping's ring.

It was Bai Ping, of course it was Bai Ping, and sure enough she wore a silver mask. Everyone raised their cups, and so I raised mine too and went to her right and clinked with her, and I said:

"First let us thank our lovely maiden for her blessing."

I believed she could tell my voice. Sure enough, when the many men all said, "A blessing on our lovely maiden," Bai Ping said:

"Let those who have clinked cups with me come and dance."

"Let those who have clinked cups with me,

Come and dance!

When we have danced out these candle-flames,

Let us sing to face the sun.

"Let those who have clinked cups with me,

Come and dance!

When we have danced these wine-bottles empty,

Let us go again to bed.

"Let those who have clinked cups with me,

Come and dance!

When we have danced through this stretch of dark night,

There will be splendid clouds at the edge of the sky."

So "Let those who have clinked cups with me, come and dance!" was a song. I saw a girl in a peach-colored mask, in one hand a drained empty cup and with the other holding up the skirt of her gown, come dancing and singing toward me, the music at once falling in with her. She sang it over and over, singing her way to where I stood, and all at once I saw on her hand the ring with the white cross in a red square: this was Miko. When the song ended, the trolley tables had been cleared away. I noted the seats Bai Ping and Miko went back to, and before the dance music began I got in first and asked Bai Ping to dance. She rose lightly, and slender, nestled against me; I led her into the crowd, and she said:

"So it was you, the child who clinked cups with me?"

"Yes, Ping." I said the word "Ping" very softly.

"And Mei...?" she asked in a low voice, as though guarding a secret.

"Not yet..."

"Not far to the right of my seat."

"Thank you, miss," I said.

"And the cross?"

"I've seen it, thank you."

After this Bai Ping said nothing, until the music stopped, when she said: "I bless you."

I saw her back to her seat and began to watch to her right, and sure enough I saw, not far off, a woman of graceful bearing, also in a silver mask, sit down with a collar of bright pearls about her neck: this was beyond doubt Mei Yingzi. I now began to take note of these seats. The seats were not fixed; the women had simply taken deliberate possession of them with their handbags and so made them fixed. So the men merely sat where there was room, and I was lucky enough to take an empty seat beside Mei Yingzi, and next danced with Mei Yingzi.

"Mei," I said in a low voice.

"Yes," she said. After a while she said again:

"The Xujiahui church, the gambling den on Columbia Road, both come?"

I knew she meant Bai Ping and Miko, and I said:

"Yes, both here."

She fell silent, dancing happily with me; and just as I was about to ask her about the key, she said:

"You really are a lovely dancer; at the next number, do remember me still."

I knew what she meant, and so did not ask again; but at the music that followed she was quickly asked away first, and so I invited Miko. As we danced I called to her in a low voice:

"Miko."

She did not answer, so I said:

"I am So-and-so."

Still she made no answer, which put me quite out of countenance. Could I have made a mistake? But I was clearly aware of the ring on her hand, and so I said boldly:

"When is Mei Yingzi's appointment?"

"What?" she asked.

"When are we to..."

"Whenever you like, you may come and ask me to dance," she said.

Her words seemed throughout as though she had no part in this business; I was much puzzled, and was silent a long while, when suddenly I thought of Mei Yingzi's instruction to trace a cross on my palm. How could I have forgotten so grave a thing? Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping had known me the moment they heard my voice; Miko naturally would not know me. Much ashamed, I then traced a cross with the forefinger of my left hand on the palm of her right, and at once she traced a cross back to me. So I said:

"I need you to lead me..."

"Dance more with me," she said in a low, excited voice. "Naturally I'll lead you."

After this we did not speak.

When I came to dance with Mei Yingzi I traced a cross on her palm too, and said:

"Can it be handed over to me now?"

At this I felt a key being passed into my palm; I tilted my hand, grasped the key, put it in my trouser pocket, and, in the same motion, took my handkerchief from the pocket to wipe the sweat from my brow. Suddenly I heard her whisper at my ear:

"Inside it is GH5xxK8." I did not catch it clearly, and traced a question mark on her palm, and she whispered again:

"GH509K8; it is inside the key." It flashed on me that this was the combination inside the safe. I wanted to fix it once more, and said:

"GH50...?"

"GH509K8."

"Thank you," I said.

"Tell it back to me," she said.

"GH509K8."

"Don't forget it," she said, lowering her voice again. "Both packets of documents inside are the ones."

I traced another cross on her palm, all the while fixing the number in my mind.

After this I danced perhaps twice more with Bai Ping and three times with Mei Yingzi, and each time she traced a question mark on my palm and had me repeat "GH509K8" back to her.—Apart from that, I danced almost every dance with Miko.

I do not know how much time passed; there were two intervals in it, when people all went out to the corridor and the back garden, one time it was Miko, another time someone else, who sang. But Miko still gave me no signal, and my heart was already very anxious. I bore it patiently, until once, when Miko and I were dancing a waltz, she whispered at my ear:

"Dance the next one with me, and take me outside."

When the piece after next ended, sure enough it was an interval; many people took their partners to the back tables, and there were some five couples who went from the back veranda into the garden, and I too took Miko and followed out.

There was a slight chill in the garden; there was no moonlight at all that night, only dim little red and green lamps studding the clumps of trees. Miko led me strolling to a secluded spot, coming and going two or three times, but not to the back of the house behind; all the while she talked with me of the amusing ball, of films, and of other diversions. At last the lamps in the garden and the corridor all dimmed, and music struck up inside. People went in one after another; Miko stood before a tree far off, murmuring to me on purpose, until everyone had gone, when she drew me to the foot of the wall of the house on the right and around to the back.

There it was perhaps six or seven paces wide, on one side the little foreign-style house, on the other the boundary wall; along the ground by the wall were planted withered flowers and grasses, and there, set down, was a short ladder. Miko pointed at the short ladder, told me I was to go up to the second window round the corner, and ran off.

Now I was plunged at once into the most solitary of situations: the bleak little garden, myself alone in the pitch dark, and faintly the lively music from inside reaching me. I do not know why, but for an instant I felt no fear or worry at all, only a desolate loneliness. From all around I looked to the building before me, looked to the sky, looked to this passage six or seven paces wide, looked to the boundary wall, looked to the earth at the foot of the wall, and so looked to the short ladder Miko had pointed out to me. At once this short ladder, as though it had a magic power, keyed me up. The ladder was painted dark green, very small and neat; I took the white gloves from my pocket, put them on, picked up the ladder and leaned it against the top of the wall, and easily climbed up. At the second-story window it was a little short, but I reckoned climbing in would still not be too hard. I pushed the window first with my hand; it was not bolted, which I supposed Mei Yingzi had arranged, and inside a curtain seemed to be drawn. I pushed the window harder, and then boldly climbed in.

Pitch dark. I took out my lighter, and only then was all around lit up. I saw the plain furnishing of the room: a typing table, behind it a filing cabinet, beside it a writing desk, and behind that the safe. In the room was a round table spread with a brown velvet cloth, ringed with single-backed leather chairs, and a leather sofa-suite set to one side; the place where I had jumped in was just behind this suite. On the wall hung a map, which I did not look at closely. My mood then was very tense, but I strove to keep calm, drawing my breath out very even and deep. I put out the lighter, stood still for two minutes, and then gently drew back the curtain. My sight had already grown used to this darkness, and I could dimly make out the furnishings I had just seen; so I went up to the safe. But just in the instant when I held the lighter to the keyhole of the safe, meaning to take out the key, I suddenly heard a sound outside the door. Startled, I put out the lighter at once and stood still. I realized that the door of that room was behind me; out of the gloom I saw the gleaming spring lock, but whether the door was locked I had, just now, failed to notice. My heart went a little cold, and for a moment I was at a loss what to do. In those few seconds I certainly heard someone pushing at the door, and in my alarm a strange inspiration came to me that made me, without a thought, hide under the round table in the room. I hid myself well in, so that the tablecloth screened my body, and I listened quietly for what went on outside the door. But for a moment there was no sound at all outside; I wondered whether I was being over-nervous, or whether people had merely chanced to pass outside. Within half a minute I thought seven or eight times of screwing up my courage to come out from under the table. But suddenly I heard someone indeed working at the lock on the door, and my heart began to leap. I drew my body in, watching lest the corner of my clothes show, and through the fringe of the tablecloth I fixed my eyes on the gleaming lock and doorknob. I saw the lock turn, I saw the knob turn; I strove to keep myself calm, but my breast still pounded, and I was aware of the clammy sweat in the palms of my white gloves. Then the door did indeed open softly, and I watched, and watched—.

But the one who slipped in through the crack of the door was a woman in a white evening gown, and my heart seemed to drop level from where it had hung; I passed from doubt to certainty, and then to anger. ...Mei Yingzi? Bai Ping? Whichever it was, it was after all an insult to me; they thought me as useless as this! As she turned her back to close the door softly, and the spring lock slid from her hand into its socket, I thought for a moment of springing out to reproach her; but I saw at once that this would be a mad act. I watched her; through the gap at the corner of the tablecloth I could see her whole figure.

She turned round, and from her bosom took out something bright, two inches long: it was a flashlight, its beam very fine and sharp. By the reflection off her white dress I saw that she also held a white parcel in her hand, and she too wore a silver mask. There were three colors of mask that night; since Bai Ping and Mei Yingzi both wore silver, this mask made me think at once of them. Perhaps, fearing I had not brought a flashlight, they had come out in person to help me; and for an instant my anger of just now seemed to subside, and I felt an indescribable gratitude. But if it were Bai Ping, she would have had to find me first, or give me a signal first. Strangely, I was muddled for a full half minute; but luckily I did not go on being muddled, for I at once thought of their distinguishing marks. This woman who had come in had no collar about her neck and no ring on her hand: plainly she was neither of the two; she was someone else, someone I did not know, come for I knew not what. At that I was at once alarmed again!

She swept the fine sharp beam of the flashlight around, and at last it fell on the safe. She came slowly over, and then, like a waning moon, her figure was slowly swallowed by the tablecloth, until I could see only her white skirt drive past before my table; then her figure came gradually back into my view, but the safe was not so far off as the door, and when she reached the front of the safe I still could not see her upper body. I had to move to the edge of the table to see a little more. This was somewhat of a risk, but had to be done; luckily my dancing shoes were very smooth and the floor was smooth too, so I could move to the edge quite easily without making any sound, and then I could see the movements of her hands. She opened the door of the safe with the key, and seemed then to be turning the secret combination inside; at last I saw her take out two envelopes, which of course were the secret documents we needed. She laid the documents on the writing desk, then opened the white parcel she had brought and put into it, into the safe, a black object from the parcel. With her back to me, I did not know how she was arranging it; in any case it took a good while. In that while, had I the skill of a pickpocket, I could easily have stolen those two secret documents from the writing desk. I saw it very clearly, gazing at it without ceasing, and several times thought of making that risky snatch; but still I did not dare; my state of mind was perhaps like a mouse's wanting to steal the food from behind someone, seeing it clear as clear, and close at hand, but not daring in the end to lay a hand on it.

At last, as though she had it arranged, I saw her seem to shut the inner door of the safe, and I had a strange clear intuition that what she had set inside was a bomb. She shut the outer door of the safe too, and at this I could not but move myself forward a step, and I found I had indeed made some sound; I held myself in, and thought at once that the safe door was making a sound at the same moment, so she would have no leisure to distinguish it.

She shut the safe door and took up the secret documents from the writing desk; and in that very instant a strange cleverness came over me, making me think that I had both the need and the chance to find out who she was, and my heart began to pound again.

By now she had put away the flashlight. She wrapped the documents in a white cloth, which I thought must be the same white cloth in which she had just brought the bomb in. Then she came over with a light step, and I saw her skirt slowly drive near to the table where I lurked. I took out the fountain pen I carried, an old-style Parker, and unscrewed the cap and the end, tucking the two covers into my pocket; and just as she drove past before me, I summoned all my courage, put out my hand, and squirted the ink from the barrel of my pen onto the skirt trailing on the floor behind her. Then I at once drew my hand back and watched her figure slowly rise fully, until I could see her whole body; she turned open the spring lock, then the knob, pulled the door open, and her light and graceful figure went out in the shelter of the door. A faint light came in through the crack, but she at once drew the door to, very softly, and only the lock going up into its socket did I hear very clearly.
