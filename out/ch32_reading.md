## Chapter 32

"…Now I can think of nothing more by which to explain you to myself. On the one hand what you show me is loftiness, purity and fidelity; on the other, you yourself would have me leap clear of this life and truly live—the only difference being that I am a woman and you a man.

"Of your own life I naturally know a little, but I had never marked it, never turned it over in my mind; today, though, Bai Ping reminded me of it and set me reflecting, and I find I have no way to understand it.

"Standing as a third party, Bai Ping sees it rather clearly. She feels that the two of us are, in exactly the same way, a pity; that we have met with exactly the same fate; that we each have work we ought to be striving at; that we each ought to give up this social life—this tedious social life—and go back to the life that was originally ours. I think she is right.

"But you see only me. You bid me live faithfully and bravely—yet have you ever put the question to yourself?

"You say that your economic life will not let you study philosophy. I think that is an evasion, not the answer of a man who truly cares for philosophy. Are you then saying that you are true gold, and I am not?

"Even granting that, I have already faithfully and bravely pulled myself free and come back to the fields of conscience—so why must you have me go into the fire again?

"Now I mean to remind you of the very thing you reminded me of, in the hope that you have the power of self-examination to look back over your own life.

"I am not breaking faith; I would say, rather, that I am keeping it. By the time this letter reaches you I shall already have set off on a journey to Nanjing—telling you, in a way that cannot be undone, that I will not attend tomorrow night's gathering.

"Will it put you in a difficult position? Perhaps. But it is only a difficulty in that life of yours—a life that is precisely false and cowardly.

"To wreck your false and cowardly life can hardly do any harm to your faithfulness and courage.

"We must live faithfully and bravely!

"Helen Manfield."

My address was a secret, and I had to go home often to see whether any letters had come for me. On the morning of December the twenty-third my errand home was in fact to fetch a set of evening clothes, meaning to take Helen that night to Umetake's soirée. But Helen had got a letter like this one there ahead of me, waiting. I read it over and over on the sofa, passing from anxious alarm into numbness; luckily no one in the house had risen, and I was alone in the downstairs room, so that no one questioned or wondered at the changes in my mood. I sat there numb for a full half hour, and in that time my thoughts and feelings seemed all to have come to a stop. When I began to recover a little power of reflection, my first wonder was what on earth Bai Ping could have said to Helen. Could Bai Ping already know of Mei Yingzi's work tonight, and have come to wreck it—or was this no more than chance coincidence? For an instant I really wanted to go and see Bai Ping at once and put the matter squarely to the test; but on second thought I felt that this would do nothing to mend matters, and that the most urgent thing now was to repair the situation of being without Helen. Why Mei Yingzi had to have Helen I did not know; but that it was bound up with Mei Yingzi's work, bound up with Mei Yingzi's fate, bound up with Mei Yingzi's very life, there was no doubt at all. Remembering the gravity of Mei Yingzi's manner the night before, I could not help shuddering. My task this time was to bring Helen to the soirée; now that Helen had gone, it was of course a failure of my task. That my failure should imperil Mei Yingzi's life—how dreadful and shameful a thing that was. To find a remedy, the sooner I let Mei Yingzi know the better: that was beyond question. Thinking so, I roused myself at once, took the evening clothes, and sprang into the car.

I drove straight to the Benner Inn, where Mei Yingzi was at breakfast in the dining room. I sat down across from her, drank a cup of coffee, and, seeing that there was no one about, without a word handed her the letter. Smoking, I composed a firm and courageous bearing, waiting for her to read it, waiting for her anger; I had resolved to bear, with the most patient and most loyal face and voice, everything she might visit upon me.

She pressed the letter flat, knitting her brows; her face slowly tightened and slowly slackened again, and then a sharp cold smile came over it as she stared at the words. She read it through, I think, at least three times. At last she handed the letter lightly back to me and, without a word, went on eating her breakfast as before; then she took a sip of coffee and asked mildly:

"Did you get this yesterday?"

"This morning."

Again she made no sound, and of course I too could only keep silent. After a good while she said:

"Have you a cigarette?"

I gave her a cigarette and lit it for her; she blew a mouthful of smoke onto the carnation on the table and said:

"Have you thought of any way to repair it?"

"I think this is Bai Ping's doing—"

"That is only natural," she said. "What one should think of is the way to repair it."

"Yes," I said. "That is why I came at once to talk it over with you—I feel I have wronged you. That even so small a task—"

"This is not your fault." She cut me off. "The question—" She broke off suddenly, stood up, and said: "Come and sit a while in my room."

I rose with her, followed her out of the dining room and up the stairs. She let out a long, deep, low sigh, pushed the door open wearily, let me go in first, and then, wearily again, closed it. She paced uneasily, and said to herself with a cold smile:

"Bai Ping, Bai Ping—"

I sat there without moving, but her voice kindled in my heart a boundless hatred and unease; it was a voice dark and cutting, desolate, shrill, with something hysterical in it. I had supposed that once we were in this room she would surely vent upon me the anger, unease and dread she had just held down; but her voice now proved that her anger, unease and dread were all grinding at her own heart within. To me this was truly more painful than if she had vented on me. It was like my mother weeping over my faults when I was a child—which I felt to be more painful than being punished. I said:

"All of this is my fault; so, Mei Yingzi, could I not take on tonight's difficulty myself?"

She made no answer, standing at the window. I said again:

"Trust me—tell me in detail the work to be done, and let me change posts with you tonight."

Still she neither moved nor spoke. I went over, and standing behind her I laid both my hands reverently and lightly on her two shoulders, imploring her:

"Mei Yingzi, trust me—I am willing to do everything you bid me, willing to shoulder every danger, I—"

"This is not the time for you to show a man's virtues." She turned swiftly and said with solemnity: "This is work—secret, exact, planned work—not like throwing a bomb, that can rely on a moment's courage of yours!" So saying, she walked away again.

"But whatever the case, I will not sow an ill-omened seed and leave another to eat the fruit in my place." I said, gazing at her retreating figure.

"You are an individualist through and through." So saying she turned back, leaned against the edge of the table, and with one hand pressed upon it said: "You must realize that our work is an organization, an organism, a single life. In our life, many a time, because the eyes have failed, the hands have had to shoulder the peril—would you then have the eyes shoulder the work of the hands?"

"But we are, after all, two lives," I said. "I have my private feelings; if you—if something were to happen to you on my account, what should I do, how could you bid me go on living?"

"Your words represent only the ethical order of the Middle Ages, and this is the political life of the twentieth century," she said. "I have no time to talk of these things with you further." She looked at her watch and added: "I must be going out."

"Then—?"

"Nothing. Everything as before." So saying she made to go into the bedroom, but stopped again and said: "So you will go alone tonight?"

"Perhaps." As I spoke she went in.

I sat on the sofa waiting for Mei Yingzi to come out, feeling by intuition that she seemed to have some superhuman power to command tonight's plan. Since I could do nothing to mend or to help, I could only pray for her—pray that she would win, pray that she would be safe, pray that she would be forever bright.

Mei Yingzi came out dressed in the most vivid and beautiful way, and I met a wave of heavy fragrance—which seemed an ill omen, making me think how many flowers, just before they wither and fade, give off a special fragrance. This was a superstition, and I at once suppressed the strange intuition; I sought out a bright thought and said to myself:

"Of course—fragrance stands for eternal life."

She naturally did not know of the many strange changes in my mind, and stood there easy and cheerful; and this easy, cheerful air was not for me, but a rehearsal beforehand of the manner she would need tonight. She had, I believed, just left the mirror, before which she had rehearsed how to betray, in her performance tonight, none of the dread and fear at the bottom of her heart—and so it was with this proud and noble expression that she spoke to me.

"If it is possible, do your best tonight to keep watch on Bai Ping." She smiled and added: "Use your feelings, not your will; and if it grows forced and others are likely to see it, then give up the watch."

"What does that mean?"

"It means this," she said. "Keeping watch on Bai Ping is a help to me; but for others to see that you are watching her would do my work still more harm. That is the principle; for the rest, act as the moment demands, as you yourself see fit."

Having said this she seemed to wish to raise the matter no more, and came downstairs with me as though she were my companion off to some amusement, and out the door. She said:

"You get into your car first; we shall meet tonight."

I got into the car. Driving along the level road, with many things on my mind, I did not know where I ought to go, nor what I ought to settle first; I needed to go home, needed quietly to think things over, before I could decide what I might do and ought to do. So I drove toward my lodgings; but just at the turning of the corner a bright red car flashed past me. It was Mei Yingzi, with a woman beside her—who, I could not tell. I thought of speeding up to overtake them and see whether I knew her, but her car was too fast, and my heart too heavy, and I did not do it.

At Weihaiwei Road I drove the car into the garage—a garage I had rented only a short while before, some twenty-odd paces from the door of my lodgings. But within those twenty-odd paces I saw, in the distance, standing at the mouth of a lane, a woman who looked like Bai Ping. Just as I was about to fix my eyes on her, she had already turned and gone in. This lane was diagonally opposite my lodgings, and I should have to walk several steps more to see into its mouth; but my mood just then was very bad, and it seemed to me too that she could hardly be here so early—I supposed I had mistaken someone for her—and, since it was awkward with the evening clothes in my arms, I went straight back into my own lodgings.

I sat down calmly in my room, fully expecting that I could gather my mind to consider what I might and ought to do; but my head was heavy, my mind in disorder, and none of the things I might and ought to do could I find.

At a loss, I ran a full bath and bathed, and then on the bed I loosened every muscle to rest, and so sank into a deep sleep. I woke at half past one, and it struck me all at once: how ought I to go to the soirée tonight? To go alone seemed, somehow, not the thing. So I got up at once—but I did not change into evening clothes. For it occurred to me that I ought to go and see Honsa Jiro. Honsa was one of the great merchants who held shares with me, the general manager of our company; I had not seen him for a good while, and he mixed closely with the Japanese military, so tonight he would of course be there. If it was convenient, it would be very good to go with him. But not changing into evening dress, I should have to make another trip back, which was awkward; so after some thought I took the evening clothes with me to the car, meaning to change into them wherever I might be, whenever I liked.

I went out and dined alone at the Kaidi Restaurant; after dinner I went to our company on Sichuan Road, but Honsa had already gone home. There was still plenty of time, and having nothing to do in any case, I drove to his house. Amid all the shifts of thought and feeling, one unchanging axis, hidden behind my mood, was the veering of Helen. Whether or not it was a kind of subconscious flight from pain, I had, since leaving Mei Yingzi's, never once thought of Helen; but now, because my car passed before her house, all at once I thought of her words, and a sudden brightness stung me—her going to Nanjing might be false; if she were now at home, then, then—

Thinking and thinking, I stopped before her apartment building, and went up with a leaping heart to knock at her door. It was Mrs. Manfield who opened it, and she welcomed me, saying:

"I never thought you would come today."

"Is Helen at home?"

"I don't know how it came about—she suddenly wanted to go off to Nanjing alone."

"Has she already set out?"

"The day before yesterday."

This was simply a bucket of cold water poured over my hopes. I wanted to leave at once, but Mrs. Manfield kept me, saying:

"Today is a holiday, and I have been lonely here by myself; nothing could be better than your coming—and I have something to discuss with you besides."

So I went in. The first thing to catch my eye was Rear Admiral Umetake's invitation on the table, made out to Mrs. Manfield and Miss Manfield. This astonished me: had not Helen said that an invitation had been sent back by her? And now another had come—or was it still the same one? I held the invitation, lost in thought; but Mrs. Manfield spoke:

"Helen went to Nanjing, I daresay, just to avoid this soirée."

"How so?"

"Last time an invitation came inviting her alone, and she lied that she had gone to Beiping, and sent it back." Mrs. Manfield sat down and said. "But others knew she had not left Shanghai, and thought she was merely unwilling to go by herself, so they sent this invitation."

"Has she seen this invitation?"

"No."

"Then do you mean to go tonight?"

"By myself I would rather not go."

Like a flash of inspiration it woke me, and my dejected manner changed at a stroke; I said excitedly:

"Go, go—I will go with you."

"You will go too?"

"I will go; I think today will surely be very lively."

"Won't you bring anyone else?"

"I had meant all along to go with you and Helen; now that Helen is away, it will just be the two of us, then."

"How good you are, always thinking of us." Mrs. Manfield smiled kindly, a strange light glinting in her eyes, her round plump face all pleasure.

I too seemed to gain some unspeakable comfort, from what quarter I could not tell; but at the least it lifted a little of the heavy burden on my soul and added to my courage. I was firmly convinced that Mrs. Manfield could be of help to Mei Yingzi's work; and if not a help, she could at the very least do no harm.

In all hopeless things there are, within their hopelessness, hopeless changes; and in Mrs. Manfield's shining eyes I saw Mei Yingzi's good fortune for tonight.
