## Chapter 32. Rich Wine, a Fair Woman, the Mandarin-Duck Swords

A salve to stanch blood and reduce swelling was spread over Muci’s wound; a low groan escaped his lips. The doctor did not slow his movements for the patient’s signals of pain: Xia Yuechun deftly wound a roll of gauze layer by layer around Muci’s injured leg, and with much patience and care gave him a simple dressing.

“Doctor Xia, how bad is my injury?” Muci asked Xia Yuechun politely.

“An old wound reopened; the good fortune is that the bone hasn’t cracked. Rest easy; a few days of rest and recuperation and your body will recover.” Xia Yuechun went back to his doctor’s seat and sat down, ready to write out a prescription for Muci.

Muci sat up and put on his shoes.

“Doctor Xia, why don’t you ask me how an old wound could suddenly reopen?” Muci raised the subject with an ulterior motive.

Xia Yuechun lifted his eyes to Muci and said, “If you’ve something to say, say it straight.”

“Last night I went adventuring in the suburbs with that old friend of yours.”

“Find any buried treasure?” Xia Yuechun answered in a joking tone.

“Found a skeleton,” said Muci.

Xia Yuechun started. “Nothing went wrong, I hope?”

“You mean with me? Or with your old friend?” Muci put on his coat.

“So you two still keep a line between yourselves?”

“Frankly, we…” Muci adjusted his tie. “We’ve only just become friends.”

“That’s wonderful,” Yuechun said with a smile. “Congratulations. And last night, the two of you?”

“We went to the Ciyun Temple, met ghosts and goblins in the night, blundered into a trap; luckily, Heaven too was willing to help us last night, and it ended in a fright and no harm.”

“You went to such great pains to come here to me, just to tell me on purpose the tale of your adventure last night. Was there any need for that? To be honest, I hardly know you, Adjutant Yang.”

“Because I feel that Mr. Yang Muchu is altogether too self-willed and headstrong; but he seems to set great store by your opinion.” Muci simply sat down and said, “The story of last night was too uncanny. First the two of us were trapped, and then someone blew up our way out; blundering along by chance, we suddenly found a woman’s skeleton of twenty-odd years ago; and hard upon that, the rainwater guided us to a new exit. We wanted to know a certain person’s true identity, and that very person delivered herself to our door, all but announcing her own name. Wouldn’t you call it strange? And besides, I’ve felt all along that it went too smoothly, as though from first to last someone were guiding us. Unbelievable.”

“What is it you want to tell me?” Xia Yuechun asked.

“What I want to tell that old friend of yours, the esteemed Mr. Yang, is that he has overlooked a fact, disregarded a latent threat. There must be a time bomb hidden at his side. No knowing when it will go ‘pop’…”

The smile on Xia Yuechun’s face froze; a stern light came into his eyes. Muci felt ill at ease, and rose without meaning to; under Xia Yuechun’s overbearing gaze he stood bolt upright. After some seconds of staring, Yuechun’s eyes suddenly cleared, and the stern brow and severe look melted, incredibly, into a faint smile. He said in a gentle tone, “Good. I understand. Off you go.”

“Yes, sir.” Muci, given his pardon, made to leave at once.

“Wait.”

Muci turned and stood at attention.

“You’re welcome for a follow-up visit next time.” Xia Yuechun rose and handed the prescription to Muci as he did so. “Don’t be so stiff; it doesn’t suit our respective stations. Be natural.” He patted Muci on the shoulder.

“Thank you, Doctor.”

After seeing Yang Muci off, Xia Yuechun sank into a spell of thought; he went back to the director’s office and put through a call to A-Chu.

“Who is it?” A-Chu asked.

“Yuechun.”

“Something the matter?”

“I was tidying my bookcase today and found a book missing; I wonder if it’s over at your place?”

“Which one?” A-Chu had probably stood up. “It’s possible; last time I was at your house I borrowed a few. I’ll have Yashu look.”

“It’s the book by Plato.”

“Plato? Did I take that?” A-Chu sat down again.

“Think about it, that one, the one with the passage about Thales observing the stars.”

“Which passage?”

“The one where he falls down a well.”

A-Chu suddenly stopped speaking.

Because he had caught the meaning under Yuechun’s words.

And at the same time he recalled that little story of the philosopher’s.

Thales, observing the stars by night, in one careless moment fell into a well, and was mocked by his own Thracian serving-girl, who said: Master, you are so eager to know the secrets of the heavens that you overlook everything at your side.

A faint smile spilled from the corner of A-Chu’s mouth.

“Yuechun, A-Ci has been to see you, hasn’t he?” A-Chu’s voice was very soft.

“It’s quite normal for a patient to come for a follow-up.”

A-Chu gave an “Mm.” “Good. I understand.”

Xia Yuechun hung up the phone.

“Lotus-seed sticky-rice congee.” Yashu, in a pink cotton gown, pushed open the door and came in carrying a large lacquer tray. The rich, savory fragrance of lotus-seed sticky-rice congee and steamed longan flesh drifted into A-Chu’s sight; his sense of smell too began at that same moment its dreamlike stage of enjoyment.

A-Chu came forward, wound an arm affectionately about the fair woman’s waist, and sat down close against her on the sofa, body to body.

“It smells wonderful,” A-Chu said.

“Have a taste.” Yashu fed him herself, silver spoon in tender hand, enough to melt a man’s soul and dazzle his senses. A-Chu’s attitude to Yashu had greatly changed; he no longer consciously resisted the lure of her beauty, but gave rein to his own desires and enjoyed the fine food to the full.

A-Chu took a mouthful; it was indeed fragrant and smooth. “Wah, this is real skill.”

“Naturally,” Yashu preened. “My ingredients are first-rate. Lotus seeds with the hearts taken out, sticky rice, lotus leaf; very fragrant.”

“The soup yesterday was well made too.”

“Yesterday, yesterday was pig’s-heart, astragalus, and ginseng soup. I specially added dangshen, danshen, northern astragalus…”

“My wife’s very capable,” A-Chu praised.

“And whose fault is it that you like to eat it?”

“Then who was it who told you I like to eat these things?”

“I won’t tell you,” Yashu laughed.

“Here’s a present for you.” A-Chu, as if conjuring a trick, drew from Yashu’s waist a string of dazzling pearls.

“How lovely,” Yashu praised in her turn.

“Let me put it on for you.” Tenderly and thoughtfully A-Chu fastened the necklace on Yashu; full of tender feeling she leaned her head against his breast, gentle and lovely.

“What have you been reading lately?”

“Jane Eyre.”

“Oh, you never tire of that one.” A-Chu gave Yashu’s powdered nose a light pinch; Yashu smiled a happy little smile, like a contented child nestling and playing coy in a grown-up’s arms.

“What else are you reading?”

“Lots. Pride and Prejudice, Gone with the Wind, Salome…”

“Wah.” A-Chu gave a strange cry: “Extraordinary, remarkable; you even read Salome? Such a bloody love; aren’t you afraid?”

“I’m not afraid.”

“Why?”

“Because the mystery of love is far greater than the mystery of death.”

“That line must have been stolen from someone’s mouth. Just like these cooking skills; they must have been stolen too.”

Yashu pressed her lips together and smiled.

“It’s a good thing to like reading,” A-Chu said. “Tomorrow go to the bookshop and buy me a few new philosophy books.”

“All right.”

“Let me write out a book list for you…” A-Chu fished out a fountain pen; Yashu threw herself over and said mischievously, “Just write it straight onto my heart.”

“Stop fooling; careful, you’ll dirty your gown.”

“If it’s dirtied, I’ll buy another.”

“Do you think I run a bank?”

Yashu pillowed her head on A-Chu’s leg and said, “Write it on my forehead, then I’ll never forget.”

“Never forget? Last time I told you to go and buy me a copy of On the Distinction of the Three Realms of Civilized and Barbarous, and the moment you reached the bookshop you brought back a great bundle of ‘mandarin-duck-and-butterfly dream’ books, and turned my bookcase into a rubbish-heap of literary ‘trash.’”

“Don’t you like mandarin ducks and butterflies?” Yashu sat up. “If life had no mandarin-duck-and-butterfly dreams, what meaning would life have left?”

“There’s some sense in that,” A-Chu laughed at himself.

“It makes sense to begin with.”

“It does!” A-Chu, as though in ambush, swept Yashu up in his arms.

“Aiya, what are you doing?”

“Dreaming a mandarin-duck-and-butterfly dream.”

Yashu wound her arms tight about A-Chu’s neck and said, “Aren’t you afraid the female butterfly will bite the male butterfly to death?”

“I am. I’m afraid you won’t bite…” Unable to help himself, Chu went to bite Yashu’s ear; Yashu laughed a coquettish laugh and trembled like a spray of swaying blossom. Just as A-Chu was about to kiss Yashu, there rose suddenly in his ear the words that lovely Thracian serving-girl had spoken: Master, you are so eager to know the secrets of the heavens that you overlook everything at your side. And into A-Chu’s mind flashed several blurred, indistinct figures: Amah A-Yue, Rong Chu, Liu A-Si, Lu Liangchen…

Who on earth could it be?

Smelling the fragrance of the lotus-seed sticky-rice congee, thinking of the sweet lily soup, the pig’s-heart astragalus-and-ginseng soup… someone wanted him to sleep on in this land of tenderness, to sleep on into old age? But that person had forgotten he was a doctor, an excellent doctor.

This was a grave blunder on the adversary’s part.

At this moment the tail of his eye swept over Yashu’s beautiful lashes; he gave a supple smile and went on with his tenderness. He felt Yashu’s love as a strength, a warmth, to his lonely self.

A-Chu had such need of someone to love him, such need of a gentle harbor.

The wall-clock in the Yang family’s luxurious drawing room now pointed to three in the afternoon.

Yang Yuhua had just finished signing a contract, the contract that sold off the Yang family’s shares, bank, and Western-style house. The buyer was a graceful young overseas Chinese, brought in person by the young master of the Tang family. According to Young Tang, this man had lived all along in England, and not long ago had returned home from a tour of Europe with his maternal uncle, meaning to develop finance in China. The newcomer was modest, well-mannered, and cultivated, plainly of a distinguished house. And of course there was one more important thing that pushed Yang Yuhua to his final resolve: a copy of the Shanghai News, which carried a photograph of the well-born young lady Yang Sitong and the returned overseas-Chinese Young Master Rong in passionate love. This photograph told Yang Yuhua, in another form, that the property he was selling would very likely one day hold a share for his daughter; he was full of confidence in his daughter’s charms.

Ming Tang had very warmly acted as go-between; Yang Yuhua knew he must have profited a good deal from it, but he could not attend to all that now; so long as he got the ready cash, he meant to vanish thereafter into the vast sea of Shanghai.

This was what he had longed for in his dreams.

For a long time Koyama Eiko’s surveillance, oppression, and menace had pressed on his chest like a great mountain, so that he felt doubly anxious and stifled. Now no one could bind him any longer; no, it should be said no one cared about him any longer. Koyama Eiko had no leisure even for herself; he had lost all usefulness to the Japanese, and their casting him off financially was the very proof of it.

Run, he told himself.

Nothing was dearer than heaped-up banknotes.

He put on a disguise, got himself up as a plain, ordinary scholar; he donned gold-rimmed spectacles, carried an unremarkable old briefcase, changed into a pair of leather shoes that were not costly but clean on the surface, tucked away an exquisite pocket-watch, and made ready to set off on a long journey.

“You mean to run?!” A gloomy voice poured into Yang Yuhua’s eardrums. “I won’t let you run.” Yang Yuhua turned around and saw the pitch-black muzzle of a gun.

“Eiko…”

“Don’t call me that.”

“Let me go, Eiko; we’re not travellers on the same road.” Yang Yuhua’s voice was mournful and full of appeal.

“We’re not travellers on the same road, but we are people on the same boat.”

“The boat has already capsized, hasn’t it, Eiko? You and I have both been cast off, sold out.” Yang Yuhua stepped toward the muzzle. “More than twenty years ago I begged you to let me go, and you would not. Fine; I obeyed your arrangements, and what came of it? In the end we have nothing at all.”

“I have a mission.”

“The mission has already been called off. Don’t you feel you’re like an expired draft, not worth a copper?” Yang Yuhua pressed his body against the muzzle.

Eiko wept.

“Let us go away.”

“I can’t; I am a soldier of the Empire.” Koyama Eiko cried out with all her might.

“Then shoot me dead with one shot,” said Yang Yuhua. “Don’t, as you did twenty years ago, put me to the torture again… It was you who seduced me, who led me step by step into the mire. You have to help me. Twenty years, and do you still remember our chance meeting at Mount Fuji? We lingered under the setting sun and looked at the cherry blossoms together, whole drifts and clusters of lovely cherry blossoms, dyed deep red by the sinking sun, like grape wine…”

Koyama Eiko’s line of defense collapsed utterly.

The first year of Xuantong, early May of 1909.

In Japan, at the foot of Mount Fuji, in a quaint little tavern, the young Chinese student Yang Yuhua drank himself into a blurred stupor; a lovely, moving geisha sang an old Japanese folk song, twisting a waist like a flower, like a colored ancient painting flowing across a screen, the lines exquisite, teasing Yang Yuhua’s heart into wandering and rousing his high spirits. He lifted the wine-jug and danced idly along to the simple music.

Until he joined that nameless geisha in a bout of hand-to-hand combat; after several rounds of wild revelry, Yang Yuhua fell asleep at the feet of a tavern geisha.

He slept soundly; he did not know that a photograph had slipped from his breast, a photograph of his elder brother and sister-in-law together. A few days later, with the slanting sun going down in the west, beneath the cherry trees, he and Koyama Eiko met by chance.

“Sir, do you like cherry blossoms?”

“Not very much.” Under the setting sun the cherry blossoms drifted down like blood.

“Why?”

“Life is too short.”

“It is just because life is short that it seems beautiful.”

“Is that so?” Yang Yuhua gave a slight smile. “But I’m very much afraid of death.”

“Fear of death is human nature.” Eiko spoke with a smiling face; her words and her looks made Yang Yuhua very uncomfortable, and so, after a brief exchange, they parted.

But fate would not let them part, for a conspiracy was waiting to draw Yang Yuhua into the jar.

“You are to tell him, say to him sincerely: Yuhua, I fell in love with you at first sight.” Koyama Chino repeated the order to the younger sister almost mechanically.

“My heart has already been given to Sakai Ichiro.” Koyama Eiko was declaring her love.

“Have you not already sworn your oath, pledged your loyalty to the Emperor? Do you know that our Army Land Survey Department, the General Staff, has lost five more fine intelligence officers? Not long after they slipped into China they were discovered by the local garrison and secretly executed. They were all the elite of our Japanese military; that our great Empire of Japan might rule Asia, they braved death and dared danger, gave their blood in the line of duty. Eiko, we need you; the Japanese military needs you.”

“But I love Sakai passionately.”

“Do you truly think love more important than the glory of the Empire? Under the great imperative of loyalty to the Emperor, love is so trifling, so utterly beneath notice. Eiko, you should make of yourself a machine in the Empire’s service, an intelligence engine that never rusts. You are a prodigy I cultivated with my own hand, and that Yang Yuhua is a goldmine I unearthed. If you cast off a soldier’s honor, you will regret it your whole life.”

Eiko’s face was deathly pale; her hand trembled as it stroked the gorgeous sash on her kimono.

“To loosen the robe and undo the sash should be an easy thing for a beautiful woman spy…” said Koyama Chino. “Make an end of this love of yours, and serve the Empire with boundless devotion! You will win the trust of the Chinese; we will help you, will get you successfully into his family, his social circle. You will be utterly remade; you will… change a face…”

Koyama Eiko seemed to see another woman’s face in the mirror; in terror she covered her own eyes.

“Why?”

“Because Yang Yuhua is not our final object. Yang Yuhua has an elder brother, a man of great power and great wealth in Shanghai; the two of them are strikingly alike in looks, strikingly, strikingly alike. He can perfectly well replace his brother by peaceful means, and you will become his woman, holding him fast forever in your grip. He will earn our Empire countless riches; you will map out for the Empire the route of the assault on Shanghai. You two will become a screw-bolt of the Japanese military, nailed fast in the very heart of the enemy, blazing out a scarlet radiance. You are the pride of the Empire! The soul of the cherry blossom will haunt your dreams forever; you are to fight to the death! The Emperor be with you! The Empire be with you!”

Koyama Eiko’s hand came slowly down; in her line of sight she saw dimly, in the mirror, another face, hideously distorted; she was shaking.

She seemed to hear a woman’s voice from beyond the world, a voice most wretched and grim. “Look at my face; you will live forever in my shadow, die beneath my countenance; you will become a walking corpse, and the reek of my rotting will linger on your face forever!” Koyama Eiko screamed with all her strength; the sleeve of her kimono swept every ornament from the dressing-table, a woman’s photograph among them.

“Is the face-changing operation safe? If it fails, what then? What then?” Eiko kept asking.

“Face-changing techniques advance day by day; set your mind at rest, we will arrange for all Japan’s finest doctors to hold a private consultation for you. Before you change your face, there is one more thing you must do without holding back.”

“What thing?”

“Get Yang Yuhua’s body, win his favor, use his greed, and drag him down into the eighteenth level of hell.”

“Will he submit? At our first meeting he had no liking for me.”

“Since ancient times: rich wine, a fair woman, the mandarin-duck swords. If he’s a man, there’ll be no mishap.” Koyama Chino pressed down on Eiko’s shoulders. “For the Empire, for the Emperor, for the great Japanese imperial army, go and fight, go and battle alone, go and shed your blood.”

In Japan in late May, the cherry blossoms of Tokyo were still blooming in great profusion; but because the Tokyo influenza was spreading, many people kept indoors. Yang Yuhua lingered in the bustling streets and markets; he wired his elder brother in Shanghai, still stretching out his hand for money, for in Japan, apart from learning to appreciate geisha performances and to speak fluent Japanese, he had achieved nothing at all in his studies. Yang Yubo wrote to him, saying he had secured him a translator’s post at the Shanghai Commercial Guild, and pressing him to come home and take it up. But how would he go meekly back? At the mere thought of returning home and once more being at another’s mercy, unable to lead a life of wine and flowers and ease, his heart was ten thousandfold displeased. So he wrote back to Yang Yubo that he was reading for a degree in international commerce at Japan’s college of finance and economics, and hoped to complete his studies with honors and one day serve his country. The elder brother, feeling his younger brother held such lofty ambition, let him have his way.

Koyama Eiko cleverly played a lost traveller, and by “accident” met Yang Yuhua again in a strange place.

Once more beneath the cherry trees they strolled again under the slanting sun. She screwed up her courage and told him she had a heart for none but Yuhua, because Yuhua bore about him a rich, classical Eastern charm. This time Yang Yuhua did not resist, nor did he mock her; he took her back to his lodgings, and did not refuse the fair one’s enticement; they gave themselves gladly to the clouds and rain of Mount Wu.

Yang Yuhua supposed this woman would be like the influenza, and after a while would go far away. Who could have known she not only would not go, but demanded he return home as soon as possible?

Only then did Yang Yuhua discover he had mistaken her meaning from the very start. Koyama Eiko was far from the simple, understanding girl he had imagined; there was always something unfathomable and mysterious glittering in her eyes; she must have some other purpose. Unwilling to be entangled by this woman, one night he abandoned Eiko, left Tokyo, and took refuge in the countryside.

He thought that a penniless girl, cast off by her lover, could hold out no long time before she left of her own accord. Who could have known that the next day, the moment he opened his eyes, he saw Eiko’s changeable, uncertain smile?

Haunted by a phantom, haunted by a phantom; Yang Yuhua felt at the time that he was going mad.

He was indeed going mad.

He was secretly taken to the interrogation room of the Japanese military; his crime was the abduction of a girl of good family. He was beaten, beaten until his head swam and his eyes darkened, beaten until he raved, beaten until he confessed his crime, and only then was the punishment brought to a temporary halt.

As in a dream, he was told he would pass the rest of his life in a foreign prison; unless he would betray: betray his elder brother, betray his family, betray his motherland.

To sit in prison unto death under the name of Yang Yuhua? Or to enjoy wealth and splendor under the name of Yang Yubo?

He chose the latter.

He believed he had chosen rightly. He had always held to Nietzsche’s words: a tree is like a man; the higher it grows toward the light, the deeper its roots reach down into the darkness.

Very soon Yang Yuhua graduated with distinction from the Tokyo college of finance and economics, and returned home without hindrance.

The gloom of Koyama Eiko soon vanished from his mind. Single-heartedly he waited to take over all that his elder brother possessed. He waited; what he never dreamed of was that what he waited for was another of Koyama Eiko’s faces.

All the evil conspiracy at last floated up to the surface here.

Recalling all this, Yang Yuhua’s heart brimmed with a sense of guilt.

“It was you who ruined me, you.” Yang Yuhua’s hand gripped Koyama Eiko’s gun. “I could have been an idle young gentleman forever; it was you, you who pushed me onto the crests of the wind and waves. You, it was you, who made me betray my motherland, destroy the Yang family, cut off my own future…”

“Without me, you’d be an out-and-out beggar.”

“It is precisely because of you that I became an out-and-out scoundrel.”

Koyama Eiko spoke with scorn. “Without us, how would you have sat back and enjoyed the fruits?”

“I’ve been struggling to survive in the cracks all along. The secret of twenty years ago is no longer a fathomless secret; someone has deliberately brought it to light. Why? Because the Japanese military has cast you off; and, of course, cast me off too.”

“Impossible! I gave everything for the Empire.”

“Is that so?” Yang Yuhua seemed greatly agitated. “Including your love as well! You never once truly loved me; you despised me, used me, you fooled about with your own man in the cellar; did you ever think of my feelings? After Yuzhen died I did not touch a woman for more than twenty years; I am a shameless man whose soul has been gelded!” His body was shaking, his voice roaring; his hand suddenly wrenched round with all its force, the muzzle turned on Eiko, and without a word he pulled the trigger with deadly resolve.

The gun went off.

“Go and die for your Empire.”

Koyama Eiko stared wide-eyed, a bright-red juice trickling from the corner of her mouth, like grape wine.

Yang Yuhua muttered neurotically to himself. “This must be the most perfect ending. This truly is the most, most perfect ending. It’s all over. The nightmare is finished. Women, why is it that even when they kill they love to run on and chatter? If you’d said not a word and simply fired, the one to die would have been me, the one to live would have been you.” He dragged Koyama Eiko’s corpse toward the cabinet in the drawing room, opened the cabinet, and turning back, saw a pair of beautiful woman’s feet.

Hard upon that, from the drawing room came a woman’s terrified, blood-curdling scream…
