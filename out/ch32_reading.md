## Oyuki

Toshizō drew his sword to the right.

Overhead was the roof of the gate of the Echizen Fukui domain's mansion.

A cluster of supple rafters, bent light as a beauty's arched hand, thrust the eaves out into the rainy dark.

"Villain."

{j} The shape that pressed on Toshizō, snarling out a few words, had a heavy Totsukawa accent. Of late, many rural samurai of the Totsukawa district of Yamato had come drifting into Kyoto.

(A Totsukawa man?)

Toshizō held level in seigan.

By habit he edged his point ever farther to the right and to the right, and gave not a glance to that bare blade on his left.

An aside: it is said that Tanaka Mitsuaki of Tosa (later a count), recalling the days when he deserted his home domain and came up to Kyoto, gave a lecture about them, around the tenth year of Shōwa, at the Kōchi Prefectural Jōtō Middle School.

—The Shinsengumi were fearful. Hijikata Toshizō most of all. When Hijikata came toward you down the broad streets of the capital, his men behind him and those eyes of his glaring and rolling, then all of us, our whole company, would flee from alley to alley, scattering like a brood of spiderlings.

To cut down that Toshizō.

This Totsukawa man had courage, it seems.

The rest hung back in a wide ring in the rain.

Close in were only two: Shichiri Kennosuke on the right, and this Totsukawa man on the left.

With a flash, the Totsukawa man struck down from the high guard.

Toshizō raised his sword and drew back some three sun to the pillar behind him. The Totsukawa man's blade cut the left-mitsudomoe crest on Toshizō's right sleeve and sank until it all but grazed the ground.

The man's upper body laid itself open.

In a breath, Toshizō's blade cut the Totsukawa man down from the right shoulder to the breast.

But Toshizō pitched forward.

For in the same instant he cut down the Totsukawa man, Shichiri Kennosuke on the right had driven in a fierce thrust.

There was nothing for it but to flee.

He tripped on the corpse and fell.

At once he was up.

Down onto his head came Shichiri Kennosuke's second stroke.

There was no time to take it.

To dodge it, he rolled once more. Toshizō's body was already clear of the gate, out by the moat-side in the rain.

At his back was the moat, which was a comfort; but right and left there was not a single tree to make a little shield of.

"Ready the dark-lantern,"

{j} Shichiri's steady voice ordered his men.

Under the eaves of the mansion gate, the very fort Toshizō had held until a moment ago, the dark-lantern was made ready.

"Throw the light on him,"

{j} Shichiri said, in a low voice.

With a flash, the dark-lantern's light lit the shadow of Toshizō standing at the moat-side.

"Toshizō. It seems the time has come to pay the reckoning owed since Bushū."

"Has it, now."

{j} Toshizō held the level seigan, edged to the right as ever. For all the lowness of his voice, both his eyes were flung wide open.

In any brawl, whenever it came, he was a man resolved on death.

"Tonight, at last, I'll be let take my revenge for Hachiōji,"

{j} Shichiri Kennosuke pressed on at his leisure, still in the high guard.

Into that interval the rain began to fall hard.

The rain-legs dashed on the ground, and in the dark-lantern's light a white breath of rain stood up, thick and rolling.

"Shichiri. Is the rice of Chōshū good eating?"

"Poor stuff."

{j} Shichiri too was a steady man.

"But, Hijikata,"

{j} closing the interval warily,

"before long it will be good. You Mibu rōnin know nothing of the times."

"Hmph."

{j} Something laughed. Only Toshizō's eyes.

"So the horse-dung-stinking fencing-peddler who used to prowl Jōshū and Bushū talks like a grown man, once he comes up to the capital."

"Come, Toshizō. Horse-dung-stinking stock—that's the same for both of us, surely."

(True enough.)

{j} Toshizō smiled sourly within his belly.

Shichiri's right foot stamped in hard, and he struck down from the high guard.

He took it.

His hand went numb.

It was a fearsome blow.

Toshizō did not strike back, but, pinning Shichiri's blade at the guard, bore down on it further and pushed him back, a step, two steps. He meant to gain the lie of the ground.

Shichiri hooked at his foot. Toshizō, disliking it, raised his leg.

"What are you all doing?"

{j} Shichiri roared into the dark around him.

"Cut him down now. Won't you cut him down? This fellow's no demon-god either."

With a rush, footfalls sounded to right and left.

Toshizō put his whole strength into it and shoved Shichiri's body away.

Shichiri, as he was flung back, stretched out his left arm and swept at the side of Toshizō's face.

But the blade turned and flowed on to no purpose. Toshizō was no longer there. Toshizō had run to the left.

In the course of his run he cut one man down with a slantwise stroke, slipped into the alley at the southern end of the Echizen Fukui mansion, and ran east.

The old hand at such brawls knows that to trade blows alone against many, be one never so fine a master of the sword, will not hold out even ten minutes.

Once he had come out on Nishinotōin, Toshizō at last eased his pace and began to go slowly south.

(It hurts. …)

{j} He pressed his left arm.

Whose blade had gone in, in that welter of swords? Feeling out the wound, he found on his upper arm a gash gaping wide enough to sink a finger into.

And that was not all.

One on the back of his right foot.

This must have come when he dodged the Totsukawa man's downward cut.

But that was nothing. His right thigh was slippery, and, turning up his hakama to feel, he found a wound of some three sun with blood running freely from it.

(They've done for me, near enough.)

{j} In his medicine-case he kept an oil-salve.

He had been a druggist's son to begin with, so, thinking to stop the bleeding for the moment, he cast a quick glance about him. To dress the wounds on this broad street would not do.

At any moment the crowd might find him and set on him.

Finding a likely alley, he slipped into it.

(If only there were some shōchū.)

{j} So thinking, he drew his short-sword and, to bind the wounds, took off his hakama and tore it to shreds.

It was then.

That the little window overhead opened.

***

"Ah—my thanks."

{j} Toshizō stepped into the earth-floored entry and went straight through to the well inside, at the back of the kitchen, and there first stripped himself naked.

It was to wash off the mud and the blood.

"Mistress, this is a great liberty, but—"

{j} he called toward the inner room.

His voice was hushed. It was out of regard for the neighbors.

"I should like the shōchū on this shelf."

{j} A large iron-glazed jar stood there. On its belly a paper was pasted, and

—shochu.

{j} was written on it in a fine Oie-ryū hand.

(A woman's household, it would seem.)

But whether the folk drank or not, in those days every house kept shōchū ready against the dressing of wounds.

"If you please,"

{j} a composed woman's voice came back.

"Do use it. There is a wound-salve as well. Hakuyukō it is called; it is compounded at the Kawachiya of Kyōmachibori in Osaka, and is said to answer very well. What is your wish?"

{j} A quiet way of speaking, but there was no waste in her words, and it gave a sense of quick wits.

"Then, without ceremony, I'll take it."

{j} Toshizō thought about this woman. In her speech there was no Kyoto accent.

It was, it seemed, the speech of a samurai woman.

(Who can she be?)

When she had opened the lattice door a while ago and let him in, Toshizō, having half tumbled into the entry, chanced to raise his face.

At that moment the woman stood holding up, a little, a makeshift hand-candle, a paper wound about the waist of a taper.

She went at once into the passage-through kitchen; but he remembers that, in that moment, he had drawn breath at the woman's unlooked-for beauty.

She was twenty-five or six, and by what she wore, no unmarried girl. Yet there seemed to be no husband about.

It was a cramped house.

One could tell by the look of it.

(It hurts. …)

{j} It stung. The shōchū stung.

Even Toshizō, for all his hardihood, came near to swooning.

In nothing but a loincloth, Toshizō crouched at the wellside. He was washing his own wounds himself. Unless a man is stout-hearted indeed, he cannot manage the like.

The mistress had come, at some point, and from across the earth-floored entry, holding up a far-off light, was watching it.

That she did not draw near was, no doubt, the discretion of a samurai upbringing.

For all that, Toshizō rubbed oil into the wounds, and with the bleached cloth the mistress had brought out bound the three places, and,

"Forgive me, but would you tell the watchman at the town gate's guard-hut to call a street palanquin?"

"Who are you, sir?"

"Eh?"

{j} The wounds throbbed as if ringing.

"The—that is, you, sir, are—"

{j} the mistress asked.

"Ah, I should have said sooner. If you will say Hijikata Toshizō of the Shinsengumi, the town officials will see to it properly."

(This man is. …)

{j} Toshizō's name rang through the capital.

That it stops even a crying child—in this man's case it is no exaggeration.

"I ask it of you."

"—"

{j} The woman nodded in silence, and seemed to be reaching a hand into a corner of the entry, and presently brought out an umbrella and went off.

Before long she came back, the fresh creak of high geta sounding on their teeth.

Toshizō's clothes were fouled with rain and blood.

"If it would suit you,"

{j} the woman brought out, in a clothes-basket, a single set of black cotton crested garments. Not only haori and hakama, but an underrobe and even bleached cloth cut to six shaku were laid ready. Her dead husband's things, no doubt.

She set them down on the earthen floor.

(An attentive woman.)

{j} Toshizō raised his face and, by the light of the taper, looked at the woman's eyes. Hers was, if anything, no Kyoto cast of face; there is this cast of face among the women who come to worship at the fair-days of the Sensō-ji in Edo's Asakusa.

Single-lidded eyes, a darkish complexion, and strong shadows about the mouth.

"You are an Edo person, aren't you,"

{j} Toshizō said, in the up-tilted Tama speech.

"—"

{j} The woman, by habit, held wide her seldom-blinking eyes and gazed at Toshizō, and presently,

"Yes,"

{j} she nodded, as much as to say.

"What are you called?"

"Yuki, I am called."

"A samurai family."

"—"

{j} The woman was silent. Unsaid, it was plain.

"No—to meet an Edo-born woman in Kyoto is a rare thing. Tonight I have had good luck."

(But why is an Edo woman living alone in such a quarter?)

{j} Toshizō thought it a question, but did not put it into words, and, with a gesture as of pressing his palm down over the clothes-basket,

"Your kindness alone I'll take, and thank you. My blood is not yet stopped, and to spoil such precious things of yours for nothing would be more than I could answer for."

{j} Toshizō, in nothing but a loincloth, wound round and round with bleached cloth, took up his two swords and stood.

"Will you go back to your quarters just as you are?"

{j} It was the look in her eyes of one who thought: a warrior of such honor as the vice-commander of the Shinsengumi, and—

"Put them on, if you please,"

{j} she said, brooking no refusal, as though commanding. Toshizō, with a feeling near to reeling where he stood, took a fond delight in the crisp ring of this woman's command. It was a savor the women of Kyoto had not. An Edo woman, if it is kindness, will bear a man down and make him do as she says.

(Ah—a savor I had forgotten.)

Toshizō was born in the deep country outside the shogunal city. From his boyhood he had longed after the women of Edo, thirteen ri away.

Because that longing lingered in him, with the women of Kyoto, whom others called fine, he could, do what he would, never grow at ease.

"Then—I'll borrow them."

{j} Passing his arms through them, what astonished him was that it was the same left-mitsudomoe crest as Toshizō's own.

"A strange chance."

{j} Toshizō gazed at the crest.

(May something not come of it, between this woman and me?)

The woman held her bearing severely, reservedly; but in her eyes there was, plainly, a warmth toward Toshizō.

Whether that warmth came from the mere fellow-feeling of one born, like him, in the eastern country, or was a warmth toward Toshizō the man himself—

Presently the landlord, the caretaker, and the town officials came with greetings and to ask after him.

The landlord was the Ōmiya, the pawnshop on the front street; the caretaker was a withered old man named Jihee.

"I'll come to thank you before long,"

{j} Toshizō, seen off by them, got into a street palanquin.

At the barracks there was a great commotion.

When, on the manservant Tōkichi's word, the units of Harada Sanosuke and Okita Sōji had rushed to the spot, there was neither corpse nor man anywhere about.

And Toshizō did not come back to the barracks. At which the troopers flew off in every quarter of the city to search.

And there Toshizō came back, wearing crested garments well pressed with the smoothing-iron.

"What has happened to you?"

{j} When the troopers asked, he only grinned, went briskly up the entrance-step, and withdrew to his own room.

At once he called a surgeon and had the dressing done over.

When the doctor had gone, Okita Sōji came in.

"You do give people a fright,"

"My apologies."

"What happened to you?"

"In front of the Echizen Fukui mansion, that fellow Shichiri Kennosuke turned up again. He is my curse, that one."

"A splendid curse."

{j} Okita drew out Toshizō's Izumi-no-kami Kanesada of two shaku eight sun, its hilt-wrapping wet with rain and blood. Notches in the edge, a heavy clouding of blood.

"You seem to have been at work."

"I came near to being cut down. Those men are being sheltered, I suppose, in the Tosa or the Satsuma mansion since Chōshū's retreat from Kyoto. The Totsukawa man was there too. From the way Shichiri works them with a jerk of his chin, it seems he has climbed to quite a face in Kyoto now."

"By all accounts, from what the searching men say, Shichiri is forever saying that Hijikata, at least, is his to deal with,"

"He does haunt me."

"Hmph."

{j} Okita laughed. (Your old conduct was the bad one)—it was that mischievous look in his eyes.

"By the way, Sōji,"

{j} Toshizō said, with lively eyes.

"It seems I've fallen for a woman."

"Eh?"

{j} Okita made a dazzled face.

For Toshizō had never once, before this, used such a word as

fallen,

{j} of a woman.

"Say nothing to the men. Even when Kondō gets back from Geishū, it's not to be told."

"Then you'd have done better not to tell me either."

"You alone are apart."

"I alone am apart? A nuisance, that—being made into a confessor-Buddha."

"Heh. There's that about you, yes."

Some ten days later Toshizō, having the manservant carry the whole of that clothing, washed and stitched anew, called on the landlord, the Ōmiya. The landlord summoned the caretaker, old Jihee, to sit in as well.

By what he heard, the woman was the wife of one Kada Shinjirō, an Edo-resident foot-guard of the Ōgaki domain. When the domain was ordered to the guard of Kyoto, Kada came up alone, as a domain soldier. Coming alone was only natural; in no domain, high samurai or low, did any bring wife and children up to Kyoto.

But Oyuki had something out of the common in her, and, following after her husband, came up to Kyoto, and, keeping clear of the domain, quietly took up town-lodging. It was not that they were so very close, as man and wife.

Oyuki had a gift for painting, and later, under the art-name Kōka, left a few works in Kyoto and Tokyo. Her skill was not the equal of her character.

She had come up to Kyoto to study painting of the Shijō-Maruyama school under the Kyoto painter Yoshida Ryōdō.

Before long her husband died of illness.

Oyuki was left alone in Kyoto. She ought to have gone back at once to her family home in Edo; but her family were temple-officials of the Kan'ei-ji, with a good income. With that remittance still coming, she was, somehow, letting the days go by.
