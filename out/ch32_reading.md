## Author's Preface

Cherishing the memory of that whole band of young brothers of mine, who in those days did their work with such color and spirit, I have longed to bring out in this book something of their boldness and mettle, and so to make known their valor and their fidelity.

Calling these bygone events to mind, I hold them for no trifling matter of small account; yet, falling as they did within a time of great upheaval, they come to look low in the scale of things, and of little weight. In truth, the theme is not only fresh, but throws into relief the resolute deeds of a great body of educated young men in the struggle against Communism and the war to suppress the rebellion; and it proves, too, that the Communist Party was really nothing so very formidable.

"Pacification of the Beiping-Tianjin Region" is the Fifth Part of the whole of my humble work Nameless Heroes, and the last Part, too, that the writer has it in mind to write.

Now, no sooner had the eight years of the War of Resistance come to a close—the very moment when a thousand things laid waste awaited their reviving, and a thousand tasks awaited their taking up—than the Communist Party men, harboring their malice within, seized the chance to rise in revolt, and in but a few months had usurped and seized above two hundred cities. The United States, out of its own interests, came forward to mediate between the two, and, blind to it, fell into the Communists' snare all unawares, yet forced my government to bow to their views. And so the gravest error in all history was thus cast, and brought bitter suffering, too, upon our hundreds of millions of Chinese countrymen!

The gist of the matter was this. In the early days of 1946, the United States sent its special envoy Marshall to China to mediate the fighting; and with Zhang Qun, the representative of my government, and Zhou Enlai, the representative of the Communists, he formed the "Committee of Three." After this body had held meeting upon meeting, it issued formally the order that "all fighting cease fire at once, and all troop movements halt." To carry the cease-fire into effect, there was set up, under the "Committee of Three," a "Military Mediation Executive Headquarters" at Beiping—formed of three men, the American representative Colonel Robertson, the government representative Lieutenant General Zheng Jiemin, and the Communist representative Ye Jianying (chief of staff of the Communist army)—the better to see the thing done. Yet the Communists, from the very outset, would not abide by the rules laid down, but went on with their ceaseless attacks upon the tracts where our strength was thin. And the Americans, for all the Communists' broken faith and breach of covenant, looked on as though they saw nothing, bent single-mindedly on giving way to them, and brought pressure to bear on us as well, holding fast to their absurd insistence that no armed force be used. Bound thus hand and foot, fighting and parleying by turns, we not only let slip many a chance to wipe out the Communists, but saw the whole situation swayed ever more to our hurt.

At that time, Mr. Zheng Jiemin, who took part in the "Military Mediation Executive Headquarters," had—by virtue of his long study of the Communist Party, and of his contact on many sides with the chief men among the Communists—gained a deep insight into them and a thorough understanding. And so the reports and analyses that Mr. Zheng brought forward, down to the very matters he proposed, were held in especial regard by the Leader. Somewhat later, the promulgation of the "Lizhi Plan" was, it may be, bound up with this.

As to the putting of the "Lizhi Plan" into practice, there is a passage of record in the late Mr. Liu Peichu's posthumous work Fleeting Glimpses of a Floating Life, which runs: "Mr. Zheng Jiemin said to me: 'The Leader, feeling how hard the work of bandit-suppression was, called to mind the work of the Special Operations Corps in the early days of the Jiangxi bandit-suppression, and would have a Pacification Corps set up under the direct charge of the Ministry of National Defense. I have already recommended you to the Leader as its Corps Commander; make haste to hand over the People's Service Corps, and come to make ready this Corps. The newly chosen cadres must first of all be strictly trained; it is now settled that a Lizhi Training Class shall be set up at the Central Training Corps, with myself to preside over it, and the whole body of students to be led by you; and when the training is done, all alike shall be members of the Pacification Corps. As for all the particulars and the carrying of it forward, do you settle them with the two comrades, the Deputy Directors Zhang Yanyuan and Hou Teng.'" The "Central Training Corps" stood at Xiaolingwei in Nanjing, and was of very great extent; the "Lizhi Training Class" was but one among its training units. The post of class director was held concurrently by Mr. Zheng Jiemin, chief of the Second Department of the Ministry of National Defense, and that of deputy director by the Deputy Director Mr. Zhang Yanyuan. Within the class was set one Student Corps; and the post of Corps Commander was the very one held by Mr. Liu Peichu—though in his posthumous work he writes it "Student Brigade," whereas I, having inquired again of several fellow-students who took the training at the time, was told by all that it was the "Corps" and not the "Brigade." Below the Corps it was divided into five brigades, and the writer was the commander of the First Brigade.

The students were of quite excellent quality, chosen severally from the several divisions of the "Youth Army," from the cadres of every rank of the "People's Service Corps," and from the middle and lower officers of the "Officers' Corps." I recalled before that each brigade held but fifty men; this is likely wrong, for by the fellow-students' account it should rather be above a hundred, which comes nearer the mark.

The training time was strangely short—each term but a single month—which shows too how the throwing of them into the actual work would brook not a moment's delay. And so the chief aims of the training could bear only upon unity of spirit, concentration of will, and oneness of view, together with the grasping and the searching-out of the task. Short though the time was, the heads of government came for the most part to address them, and the Leader himself came four times: which shows how greatly the thing was valued.

The "Lizhi Class" ran for three terms in Nanjing, and the students it trained came in all to a thousand and some hundreds. As the first term graduated and was posted out, the "Ministry of National Defense Pacification Corps" was at the same time set up. The Student Corps Commander of the "Lizhi Class" was likewise the Corps Commander of the "Pacification Corps"; and below him, the several brigade commanders were the very ones marked out to be sent to the several working regions, the brigade commanders belonging to the "Pacification Corps." In fact, each brigade was attached to the highest military organ of its place—such as a "Pacification Office" or a "Bandit Suppression Headquarters"—and was then given a unit code-name, so that a "brigade commander" became a "unit commander" as well. In the mind, or in the feel of it, "unit commander" seemed a good deal grander than "brigade commander." The headquarters of the "Pacification Corps" was set in Nanjing, and had within it many a staff unit besides. Under the Corps headquarters there were formed, first and last, seven brigades and three or four directly subordinate companies, their working regions reaching over more than twenty provinces of the Northeast, North China, and Central China—vast enough indeed.

Since the several units sent out might, by the rules, take up and absorb the popular armed forces on the spot, the sheer number of the whole at its fullest was well-nigh past all reckoning; to speak of the Second Brigade in the Northeast alone, there were, by the roll that the Corps headquarters sent men to call, above twenty thousand souls.

The several units belonging to the "Pacification Corps," by reason of the difference of the regions in which they stood, were each in a different case; and how their relation of attachment ran with the local powers was likewise, in each, now smooth and now crossed. And as for their doings in the meeting of the Communist Party, there was, of course, in each its own several worth.

What is set forth in this book is but an outline of the "First Brigade of the Pacification Corps"; and even this scant outline is not wholly the writer's own remembering—the greater part of it is material furnished, by word of mouth and in writing, by the comrades now in Taiwan; while as for the relevant documents that might serve for reference, there is nowhere left to seek them.

The First Brigade headquarters lay at Beiping; the working region our superiors marked out for it took in the three provinces of Hebei, Chahar, and Suiyuan, the two cities of Beiping and Tianjin, and a part of Rehe province besides. This compass was in truth too vast by far. Were you to open a map and look, you would feel on the instant that, with but a single small brigade to draw on, how in the disposing of manpower was this to be laid out? How deployed?

In the "Lizhi Plan" our superiors handed down, there were set forth four great tasks—namely, "pacification," "intelligence," "action," and "assault." As conceived, the "work of pacification" was to be carried out in the "recovered areas"—those once seized by the Communist army and then retaken—and in the "combat zones" where we yet stood face to face with the Communist army. Parting out the several heads within it, there were the organizing and training of the populace, the checking of household registers, the succoring and gathering-in of the displaced, the rooting-out of Communist spies, and the rest.

To put it by a figure, they were the road-breaking engine and the street-sweeper. The "work of intelligence" fell into two tiers, the tactical and the strategic: that which was tactical in kind was furnished to the local military and civil organs for use on the spot; that which held strategic value was reported up for our superiors to deal with. To gather intelligence was, of course, for the strengthening of the effect of eyes and ears, and herein lay much nicety of art. The "work of action" lay not in the sanction of Communist chieftains and cadres, but bore in general upon destruction—above all upon transport and communications, military installations, and the like. The "work of assault" was of a newer sort; for we had the very latest American automatic equipment, of especially strong firepower, and to strike at a small strongpoint with it was truly to take a thing as easily as plucking it up by hand. Yet in this there was no great meaning; and later, feeling keenly how the gathering of intelligence grew day by day harder, it occurred to us: why not use the way of assault to "raid for intelligence"? And sure enough, we drove off the Communist army's small detachments and carried off many documents—thus, at a single stroke, gaining two things at once.

The four tasks set forth above, though they held no independence one from another, had yet the spirit of those who will go through fire and flood and shrink from nothing. Writing to this point, the writer, out of the very ordinariness of it, is moved to sigh: it seems to me that the "division of labor" in this work of revolution needs likewise to be set upon the scales and weighed—carefully measured in both the light and the heavy—that it lose not its fairness.

Next let me speak of the establishment and make-up of the First Brigade. The First Brigade headquarters was set up at Beiping in September 1947, its address the rear court of the Police Officers' Academy in Tieshizi Hutong. The brigade had under it five command rooms and six or seven directly subordinate sections, and had leave, besides, to set up an assault team. As for the local armed forces that the brigade headquarters furnished with weapons and helped to form into units, these are not counted in. In a word, by the latter half of 1948 the number outside the establishment far outran the number within it.

The five command rooms were spread along the "Beiping-Liaoning line," the "Tianjin-Pukou line," the "Beiping-Hankou line," the "Beiping-Gubeikou line," and the "Beiping-Suiyuan line"; and their stations shifted with the state of the fighting.

The head of a command room ranked in the establishment as a lieutenant colonel, and was by the rules styled "commanding officer," not "commander." Whether, out in the field, any called him "commander," I have not looked into closely.

The reason I cannot recall clearly the number of the directly subordinate sections is that they changed too much, first and last. Those that left the deeper mark were the "North Suburb Section" near Beiping, the "Intelligence Section" in the city of Tianjin, the "Directly Subordinate Section" in the city of Guisui, the "Huailai Section" on the Beiping-Suiyuan line, the "Baoding Section" on the Beiping-Hankou line, the "Yuguan Section" on the Beiping-Liaoning line, and so on. Besides these there were "directly subordinate agents" who worked alone.

There was besides one "assault team" of no small fighting power, some two hundred and more men, that had roamed the country from Jinghai to Qingxian along the Tianjin-Pukou line.

These working units set forth above had all, more or less, crossed hands with the Communist army; and this makes up one main part of what the book contains. When the numbers on the strength outran the establishment, pay and supply became a great problem; but happily, on a sudden impulse at the outset, I had asked the "Sixth Base Depot Command" in charge of logistics at Beiping to recommend us a quartermaster, and the man who came brought us the greatest convenience besides. By the brigade's full grant of authority, this quartermaster too discharged his duty well, and kept the whole unit never wanting in grain and pay. The thing that struck me deepest was that, at the very time when Lin Biao's Communist army had ringed the four walls of Beiping round about, sack upon sack of flour was heaped to filling a great hall that might hold several hundred men, stacked higher than a man—which shows how marvelous past all telling were the "personnel" and the "system" of that age.

Let me explain the "relation of attachment" a little more simply. The First Brigade of the Pacification Corps was, from September 1947, attached to the "Beiping Field Headquarters of the Chairman of the National Government"; the director of the Field Headquarters at that time was Li Zongren. I recall that my first going to Zhongnanhai to present my papers was arranged beforehand through Brother Zhang Jiaquan, chief of the Second Section of the Field Headquarters; the one who came out to receive me was the chief of staff, Lieutenant General Xu Qiming, and in a few words the formalities were counted done. Thereafter the relation was thin, with little coming and going, it seemed, and never once did they "order" us to do anything at all.

After the "Beiping Field Headquarters" was dissolved, we were attached to the "North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters." To tell the truth, I filled the post ill, for I never once went to wait upon the commander-in-chief, Fu Zuoyi. As for the routine liaison, that the deputy brigade commander saw to, calling on Brother Shi Hong, chief of the Second Section of the "North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters," and on Director Lei of the Third Section. That such a state of things should be—to speak it plainly—was because on both sides there was bound to be something of the sectarian spirit, so that a warmth of feeling was hard to come by.

As for the branch units openly sent out to the several places—the several command rooms and directly subordinate sections and the rest—they got on well enough with the local military and civil organs; and the reason of it, weighed out, was chiefly that they neither raised funds nor levied grain, so that, if besides they did the locality some useful turn, they were the more welcome still. In the winter of 1948, just as the work was coming by degrees into its stride, the general situation, alas, was already worsening day by day. Word came that the Second Brigade in the Northeast, the Third Brigade in Shandong, the Fourth Brigade in Henan, and the Fifth Brigade in Shanxi had suffered, one after another, grievous losses, some routed past all keeping the field. Intelligence disclosed that the forces of the Communists Nie Rongzhen and Lin Biao were about to come out in full strength to storm Beiping, and every region suddenly gave word of peril.

At first the brigade still ordered the working units of the several places that, save those who withdrew along with the army, they should contrive to leave a part of their people behind and swiftly devise a plan to lie hidden. But thereafter, in any place fallen to the Communist army, an outlander, without root or faction and ill-prepared besides—how could he ever lie hidden there? Only at this pass did I come to a rueful sense that to ask such a thing was in truth to press men beyond their power, to make a vain and meaningless sacrifice, and was greatly not to be done.

Taught by this, I took counsel with the deputy brigade commander and, willing to brave the gravest reproach, put a plan before our superiors to draw the whole body of officers and men of the First Brigade back to the rear; and then, having by way of free choice won their consent, we set about at our leisure the disposing of the "stay-behind work," to meet the hard days to come.

Unlooked for, our superiors took up our proposal; and in the early days of 1949, on the day before the Beiping-Tianjin railway was cut by the Communist army, the whole brigade, led by the deputy brigade commander, went first to Tianjin and there sought a ship to carry them by sea to Shanghai and thence to Ningbo. Thereafter, the Leader happening to be at Fenghua, they were ordered to stand guard there; and before long, crossing the sea by way of Fujian, they reached Magong. Of those now in Taiwan there are still several hundred—though all, too, are men past sixty.

After the whole brigade had left Beiping, the writer himself, together with some forty-odd comrades of the brigade headquarters, carried on the "stay-behind work," each apart. There was at the time a very, very small notebook, wherein were true names and cover names, the rules of liaison, the call-signs of the wireless stations, the cipher code-names, and the rest. When the disposing was done, I sent this little book—on which the safety of a good many men hung—up to my superiors to be kept; and how it fared thereafter, having left the post, I had no way to learn.

It was either 1950 or 1951 that the writer, in Hong Kong, saw in a Chinese-Communist newspaper published at Beiping a piece of news which said: "One Ji Zhaoxiang, having fired his gun to resist arrest, then took a bullet and made an end of himself." Just so—the martyred Ji Zhaoxiang was none other than one of the wireless operators in the "stay-behind work." The fate of the rest was much of a piece; forgive me that I can say no more.

When the soldiery came up beneath the walls of Beiping, the writer and some forty-odd comrades were shut up within them too; and because what I saw and heard was much indeed, here is matter well worth the writing, and the book shall have its account of it from more angles than one.

After the two airfields of Nanyuan and Xiyuan outside the walls of Beiping had fallen, the great parade ground at Dongdan Pailou within the city, and the open ground in the Temple of Agriculture where a great swath of cypress had been felled, were both turned into makeshift small airfields; small aircraft could still, under the fire of the guns, contrive to take off and land. When Mr. Zheng Jiemin came from Nanjing to Beiping bearing a letter in the Leader's own hand to Fu Zuoyi, it was within the city that he came down. And the writer, too, flew back to Nanjing from the Temple of Agriculture aboard Mr. Zheng's own plane.

At the end of January 1949—a few days, it seems, before the Spring Festival—another party of some forty-odd men left in Beiping likewise took plane to Qingdao, and thereafter went their ways, some back to their units, some to seek other posts.

At Nanjing the writer twice asked to resign, and was twice refused. In the autumn of 1949, still under Mr. Zheng's leadership, I worked, in concert with a certain quarter, wholly upon operations directed at the mainland.
