## The Battle of Toba-Fushimi (I)

A few days ago, to visit the site of the Fushimi Magistrate's office where Toshizō had been, this writer went south from Kyoto down the Fushimi road.

On the way,

{j} "Gokō-no-miya"

{j} there is a shrine with a vast sacred precinct. It makes a wood on the west side of the road.

A little to the south of it, over an area that was probably ten times broader than the Gokō-no-miya,

{j} "the Fushimi Magistrate's office"

{j} had run its walls around.

"What stands there now?"

{j} When I asked the priest of the Gokō-no-miya,

"It's a housing estate,"

{j} he said, as if spitting the words out.

And indeed, standing on the spot, the place where the Magistrate's office had been was scraped flat by bulldozers, and star-shaped buildings and blocks like slabs of yōkan stood there.

"In the old days — though it was only until quite recently — on a plot of some ten tsubo here by the roadside, there stood a memorial tower of fine natural stone to the shogunal dead of the Battle of Toba-Fushimi. Their descendants had raised it, and ever since Meiji my shrine held a service for it every year. Now it has been cleared away, the land sold off, and not a trace of it remains."

I gazed out, dumbstruck, over the housing-estate scene.

Japanese history turned at Sekigahara, and turned again at the Battle of Toba-Fushimi.

On that spot there is not one fragment of a monument, only the housing estate, and as far as the eye can see, rows of laundry hung out to dry.

"Warm, isn't it,"

{j} an acquaintance happened to call out to me.

He was an old fellow who caught river-fish at Yoshijima in Fushimi, and like a Kyoto old man he had a rusty, gravelly voice.

"That year, they say, was a cold one."

{j} and the old man told me a story passed down to him from his great-grandmother.

"By the Magistrate's office there was a pool with little crucian carp and the like in it, and from the year's end into the New Year, they say, ice a sun or so thick lay over it."

It was on the eighteenth of the twelfth month of Keiō 3 — when thick ice must have lain over that pool — that Kondō was shot at Sumizome.

When the doctor saw it, the wound was graver than expected: the shoulder-blade was cracked.

"It must hurt."

{j} said Toshizō.

The lead ball had bitten into the flesh and seemed to have shattered to fragments; the flesh of the shoulder was pulped to ruin over an area the size of a fist. The blood came without cease. Through the night the white cloth was changed any number of times, and each time it soon went crimson.

"Bah — it's nothing much."

Kondō was bearing the pain.

That he had not fallen from his horse with a wound like this was Kondō all over, and Toshizō marveled at it.

"— Toshi."

{j} said Kondō.

"The Shinsengumi — I leave it to you."

"Aye."

Toshizō nodded. They had been comrades since they were brats playing along the banks of the Tama River. With that one word alone, the handing over of the command was done.

After that, a high fever came over Kondō's body. For about a week he could scarcely take food, and did no more than doze, his eyes closing and opening.

(If only it does not fester.)

{j} Toshizō fretted; but something yellow was beginning, little by little, to mingle with the blood.

From the shogun Yoshinobu at Osaka Castle, too, came a messenger to inquire after him.

"Come to Osaka"

{j} was the message. There was no decent surgeon in Fushimi. Happily, at Osaka Castle there was the shogun's personal physician Matsumoto Ryōjun, said to be the finest doctor in the land.

Matsumoto Ryōjun was thirty-six, two years older than Kondō. Adopted into the Matsumoto house of shogunal physicians, he studied Western medicine at Nagasaki under the Dutch doctor Pompe, and while still a student founded at Nagasaki Japan's first Western-style hospital (then called the Nagasaki Yōjōsho; it is the forerunner of the present medical faculty of Nagasaki University), showing a political ability almost too great to waste on a doctor. Later he became a shogunal physician and received the rank of hōgen. A man of high brilliance, but of hot blood too, so that after the collapse of the shogunate he fought on from place to place. For that he was imprisoned for a time after the Restoration, but the new government had need of him, and he took office, changing his name to Jun. He built up the army's medical system and became the army's first surgeon-general (then styled "chief of military surgeons"). He lived to seventy-six and in his last years was made a baron. In its bearing on our own lives today, he was the man who first encouraged and taught sea-bathing, opening the first bathing beach at — Zushi, I believe. The Japanese of that day held that to swim and play in the sea was a wild and outlandish notion.

This Matsumoto Ryōjun (Jun), after treating Kondō at Osaka Castle, became a most ardent supporter of the Shinsengumi; the monument bearing the joined names of Kondō and Hijikata that now stands at the east exit of Itabashi Station in Tokyo was brushed by this Matsumoto Ryōjun's own hand, and to the end of his life he would often tell of the Shinsengumi. Among the high dignitaries of Meiji he was, one may say, perhaps the only man who felt for the Shinsengumi.

It was arranged that Kondō should be sent from Fushimi to Osaka by a shogunal boat. And the bedridden Okita Sōji with him.

The night before —

{j} "Toshi — Oyuki, is it, they say her name is."

{j} he said, out of nowhere. Kondō knew that this man "Toshi" had, from his youth, a temper that would say not a word about his own love affairs; but this night it was Kondō who raised the subject.

"That's right. Yuki."

{j} said Toshizō, without expression.

"Why, Toshi. With such a woman as that, why did you not go to see her on the night you left Kyoto?"

"Because there was no need."

{j} Toshizō said hurriedly, and short.

"No need of what?"

{j} Kondō was slow to it.

"Of meeting her, is what."

"No need, you say? Settling the household, an allowance for the woman, that sort of thing?"

{j} said Kondō. The truth was that when Toshizō sent the letter off by a town courier, of the two hundred ryō he had by him he had kept back only fifty, and had the rest delivered secretly to Oyuki.

But that, in Toshizō's own feeling, was no "allowance."

Oyuki was Toshizō's precious lover. She was no such vulgar thing — to hear Toshizō tell it — as a wife or a mistress.

"Kondō-san. I'd not have you mistake it — Oyuki is no mistress."

"Hm?"

To Kondō's head it was hard to grasp.

"If not a mistress, then what?"

"A lover."

{j} In the present day there is that handy, convenient, and fairly exact word, and so Toshizō would have answered thus. But,

"Someone dear to me. Mine —"

{j} was all he said.

"If she is dear to you, why not meet her?"

"Who's to say."

Toshizō made a sour face, of the sort that says he wished to carry the subject no further. To a man of Kondō's stamp, who kept three mistresses besides a wife, it would be wasted breath.

That night Kondō talked in a very faint-hearted way.

"The times have changed for good."

{j} was his meaning. Sooner or later, he said, it would become a world with the Court at its center; and when that came, he did not want to be of the rebel army.

"Kondō-san, give it up now."

{j} Toshizō checked him again and again. It was bad for the body. And not for the body only: the weakness in the man Kondō was showing through, and Toshizō hated it.

(This man is, after all, a hero.)

{j} so Toshizō thought; and yet it was a heroism only for when he rode the current, rode the momentum. Ride the momentum, and he was a man who could bring out twice, three times his true ability.

But he was weak in decline.

Let the situation turn against him, let the ground begin to crumble underfoot, and Kondō became a man less than his true worth.

(He is like a kite. In a fair wind he is a great kite, lifted by the wind, riding the wind and riding the flattery, climbing on and on; but let the wind once drop, and he flutters down to the ground.)

He was of that type, and it was no thing to be blamed for.

(But.)

I am not so, thought Toshizō.

Rather, the deeper the decline, the stronger Hijikata Toshizō grew.

He was no kite riding the wind.

He was a bird, flying by its own power.

{j} So Toshizō rated himself. Or at least, he wished to be so from here on.

(As far as my wings hold out, I will fly, and fly.)

{j} he thought.

The next day, Kondō and Okita were sent away under escort.

***

Osaka was the great stronghold of the shogunal army.

They — though it was above all the two Matsudaira houses, the Aizu han and the Kuwana han (whose lords were brothers), who were the hot spearhead — held against the Satsuma han, which in Kyoto kept the boy Emperor in its keeping and worked its schemes as it pleased, an indignation so fierce that nothing would settle it now but war.

Yoshinobu had already resigned the office of shogun, given up too the rank of Inner Minister held since Ieyasu, and at Osaka, a full thirteen ri from Kyoto,

{j} "in confinement"

{j} though he was — now a preposterous new demand was brought against him: that the shogunate hand back to the Court its three million koku of directly-held domain.

Already Yoshinobu had fallen to the position of a single daimyō.

And besides, on what charge was he to give up his lands? If a daimyō must return his domain, then Satsuma and Chōshū and Tosa and Geishū, and the three hundred lords with them, should all alike return theirs. But of that, no mention.

Yoshinobu alone was told to give his up.

It was outrageous.

There was neither reason nor sense in it, and against this even the lords of Tosa, Echizen, and Geishū — who were in Kyoto assisting the Son of Heaven alongside Satsuma — fiercely objected.

But the court noble Iwakura Tomomi and the Satsuma han's go-between Ōkubo Ichizō (Toshimichi) — just the two of them — were laboring with a six-armed, eight-faced energy to force through their "minority opinion."

Ōkubo's thinking was, to the last,

{j} "the destruction of the House of Tokugawa."

{j} Leave the House of Tokugawa standing with its military force and authority intact, and the "Restoration" that Satsuma and Chōshū envisioned could never be brought about. From of old, there has never been a revolution that did not go by the means of war.

And so: strike it down.

To strike it down, a pretext was needed. Ōkubo Ichizō, a schemer without equal in his age, pressed Tokugawa Yoshinobu at Osaka Castle with the impossible demand that he return his territory, and if he would not consent, to strike him down as an enemy of the Court. With that in view he pushed forward his maneuvering upon the Court.

But the court nobles were cold to the plan.

The pro-Court lords, the lord of Tosa foremost among them, were against a "revolution" of the Satsuma kind. In all likelihood, had one polled the samurai of the whole country at that time, ninety-nine in a hundred of them would have favored rather the Tosa plan, or the Aizu han's way of preserving the House of Tokugawa; for no man likes the present order to change all at once. But a revolution comes to pass when a minority opinion grips the preponderance of armed force. Public opinion, or so-called sound argument, is to the side making the revolution no better than wind.

The evil precedent for it was left by the very founder of the House of Tokugawa, Ieyasu himself. Two hundred years and more ago, to destroy the Toyotomi house — already sunk at Osaka to the position of a daimyō of over seven hundred thousand koku — he thought up every kind of impossible demand and forced them on it, until at last he drove the Toyotomi to have no choice but to rise, brought on the Winter and Summer Sieges of Osaka, and in the end wiped them out.

In that castle of fated destiny sat Yoshinobu, the last shogun of the House of Tokugawa.

Yoshinobu was a man of learning. Having come out of the Mito house, he was also a believer in reverence for the throne. He dreaded to leave to later ages the name of an enemy of the Court. Had Yoshinobu been an Ieyasu, or a hero of still earlier times, he would have raised the whole military strength of the shogunate and fought. Yoshinobu's misfortune was that he was a disciple of the Mito school of history — a historiography that sorted the great men of the past into "enemies of the Court" and "loyal subjects." He cannot have wished to be an enemy of the Court.

That was what made Yoshinobu's stance weak.

But the Aizu han and a part of the shogun's vassals were adamant. Satsuma must be struck down, they pressed on Yoshinobu.

At last,

{j} "a Memorial to Chastise Satsuma"

{j} was raised up, and in the guise of a forceful petition to the Son of Heaven, it was resolved to march a great army upon Kyoto.

The shogunal side had risen beautifully to the Satsuma side's challenge. The precedent for it lay in Ieyasu, who had provoked Toyotomi Hideyori and destroyed him.

The shogunal army — the Tokugawa army, to be exact — at the close of the twelfth month of Keiō 3, disposed its several corps under the commander-in-chief Matsudaira Masakata, of councillor rank.

Its reserves numbered tens of thousands. The attacking force was a great army of 16,400 men.

— And the Kyoto side that met them?

Their strength is unknown even now, but there were probably not five thousand of them.

In numbers, the shogunal side was overwhelmingly the stronger.

"This battle we win."

{j} Toshizō believed.

"Now hear me, all of you."

{j} Toshizō gathered the corps men and said.

"From the time I was a child I've been in fight after fight. The thing about a fight is this: the moment you start it, you reckon your life already gone. You make yourself believe you're dead. Do that, and you win."

But in his heart,

{j} (Can we win, I wonder.)

{j} there was a misgiving. The one and only ground of that misgiving was the person of Yoshinobu.

That the shogunal army should set out from Osaka with the Memorial to Chastise Satsuma (a petition) held aloft was well enough — but why did Yoshinobu not stand at its head?

Yoshinobu stayed planted in Osaka Castle. And his posture was anything but warlike; was he not simply submitting, like a woman or a child?

(A bad omen, this.)

{j} so he thought.

The war-tales of the Summer Siege of Osaka, Toshizō knew almost by heart.

The commander-in-chief Toyotomi Hideyori never, in the end, set a single step out of Osaka Castle. Sanada Yukimura, fighting his hard and bitter fight over toward the Shitennō-ji, sent his son Daisuke again and again as a messenger,

{j} "Let the commander ride forth!"

{j} he entreated. When the commander goes forth, the soldiery is roused and puts out twice its strength. But Hideyori, though his enemy Ieyasu — at the age of seventy-odd — had come all the way from Sunpu Castle to stand at the head of his army in the field, never in the end came forth.

(It is like that.)

The place, too — Osaka Castle.

(A bad omen.)

{j} That was what he thought of.

Toshizō climbed the watchtower every day.

Right on the north side of this Magistrate's office — near enough to say under one's nose — stood the Gokō-no-miya.

There the Satsuma soldiers were quartered. Their commander was Shimazu Shikibu, a kinsman of the han's lord, with a strength of eight hundred.

The staff officer was Yoshii Tomozane (commonly called Kōsuke; later a privy councillor, and a count).

This Kōsuke Toshizō also knew — a sharp man of the Satsuma han, next after Saigō and Ōkubo, who had been at the work of scorning and toppling the shogunate from early on.

(I ought to have cut Kōsuke down.)

So Toshizō thought. But by the diplomatic policy of the shogunate and the Aizu han, they had been careful all along not to provoke the Satsuma party, and in the end he had not been able to cut him.

Should war come, it would be with these eight hundred Satsuma of the Gokō-no-miya that they first joined battle.

The Shinsengumi numbered one hundred and fifty.

Besides them, of the shogunal army lodging in this same Magistrate's office there were the thousand "shogunal infantry" under Jō Izumi-no-kami.

They were all men who wore baggy trousers, carried Western guns, and had been drilled in the French manner.

But they were not to be relied on.

Recruited from the common folk of Edo and Osaka, many of them were riffraff, and in ordinary times they would break into people's houses to plunder, and violate their daughters, and swagger about; but once it came to war, what then?

(Rely on your own strength — so reckon it.)

{j} So Toshizō had made up his mind. The Shinsengumi — and, boiled down, the twenty or so men who had shared hardship and ease from Edo through to Kyoto — those, in all likelihood, would fight like fury.

(When will it begin?)

Toshizō was by nature a man with an earth-colored face out of which only the eyes shone, but these last few days his color was wonderfully good.

He was a born lover of a fight.

And besides, even should he lose a battle or two, he had the belly to keep on fighting for a hundred years to come.

(You'll see, before long.)

Toshizō's heart, strangely, danced. What could it be? — an unaccountable joy welled up in him, that his own life was only now beginning. The boy Toshizō, who had spent his days from dawn to dusk in fights along the banks of the Tama River, was now about to fight one great, historic fight.

Perhaps it was the excitement of that.

Presently the year drew to its close, and a new year opened.

The first year of Meiji.
