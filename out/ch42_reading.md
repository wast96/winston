## Chapter 42

"—To find, in this world of mine, a friend of some taste and reflection, one with no bearing on the practical calculus of gain and loss—that is a hard thing; and just when I stood in need of such a one, Xu appeared in my life."

"It was, in truth, only a chance meeting, but it left a fine impression on me. The reason is that all my acquaintance runs between two extremes: on the one side the lofty, sacred 'business'; on the other the romantic, muddle-headed dealings, laughable and maddening at once. The first makes too much of me as a heroine, the second too much of me as a plaything. And so I have no life of my own—as though everyone who comes near me comes on some errand, either to set me a piece of 'business' or to buy me for their sport. And Xu, drawing near me now and then with no purpose whatever, became the very friend I had wanted.—"

My note: by "business" she means, of course, her work.

"—Stephen asked me out alone, and I refused him. He has, perhaps, a curious sort of love for me, thinking to find in me some exotic romance; but there he is wrong. I have no need of that romance, nor of that money."

"To thwart Stephen's designs on me, I deliberately made a show of drawing close to Xu; and it worked at once—Stephen seems to have yielded me up to Xu."

"Men are fragile things indeed: Xu has actually taken it into his head that I love him. How absurd."

"The cleverer the man, the greater a fool he is in things of this kind."

"To watch such a fool show off his love before me is an amusing enough thing."

—

—

"Mei Yingzi—I have long admired her, and she seems to give me much attention. In fact, I think there was a time when we met often at the dance halls; not a few of her Japanese friends were guests of mine, and at first I took her for a Japanese, though I learned later that she was not. But that she is one of my 'clientele' seems past all doubt. What a beautiful, lovely creature she is!"

"We have known each other this long while, yet only today were we formally introduced. How careful I must be in making a friend of her!"

"I was laughing to myself that she takes Xu for my lover—and now she, in her turn, has begun to draw him away."

"To seize this man for a screen…"

"And Xu will play the self-satisfied fool."

"Mrs. Stephen—stately, refined, gracious. And yet how ill-matched she is with Stephen! As I danced I made a show of great intimacy with Stephen, which put him a little ill at ease, though it did not ruffle Mrs. Stephen's composure in the least. She is truly an admirable, cultivated lady. She seems bent, with all her might, on breaking down Xu's bachelor creed."

…

My note: what she calls her "clientele" means, most likely, the enemy, or an adversary.

"Chance? Heaven's decree? Man's contrivance? It was as though we had long admired each other, and, meeting at last, wished to trade all we could; we settled on four days of merry company."

"The one I 'love' is Mei Yingzi; and, needless to say, the one Mei Yingzi 'loves' is I."

"What a foolish man!"

"Xu, for his part, is neither frivolous nor loose with me; I see the fine feeling in him, though at times he cannot help playing the clever one."

"Some people have love in them, and some have none. Xu is one who has love: he loves philosophy, he loves life, and it is for this that I think well of him."

"Xu is a Chinese, a man of cultivation, and his feeling for me too is of a noble kind; and in truth he is much to be pitied, so I resolved to warn him. But he actually supposed I was jealous—how absurd—and yet his refusal only roused my will to win."

"Perhaps Xu is not so much overthrown by Mei Yingzi's beauty as that he feels her mixing with the Japanese to be a pitiable, lamentable thing—just as I, out of pity for him, would draw him back."

"The way Mei Yingzi carries on in the night bars ought to grieve Xu, and yet it does not. And my counsel he will not take. Go on like this, and in the end he will simply be taken on as Mei Yingzi's disciple and turned into a ghost, nothing more."

…

…

"I could wish I were still naive enough to take Mei Yingzi's false sincerity for true. She is really too charming—not to speak of men; even I am quite conquered by her."

"But what does she mean by it—bidding me treasure Xu's life, urging me to love him, to follow him, to leave this place at his side? Does she know I am her adversary, and use Xu to draw me away from her? Or does she not know I am her adversary, and, pitying my lot, bid me find a dependable man before it is too late?"

"How I envy the ring Xu gave you, my dear; when I slipped it onto your lovely hand, I truly longed to be a man."

…

"…In the garden I thought to pick a flower for Xu. But he had already left a letter behind and gone. The reason cannot lie in the letter's words alone; to follow after him, I think, would be a thing to astonish Mei Yingzi—and I may well do it."

"To keep close to Mei Yingzi is part of my duty; but the ground here is to her advantage, and I must take the greater care."

"I am no more than a 'dance-hostess,' while she is a 'society lady.' This world is hers."

"When I reached the platform I saw that Xu had only just boarded; I got on and took a seat behind him."

"To leave us and go back to his own work—that is the right thing, and I ought to encourage him. For a man to have the resolve to walk away from the temptation of a woman like Mei Yingzi—he is either a fool or a great man."

…

…

"I have fallen ill. Illness is the frailest season of a life; in that moment loneliness, emptiness, and vexation all seize their chance to rise, and I become their prisoner!"

"At such a time I truly need a hand to lean on, need comfort, need love."

"Last night, after Xu had gone, he actually came back again with aspirin and fruit and the like—and this did move me."

"When one is weak oneself, one is easily moved to gratitude for another's help; and so gratitude, perhaps, is the act of the weak."

"And every clever man knows to press his attentions on a woman just when she is lonely, friendless, and laid low by illness."

"Xu, though—he has a noble heart!"

…

…

"…I am, after all, a weak-willed creature. There has risen in me toward Xu a feeling of respect and affection I cannot put into words. I had wanted such a friend only as an ornament to my life; but now I feel that my teasing and my flirting have been so many acts to draw him away from his own true life and toward the surface life of mine. In this respect he is still a child."

"I have resolved to encourage him, and to make something of him; he is, after all, a young man of promise."

"My words he received as an article of faith. I felt it a kind of glory."

…

…

"Mei Yingzi and I ought not to be so close and intimate; I mean to move house."

"E's flat is over-large; I thought to trade with her, which would answer very well, but as luck would have it she had sublet one room a few days before and taken a deposit on it, so the letting would have to be undone by negotiation."

"How strange—the tenant should turn out to be Xu. Did he take it for a friend? Yesterday he said he was going back to the country; so his aim is to sink out of sight into his work."

"His amusements of late, though fewer than before, still seem to cost Xu an effort; he loves his work above his pleasures, and that is a thing much to be admired."

"A wild fancy took me all at once: to tell Xu the truth of it and have him lodge at my place. Xu was delighted, and so the matter was settled.…"

"I would live vividly, or else die at peace."

"And here am I now, wounded, laid low in the hospital."

"Mei Yingzi has gone to stay at my place; luckily it is the tail of the cold season, and she cannot in any case room with Xu—so I shall watch quietly and wait upon what comes."

"I sent for W to come and see me, and we talked a long while."

…

My note: W must be a comrade of hers in the work.

…

"I am recovered now."

"I must have Xu move out; now that Mei Yingzi knows our address, his being there is in the way. Xu came to see me and told me that Mei Yingzi, though she had found out he was lodging at my place, was willing to help him keep it from Mrs. Stephen and Helen—which is just the sort of stroke I had looked for from Mei Yingzi."

"For one who toils, a spell of illness, a wound, seems the best of rests.…"

"—Xu seems to have no reason to be in Shanghai; out of the store I set by him, I urged him to leave this place. But he deliberately taxes me with my own not leaving—how absurd. It may be that Mei Yingzi has hinted something to him; or else he simply does not care to leave, and makes this his pretext. Does he really think to make me believe he is in love with me?—Mei Yingzi is forever working on me like this, on purpose.—"

"—I have begun to be busy, and have no strength to spare for Xu any longer; I have not seen him this long while.—"

"The war in the Pacific has broken out at last.—"

"I must live a calm life in the midst of turmoil, and a turbulent life in the midst of calm."

"In my life I must be composed within disquiet; in my living I must be disquieted within composure."

"People, people, people—a thousand kinds of people, ten thousand kinds; among the thousands and tens of thousands I bustle and whirl…"

…

"Now I know that Mei Yingzi has drawn Xu off into the life of the flesh and money, just as she drew Helen. She has him go shares with the Japanese merchants to make money, and so lures him into spending—spending on me."

"It seems Xu is slowly falling into her snare and coming to do her bidding."

"I have no leisure to pity Xu; there are far too many things I must strive at, must be busy with."

"X is dead!"

"Dancing with Xu, I brought up that X was dead; he pretended not to know."

"The gulf between friend and enemy is like that between numerator and denominator."

…

My note: X, I take it, was some puppet official assassinated about that time.

…

"How strange—it was Helen, of all people!"

"So this is how she drags Helen down into the sea, leaving a lovely girl no way of escape. I know the old bawd's stock tricks: the first step is temptation, the second is entrapment, and so the girl becomes her creature."

"My rescue of her sprang from a moment's righteous anger. Justice, after all, is the thing I love best."

"Poor child! In that instant I actually vowed to make myself her protector."

"But in the car I felt that I could not, in the end, keep watch over her forever; the playing-out of this tragedy was, from the first, only a matter of sooner or later. And so my taking her off the stage means nothing much."

"I resolved to give up one whole night to her, in the hope that her heart was a clever and a noble one."

"She has indeed a noble heart and a clever head; but the doing of it will still take resolve on her part."

"What a pitiable girl she is."

"Under a great age, every single soul is a small thing."

…

My note: the first "she" refers, of course, to Mei Yingzi.

…

"So he had moved over there long since? Why could he not have told me."

"'Hard at his studies'? The reason is that he no longer cares to be forever mixed up with us."

"Such a confirmed bachelor…"

"If Mei Yingzi knew of it, then it means she has already brought this hand to heel."

"Helen gives up her art, and seems not to know herself what a dreadful joke it is. He gives up his philosophy—but he knows the part he himself is playing."

"For lust? For gain? For both—that is a man.…"

…

"I carry all the mystery on my own shoulders, and let you fix your eyes on me, while I hand the most secret thing of all to the one you never notice."

"A great win at the tables, a deep drunkenness on wine—and for a moment I felt the pleasure of retiring early with the work well done; but…"

"There was no second person there; Mrs. Manfield was at my side, and I asked her what had become of my purse."

"So, my friend, it was you playing a joke on me after all—you who stole away my 'money'!"

"And now—this is proof enough!"

"She has won; it has all gone as foreseen—from the lure of the flesh and money to the selling of body and soul."

"W and the others suppose that the 'banquet' calls for no 'appearance' of mine in person, and do not believe I have the nerve to come to the feast. And why should I not have the nerve?"

…

My note: by "money" she means, of course, that document; by "the banquet," the plan to kill me; by "appearing in person," carrying it out with her own hand.

"My hand shook, and I played the wrong 'card'; I truly wondered why he did not cry out."

"He actually told me to go—a character so great; why should it be…? Then he has been made a 'harlot' without knowing it himself; he vouches for himself, and I believe him—he would not lie."

"Drunk with wine! I went over to hold him up. For the first time I saw that here was a lovable life—but we might be parting forever, and I kissed him! Am I in love with him?"

"At my utmost speed I sent for Dr. Philip.…"

My note: "playing the wrong card" must mean that the shot did not kill; "drunk with wine," that he was struck down wounded; "harlot," a puppet made use of by others.

"—I hear he has already been taken into Gaolang; I wired Dr. Philip, who says the fall was too severe—nine chances in ten he will be crippled."

"Then with what am I to make my atonement?"

"If I may live to see peace come, then I would marry you, nurse you, serve you out my few days—is this a person's destiny?"

"Strange, this fancy—does it spring from the love of the greater self, or from what men call being in love? I turned it over a long while, and found it to be the will to win of the personality, a self-respecting feeling of the purer kind."

"May the victory fall to that one-tenth.—"

My note: "the fall was too severe" must mean he was wounded too gravely; "falling to the one-tenth" is said with reference to the nine-tenths chance of his being crippled.

"—She never dreamed that I still had 'matches' hidden under my mattress; I struck a light and turned it on her—today I mean to admire this young lady at my leisure."

"She was my sister all along, and all these many days we have treated each other as rivals in love!"

"In that instant my tears came; I held her, I kissed her, and only then did I see that I had loved her from the very first meeting."

"Hereafter I shall have a stronger light; I mean to astonish her."

"What a brilliant life, what a shining road ahead!"

"I held her long, and wept long; this meeting has come too cruelly late."

"But ahead—love lies ahead, the dream lies ahead, beauty lies ahead, the light lies ahead."

"I hear he has indeed won that one-tenth victory—heaven be thanked! Everything of mine is saved; and yet I still have no face to look upon this brilliant friend."

"I only hope he may come home soon, both his arms stretched out and lively again!—"

My note: "matches" must mean the pistol—she is speaking of how, under the covers, she secretly drew a pistol from beneath the mattress and leveled it at Mei Yingzi. The "sister" that follows means, of course, a friend among the allied nations. "Both his arms stretched out and lively" means, of course, that I would leave the hospital without being crippled.
