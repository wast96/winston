## Chapter 10. Fallen Leaves Return to the Root, Seen Through to the End

The Pacification Corps, from its founding to its reorganization, lasted all told less than two years; and this at a time when the Communist rebellion had turned sharper and the fires of war spread across the whole sky. If one is to speak of its successes and failures, its gains and losses—not shutting the door to talk to oneself, not closing one’s eyes in self-satisfaction, and without troubling over whether the words are becomingly put—then the plain conclusion must be this: the conception had not yet fully ripened before it was hastily put into practice, and so, of course, it could not turn out wholly as one had wished.

The original design differed considerably from the actual conditions, and so was hard to fit to them; and where things were altered on the spur of the moment, there was the failing of a disordered pace.

Suppression and pacification are not a mass movement; and the situation we faced was, moreover, one never seen before, so that both method and manner became matters demanding every effort to adapt.

The demands laid on the work were too high, the tasks assigned too heavy, and yet no regard was paid to the limits of the cadres’ abilities—and men are not omnipotent.

It cannot be said that this unit accomplished nothing at all in the war of suppression; only that the part it played fell truly short of what had at first been looked for.

The writer, being one of its members, could hardly stand aside; and one might say, too, that it is not as though I had discharged in full the duty that was mine to discharge, and so am without regret.

Among the whole record, what is worth setting down lies also in the honesty, the loyal courage, the sacrifice, and the devotion to duty of all the students; besides which the book holds, too, a few true stories that others have not told—incomplete though they be, yet precious all the same. At the same time, it points, through the facts themselves, to the cruelty and inhumanity of the Communist Party. In December of the thirty-seventh year, the First Pacification Brigade, ordered south, set out from Beiping, and over ten thousand li of pass and mountain fought its way along the whole road, reaching Magong and going ashore only in September of the thirty-eighth year.

In the forty years since, the students, struggling on their own, have each in turn made something of themselves; and so I say that we are, all of us, the fortunate ones of a turbulent age.

Today, though the students are all past sixty and looking toward seventy, their high spirit is undimmed from what it was; and whenever they gather, after they have talked their fill of old times, they must always shout out a few times, “The brigade commander is leading us back to fight our way into Beiping!” before they feel they have had their fill. Near to a jest though the words are, they serve well enough to show a resolve against the Communists that will never change its nature, and a spirit of dauntless courage.

### 1. Impulse and Rashness, Nearly Off the Rails
It was the first ten days of the first month of the thirty-eighth year, and the state of this unit of ours was this: the main body of the brigade, led by Deputy Brigade Commander Li, had already left Beiping and reached Zhejiang, and was on the road, marching toward Ningbo.

The “stay-behind personnel,” in some scores of units, single-form and compound, had each taken up its own post and gone into hiding across the Beiping-Tianjin region; for the moment nothing was asked of them by way of work, lest their identities be exposed.

The brigade headquarters’ caretaker party, some fifty-odd men, were still in Fuxue Lane in Beiping, waiting for aircraft, in hopes of rejoining the unit at an early day.

I myself, having flown with Mr. Zheng Jiemin to Nanjing, stayed but a short while before going on to Shanghai.

The headquarters of the “Pacification Corps” was not in Nanjing; it had probably already been moved to Zhejiang and was in the midst of reorganization.

What came after this I shall tell along three separate threads, beginning first with myself.—When Mr. Zheng bade me leave Beiping ahead of the rest with him by aircraft, there were only some ten-odd hours left before the next morning’s take-off. There was no little to be made ready and settled; yet I went all the same to see once more my two schoolfellows and old friends, Jiang Tian and Zhang Zuoxing.

Jiang Tian and Zhang Zuoxing had both, on my recommendation, been appointed commanding officers of this unit of ours, though neither did especially well at it. The three of us had together laid a design, taking as our targets Lin Biao (commander of the “Northeast People’s Liberation Army”) and Tao Zhu (director of its political department), and working through our ties as Military Academy schoolfellows and personal friendship, by way of the go-between offices of yet another Academy schoolfellow, Li Mingqiu; but beyond the gathering of some few pieces of intelligence, the line—with the death by illness of our schoolfellow Li Mingqiu, and the sudden turn of the Northeast situation—could never be joined up again (see Chapter Three above).

After the two men, Jiang Tian and Zhang Zuoxing, were relieved of their posts as commanding officers, their ties with the unit grew loose and uncertain; and though I cannot say they had already turned disloyal, there was no avoiding a certain estrangement of heart. In this Jiang Tian was the worse: more than once he let me know that he had a great mind to go off to the Jinzhou–Shanhaiguan country to seek out Lin Biao—though now his purpose was a far cry from what it had been before.

My reason for going once more to see Jiang Tian and Zhang Zuoxing was, first, to take my leave; and second, that I still harbored the wish to persuade them to quit Beiping and come south, and so to do all that friendship asked.

And so, without waiting to return first to brigade headquarters, I called a pedicab and came to the Jiang house.

Brother Jiang Tian, hearing that I was about to leave, pressed me hard to stay to supper and see me off; and at the same time he telephoned and had Brother Zuoxing brought over too, so that we might talk a while and be together a while—for all of us understood that when next we might meet was past all knowing.

Wine can neither add to the warmth or accord between men, nor shake the stubbornness that certain men cling to; and so the eating and the drinking could serve only as an ornament to the evening.

As we talked of everything under the sun, I proposed once more that the two of them keep in touch at all times with brigade headquarters, so that when aircraft came we might all leave together. Brother Zuoxing was neither for it nor against; but Brother Jiang Tian declared flatly that he would on no account go.

Something in Brother Jiang Tian’s words let slip a hint that he had already reached some degree of tacit understanding with Lin Biao—or had, say, been in communication with him—so that he turned it about and urged me instead to stay and not to leave at all.

To argue now over who was right and who was wrong would plainly yield no conclusion; and so there was nothing for it but, in low spirits, to go our separate ways.

After Beiping fell to the bandits, I knew nothing of the particulars of what became of Jiang Tian and Zhang Zuoxing, nor whether they ever met with Lin Biao, Tao Zhu, Li Yunchang and the rest. In the forty-first year of the Republic I met, on a Hong Kong street, an old acquaintance who had just come out of Beiping and who was, as it happened, the fellow townsman closest to the two of them. So far as he knew, Jiang Tian had been placed in a “children’s school” attached to the puppet Ministry of Railways, styled a “clerk” though in truth little different from a workman. Had he been able to live out his days so, it would have been his good fortune; but instead, in the time of the so-called “Suppression of Counter-Revolutionaries,” he was, with no word allowed him, dragged off and shot. The crime proclaimed against him was that within the “0760” unit he “had once made himself an enemy of the people.” If Jiang Tian ever regretted his choice to stay, it can only have been in the instant before his execution. Zhang Zuoxing had all the while lived idle in Beiping; in the atrocities of the so-called “Suppression of Counter-Revolutionaries” he too could not escape, and on the same charge was seized and publicly tried. Because he was “of proletarian origin” (he had once been a worker at the Changxindian railway station), the sentence handed down was a “death sentence, suspended,” and he was sent to the “Chading Farm” near Tangshan for “reform through labor” first, his life or death to be settled later as circumstances should decide.

Whether out of a large-hearted ease or out of sheer helplessness I cannot say, but at an evening entertainment at the “Chading Farm” Brother Zhang Zuoxing even took the stage in paint and costume to play the painted-face role of Liu Jin, singing away at “The Famen Temple” with its great trial scene.

All the above the old acquaintance told me with his own mouth, and it can surely be no falsehood. Later the great earthquake struck Tangshan, and one must suppose the odds were more ill than good; but if his life was hardy enough that he did not die, Brother Zuoxing would this year (the seventy-sixth) be eighty-two.

Old things told over deepen one’s sorrow, and are worth taking warning from as well. But to return to my tale: when I reached Nanjing, Mr. Zheng pressed me to rejoin the unit at once. He said that for the present he would likely have no time to meet; though perhaps he would make a trip to Shanghai, and we might be in touch again then.

A friend, before I boarded the aircraft in Beiping, had entrusted to me a pistol to carry on my person; this I delivered to the residence of that general, and so at last discharged the errand. Then, alone, I took a room at the Central Hotel, meaning to sleep a good night and go on to Shanghai the next day.

The Central Hotel had once been among the very first of Nanjing’s great hostelries; now, by some means I cannot fathom, it had turned filthy and squalid. The weather was cold, and because there were guests lodging there a brazier of charcoal had been set in the room; the charcoal was half-cured, and now and again a stick or two, not fully caught, would send off white smoke, and the smell of it was truly hard to bear. Out of sheer exhaustion I lay down and, without knowing it, “fell asleep”!

Once more fortune came down upon me: when I woke, I was lying sprawled on the cement floor of the corridor. Had I died of the poison, I should not have had so much as the chance to cry my wrong. How near a thing it was!

By this time the “Pacification Corps headquarters” was no longer in Nanjing, and the center of gravity of the “Baomiju” had shifted, too, to its “Nanyang Road office” in Shanghai. This was also the moment when the Xu-Bang Campaign was going ill point by point, giving one a feeling of a heavy air and a bleak scene. When I reached Shanghai and made inquiry, the unit had already moved off toward Ningbo. In passing through Shanghai, they had, as it happened, left behind the Ford I had borrowed. Just then my purse was tight, and so I traded that motorcar for two bales of cotton yarn; and selling the yarn in turn, I came at last by a sum of money. This was both my living expenses and my traveling money for the whole road to Ningbo. And why did I dare to sell a borrowed car? Because its owner was none other than my old friend Brother Bai Shiwei.

There were, in the unit, some who had not come up through the “Lizhi Class” and who, staying on in Shanghai, had no wish to serve further; I sought out one of these, a Brother Wu, and prevailed on him to go with me to Ningbo to rejoin the unit. If he did not care to serve, no matter—he might see me to Ningbo and then return. We went first by train to Hangzhou; but how we got on from there to Ningbo I can no longer recall.

Here I must say over again that, until I reached Ningbo, I truly did not know that the “Pacification Corps” had already been reorganized; I still meant to go on—full of ardor, single of heart—doing that work of mine as “brigade commander.”

Brother Wu, who had come with me, so as to keep himself free to advance or withdraw, was against going straight to the unit. We took a hotel room first, ate a meal out, and then I went ahead alone to “learn how matters stood.”

I no longer remember who it was that told me; but the moment I heard, a blaze of anger shot up in me, and I turned on my heel and went, hurrying back to the hotel, ready to return at once to Hangzhou and have done with it all.

Brother Wu, who was with me, asked what vexing news I had heard, and why I was in so great a rage. I said, “Beforehand they never sought my opinion, and afterward they never so much as told me of the transfer; without a word they moved me out of my post—and this when I had willingly stayed on in Beiping at hazard to lay the groundwork for the work to come. Is not such conduct of theirs a gross want of respect for a man?” I spoke a long while, yet my friend Brother Wu, who had come with me, did not wholly grasp what it was all about; he looked at me, waiting for me to go on.

My anger unspent, I went on to tell him: “An order has already been published, transferring me to be a deputy corps commander in name without substance, and my old post of brigade commander has been taken over by Li Yulin. In itself this is nothing to be angry at; what galls me is that in all reason they ought to have waited for my return before publishing it. Since they set my very existence at naught, what savor is there in serving on?” He was, of course, sorry for me, and naturally did not oppose my leaving in indignation. But by now the sky was late, no car or boat was to be had, it was too late to return to Hangzhou—and yet in my heart I could scarce bear to stay a moment. What was to be done? As I wavered, he bade me be a little calm and not fret, and let him go and think of some plan.

After about half an hour he came back in high spirits. Sure enough, he had got hold of a medium jeep from brigade headquarters, and only so did we return through the night to Hangzhou.

In Hangzhou I had no kin or friend to turn to, and could only put up at a hotel for the time being. But lodging costs money, and eating costs money; a day or two later, though I had come to my senses and saw that this was no way to go on, I was already sunk in a fix and could not so much as afford the fare to Shanghai.

After talking it over with my companion, it fell to him to go once more to Ningbo to find the quartermaster and reckon up the pay and rations. The quartermaster, Nie Enjun, came back together with him. After the pay was settled and the advances deducted, little enough remained; but, past all expectation, Nie Enjun had long since made ready several taels of gold, which he handed me to use first, saying it was his own private money and might be repaid another day. This too is a thing not to be forgotten.

It was when Quartermaster Nie was settling my pay with me, and explained that my name was no longer borne on the rolls of the First Brigade but had been moved to those of the corps headquarters, that I came clearly to know: the “Pacification Corps” had been reorganized into the “Second Corps of the Youth National-Salvation Corps,” with Mr. Hu Gui as its director-general, Mr. Liu Peichu reassigned as commander of the “Second Corps,” and myself one of the deputy commanders of the “Second Corps”—that is, there were one or two other deputy commanders besides. Brother Li Yulin, for his part, was commander of the “First Brigade of the Second Corps.”

In this reorganization, besides the “Pacification Corps” there were also the “Suppression-and-Reconstruction Corps” and the “People’s Service Corps,” so that it looked to be a thing done across the board, and no piecemeal reshuffling of personnel. That being so, my flash of temper had been rash indeed. Reasonably considered, the height of one’s post and whether it be substantive are matters worth minding, to be sure; but the fitness of the nature of the work matters more still. Given the state of things after the reorganization, I could of course not have carried on; yet I ought to have parted on good terms and with some grace, and had no need to turn on my heel and go—and all the more so as I still bore the standing of a soldier!

The first thing now to be done was to report to Mr. Zheng Jiemin the circumstances of my leaving the post, and to fix my course only after I had his instructions.

In Shanghai I saw Mr. Zheng, and no sooner had we begun to talk than I found that he too seemed beset by a host of troubles of his own. To me he said only the one sentence, “Better go first to Taiwan,” and gave no further direction. Very well—Taiwan was to be gone to in any case; and I could but hope that, with luck, my wife and children, scattered here and there in the keeping of kin and friends, might all come safe together in Taiwan. In the second and third months of the thirty-eighth year, drifting about Shanghai, I was, strictly speaking, no more than a “straggler.” Yet in those days men like myself, half-baked things neither officer nor civilian, must surely have been many.

Having no post of duty, I was of course under no restraint; and having lived a taut life for twenty years, why not let myself go slack a while?

Here I must make one thing plain: I was not disheartened, nor did I think the road ahead was dark; it was, as I have said above, only that I wished to snatch a short “idleness,” free of all constraint. Beyond that—my taste for play was still too strong.

A stout fellow without money finds it hard going everywhere, and nowhere more than in this place called Shanghai. Luckily I was lodging in the house of Fan Xing (see Part Four, Chapter Twelve), and so had no need to spend at first. Each day, as if going to and from an office, I would first go and sit a while in the writing-room of my good friend Sun Shilin on Zhongyang Road, read the papers, drink tea, and chat idly of this and that. When noon came, three or five of us would go together to eat at some local eating-house, warm a pot or two of old wine, and lay out a few little dishes of relish—what a fine thing it was! In that great building on Zhongyang Road there were two or three stock brokerages, with no small coming and going of people every day. The proprietor of one of them was well acquainted with Mr. Sun, and there was a little friendship between them; a word of introduction from Mr. Sun, and one could open an account and trade without laying out a single copper of capital. There is no need to hide it: I am by nature fond of gambling, and with a ready field before me, why not have a try? So I opened an account—without so much as paying a deposit—and set to speculating.

The rise and fall of stocks is bound, of course, to the political situation; and Shanghai at this hour was already tossing in wind and rain. Public order was still barely kept up, but men’s hearts were greatly unsettled; and in such a case, how could the market be looked on with hope? So I laid my whole stake on the “short” side—and who could have known that within minutes of the opening it would hit the day’s ceiling, and so every day, several days running turning up the same, forever climbing and never stopping.

The brokerage’s proprietor could hold out no longer, and through Mr. Sun conveyed to me the gentle word, “Cut your losses”—or else, “settle up and then go on.” To “settle up” meant to reckon the accounts clear; and when the reckoning was done—good heavens—I was thrown into a fright past all describing, and fell into sore straits.

A blunder once made must somehow be put right: I could neither drag my friend into it nor slap my backside and walk away. After much going back and forth, I got a minor figure of the streets—a man of some small fame—to come forward and speak this way and that; and it was he who advanced a sum on my behalf and gave it to the brokerage to “have done with the matter.” The sum was no more than a tenth or a fifth of what was really owed. This man and I were no close friends; as for our tie, we had once been fellow inmates in trouble together at “Tilanqiao.”

Trifling as the affair was, it yet threw into relief a few things. First: this was all trouble brought on by my own overreaching, and it showed too a “greed” I had still not managed to be rid of.

Second: Shanghai’s social order was a thing apart—have a “name and standing,” and one “gets on” everywhere. This same fellow, after the Chinese Communists usurped Shanghai, went on to Hong Kong; and because he had changed his ground, nothing he said carried any “magic” there any longer.

Third: what I turned over in my mind a hundred times and never could fathom, what remained an utter mystery, was this—with the Communists plainly about to bring their ruin down upon Shanghai, when neither life nor property could be kept safe, by what reason did the stocks, far from falling, rise, and hit the day’s ceiling every single day?

The whole business was a case of reaching for the chicken and losing the rice, and not much to my credit either: what had begun so leisurely and pleasantly turned, by this, to a grimy-faced disgrace—truly it was not worth it.

One day the “Nanyang Road office” sent someone to tell me that the director, Mr. Mao Renfeng, asked me to come by—though without saying what it was about. I could of course not fail to go. Truly, in those days, to hear that someone had business with me was to feel a certain fear at heart, and only when the matter was made plain could I set my mind at ease. This, perhaps, is just the never-quiet heart of one who does secret-service work.

Mr. Mao Renfeng had sent for me, it turned out, meaning to promote me to be chief of the “Qingdao station”; but a stranger both to the men and to the ground, I could not do it, and did not see fit to accept. As for what Mr. Mao made of this, there was nothing for it but to leave him to his own thoughts.

As things stood, no good was to come of staying long in Shanghai; better to go to Taiwan a little sooner. It shames me to tell it, but I first borrowed ten Yuan big-heads from Brother Li Guangyuan (Tu Wei) for traveling money; and then, through Hu Yong’an, who found his nephew (a son of Hu Yongquan, serving as purser aboard ship), I was got onto the SS Haiqian, and without ever buying a ticket, muddled my way to Keelung.

Once ashore, guided by others, I took the train to Taipei and learned that my family was living on Pucheng Street. It turned out that Brother Bai Shiwei had settled a small Japanese-style house upon me, and my wife and children had already been living there some good while.

### 2. Bearing Boundless Hardship, Bravely Staying On
To turn the subject about, I shall now set down how those several score of working comrades who stayed behind in Beiping made their withdrawal.

After I left Beiping, the business and affairs of the stay-behind personnel were handed over to the charge of brigade adjutant Zhang Luying. The passage that follows is the precious material supplied by Brother Luying under the title “The Course of the Withdrawal of the Working Comrades Who Stayed in Beiping.”

At that time the Beiping-Tianjin situation was pressing hard, Beiping was besieged, and all traffic with the outside was wholly cut off. If one must leave Beiping, there was but the single road of air transport to depend on. But the Xiyuan airfield had already been seized by the bandit army, and civil aviation had long since stopped flying; the one place that could still barely keep up a small volume of air transport was the “Temple of Heaven temporary airfield,” and its capacity was limited, and it had moreover been taken under military control.

The case was urgent, and rear-guard units like ours, eager to leave Beiping, were past all counting; whether a man got away or not was left to each to show what powers he had. To describe it by the phrase “each vying to be first and dreading to be last” could not be more apt.

The background being explained as above, below is Brother Luying’s own text:—In the early winter of the thirty-seventh year of the Republic, with the war going against us, the brigade was ordered south, and provision was made in advance for the disposition of the stay-behind personnel. The southbound troops, once formed up for the march, were led by Deputy Brigade Commander Li Yulin down by the Beiping-Tianjin line, and by way of the Tanggu seaport took ship southward. Brigade Commander Mr. Chen, with the necessary personnel, remained on in Beiping to carry forward the “stay-behind work.” By the time the disposition had reached a stopping-point, the Beiping-Tianjin railway had already, before this, been cut, and Beiping had fallen into isolation.

The Nanyuan and Xiyuan airfields, under the pressing approach of the bandit army, were abandoned; thereafter the drill ground at Dongdan Pailou within the city was repaired for aircraft to land and take off, but because the runway was too short it could serve only small aircraft. Later, though the Temple of Heaven temporary airfield was built as well, it too was from time to time harassed by the bandit army’s gunfire.

At the beginning of the first month of the next year, when Brigade Commander Mr. Chen left Beiping with Mr. Zheng Jiemin, he charged Luying to look after the stay-behind personnel and contrive to get them out of danger and back to the unit. Having received this charge, the measures I took and the course of the handling were roughly as follows: I called the stay-behind comrades together, gave a brief report on the present situation, and bade each hold to the duty of his own post, keeping severally in his charge the equipment, arms and ammunition, and all public property; I laid down rules of conduct, and enjoined that close touch be kept with unit headquarters at all times.

I pressed the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters” vigorously for aircraft, so that we might leave Beiping as soon as could be and go south to rejoin the unit.

I kept, too, in close and constant touch with the Second and Third Sections of the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters,” and we passed each other reports of the bandit situation.

Afterward the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters” (over the drafting of its Logistics Section) replied by letter that no aircraft could be spared. So I sought another way, applying directly to the Second Regional Command of the Air Force, attaching a roster of personnel and an inventory of equipment, and asking that aircraft be sent to carry us. At that time the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters,” to keep control of Beiping’s air transport with the outside, had stationed a liaison staff officer, Lieutenant Colonel Li Haokun, at the Second Regional Command; and Lieutenant Colonel Li Haokun had been a schoolfellow of mine in the Staff Class of the Army War College, so that by this tie I asked, as a favor, for his strong help. He then represented to the Air Force, in Commander-in-Chief Fu’s name, that word had been given by mouth that this body of Central personnel was to be given priority in withdrawal.

The situation grew tenser by the day, and still there was no sure word of aircraft; the comrades were beside themselves with anxiety. After assembling and deliberating, it was decided: (1) that the cash on hand in the unit (silver “big-heads”) be divided equally among the comrades to keep against all contingencies—but with it made plain that this money was for the purchase of air tickets and for transport expenses, and might not be spent on one’s own account; failing which, should one thereby be unable to make the journey, one must bear the responsibility oneself.

(2) that those comrades not lodging at brigade headquarters have their addresses, telephones, or means of contact duly registered, and be formed into small groups by nearness of address, keeping close mutual touch, against any urgent notice.

(3) that the equipment, arms and ammunition, and articles in each comrade’s charge be carried by each on his own responsibility.

In the middle to late part of the first month of the thirty-eighth year, word came suddenly that Fu Zuoyi’s troops had begun moving outside the city, and it was rumored that the Chinese Communists’ advance organs and working personnel had already entered Beiping; I knew then that the situation had changed. On a certain day—the exact date I no longer remember, but it seems to have been three or four days before the old-calendar New Year (the writer’s note: it should have been about the twenty-second of the first month of the thirty-eighth year)—a little after nine in the evening, I had a telephone message from Staff Officer Li Haokun of the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters” that there would be aircraft the next day, and that we must without fail be at the Air Transport Section by six the next morning to see to the boarding formalities.

At that time Beiping was under curfew each night from eight until five the next morning; and when Staff Officer Li’s telephone word reached me, there was already no passing through the streets. I could only notify the comrades severally by telephone that they must without fail reach brigade headquarters on time, and, once gathered together, set out all together.

Some of the comrades reported back that the money had already been spent, so that if the air tickets required payment there was truly no way to meet it—and this became a problem. So again I begged Staff Officer Li over the telephone to help to the end, arranging with the Air Force unit to allow the passage to be booked on account and shipped, the brigade to give a note for it and ask that the Air Force Command pass it on to the Ministry of National Defense for settlement. A little after six the next morning, the comrades and their dependents, some fifty in all, reached the Air Transport Section and saw to the formalities; Staff Officer Li Haokun came in person to see to it, and we boarded two aircraft severally (one filled full, the other shared half-and-half with another unit), taking off from the Temple of Heaven temporary airfield, our objective Qingdao.

Before the take-off it was found that the radio operator Wu Shangyou had not managed to get there in time; only after all the formalities were done and everyone had boarded did Comrade Wu arrive, and though it was taken up with the Air Transport Section, there being no vacant seat and no overloading allowed, he was permitted only to change to a later flight. The day after we reached Qingdao, we saw on the Qingdao streets several of the Air Transport Section men who had once handled our boarding formalities, and from this we learned that among our own people the fortunate had already withdrawn by air, while the unfortunate could no longer get out at all.

After reaching Qingdao, we first sought lodging; but the town was in confusion and the hotels full, so that we could no longer keep together, and each comrade had to find a place to stay for the time being, while a few comrades were appointed to contrive to arrange sea passage on to Shanghai.

Because each man’s means of payment differed—some could still hold out, some were already without a copper—we thought to ask the higher command for funds to help us; but we did not even know where the “Corps headquarters” was, and truly there was a feeling of having nowhere to turn to borrow.

With no date for sea passage, and no good in all of us wearing the days away, it was agreed after discussion that men might act singly and go south on their own to rejoin the unit.

On further recollection, a few points to add: (1) The radio operator Wu Shangyou may have had no chance to get out—a thing to be regretted.

(2) The cash (silver “big-heads”) divided among the comrades at Beiping in the crisis was come by thus: after the brigade commander left Beiping, when Luying called a meeting requiring each to complete the withdrawal preparations for the matters in his charge, the quartermaster put forward the idea of whether the flour in the unit’s store might be sold off; and this, after all had discussed and agreed to it, was so dealt with.

(3) Of those who boarded the aircraft, apart from the working comrades of this unit and their dependents, the only persons not of this unit were Mr. Zheng Enpu and his party of two.—The above is the account of “The Course of the Withdrawal of the Working Comrades Who Stayed in Beiping,” as set down for me by Brother Zhang Luying.

I shall now, from what I myself have heard and know, add some further explanation below.

Had Brother Luying not told me to my face, then for these several decades the students of the unit, myself among them, would have had no clear idea at all of how the aircraft came, and how they could hold more than fifty men at one time. This was too hard—harder than one can imagine.

Some may suppose these were sent by the Ministry of National Defense for our sole and appointed use; but it was not so, for at that time small units like ours were far, far too many, and which of them did not wish to withdraw in time?

It is said that those anxious to leave Beiping at the time were in their thousands and tens of thousands; to escape the danger, they schemed by every means to come by a seat, and even men of no great wealth would empty the bottom of their trunks, calling on connections and working their way through back doors to buy a single “flight.” As for the wealthy, they thought nothing even of thirty or fifty taels of gold. Such a thing, in the past, was common enough whenever disaster drew near, and no wonder; but this time it was the worst of all—one sees by it how precious safety is.

There is another thing I do not understand: the aircraft belonged to the government, and we too were troops belonging to the government, ordered to withdraw in emergency—so why, to ride the aircraft, was “money” still “wanted”? This question I never asked Brother Luying about in detail; I remember only that he once told me that, because none of us had money—or, having it, had not enough—a “promissory note” was made out in the unit’s name, to be passed by the Air Force Command to the Ministry of National Defense for settlement, and so the thing was smoothed over. Brother Luying also mentioned that, apart from the unit, the only ones who took the aircraft on this journey were Mr. Zheng Enpu and his party of two. Zheng Enpu, too, was a friend of mine; a small part of his doings I have set down in Chapter Three of the first part of my book, “Rooting Out Traitors in the North.” What I would tell here is the grievous matter of his being unable, when he left Beiping, to bring his wife (Miss Yang) away with him. Though Brother Luying said only “Mr. Zheng Enpu and his party of two,” without naming who went with him, I know that the other was not Mrs. Zheng. Around the fiftieth year of the Republic I had dealings from time to time with Third Master Zheng, and whenever Miss Yang was spoken of, he would rue that he had not brought her out! His longing for her overflowed his words.

Third Master Zheng was an upright man who kept to his station; in point of moral cultivation his friends all raised their thumbs in praise of him one and all. Take this very matter of catching the aircraft in Beiping: because the seats were few, he chose rather to leave together with the working comrades than to bring his own household—and by this one thing alone his character may be seen. In Taiwan he lived alone, a solitary man; when his friends urged him to take a wife to grow old with, he would not, and for years kept to a vegetarian diet in her memory. Not long after, he took refuge in the gate of Buddhism, left the world, and became a monk—and, to put it plainly, he was a monk of accomplishment: first he planted fruit trees on the hills at Beitou, and afterward built a temple halfway up the slope, and there at the last he entered nirvana. After Mr. Zheng’s death, his lay disciples—among them Tian Yingjie, Mei Changling and others, likewise schoolfellows of the “Lizhi Class”—pooled their strength and funds to build for him within the temple a pagoda for his relics, to show that he would be remembered forever and to console his valiant soul. As for Mrs. Zheng, trapped on the mainland, no word of her was ever heard from first to last.

To return: when our brigade headquarters’ stay-behind personnel reached Qingdao, though they had none to lean on, not one of them was left stranded there; by what means I cannot make out, they came—singly, in twos and threes, one after another—to Shanghai. Of these, all who had come up through the “Lizhi Class” went to Ningbo to rejoin the unit; a few who had merely enlisted with the unit stayed on in Shanghai.

Since the “stay-behind work” has been mentioned above more than once, and since it has been told, too, how many were anxious to leave Beiping and flee the calamity, I cannot but be moved to reflection. Though it is said that the “stay-behind workers” were all volunteers, yet how could they not have known how vague and how hard the days to come would be, and how heavy the burden laid on their shoulders? Only think: without a courage beyond the common, such a thing could surely never be borne.

Men are much of a muchness; whence, then, this more-than-human courage? It was woven together of the conviction against Communism, of a firm will, of a sense of mission—or say, of a sense of duty. This it was that made their special quality.

At that time they may have thought that before long the national army would return; none could have foreseen that once it passed it would be forty years—and in a whole life, how many forty-years are there? And so I would pray to Heaven above for their braving of danger and hardship and their long endurance of bitterness; and still more would I extol as great their loyal courage in their country’s service, their sacrifice and their giving.

What follows is the supplementary account of Brother Yuanshen: In the latter half of the thirty-seventh year, the Beiping-Tianjin war turned wholly against us, and the thing was already past doing. I was transferred from my post in the First Command Room at Zhuoxian and hurried back to Beiping to give my whole effort to helping Mr. Chen with the disposition of the “stay-behind work.” By that time our own unit had already gone south ahead, under the charge of Deputy Brigade Commander Li Yulin. Before long Beiping was besieged: the national army and Fu Zuoyi’s own direct-line troops, nearly 200,000 strong, held fast within the city, keeping their heads down and not coming out. At unit headquarters in Fuxue Lane there remained only Mr. Chen, brigade adjutant Zhang Luying, and myself, together with staff, clerical, and stay-behind personnel under preparation, some fifty-odd men. Almost every day we telegraphed to Nanjing, begging that aircraft be sent to rescue us, but never with any result. As the case looked, there was nothing for it but to “sit and await death.”

Mr. Zheng Jiemin’s last flight by special aircraft to Beiping, to convey the Leader’s instructions to Fu Zuoyi, was already a matter of the first month of that year. After Mr. Chen had had his audience with Mr. Jiemin, he came back and told me: “When it came to the question of getting out, Mr. Zheng’s special aircraft was already packed full of military and political notables on the return trip, and he bade me squeeze in, the one man, as best I could. I asked whether one more might be added; he said there was absolutely no room. My thought was this: if we were to go, the two of us would first go together; and if you could not get away, then neither would I.” These words moved me deeply. I said, “There is no need for that; no good comes of your staying here—should you fail to get away, you would be sacrificed for nothing, and we should but perish together. Better that you go back first to Nanjing and press in person, directly, for aircraft to be sent quickly to the rescue; otherwise, though we send ten telegrams a day from here, it will be of no use.” After urging and arguing with him again and again, Mr. Chen agreed at last to go first, alone. Though the stay-behind work was on the whole already brought to completion, the many technical questions that remained, and the final settling of personnel matters, all fell upon my shoulders alone. Brother Zhang Luying and I kept unit headquarters; Luying had charge of general administration and liaison, while I dealt with all the unfinished business bearing on the stay-behind work. We were busy day and night, and often could not sleep of an evening.

I gathered in and concentrated all the light weapons on hand—submachine guns and Canadian pistols, more than ten in all, with a quantity of ammunition—and sent them to be kept in the house of a Mr. X, a good friend of Mr. Chen’s and also a kinsman of Mr. Zhang Zuoxing’s.

By this time the common people within the city of Beiping went about their daily round as usual, but the Communist Party’s spies, its fellow-travelers, and those politicians and literary men who cared nothing for life or death grew active without the least restraint. They stirred the workers to strike and the students to boycott their classes, and carried out open demonstrations and marches, posting slogans and shouting watchwords, so that a tide of anti-government clamor filled the whole city. On every thoroughfare great-charactered slogans were written in ink on the walls—such as “Down with so-and-so,” “All under Heaven too poor,” “Ten thousand taxes for the Republic”…—the characters a full ten feet, meeting the eye everywhere. When the Bandit-Suppression Headquarters and the public-security authorities saw that things had grown past all decency, they could not but step in and intervene, arresting on the spot several of the leaders of the class boycott; but early the next morning all the city’s leftist papers with one voice raised an outcry of denunciation and fierce opposition. And when, above all, the Communists’ high-placed inside men—such as the Beiping mayor He Siyuan and the city councillor Liu Butong—put in their word between the parties, then, a single night having passed, these makers of revolt were released again unconditionally; and so, once and again and a third time, the whole city of Beiping fell into a state of anarchy, and the prestige of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters had long since been swept clean away. The Communists called this a “political entry into the city.”

Passing the small airfield newly opened at Dongdan, one could see a single silver aircraft standing there apart; everyone knew it was Fu Zuoyi’s own plane, named the “Tianxiong,” held ready for withdrawal should the case grow urgent. But alas, Fu Zuoyi was already ringed about by Communist spies, and had already settled his mind on a “covenant made beneath the city walls”—so that it was never to be used at all.

To break out of the siege of Beiping seemed already past hope; and to think that, having not died at the hands of the Japanese through eight years of the War of Resistance, one should die instead at the hands of the Communist Party—the heart was truly unreconciled to it; but the situation being what it was, what could be done?

On a certain day about the twentieth of the first month of the thirty-eighth year, close upon ten in the evening, there came of a sudden a telephone message from the Bandit-Suppression Headquarters that there would be aircraft the next morning, and we might go. This was truly good news dropped from beyond the sky; and so through the night we sent word severally to each man of our unit who was to prepare to withdraw, while I, at the same time, took men and hurried to Mr. X’s house to fetch back the whole store of arms and ammunition left in his keeping. By six the next dawn we reached the Temple of Heaven airfield and boarded a military transport without hindrance, flying straight to Qingdao. From this time the fish returned to the sea and the fierce tiger to the hills; one may foretell that some would roam at ease, and some would set their standards in order and fight again.

### 3. Nine Months of Shifting Posts, over a Thousand Mountains and Rivers
At the beginning of the thirty-eighth year, besides the “stay-behind work” personnel in the North China quarter, the “First Brigade,” led by the deputy brigade commander, had already reached Shanghai, and from Shanghai had gone on to Ningbo in Zhejiang.

While the unit was at Ningbo, I had already left my post of my own accord—a thing that, properly speaking, was not permitted; at the least it was a breach of discipline, at the worst a breaking of the law—but the higher command let me go and did not pursue the matter.

Because I had already left the unit, for what happened in it after this I can go only upon the materials supplied by the students.

After the Beiping caretaker personnel had come in batches from Qingdao to Shanghai and thence on to Ningbo to rejoin, the whole unit was gathered here and ordered to take up the watch-and-guard duty about Fenghua and Xikou. Thereafter it was moved again to Fujian, halting a while in the two prefectures of Zhangzhou and Quanzhou, still ceaselessly opening armed combat against the Communist forces. Only in September of the thirty-eighth year did it manage to cross the sea from Xiamen to Penghu.

This long stretch of the journey holds much that is worth setting down, and several of the students have kindly supplied no little material. Though there is in their writings some overlap and some things out of order, yet the good of it is that it is not without all arrangement. I have put it into a little order and set it down severally below.

In the previous chapter I wrote of the account by our schoolfellow Wang Hongzhu—of how the brigade reached Shanghai and, lodging by night in a funeral parlor, heard fresh tales. Taking up now where that left off, this is again the substance of what our schoolfellow Wang Hongzhu set down, under the title “Guarding the Native Place of the Late President His Excellency Chiang”:—The brigade went from Shanghai by the Shanghai-Hangzhou-Ningbo railway, and, after crossing the great Hangzhou bridge, changed at Xiaoshan to small boats and went on to Shaoxing. All the guard work along the whole road was borne by the assault team, who tasted toil to the full yet felt not the bitterness of it.

At Shaoxing we gathered for training, and in a trice came to understand the local conditions and grow familiar with the ground, still keeping a combat-ready watch.

After a short halt at Shaoxing, we were ordered to move up and take station at Ningbo. Brigade headquarters was set up in a small school at “Yinjiang Bridge” in the southern suburb of Ningbo; here, apart from the brigade headquarters staff and its guard, the main fighting strength was stationed severally at “Xiaolingfeng,” “Jiangjia,” “Xikou” and other points in the Siming Mountains, with the assault team ranging on patrol through the hill country. Several times, too, we fell into chance engagements with the local Communists, but because our unit’s strength was firm and its spirit high, the bandit Communists were struck with terror at the mere rumor of us. And so, from the time our unit took station in the Siming hill country, that region was reckoned still and at peace.

During this time the Corps Commander Mr. Liu Peichu and the brigade commander Mr. Li Yulin were granted an audience with the Leader and his commendation. Clothing and provisions were thereupon bettered, and flour, army blankets and the like came flowing in without cease.

One day His Excellency Chiang went out borne on a mountain-chair, and, seeing on the road the men of our unit on guard duty, made inquiry; learning that they were students of the “Lizhi Class of the Central Training Corps,” and northerners all, he thereupon directed the officer in charge to issue extra flour as their staple. So earnest was the Leader’s tender care for us that all vowed never to forget it.—After this the unit was moved again to Fujian, and our schoolfellow Wang Hongzhu, still going upon memory, wrote on: “Bandit-Suppression in Southern Fujian, Pacification of the Land.” His text is copied out below:—Ordered south, our objective was Xiamen. The whole unit took the two steamers Jiangning and Jiangjing by way of Shanghai and reached Xiamen. On landing, we at once changed to motor-junks, passed Haicheng, and came to Shima. It was by then the dead of night; we halted about a week, and then moved up and took station at Zhangzhou.

At that time our First Brigade, by agreement with Li Liangrong’s army group, first pushed forward to Yanxi, some fifty li north of Zhangzhou. That place was already a “gray area,” where the local Communists came and went constantly; on the march, from time to time we found traces of the enemy, but because our valiant assault lads pressed boldly forward, the local Communists, knowing the odds, drew back and dared not encroach.

At Yanxi miasmal vapors hung thick the year round, and, our bodies unused to the water and soil, many of us caught malaria; and with no medicine to be had, all our activity was greatly hampered. During this time our schoolfellow Wu Ruishan (a Mongol) died of an illness past curing.

After our unit had grown a little used to its surroundings, in order to sweep clean the neighboring local Communists we struck out severally against the two strongpoints northeast of Yanxi—Lindun and Fengyang; over the course of half a month we killed the bandit chief Huang Wenbing and wiped out several hundred of the enemy, and our name and might rang loud throughout the Zhangzhou country.—The above, supplied by our schoolfellow Wang Hongzhu, from the boarding of ship at Tanggu to the wiping-out of the bandits at Zhangzhou, has now been copied out in three parts; herewith my thanks for it. Next, I copy out again the original text written by our schoolfellow Feng Zhijun, “A Record of the Xiaolingfeng Garrison,” as follows:—I remember that we passed the Spring Festival of that year aboard a train on the Shanghai-Hangzhou-Ningbo railway; and though every man felt the melancholy of keeping the festival far from home and native place, what showed on the surface was all joy and excitement, for we all knew that we were going to take up a task of the utmost importance—to keep safe the Leader’s person.

After we came by train to Hangzhou, the railway having been wrecked by the local Communists, we changed to motor-cars to go forward, and it was already dusk when the cars reached Shaoxing.

This place Shaoxing is likewise a famous city of the Jiangnan country; though the sun was low and the bright lamps just coming on, one could still see its broad and level flagstone streets and its handsome, well-ordered buildings—among the cities of the mainland in those days, it might be reckoned a place of some scale.

Our schoolfellow Ma Shouquan of the command room took the lead, walking at the very front; on the sleek, glistening road he strode with his head high, pressing boldly on. When he came to the empty house the local people had made ready for us, he went from the front court to the back yard, pushed open the back door and looked—and there again was a gleaming “broad road”; he stepped out, and with a splash fell into a river ditch. Had the schoolfellows behind not come up quickly, Schoolfellow Ma would have drunk his fill.

For it turned out that in Shaoxing the front door gives on a road and the back door on a waterway; a little skiff drifting down with the current, in twos and threes; fields and gardens, shops and stalls, each in its place—all of it leisurely and steeped in the mood of poem and painting. This too is one scene of the life of the Jiangnan people.

The unit left Shaoxing and, by way of Yuyao and Ningtai, made straight for Xikou in Fenghua. Though we all passed through in a very short time, every schoolfellow seized the rare chance to open his eyes wide and see for himself the true face of that place where dragon coils and tiger crouches.

The great Wuling gate, the Miaogao Terrace, the Qianzhang Rock waterfall and other famous sights, and that little building where His Excellency Chiang was born—all these were set before our eyes, one by one. We had opened our eyes at last, and every one of us felt the trip had not been made in vain.

Hard upon this came a stretch of grueling travel: no trains, no motor-cars; nothing but rugged mountain paths and little streams barely fit for a boat. Over one mountain and another, across one water and another, we came at last to our destination—an important strongpoint on the outer ring of Xikou, “Jiangjia.”

Our command room, led by the commanding officer, our schoolfellow Wang Zhaofen, moved up and took station here. Our own small group was appointed to guard the vital pass, “Xiaolingfeng.”

“Xiaolingfeng” lies in the Siming hill country, in a ravine sunk deep between two peaks—a place of steep and vital ground, with not a single household anywhere in it, only one ancient temple, though its incense fires were long since gone out. A winding little path was Jiangjia’s one way to the outside. The path was about a meter wide, no cart could go on it, and its grade was so sheer that it served for men on foot alone.

On either side high mountains rose sheer into the clouds, and dense on the slopes were bamboo groves stretching past the reach of the eye. The bamboos were fifteen to twenty centimeters thick and could stand ten-odd zhang high; to be among them was a world of its own. Whether, if one gave a great shout in the grove, there would be any echo, none knew; but for fear of raising a false alarm, no one dared to try.

But these clustering bamboo groves also gave the local Communists some convenience: they would hide in the grove, lying up by day and coming out by night, from time to time harassing the countryside—stealing chickens and snatching dogs, at anything and everything, hateful past all bearing. Our basic task was to guard against these stirrings of the local Communists; should they come to attack, we were bound to wipe them out.

Our small group took up this hard task with the greatest care, truly not daring to be in the least remiss. The members of this group of ours were, as I recall, fourteen men: besides the group leader, there were the schoolfellows Liu Maiqing, Men Lizhong, Guo Fengzhuo, Zhan Baoting, Weng Junying, Wang Hongjun, Liu Shiwei, Shen Huai, Liu Guijin, Gao Shiqing, Wang Zemin, Han Guangfan, and Liu Wenzhong (who died of illness in Taiwan—to whom I offer my respectful mourning). (It has been thirty-nine years; if there be any error in my memory, I beg the indulgence of my schoolfellows.)

On reaching the place of our task, we first scouted the ground, assigned each man his fighting position, and made all speed to finish the fortifying work—digging skirmishers’ trenches. That one path, fit for men on foot alone, was the very heart of our task; by day travelers were let pass after inspection, but at nightfall traffic was controlled and none allowed through. At the same time, to make up for our want of manpower, we set abatis as obstacles at both ends of the road. Properly, laying mines would have been more effective; but among our equipment there were neither engineers nor explosives. “Poverty brings change, and change brings a way through”: so we bound one end of a home-made grenade fast, tied its fuse at the other end with a cord to the far side of the road, twenty centimeters off the ground, so that whoever passed and touched the cord would surely set off an explosion. Thus we laid our mines in the dark of night and withdrew them at daybreak, and by this added no little to our safety.

To make every member familiar and well-versed in the ground and its features, in their fighting positions, and in the ability to meet the unexpected, we once held a combat drill. A little past two in the dead of night, having arranged it beforehand with the two schoolfellows Zhan Baoting and Weng Junying, I used gunfire in place of the emergency-muster order to startle every man in the squad awake from his dreams; they took up their weapons and swiftly entered the skirmishers’ trenches, completing their combat readiness in under a minute, and on inspection all scored a hundred. And why so high a mark? To tell it is simple enough: we had no army blankets or bedding at all, and so had no need to loosen our clothes; each night, to sleep, we could only burrow, clothes and all, into a heap of straw, which was our warm bed—so that in an emergency it was of course far the more convenient.

The men in the group were few and the duties heavy; every man had to stand sentry, and I was no exception. Two men to a double post, two hours on and four off, was of course the standard rule; and I, as group leader, besides doing the same duty, had also to inspect the posts, so that my sleep was of course not enough. Hence each day at noon, if time allowed, I always hoped for a short nap to make up the want. One noon, my head covered with my jacket, I woke from a sleep and felt at once that inside my left ear something was wriggling; I dug at it with my finger, and it seemed the more I dug the deeper it crawled. I stood up and hopped a few times on my left foot (as one often does when water gets in the ear while swimming), but still to no effect—and it was stirring inside more violently than before.

There was nothing for it but to get two schoolfellows to go with me to a little village three li off and find a barber to see what could be done. The barber had a real knack: he had it in hand at once, and straightway drew out a louse. When I took it and looked, it was fat and plump, white flushed with red, far bigger than a sesame seed. To take my revenge, I flung it on a cigarette-end, then threw it into the ditch, and so gave it a “burial by water and fire.”

Speaking of lice, they were nothing fresh in those days—every man’s body swarmed with them in droves. When the weather was fine, we would often squat in the sun at the foot of a wall and hold a great contest; the winner once set a record of more than a hundred, and even the man who came last had his twenty or thirty. The eggs of the louse are called “nits,” gleaming and white, ranged in rows along the seams of one’s clothes like strings of pearls. Press them dead with the thumbnails of both hands aligned, and they go crack—and with practice one can even beat out a rhythm.

One fine, sunlit morning I suddenly heard heavy gunfire near the command room; listening close, I could make out among it the sound of our unit’s own “big eight-rounder” (the M1 semi-automatic rifle) and of submachine guns. Thinking there must be some incident at the command room, I at once posted the men each to his place, entering their fighting positions to await orders.

At that time, with neither telephone nor wireless, I could only send one of the group in place of a runner to go and find out the news; in about ten minutes the man came running back out of breath and reported in haste: “The President has come! He is resting now at the command room.”

This threw us all into both tension and excitement; but I saw at once that, the President having come, our charge was the heavier, and, before any further instruction reached me from the command room, I could only, as a makeshift, enjoin them to strengthen their watch and give their whole heart to their duty, and sent a man to keep touch with the command room. After about half an hour, I saw on another little path a hundred meters off a bamboo sedan, and seated in it an old man wearing a peaked cap, dark spectacles, and a Chinese-style long gown, with some ten-odd men going before and behind him.

Looking close, I saw that this old man was far more aged than when I had seen him at Nanjing a year and more before. To think of his old age spent in care for his country and his people made one, in spite of oneself, stand in solemn reverence.—The above is what our schoolfellow Feng Zhijun set down; between the lines there is still something of the playful in it. In those days he was a little past twenty, his rank about that of a lieutenant; and now he has been some years retired as a major general—how swift the years have been.

As to the course of the guarding of Xikou, our schoolfellow Wang Zhaofen was the commanding officer of the time, and what he knows is not only detailed but clearly remembered. On the fourteenth of June he telephoned me from Taichung to ask whether more was still wanted; but the closing of the manuscript being at hand, there was no longer time to wait.

In the second and third months of the seventy-sixth year, Schoolfellow Zhaofen had told me of this matter by word of mouth; only, my memory being so much diminished, to write it out as he told it was quite beyond my strength. Nor did I dare to go about it carelessly—should there be any error, the whole point would be lost. The passage below was supplied by our schoolfellow Xiao Runyu. In Chapter Four above, Schoolfellow Xiao wrote of what he saw and heard when he set out from the county town of Anci, pushed deep into the Communist area of Anguo county, and came within an ace of taking Mao Zedong alive; this passage tells the rough course of our brigade from its setting-out at Fuxue Lane in Beiping all the way to the Penghu islands. The original is titled “How Our Unit Was Moved South,” and its content is not wholly the same as what our schoolfellow Wang Hongzhu wrote—one may say, without harm, that each has its own flavor. It is copied out below:—Preparations had already begun some days before; on the morning of the ——th of December in the thirty-seventh year we assembled at brigade headquarters in Fuxue Lane, and set out at once for the West Station outside Qianmen, taking the train on the first stage to Tianjin. That same day we reached the “Xingang”—the New Harbor—at Tanggu, to wait for a ship.

We waited some days, and only with great difficulty was a ship allotted to us—and that only got by armed men pressing for it in person. The ship had been carrying iron ore, and it seemed had not yet finished unloading. Whether her name was the “Hengchun” I can no longer clearly remember.

Aboard ship there was still more waiting—this time for the tide to rise; at midnight, at full flood, the hull floated up, and only then did she slowly get under way. This ship was likely well on in years, her displacement some three thousand-odd tons, and she gave the impression of a doddering old age; under way she made only three nautical miles an hour, and against a head wind no more than a nautical mile and a bit.

Setting out from Xingang, after I know not how many days and nights we reached the outer waters of Wusongkou; but there we met with a thick, drifting fog and could not enter port, and had to lie moored to a buoy in the harbor to wait. Truly it was “the leaking roof met with a night-long rain”: the propeller of our ship, the Hengchun, had been a three-bladed screw, but on the voyage, unlooked-for, it was wrecked by wind and wave, two blades snapped off and only a half-stub left; for fear of raising alarm at sea, it was owned to only when we had moored to the buoy. To read one’s prospects thus beforehand, one may well know which way fortune lay!

Landing from the ship, we were quartered in a funeral parlor in the Nanshi district; before long we passed through Hangzhou and Xiaoshan, and moved up to take station at Shaoxing and Ningbo. Fair though the scenery of Jiangnan was, we could not, alas, afford the leisure of heart to enjoy it. What we could do was, for the huadiao and chenshao wines whose fame we had long known, often to slip out of barracks of an evening to savor a taste of them. In those days we were all young, muddled and unknowing, ignorant of sorrow, yet our high ambition was still in us, undimmed by the roughness of the road ahead.

In the first month of the thirty-eighth year, the late President His Excellency Chiang stepped down; our unit was charged with the sweeping-out of the local Communists in the Siming Mountains region, and with the watch-and-guard duty about Xikou in Fenghua, while my own First Company was stationed from within the city of Ningbo to guard the town of Yinjiang Bridge.

Before long, under the smoke-screen of peace talks, the Chinese Communists were already laying their plans to cross the Yangtze, and our garrison at Shanghai had begun its dispositions for the defensive battle.

It was learned that Tang Enbo’s headquarters had strongly invited our Corps to join the defense of Shanghai, and meant to arrange for the schoolfellows to go deep into the base of society as “baojia instructors,” with charge of counter-spy and traitor-rooting work. For a time opinions ran every which way—up hill and down dale—but as no conclusion was reached, the thing was let drop.

Thereafter we were ordered south. On the march we first learned that the Ministry of National Defense had merged and reorganized the “Pacification Corps,” the “Suppression-and-Reconstruction Corps,” and the “People’s Service Corps” into the “Youth National-Salvation Corps,” with Mr. Hu Gui as its head, ruling four corps beneath him: “Suppression-and-Reconstruction” was the First Corps, our “Pacification” the Second Corps, and the “People’s Service” split into the Third and Fourth Corps; below the corps the establishment was unchanged.

At the time we received the head Mr. Hu Gui’s “Letter to All Comrades,” in which was set out this striking sentence: “We must, in the spirit of loyal outcasts and rejected sons, be the Whampoa of the political realm; the gendarmes of the economic; the guardians of the nation; the soul of society; the special-operations corps of resistance to Communism and to Russia…” But hemmed in by the great tide of things, we did little and showed little; and dragging a heavy heart, we went by the steamer Shengjing from Xiamen over to Taiwan, and went ashore at Magong in Penghu. It was the twelfth of September in the thirty-eighth year of the Republic.

The “Penghu Defense Command” took us in, and we were formed into the “Penghu Defense Command Civil-Training Guidance Brigade”—most of us officers—which some, to those outside, also called the “Political Battalion.” Under the brigade were a number of working groups, their tasks divided into “organizing and training the populace,” “grouping the fishing boats,” “fishermen’s self-defense,” “social security and defense,” “sea-and-air defense,” “harbor inspection” and the like, with one fast patrol boat allotted them. The schoolfellows jestingly called it the “Army Marine Corps.”

Besides these, a “Magong Fortress Garrison Regiment” was formed as well; and apart from those taken into the above units, the rest of the schoolfellows straightway left the unit and came severally to the main island.

To speak of the organizing and training of the populace in Penghu, it might be reckoned a thing done with color and life: of the sixty-odd islands of the Penghu chain, wherever there were inhabitants there were our schoolfellows too. Our way of working—serving in place of leading—won deeply the trust of the local people, and we got on together as one family; by this, too, one may see how strong our schoolfellows’ power of adapting was. Before we came to Penghu not one of us could speak the Southern Fujianese tongue; after we came, we learned it and used it on the spot, and spoke it not much worse than the natives. Schoolfellow Liu Guijin, for instance, not only spoke the common Southern Fujianese fluently as flowing water, but could even play the “spirit-medium boy,” and at times could hoodwink people till they stood dazed and blinking.

In those days our finest advantage was our youth: most of us were a little over twenty, and in character and learning alike we were above the mark, fit to be counted fine young men. And so we won no little favor with the local lasses. Later many of the schoolfellows found flower-fair brides in Penghu, and today they too all have their rows of sons and daughters, grown up now and themselves talking of marriage.—Our schoolfellow Wu Chunxiang’s material has now been received. It was not printed earlier in Biographical Literature; I here extract its main points, and they differ somewhat from what our schoolfellows Wang Hongzhu, Feng Zhijun, and Xiao Runyu supplied—especially after the coming to Penghu.

Our schoolfellow Wu Chunxiang’s post at the time was leader of the Fourth Company of the First Brigade, afterward reorganized as the First Company directly subordinate to the Corps, its cadre being none other than the former “assault team.” What follows is a digest of Schoolfellow Wu’s account:—In the latter part of the fourth month of the thirty-eighth year, the unit was ordered to Changtai in Fujian. The unit took ship from Ningbo, passed Shima and Zhangzhou, and reached Xiamen; landing, it went on foot to Changtai county. By this time the directly-subordinate First Company was strongest in fighting power, its weapons and equipment all modern, so that it had become a well-trained fighting body. In the summer of the thirty-eighth year, the friendly troops garrisoning Lindun were suddenly attacked by the bandits, and the fighting was fierce; the Corps headquarters ordered the directly-subordinate First Company to go to their aid with all speed, and it was by then about five in the afternoon. The First Brigade commander Wang Zhaofen called together the company commander Wu Chunxiang, the district-company commanders Chen Zhongyi, Han Shirong, Lu Deming, and Jia Shuming, and the instructor Ge Baokan, to study the essentials of the action. Lindun lay fifteen Chinese li from Yanxi, but our troops did not very well understand the enemy’s whole situation, and so Brigade Commander Wang Zhaofen made his decision: we could only go, step by step, along the right-hand side of the mountain road, in the manner of a “snake shedding its skin.” Having explained the task of the march to all officers and men before setting out, we put Lu Deming at the point, and, keeping to a set direction, crossed sixteen ridges high and low one after another, reaching the summit southwest of Lindun about nine in the evening. Having got touch with the Second Company’s Chang Shaozeng by signal flare, we straightway set up nine machine guns in sections; and at a single order to fire, we wove a dense net of fire, and the enemy, unable to hold, broke and scattered away.

With the great situation in upheaval and the Communist bandits’ activity growing daily more rampant, small local actions seemed already of no great weight; yet what is worth setting down is the spirit of a struggle that a hundred setbacks could not bend. In the latter part of the ——th month of the thirty-eighth year, our unit gathered at Xiamen and took a large cargo steamer, reaching first the outer waters of Wusongkou at Shanghai; it was said we were to join General Tang Enbo in carrying out traitor-rooting work, but this came to nothing. The ship lay two days in the outer sea, then weighed anchor, and on the twelfth of September of the thirty-eighth year reached Magong harbor in Penghu.

The Corps headquarters was stationed at the Dachikan primary-school grounds in Baisha township, Penghu; the rest were stationed severally at Dingwan, Xiaochikan and other places.

When the unit went ashore at Magong harbor, the commandant of the Magong fortress, General Li Zhenqing, came to inspect us, and voiced his commendation of the various types of automatic weapons we carried and of our excellent quality.

In the second month of the thirty-ninth year the Corps was reorganized once more: the non-commissioned officers and men were gathered into a single battalion, designated the “First Battalion of the Magong Fortress Garrison Regiment,” ruling four companies. Wang Zhaofen was battalion commander; Wu Chunxiang, Sun Wenliang, Shi Jihuan, and Huang Jie were the four company commanders. Shi Defa was deputy battalion commander; and Cai Gan, Yan Fengyi, Feng Zhijun, and Mei Changling (once general manager of the Central Motion Picture and of China Television, now deceased) were severally the instructors of the companies.

The officers, for their part, were gathered into a single political-work brigade, in which “political small groups” were severally set up, charged with organizing and training the populace. And here the “Pacification Corps” came to its end.

### 4. We Are All the Fortunate Ones of a Turbulent Age
In Taiwan, for a spell everything felt so fresh; but before long it faded again.

At that time I had no post of duty, and grew very slack, with never a thought in my head, still less any drive to get on. This was not from any downheartedness; and if it were said that I had wearied of secret-service work, that would not be wholly true either—I truly cannot put into words what kind of mood it was.

To tell it, I was muddled indeed: real life was a great problem that had to be solved, and yet I had not a single plan; a man on his own is easy enough to manage, but what of that whole household of mine? For this I must thank our Corps Commander Mr. Liu Peichu. Of my circumstances—from whom he heard, I do not know—one day he brought me six taels of gold with his own hands, and, saying nothing at all, went off. My family and I lived on this for a good long while.

In the sixth or seventh month, the Bureau sent me a notice, engaging me as a “planning commissioner”; but it was, alas, an unpaid post, without so much as a carriage allowance, and it seems there was only ever one meeting held. Such a thing must be told on two levels. Those who could be engaged as “planning commissioners” were to be reckoned among the valued men, all of a rank above colonel and still holding a work-standing of some weight; on the other hand, it was not that any “planning” was really wanted for the work—it was no more than a way of “setting bounds” or “holding one in check.” It differed a little from the advisers and consultants of the ordinary administrative offices.

Not long after, a lieutenant-general deputy chief of the Second Bureau of the Ministry of National Defense came to see me; being an acquaintance, he came straight to the point and said that the “Second Bureau” had a mind to ask me to be chief of its “Sixth Section,” with charge of mainland intelligence. Much though it was in my line, it was too sudden, and I could not but consider it carefully. Why in the end I did not go I can no longer recall—very likely it was that the chief was no longer Mr. Zheng Jiemin. By the tenth month, my good friend Brother Lian Mou (Liangshun) had a fellow townsman of his bring me a sum of traveling money from Hong Kong, with never a scrap of writing; the townsman only passed on a word by mouth, bidding me come at once to Hong Kong to take counsel together over the road ahead.

Though I did not know at all what I was going to do, I set out without the least hesitation. I remember that in those days the restrictions on entering and leaving the country were not strict, and it was quite convenient; without asking leave of anyone or taking my farewell, I simply took my own free course.

Brother Lian Mou, courtesy name Liangshun, was a man of Hui’an in Fujian, of the fourth class of the Military Academy, an elected member of the Legislative Yuan, and after the victory in the War of Resistance had been mayor of Kaohsiung in Taiwan Province. How he came to build up his ties with the foreigners in Hong Kong I never once asked him, nor did he ever plainly tell me.

Brother Lian Liangshun and I were bound in deep friendship: it was I who, in the twenty-first year of the Republic, introduced him to Mr. Dai Yunong to join the Juntong Bureau. In the twenty-fourth year, when I was station chief in Beiping, I made a grave blunder, and, not daring to face the reality and bear my fault, ran off alone to hide in the wild country of Ulanhua in Suiyuan; and in the end it was Brother Lian Liangshun who came all the way from Hankou to Beiping to take me back to Nanjing to receive my punishment (see, for the full account, Chapter Four of my book “Rooting Out Traitors in the North”). Our bond being as close as this, I trusted him, of course, entirely.

I left the country by aircraft; and when we came over Hong Kong, it circled and circled without coming down. The passengers, at a loss, asked the stewardess, but she would give no sure answer, only soothing everyone to fasten their belts tight and keep calm. At this the passengers’ misgivings grew heavier still, yet there was nothing to be done: some closed their eyes and pressed their palms together, saying not a word; some crossed themselves furiously, up, down, left, right; some told over “Amitabha” aloud without ceasing; a few, quite beside themselves, cried out to Heaven and beat the earth; and others asked this and that and got no answer. Such a gallery of living souls as one rarely sees, a picture could hardly show, nor a novel paint.

Whether it was the work of men or the guarding of Heaven I do not know; but the aircraft came down at last—a “forced landing” would be the apter word, for one of the wheels had jammed, and only by burning off all the fuel aboard could it come down without catching fire and exploding. Fright there was, but no harm: an auspicious start to the journey out.

In Hong Kong I put up at Happy Valley, where Brother Liangshun had long since arranged lodging for me. He told me that he was preparing to “cooperate” with a certain great power in mainland work founded upon opposition to Communism. The thing had been brewing some months and had now reached a ripe stage; he had sent for me to take general charge of the inner affairs while he handled the diplomacy—that is, I was to direct the business, and he to have charge of liaison and outside relations.

After the two of us had reckoned it over a little, it was as good as decided. This was at the end of the thirty-eighth year of the Republic.

This “cooperation” had no title or name of any kind, nor was any “contract” of any form signed for the two sides to keep faith by. It was only that a single, thread-thin line of contact was kept up for periodic touch. Plainly put, it was wholly the “secret-service pattern.” This is no false modesty: “cooperation,” so called, is a pleasant enough word, but in fact the two characters are quite unfit, for at bottom the standing of the two sides was not equal.

The terms of the “cooperation” were, in the main, that we furnished the men, and our wits besides, while they only paid over on schedule a fixed sum of money; beyond that there was no technical aid of any kind.

The most important point was that the other side strictly required that all the fruits of the work must on no account be furnished to the relevant departments of our own government; and they declared again and again that, should such an act ever be found, they would at once “dissolve the cooperative relationship.”

In fact, neither Brother Lian Liangshun nor I would ever do such a thing; and so, on one return to Taiwan, Brother Liangshun made a report to Mr. Tang Naijian (Zong), who then headed the “Sixth Section of the Central,” while I, for my part, reported in detail to Mr. Zheng Jiemin, who then headed the “Mainland Work Office.” At once Brother Han Shangying (a Guangdong man, of the fifth class of the Military Academy; see, for the full account, Chapter Twelve of my book “Counter-Agent Work in the Latter Period of the War of Resistance”) was sent to be stationed permanently in Hong Kong to keep close touch, bringing with him fast communications equipment.

In the thirty-ninth year Brother Lian Liangshun was invited to the Philippines to set up a “mainland-work base,” and the whole of the Hong Kong business passed to me, to take over from him and bear alone.

Half a year later, Brother Lian Liangshun moved on again to Thailand, and the Philippine business was taken over in turn by a Jiang So-and-so, who had been in Hong Kong before.

Though the other side held to working independently and took up a policy of isolation, we had a way of our own of keeping in touch with one another, so that we could still hold together as one whole and not be split apart.

In this year or two the work grew fast and full of life, and we did indeed build up no few connections on the mainland—connections that were, of course, held in my own hand.

To boast is a thing to be ashamed of; but the pity was that between the three—“intelligence activity,” “political movement,” and the fleeting “moment” that is gone in a flash—a close coordination had to be reached, and let there be but a gap or a break, and the effect was greatly lessened, so that no positive part could be played.

It was just because on our side there were leaks and a want of nicety that the other side got a “handle” on us, and so it came to a falling-out and a parting on bad terms.

The other side’s way of handling this matter wore a ferocious face, and if not downright base, was low at the least. Yet they could do nothing against a body of men firm of will, who would not turn their backs on their country.

As for the particulars, they were twisting to the point of the near-fantastic; and were they laid on a little more, they would be matter enough and to spare for a whole novel of battling wits.

In the forty-sixth year of the Republic, Mr. Zhang Yanyuan (Binghua) succeeded Mr. Mao Renfeng at the head of the Intelligence Bureau of the Ministry of National Defense. Mr. Zhang sent me to Japan to open up mainland work. Mr. Zhang and I had trained in the same class at Nanjing in the twentieth and twenty-first years of the Republic, and afterward had passed a stretch of bitter, hard-tasting life crowded together in one small room (see, for the full account, Chapter One of my book “Rooting Out Traitors in the North”). So, he having an errand for me, how could I in reason not take the charge?—and all the more as it was a mark of his regard for me.

That winter, on my way by air from Hong Kong to Japan, passing through Taipei, Mr. Zhang Binghua even came in person to Songshan Airport to talk with me for ten minutes and to lay the task on me face to face; and this I felt to be yet another honor.

By this time Mr. Zheng Jiemin had moved to become director of the “National Security Bureau,” and concurrently head of the “Second Section of the Central.”

In Japan I found Brother Cao Xiaoqing, my fellow in the same company and the same class from the days of enlistment at the Military Academy (a Shandong man, of Meiji University in Japan, of the fourth class of the Academy; see Chapter One of my book “Rooting Out Traitors in the North”). The tie between us was very deep, and our feeling for each other very good; we could talk of anything, one might say. This time, however, he held something back; and only later did I learn that he had already been won over by Mr. Zheng Jiemin and had long since joined the work.

Though the road we walked was the same, he still helped me with all sincerity. Through his arranging, and even his serving as interpreter, I met with, and made use of, no few old soldiers—among them Watanabe Wataru (a lieutenant general), who in Japan’s war of aggression had served as military-administrative governor of Singapore; Wachi Takaji (a lieutenant general), the well-known head of the South China special-service organ; and Nemoto Hiroshi (a full general), who had once been the commander in the Zhangjiakou region of the North China Expeditionary Army. I sought out Nakajima Shin'ichi as well (a captain; see Chapter Two of my book “Counter-Agent Work in the Latter Period of the War of Resistance”), but in the end did not find him—it was rumored he had already died.

By that time these men were all around sixty, most without any occupation and living in fair hardship; it was said the Japanese government still paid them some small allowance to look after them. In point of thought they were on the whole anti-Communist, though not every one of them had a way with mainland work. My wish to “use” them started, of course, from what would help the work; but there is no denying that there was also, in some measure, a certain vengefulness mixed in. These men, by now, had lost that conqueror’s swagger of the days when they occupied the Chinese mainland; what showed in their bearing was a thing at once meek and deferential—contemptible, and fearful too!

After some months of work in Tokyo, when things had just begun to take shape, I suddenly had a telephone call from Mr. Zheng Jiemin in Taipei, telling me to take the next day’s early CAT flight and come back to Taipei at once. I could not think what it was for; but his word, to me, was as good as an order, and so I entrusted a few unfinished matters to Brother Cao Xiaoqing and made ready to set out the next day.

A few hours later, the man in charge of personnel at the Intelligence Bureau in Taipei telephoned too, bidding me first arrange a seat with the CAT Tokyo office; as for the entry formalities, they might be seen to on the spot once I reached Songshan Airport.

In Taipei, after I had seen Mr. Zheng Jiemin and Mr. Zhang Binghua, I learned that my recall was because it had already been submitted for sanction that I be appointed chief of the Second Section of the Intelligence Bureau of the Ministry of National Defense. And why must it still be “submitted for sanction”? There is much writing to be done on that, and to set it all down would draw longer and longer. What made trouble, rather, was how the record-of-service form was to be filled in—for the parts would not join up front to back, and no documents could be produced; over this one thing alone the business dragged on some months.

As this section chief I did not do so badly; I won decorations and awards, and in the forty-ninth year of the Republic was promoted to army major general. I remember very clearly that only on the one occasion when the Chief of the General Staff conferred the rank did I have to put on uniform, and never wore it again after. As for why I do not care for military dress, there is no particular reason—simply that I am not used to it.

To return: when the schoolfellows of the “Pacification Brigade” heard that I had come back, they gathered in joy at the “Servicemen’s Friendship Association,” more than two hundred of them, some hurried in from other parts—and there were still those held by their posts who could not stir, and those in far-off places who could not be reached in time with word. Since we all parted at Beiping at the end of the thirty-seventh year, we had been sundered a full ten years. In those ten years we had passed through every vicissitude, each with his own bitterness, and the changes had been too great; and this coming-together had in it more that plucked at the heart than could ever be told. From then on, in Taiwan, my comings and goings with the schoolfellows and colleagues never ceased, and have kept up right to the present of the seventy-sixth year.

Mr. Li Yulin—of the deepest tie to this unit, of the greatest contribution to it, and most tender in his care for the schoolfellows—may be called one of the more accomplished men of this band. Having tasted every kind of toil and hardship in bringing the unit to Penghu, he labored on there through bleak straits and struck root in that place, and so came to hold, term after term, the office of Penghu county magistrate, both by appointment and by election.

For a mainlander in Taiwan to be elected county magistrate was, to say nothing of his record in office, already no easy thing; and all the more when his good name in Penghu was very fair besides. I think that, apart from his own capability, he had the benefit, too, of the groundwork laid by the “pacification methods.”

It is said that after the unit reached Penghu and was reorganized once more, a small part of the schoolfellows stayed on, while most scattered and went each his own way.

Today the schoolfellows have gone deep into every stratum of society—scholar, farmer, artisan, merchant, every trade and calling—and at whatever they do they make a fine showing. Some, giving themselves to party work, have been elected central committee members; some, pursuing their studies further in the army, have risen to general’s rank; those in business, chairmen and general managers, go without saying; in education, secondary-school principals and university professors are by no means few; and besides these, playwrights, actors, music conductors, association officers, scholars abroad, and so on—and a big proprietor of a little eating-house as well. Speaking of this, I am glad; for they all call me by the name of “teacher.”

But if one is to speak of the successes and failures, the gains and losses, of the Pacification Corps, that is a tale of another kind.

This Pacification Corps, from its founding to its reorganization, lasted all told less than two years; and if one does not shut the door to talk to oneself, close one’s eyes to praise oneself, or trouble over whether the words are becomingly put, then it must be said: First, that being put into practice under conditions where the conception was not fully ripe and where experience was wanting, it could of course not turn out wholly as one had wished.

Second, that it still had not shaken off many of the stale ways of a mass movement, and slighted the new situation, one never seen before.

Third, that because the demands laid on the work were too high and the tasks too heavy, no regard was paid to the fact that the cadres were not “omnipotent.”

It cannot, of course, be said that this unit accomplished nothing at all in the war of “suppression”; only that the part it played fell truly short of what had at first been looked for.

The writer, being one of its members, could hardly stand aside; and one might say, too, that it is not as though I had discharged in full the duty that was mine to discharge, and so am without regret.

Among the whole record, what is worth setting down lies still in the honesty, the loyal courage, the sacrifice, and the devotion to duty of the students and comrades.
