# Chapter Fifteen — synopsis and reliability

*Printed pages 299–325. Sections 50 ("Trapped at Wuzhou With No Way Out, Then a
Turning, and a Dream of Northern Shaanxi"), 51 ("A Beautiful Woman Chooses
Money Over Loyalty"), 52 ("Two Mysterious Visitors Come to the Li Compound"),
53 ("Cold Moonlight on Noble Blood"). Chapter title: "Wuzhou, the End of an
Outlaw Hero's Life."*

---

## What happens

**Wuzhou, Guangxi.** Wang is living in Li Jishen's ancestral compound — a
four-courtyard house whose rear garden Li rebuilt in 1923, when he commanded a
Cantonese division, as guest quarters for Bai Chongxi and other military
friends. Wang arrived from Hong Kong with his family in **March 1936** and by
high summer is still there, with his wife telling him plainly that they should
never have come and should have listened to Hua Kezhi.

The section ends with Wang writing through the night: an offer to bring his
handful of men and serve in a "just army," asking nothing for himself. The book
frames this as the road he has finally found after years of turning — and the
section's title names the destination, northern Shaanxi.

**Hong Kong, meanwhile.** Yu Wanjun, the wife of Wang's imprisoned lieutenant
Yu Likui, has been seduced by a handsome young man who is an agent, and finds
herself unexpectedly content — with her husband in a Nanjing prison and Wang
gone, both absences have started to feel less like losses. She is then worked
on directly: what future is there for her in Hong Kong, and what could Wang give
her even if she saw him again, when there are two hundred thousand waiting? The
book catches the moment her wet eyes brighten.

**Wuzhou again.** Wang Yaying notices that Yu Wanjun keeps glancing at one of
the men, and presses her husband: this is no time to trust anyone, and if Dai Li
has driven a nail in beside them the new plan is finished. Wang gives way
reluctantly and has Yu Wanjun moved out to a rented room on East Street, sending
her off with two hundred yuan in her hand.

**The last walk.** Zheng Baozhen and another man try to stop him going to her,
citing his wife's instructions. Wang loses his temper — is he not free even to
call on her, and does he not keep his word once given? He is delivering a
letter, he says, and will be straight back.

## How it holds up

### The outline is stable; every detail conflicts

This is the pattern of the whole corpus in miniature. What every account agrees
on: Wang was killed at Wuzhou in autumn 1936 by Dai Li's service; the mechanism
was Yu Likui's woman, bought with money and a promise of her husband's release;
lime was thrown in his face as he came through the door; he fought; he was shot
and stabbed; his face was flayed afterwards.

What no two accounts render the same way:

| | Variants |
|---|---|
| **Date** | 20 October 1936 (best supported, and Chinese Wikipedia's) · 20 September · November · 20 December |
| **Place** | "her lodgings" · a named hotel on East Street · a different named hotel |
| **Wounds** | five shots and three stab wounds · four shots and six stabs |
| **The price** | 100,000 · the book's 200,000 |
| **Her status** | Yu Likui's wife · his concubine · in some accounts Wang's own |
| **His age** | 47 · 48, since even his birth year is disputed |

The flaying is near-universal and comes with three mutually exclusive motives —
to prevent identification, to stage a crime of passion, or as proof of death for
Dai Li. One widely circulated version has Dai Li ordering the face sent to
**Chongqing**, which is impossible in 1936: the service was at Nanjing, and
Chongqing did not become the wartime capital until late 1937. That is a clean
tell that the story has been embroidered in transmission.

**No contemporary document could be located for any of it.**

### The names

Of the agents usually named, most dissolve on checking. The one worth having is
**Wang Luqiao**, named in several accounts as the man who fired — and
independently documented, because he went to Taiwan, became Taipei police
commissioner, and died in a car accident in 1974. His later career is checkable
even though his role here is not. Note that the version naming him also gives
the divergent date and wound count.

**Yu Wanjun's own end** — killed by the agents the same night, to silence her —
is internally consistent and attested nowhere outside the popular corpus. It is
also narratively convenient in a way worth noticing: it removes the only outside
witness.

### The refuge is real

The move from Hong Kong to Wuzhou in **February or March 1936** under Li
Jishen's protection is corroborated in outline, and the geography is coherent —
Wuzhou is Li Jishen's home region. Li consulted Li Zongren and Bai Chongxi about
sheltering him and a monthly stipend was arranged.

The famous last dinner at Li Jishen's house is told and contradicts itself: some
accounts have Li hosting, others have him deputing a local notable, and one
source misidentifies that notable as Li's elder brother, which he was not. The
scene always closes on the same line about a sworn brother's wife being
incapable of harming him, which is a storyteller's beat rather than testimony.

### The Shaanxi ending

The book's closing turn — Wang resolving to go north and join the Communists —
converges with something real in the source literature, though not in the way
the book intends. His brother later argued that Wang was killed **because** he
meant to go to Yan'an, and that Li Zongren and Bai Chongxi opened the door to
Dai Li to prevent it while pursuing their own accommodation with Chiang. That is
a family claim, not a finding. But it is the same brother who is the origin of
the Hongkou Park attribution, and it belongs to the same 1980s effort to
establish Wang as a patriot rather than a contractor.

### What became of him

Buried at Wuzhou; remains moved in 1997, cremated at Shanghai and reburied in
the family cemetery near Hefei, which the municipal government listed as a
protected cultural relic in 2011. In January 2016 one of the graves was dug
into, which is why this is the best-reported fact in the whole aftermath.

**On martyr status: I could not find it.** No ministry designation, no
provincial designation, no date. What exists is county-level material calling
him a patriot, the widely quoted Mao assessment whose provenance is
unestablished, and the 2011 **heritage** listing of the tomb — which is not a
martyr designation, and is the likeliest thing the claim is a conflation of. I
would not repeat it without hedging.

## The last word on the title

**暗杀大王, "king of assassins," appears to be retrospective.** No period
newspaper using it could be produced, and the whole cluster of epithets —
Republic's first killer, the Far East's first assassin, and the rest — reads as
modern popular-history branding, most likely propagated from Shen Zui's memoirs
and 1980s recollection materials.

The book's own title participates in the branding it is retailing. That is the
fairest summary of what this book is: not a fraud, and not history — the most
readable node in a story that a family, a genre and forty years assembled around
a man who was, on the documented record, a very good contract killer.
