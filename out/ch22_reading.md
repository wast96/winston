## Chapter 2. Spring Clouds Unfurl, the First Thunder Stirs

### Kill One to Warn a Hundred: Clearing Obstacles to Show Our Might

The "Shanghai District" was able to weather its crisis by steady, solid steps, and that was wholly the fruit of collective cooperation; in the perilous circumstances of that time it truly came through as if out of raging billows, and to say "it was no easy thing" would be no overstatement. What most concerned my superiors, rather, was that the general work had already fallen into a state of half-suspension; and though this was forced by the pressure of events and might be excused, we could not overlook that these were wartime, the larger situation critical, and that in the demands of the work, and in coordination with national policy and strategy, every second was contested and no moment could be spared. The time factor was of the very greatest importance, and "taking it slowly" was of course not to be allowed.

One day I received a telegram from Mr. Dai, deciphered by his own hand; never had I dreamed that he would utter, without the least regard for my feelings, words so mortifying—this was a thing without precedent since I had entered the work. When I had read it, shame and indignation came upon me at once, and my very face flushed red; and raising my head, I saw that Brother Wang Shaoqian, who had deciphered the telegram, still stood there and had not gone—which made me more ashamed still. The original wording of the telegram I can no longer set down in full, but its opening two sentences I could not forget though I died; he said: "Brother! The Hanoi blow failed, and you have turned as timid as a mouse!" It was truly not to be borne.

Whether he was venting his temper, or digging up old scores and finding fault, or even using the goading method to drive the work forward, I had nothing to say for myself; had I, in the heat of feeling, acted on some sudden impulse, I should truly have wished to resign and be done. But when my mood had steadied and I had thought it well over, resigning was no solution either, for we simply had no such option: willing, one must carry on; unwilling, one must carry on all the same. That being so, what could I do but swallow the grievance in silence?

What galled me most was that phrase which wounded my self-respect, "timid as a mouse." I asked myself: as for the responsibility for the failure of the Hanoi blow, I had already shouldered the whole of it without the least evasion, and had come back from Hanoi to Chongqing in the spirit of a man awaiting sentence, prepared to accept my punishment; now that I had been given the chance to atone for my past fault by fresh service, would it not be well to bear all in patience and let my later conduct be the answer? To rake up the old matter now must surely spring from some feeling in him; and I guessed—

Could it be that he was under pressure from above, or even under reproach?

Perhaps he had received some tale-bearer's report, that I mixed too little with my working comrades?

If not that, then he was displeased that I kept my movements too closely hidden?

And if none of these, then it was of course the "goad."

To sum it in a word, it could hardly be other than the several things I have surmised—perhaps a mixture of them all.

After I had reasoned it out, my mind eased a little and my anger by degrees subsided; the best course now was to seek results in the work—that alone would be the finest answer. Beyond that, all words were empty. And so I resolved not to send him a telegram.

At the time when Wang Jingwei was in Nanjing brewing the organization of his puppet regime, the focus of our work was thrown wholly into action; and Mr. Dai, in another telegram, gave this direction: "In both Nanjing and Shanghai, and along the Nanjing–Shanghai line, we must enlarge our action, so as to make our authority felt. Pray spur on those under you to act vigorously, and thereby chill the traitors' gall." From this we may see how ardently our superiors set their hopes upon the action work.

Ever since the internal trouble arose within the "Shanghai District" and Chen Mingchu turned his coat against us, the action work that displays our might had all but come to a standstill; and though several action teams were still pressing on without cease, they had achieved nothing. This state of things, reckoned by time, had lasted a good half-year, and it was for this above all that Mr. Dai kept pressing me by letter and telegram with so many rebukes.

I too understood that action work at this moment could produce three great effects: first, to hearten ourselves; second, to overawe the enemy; third, to show that, though we had suffered a heavy blow, our strength yet remained. And all of this, of course, was fundamentally in coordination with national policy.

By October 18, 1939, a little over two months after I took office, the "Shanghai District" at last completed a sanction case of some considerable influence; and the course of it, in outline, was thus:

In the past year or two, the French Concession police station had been the most zealous of all in arresting anti-Japanese elements, and had repeatedly led parties, together with Japanese plain-clothes gendarmes, to search out the resistance-work units we had hidden about Shanghai; and the man found upon inquiry to be the most zealous of them was Cheng Haitao, the Chinese chief inspector of the political department of the French police. It was this same man who, on July 14, 1939, had brought Japanese gendarmes to search the fourteen office premises of our "Shanghai District."

Much as we detested such men, the attitude we had always held toward them was not one of "hostility"; for the great majority of the Chinese police and detectives serving in the French and the International Settlement stations did so for their livelihood, and even when, in carrying out their "duty," they did us some harm, we bore it with forbearance and never took reprisal. But when we met with one willing to play the tiger's accomplice, bent in his heart on being our enemy, then he was a degenerate of the War of Resistance, and at the same time a great obstacle in our work; and, forced by the situation, he must of course be cleared away. The French police chief inspector Cheng Haitao was just such a man.

We had warned him over the telephone, hoping he might change his ways: if he would but go through the motions of his post, let that be enough—why be so very much in earnest? At the same time we admonished him with the greater principle, telling him: do not forget that you too are a Chinese; we are "soldiers" standing on the front line to defend the country and grapple with the enemy, and by no means "ruffians" who do evil—pray understand this clearly. Otherwise, we should be obliged to deal with him.

But this fellow Cheng was obstinate past all mending; not only was he stubborn and arrogant, he answered in a contemptuous tone: "I don't care; do as you please." The man's conscience was dead, past all cure; and so, through the proper channel, we petitioned our superiors to sanction him.

Here a word of explanation is needed: the "Shanghai District" was merely a work-executing unit, and had in itself no authority to sanction whomever it pleased; by the rule of an unwritten law, the personal data of the "proposed object," together with the relevant facts, had to be reported item by item to our superiors for their ruling, and only after approval could the matter be handed to a subordinate unit for execution. For instance, a given man might on the surface already meet the conditions for being sanctioned, and yet behind the scenes he might just possibly be one of our own most capable and trusted comrades—there was no telling; and so the necessary procedure had first to be completed.

After the Cheng Haitao case had been granted sanction by order, the "Shanghai District" issued separate orders to the First, Second, Third, and Fourth Action Brigades, each to carry it out as opportunity offered along its own line of work, and first to report for approval its work leads and plan of execution. The order went out, and none of the brigades made any response; this was at the end of September and the beginning of October.

Half a month passed, and still there was no word from any brigade; and because the objects designated for sanction by order were many, the assistant secretary in charge of action at the "Shanghai District" had made no special proposal to press on the Cheng Haitao case, so that it was carelessly left lying there. On the morning of the nineteenth of October, the first thing I did by habit upon rising was to open that day's newspaper and read first the important international news, then the local news; and this day, of course, was no exception. When I saw upon the paper the item "A certain Cheng shot dead," it dawned on me at once that this must beyond doubt be our doing. Reading the content closely, the report said: on an electric-light pole near the scene where Cheng was shot, a small slip of paper had been pasted, on which were written the words "the traitor's lackey Cheng Haitao has been duly executed." This was strange, for by our invariable practice we leave no clue and no trace; and by these signs it seemed, after all, not our doing.

When I hurried to the office to inquire whether any report had come, they told me that no report from any unit had yet been received. The courier who collected the mail would not be back for another hour or more; it was now nine o'clock, and there was nothing for it but to wait for her return, see whether there was word, and then deal with it.

Because of the repeated accidents of the preceding period, the courier liaison between the District headquarters and the field had been added to again and again, and contact reduced, which was not only wanting in flexibility but also failed to seize the moment; it helped security, yet in the end was no sound policy. By the present rule, from the top downward it ran: a courier of the District headquarters would deliver or collect the mail at the inner courier station; a courier of the inner station would deliver or collect at the outer courier station; and then the outer station would reach each field unit, and the field unit in turn its own subordinates. Back and forth like this, it was really too sluggish and too cumbersome. Beyond this there was no other auxiliary or remedial method (afterward it was greatly improved). Had I not felt its inconvenience in the flesh, perhaps I should not have been in such haste to seek improvement.

I waited until a few minutes past ten, and the courier came back as usual. Among the letters she brought was a report from the Fourth Action Team; and, unable to wait for the assistant secretary in charge to deal with it, I took it over and opened it myself. Such an act was, strictly speaking, again an overstepping of authority.

This report was only a small paper packet, about the size of a large matchbox; opening the outer layer of wrapping paper, inside was a small slip of paper, folded up. Though it stood on no ceremony, it yet bore a serial number, and the signatures above and below, and the time of dispatch—which, to us, made it a formal official document.

The content was written in plain text, with no cipher in place of it and no code words. The report said: "On the eighteenth of October at six-twenty in the afternoon, at the corner of Beile Road and Kangti Road in the French Concession, this team has shot dead the French police chief inspector Cheng Haitao, who was on record as ordered to be sanctioned."

The report went on: it was learned by investigation that Cheng Haitao sometimes passed through the area of Beile Road and Kangti Road in his own private rickshaw, seemingly with no special guard, though his destination was not fixed. Four team members, xxx and others, had therefore been assigned, in two shifts taking turns, to lie in wait at the corner of that road, and had done so for several days. About six in the afternoon of the eighteenth of October, sure enough, they spied Cheng riding along the right side of the road, borne complacently on; whereupon the member xxx gave cover at hand, and another member xxx drew his pistol and fired two shots, killing him on the spot. At the end of the document it further said: "Because there was no certainty of carrying out this case, we were unable to report for approval beforehand."

The original report did contain the names of the team members, but they have slipped my memory; and for this I must gravely offer my apologies to the "Nameless Heroes," hoping that in the Bureau's official record your names may appear, and hoping too that the relevant persons who know of this matter will offer their guidance.

As for the team's performance and the handling of the case, it was broadly thus: besides reporting it up and requesting a citation, we at once wrote back to the team to commend it. Only on the question of whether the team had posted the slogan "the traitor's lackey Cheng Haitao has been duly executed" near the scene, the team was still asked to inquire clearly and make a full reply.

Afterward, the Bureau headquarters swiftly wired its commendation, and issued for the case a reward of three thousand yuan in fabi, by way of encouragement. In those days three thousand yuan was already a fairly large sum. To keep the apportioning of the reward fair, the "Shanghai District" made a certain decision, and this decision by degrees became a fixed usage—one might even say it was made a system: fifteen per cent of the whole was issued to all the inner-duty comrades together, and though what each received was little, its meaning lay not in the size of the sum. The other eighty-five per cent was handed to the unit that had carried out the work, and the head of that unit distributed it to the meritorious as he saw fit, reporting afterward; the District headquarters laid down no rigid rule. This method was in force for years, and never once did any comrade, inner-duty or field, voice any opinion over the matter of the reward money—one might as well say that all reckoned it fair. There is one small point I should like to add as well: the District Chief ought to be counted among the inner-duty staff, yet I was too ashamed to share in the reward. If anyone should think this merely the working of a "stratagem," or a petty trick for angling after fame, I need not deny it; the truth was simply that I had already had my fill of the "sense of achievement" in the work, so that a paltry bit of reward money could not move me at all. As for what a "stratagem" might be, in those days I really did not yet understand.

Now to speak of the French Concession police authority, which had a twofold connection with this case—the body commonly called the "French police station." The dead man, Cheng Haitao, being the chief inspector of the French police, must first be reckoned a party to it; and the shooting having occurred within the French Concession, within its jurisdiction, the station was of course also the executor of the Concession's law. From the moment the Cheng case broke, they showed, on the surface, no reaction whatever—they did not so much as issue a declaration that the gunman would be run down and brought in; it was as if the thing had never happened. And yet, in fact, we did indeed reap the effect we had looked for—kill one to warn a hundred. According to a report from our inside man in the French station: when the Chinese police and detectives of the station—especially that set in the political department who dealt specially in hunting down political prisoners—heard that the chief inspector Cheng Haitao had been shot dead in the street, they truly went pale and looked at one another; those who had some acquaintance with each other put their heads together and whispered in secret, warning one another, for fear that a single false step would land them in trouble.

As for the foreign police of the French station, they of course did not treat it as a common criminal case; and any man of a little discernment naturally saw at a glance how the matter stood, and so kept a profound silence. From what could be learned, and from the facts themselves, it appeared that the French Concession authorities, beyond the routine filing of a record, never afterward, within their own ranks, made any demand that the "case be cracked."

The whole affair passed off in quiet, and for our future security a single obstacle had, after all, been cleared away.

In the aftermath, on the matter of the Fourth Action Team's posting of a slogan, the team affirmatively replied that there had been no such thing, on this ground: there had beforehand been no preparation for any such act, and the scene at the moment would not have allowed it; and knowing full well that it was against the rules, why do this needless thing? This was strange—then who had done it? Could it be that someone had embroidered the tale?

This was a trifling matter, and in fact one that did good and no harm, so it was not pursued further. With this the case was wholly at an end—though the internal trouble of the Fourth Team was only just then gathering. What we knew was that its leader and deputy did not get on; today the leader Liu Shiyong would file a report, tomorrow the deputy Wan Lilang would put in his word too, and each accused the other, yet neither with any concrete fact. It looked plainly like a fit of temper, and perhaps, given a while, would come to nothing.

### One Wave Not Yet Stilled, Another Rises: Fresh Trouble from Within

This was a thing that happened in about November 1939, when I had already been three months in office. The rearrangement of the several inner-duty units of the "Shanghai District" was broadly in order; the general working of both inner-duty and field was drawing daily toward the normal, and by degrees opening out. I had supposed no further internal trouble would arise—but the affairs of this world so often confound expectation. On a certain day the Shanghai District secretary, Brother Zheng Xiuyuan, told me to my face that he had again met with a fresh accident; and though I dared not readily believe such a thing could be, the facts were all there and left me no room not to believe. The course of it, as I remember, is far less full than what Brother Xiuyuan set down himself; and so I again abridge and quote from his original text, "The DDS Café: Another Narrow Escape from Kidnapping," as follows:

"In about October of the twenty-eighth year of the Republic, the Shanghai Special District received a telegraphic order from Mr. Dai to sanction a certain high-ranking employee of the French police, surnamed Geng, who had been bought up and used by the enemy. This task the District then ordered the Fourth Action Team to carry out; and early in November the team leader Wan Lilang reported that he had found a small photograph of this Geng, but could not be sure it was truly Geng himself, and hoped the District would find someone who knew Geng and could make the identification. I therefore wrote to arrange with Zhang Shengcai, leader of the Third Intelligence Group (having learned beforehand that Zhang knew Geng's face), to meet me at five-twenty in the afternoon on the ninth of November at the ticket window of the Grand Theater on Bubbling Well Road; and at the same time I bade Wan Lilang bring Geng's photograph and meet me at half past five at the Bright Café next door to the Grand Theater, so that he might hand me the photograph, and I pass it to the group leader Zhang Shengcai to check. For the sake of keeping the organization secure, no lateral connection was ever permitted between the several field units under the District, and so I did not let Wan Lilang meet Zhang Shengcai directly, but kept the liaison in my own hands between them. At the appointed time the group leader Zhang kept his engagement punctually, and I too arrived on time at the Grand Theater ticket window, and, having met him, went on to the Bright Café next door to wait for Wan Lilang. But I waited until five-forty, and still did not see him come. I therefore went back to the theater and told Shengcai that the comrade with the photograph had not kept the engagement; perhaps some accident had occurred, and we had better withdraw first. For in doing secret work in the fallen zone it was a rule that, whenever a rendezvous between comrades ran more than five minutes over, one must heighten one's guard and withdraw at once, lest the other party had come to grief and one be dragged in with him. On this occasion, because Wan Lilang was the head of an action team, and no intelligence had before this been had of any improper conduct on Wan's part—and only a little before, on the eighteenth of October, Wan's team had carried out a sanction case against a certain Cao, and carried it off successfully—of course I looked for nothing amiss. So at the Bright Café I stayed five minutes longer than usual, and only at five-forty did I leave. The next day (the tenth of November) I suddenly received a letter from Wan Lilang, saying that, the photograph of Geng not having been got the day before, he had not kept the appointment; but that he had now got the photograph, and asked that at half past five the following day (the eleventh of November) we meet instead at the DDS Café on Bubbling Well Road (note: of the DDS Cafés there were then two in Shanghai, both kept by a White Russian—one at the above address, the other on Avenue Joffre in the French Concession), and that he would hand me the photograph in person. Having received this letter, I made ready to go in person to the appointment the next day; but I did not again arrange with the group leader Zhang, meaning, when I had the photograph from Wan Lilang's hand, to go to Shengcai's lodging and let him see it. At five the next afternoon I first went to a café on Avenue Joffre kept by a German, to confer on work matters with Shao Piaowei, the inner-duty head of the Second Action Team (Shao was the able assistant of the Second Team's leader Zhao Sheng, and was likewise of Mr. Du's following. This man bore the same name as the famous newspaperman who had been killed years before, but was not the same person). Our talk over, the hands of the clock stood at a quarter past five; and I took a rickshaw by way of Route Ratard and Avenue Foch, and out along Yates Road, meaning to go to the DDS to keep Wan Lilang's appointment. But when I had got to Yates Road near the Bank of China, I suddenly saw Wan Lilang coming toward me; and I stopped the rickshaw and went over to greet him. I said: 'Did you not appoint me for half past five at the DDS Café? Where are you off to?' Wan answered: 'I am going for the photograph; please go on ahead and wait for me there, and I will be along at once.'"

"At the time I did not doubt him in the least; I sprang back into the rickshaw and had it pulled to the DDS Café on Bubbling Well Road, not far off, and got down before the door. But I had barely entered when suddenly I caught sight, at a square table to my right front, of three fellows sitting on three of its sides, each with the look of a hired thug; among them was a tall, middle-aged man facing the door, in a navy-blue Western suit. Seeing me come in, he moved his lips twice toward the two who sat with him, with a nod at me. I took warning at once that something was not right, but still went straight on toward a train-carriage booth, and just as I was about to sit down, I turned my head to steal a glance at the three big fellows—and they were all watching me with their eyes fixed. Feeling then that the matter was grave, I did not sit down after all, but turned on my heel and walked straight out. I had just pulled open the door and was standing before it, facing the road, meaning to move quickly off, when the very fellow who had jerked his chin at me also hurried out and barred my way, and with his right hand gripped my left, near where I wore my wristwatch, and asked me in Shanghai dialect: 'Who are you here to see?' I said, 'A friend.' At that moment I thought to myself: today I shall surely be kidnapped by the enemy and the puppets, and there will be no escaping my doom. Just then, on the pavement to my right front, no more than a few steps off, I spied a street constable; and I cried out loudly that here was a bad man. The big fellow too turned his head at the cry to watch the constable, and the hand that gripped my left wrist slackened a little; whereupon I wrenched free with all my might, ran behind the constable, flung my arms about his waist, and cried aloud that this was a bad man, and would he protect me. The constable, who seemed not old, only kept his eyes on the big fellow still standing before the café door, and did not go up to interfere or question him. Because I knew there were still two of their party inside the café, and that if all three came out together to seize me (before the DDS, two or three shops away, there stood a black empty sedan) I should still be in great danger, I loosed my arms from about the constable and hastened across the road into a ladies' fashion company called Hongxiang. Going in to the back, near the lavatory, I took off a gray felt hat and threw it away, and stripped off the gray patterned autumn overcoat I wore, turned it inside out, and hung it over my arm. Then, from the back door of the Hongxiang company, I walked to the corner of Moulmein Road and Weihaiwei Road and went into the Hengfengchang silk and cotton-piece shop, and, as a customer buying goods, made small talk and had a length of cheongsam cloth cut, taking that chance to put through a call to number 40000, and summoned a hired motorcar from the Chang-sung Motor Company (this was one of the largest hire-car companies in Shanghai, with as many as sixteen branches; merely to pick out the telephone number 40000 from the exchange, it is said, cost them ten bars of gold). I first went to the Sino-German Maternity Hospital on Avenue Foch to look in on my wife Yunzhen, who that very afternoon at half past five (the very time I was nearly kidnapped) had borne her first child, a boy—this was my little son Weijian, to whom I afterward gave the name Ruyi, in the sense of 'crossing danger as though on level ground.' Fearing that my wife, weak after childbirth, might take a fright, I did not then tell her of the perilous scene I had just been through. My breath a little recovered, I drove to a flat on Shanzhong Road, and reported the whole course of it to Mr. Chen Gongshu, who had not long before taken over as District Chief; and even then he was somewhat unwilling to believe that Wan Lilang had gone over to the traitors. His reason was the same as I have set forth above: since only three weeks and more before this, on the eighteenth of October, Wan Lilang had completed a sanction case of some considerable weight, and no word of any wavering in Wan had ever been had. To verify the true state of the Fourth Team, we first sent a courier to the place where we regularly liaised with the Fourth Team; and only on inquiry did we learn that two days before they had given up the lease and moved away, none knew whither. We then further ordered the working comrades we had lying hidden at the puppet Special Operations Headquarters on Jessfield Road to investigate by every means, and only so did we establish that the traitor Wan Lilang had indeed turned his coat and gone over to the enemy."

"Not long after, we came by intelligence that the puppet Special Operations, because their two attempts to kidnap me had both been foiled by my quick escape, had ordered their executing unit to send out a small squad of marksmen, among them one who knew my face, with a motorcar for their sole use, to patrol and search the roads of the two Concessions at all hours; and that if once they sighted my whereabouts, they were to open fire, with no need to seize me alive."

Having read Brother Xiuyuan's account through, let me add a few points by way of supplement, as follows:

One. The "high-ranking employee surnamed Geng" spoken of above was a figure full of the legendary; and I shall sketch him briefly, so far as I myself know—

This Geng, named Jiaji, courtesy name Jizhi, was a native of Songjiang in Jiangsu, and had studied in France. When Wu Tiecheng was mayor of Shanghai he served as Wu's French-language secretary; later he was secretary in a certain bureau of the French Concession's Municipal Council and in the French police station, so that all Shanghai knew there was a "Secretary Geng." In 1939 he was about forty years of age. When the Wang puppet regime usurped its seat, Geng too, on one pretext, resigned his French Concession post, and, retiring as a way of advancing, set up as a gentleman of leisure, in a deep mansion with a great courtyard, giving himself quite the airs of a "little tycoon." He walked the path of the "second-rate notable": on the one hand he widely gathered disciples and enlarged his standing in society; on the other he made friends among the officials, brewed lawsuits and pleaded them, yet did not batten on the common townsfolk. Now and then he too would play the "good man" and do a "good deed"; and about this time he further contrived, through the introduction of a certain Jin of the Shanghai press, to make the acquaintance of the likes of Zhou Fohai, Ding Mocun, and Li Shiqun, and had much to do with them, sometimes playing both sides to mediate some quarrel neither great nor small.

Men of this sort are, in Shanghai, plenty. Why our superiors ordered him sanctioned, I do not understand. Ever since Brother Xiuyuan tried to get a photograph, in order to make out Geng's likeness, and was nearly carried off by Wan Lilang, the business was—the work being manifold, and the objects ordered sanctioned being many besides—never given any further careful working.

On a certain day in 1943, Geng Jiaji suddenly, in the night, put a pistol to his temple and shot himself dead. Why? The tales flew thick, and for a time it became a hot topic. Some said it was because his debts had piled mountain-high; some that it sprang from an affair between man and woman. A certain Miss Liu, of some little fame, who had once had a spell of love with Geng, told me: "He was hounded to his death, alive and whole, by a few men come up from the country." This was stranger still—could it be that he had done deals with the Communist party too? Turning it over closely: a man like him, all smooth sides and slipping into every crack, sinking himself into the mire and burying himself—who is to say it could not be?

But by another concrete account, which comes nearer to reason, the course of it was this: in 1943, Hou Dazhuang, the puppet director of the Jiangsu Provincial Grain Bureau, together with one Hu Zheng (probably the deputy director), using the convenience of their office, sold off privately for profit, through the hands of Geng Jiaji, a great quantity of grain that had been requisitioned on the Japanese army's behalf, and so could not deliver it on schedule. The thing coming to light, the Japanese military were angered; and Ōi Hideo, then chief of the special-higher section of the Japanese gendarmerie in Shanghai, came forward and pressed hard for severe punishment. In the end Hou and Hu, on the grave charge of corruption, were shot; and the puppet grain minister Gu Baoheng too was sentenced to ten years' imprisonment for dereliction. Geng, seeing the affair grown big and past all mending, thus took his own life in dread of his guilt, to escape the penalty. It is said that Chen Bijun, over this business, was much displeased at the overbearing insistence of the Japanese side; and before long this Ōi Hideo was accordingly transferred away.

In sum, the causes of Geng's suicide were rather complex; and were one to string all these several grounds together and draw them out, it would turn into a tale of deduction.

Two. In Brother Xiuyuan's text there is the phrase, "and only a little before, on the eighteenth of October, Wan's team had carried out a sanction case against a certain traitor Cao, and carried it off successfully." This is the very case I have recorded above, of the sanctioning of the French police chief inspector Cheng Haitao. Brother Xiuyuan set it down as a certain Cao—no doubt a slip of the pen; it was in fact one and the same man.

Three. There is one point worth savoring. In Brother Xiuyuan's text it is written: "The next day I suddenly received a letter from Wan Lilang, saying that, the photograph of Geng not having been got the day before, he had not kept the appointment…" This was simply not the sort of thing the head of a field unit should say to a responsible superior. By our rule, whether the photograph could be got or not, one must keep the appointment at the appointed time, meet and explain the whole, and set a fresh appointment—that would have been the proper way. To say that, not having got it, he would not come, and moreover to take it on himself to alter the time and name the place—that offended our regular practice all the more. It was just because he had lately carried out an action case that everyone took him for faithful and reliable, suspecting nothing of him, and so was hoodwinked by him. Looking back on it now, it was truly too careless.

Four. In the text, Brother Xiuyuan, coerced by the three big fellows, in his sudden extremity flung his arms about the "constable's" waist and begged his protection; and his quick wit may be seen in this. Yet, though this might get him clear of the moment's peril, to cry for help in the open street and point at the other man as a "bad man" would not necessarily win the constable's belief. Thought through more deeply, it was in truth a risk.

The "constable" here spoken of was in fact simply a policeman. The name "constable" was one the Chinese in the foreign Concessions had always been used to. To the foreigners, "constable" or "policeman," it was all one—they were alike a policeman. What is amusing is that, though within the Concessions one said "constable," the moment one came into territory under our own government's rule—the so-called Chinese quarter—one changed one's tongue and said "policeman" throughout.

Five. When Brother Xiuyuan told me that Wan Lilang had already turned traitor and gone over to the puppets, I did indeed somewhat disbelieve it; for only twenty-odd days before, the Fourth Action Brigade, of which Wan Lilang was deputy leader, had carried out the task of sanctioning the very French police chief inspector Cheng Haitao who had made himself my particular foe, and it had been Wan Lilang who planned and commanded it—how could he change so fast? This was my reason for half-disbelieving; yet the facts were all there and left me no room not to believe.

The above is an abridgment from Mr. Xiuyuan's veritable record, together with a few points of supplementary explanation added by me.

Seeing how perilous the case above had been, Brother Xiuyuan truly could stay no longer. Loath as I was to send him from Shanghai, yet for his safety I had to recommend to my superiors that he be transferred and another secretary appointed to take over. The telegrams back and forth took time, and it was late—the end of December 1939—before Brother Xiuyuan, in disguise, took ship from Shanghai for Hong Kong and another posting. He left behind him too a lasting remembrance in us. In after years we met several times more; and my own lot lying, for the most part, amid hardships, I owe it to his tender solicitude that he shielded me, which makes him the harder to forget.

At this juncture there were two great matters urgently to be understood and dealt with: the one, to ascertain the cause of Wan Lilang's going over to the puppets; the other, the reorganization or rebuilding of the Fourth Action Brigade.

Of Wan Lilang's defection, it may be said that beforehand there was not the least sign; and yet certain factors worth notice had been overlooked by us. As Brother Liu Yuanshen lately told me in idle talk: the leader and deputy of that team—that is, Liu Shiyong and Wan Lilang—had never seen eye to eye, and reports came to the District from both sides, each with its own tale, attacking the other. In a case like this, one ought at once to have taken suitable measures, and by no means to let it develop, lest it breed some incident. And so it fell out: Wan Lilang's straying into the crooked path was perhaps just what our own drifting and letting-things-slide brought about. What none foresaw was that he should go so far as to take this road!

During my time as District Chief I never once saw this man Wan Lilang; and had I then been able to meet him, and had we opened our minds to one another—or, say, when he and the team leader Liu Shiyong fell into their quarrel, had I talked with each apart, listened more to what they had to say, and done a little smoothing over as the moment offered—perhaps the thing might have been headed off. A pity that this was not done.

There is a tale which cannot be believed; yet, since beyond it I have never found any firm corroboration, I set it down for what it is worth.

It is said that on a certain morning of a certain month in 1939, about nine o'clock, with a fine rain drifting from the sky, before the great gate of the puppet Special Operations Headquarters at No. 76 Jessfield Road in western Shanghai, there suddenly appeared a mysterious man, short of stature and mean of aspect, some thirty-odd years of age, a small parcel wrapped in newspaper tucked under his arm; and he gave the sentry on duty his own name as Wan Lilang, saying he had a secret matter and begged to see the officer in charge of the place and report it to him in person. The sentry, finding his coming so abrupt, was about to drive him off, when Wan opened the parcel he carried and handed it over for the sentry to inspect—and lo, it was a brand-new revolver; and only then did the man believe that this fellow had indeed some standing. He was thereupon sent in to be received, and from that time Wan was kept and employed; and the "Shanghai District" got, along with it, word of Wan Lilang's going over to the enemy. Told thus, it seems truly like a tale of legend: for in truth Wan had at first no tie whatever with No. 76, knew neither Ding Mocun nor Li Shiqun, and had no dealings before with any other traitor or puppet—so what after all were the motive and the aim of his defection? Of that, every man says his own. His manner of "offering his service" in especial—delivering himself up and recommending himself unasked—was the more a wild fancy, past all understanding. At the time, having but lately taken over the "Shanghai District," I had not yet come to grips with the work or the personnel; and though now and then I saw the tale-bearing reports in which Wan and the team leader Liu Shiyong accused each other, the assistant secretary in charge of personnel had never endorsed any opinion for handling them, so that I too let it pass as of no moment. Whether Wan's defection was thus caused by his falling out with Liu Shiyong, and so was a rash change, or whether there was some other and weightier cause, is hard to determine. And because Wan's going over to the enemy just met the moment, and answered the puppets' pressing need, it clicked at once and he was given weighty employ; he rose from First Section chief of the puppet Special Operations Headquarters all the way to director of the puppet government's Political Security Bureau, setting himself specially against the Juntong, and in the end became a great scourge of our work.

### Personnel and Funds Forever Plague the Units in the Fallen Zone

After the Shanghai District secretary, Brother Zheng Xiuyuan, was transferred away from Shanghai, the new secretary, awaited a good long while, at last arrived and took office. This post could indeed not go a single day without its man.

The new secretary Wang Yixin, a native of Wuxi in Jiangsu, was a returned student from Russia, though of his career nothing was known—only that he had been brought into the Juntong from the "Field Headquarters Investigation Section."

He came to Shanghai with his family, and after taking office lodged together with them in the newly moved First Office of the District headquarters—a place on the second floor of a Western-style flat, not far from the corner of the far end of Shanzhong Road and Avenue Haig in the French Concession. The rooms were dim of light, with a gloomy, forbidding air, and, dwelt in long, would bring on a stifling feeling, most uncomfortable. It is said such surroundings can affect a man's mood; and truly so—in this place a small unpleasantness did indeed arise.

Brother Wang Yixin was diligent in his work and most careful in the handling of daily affairs, only that he weighed things too much and had a way of being unable to let go or lay a matter down. Though he respected my views, he had his stubborn side too. For the rest, as an aide, there was really nothing in which he fell short of his post. Yet I always felt he was not forthright enough—or, say, too long-winded. I once talked over Wang Yixin's character with the reviser of this book, then the assistant secretary, Brother Liu Yuanshen; and in Brother Yuanshen's view, because Wang had undergone GPU training in Russia and after his return had for a good while taken part in Communist party work, the chief reason Wang was so hedged about was that his spirit had suffered long oppression, and he was afflicted with the fear-sickness left by a long spell of "political struggle": in his eyes every affair carried a hidden purpose, and every man harbored a plot, and to protect himself he could not but be forever on his guard, tense and wary at every turn, so that he seemed nervous and a little out of sorts. A pity that in those days I did not understand a great deal; had I known this early, we ought to have made allowance for him.

Wang Yixin and his wife were not on good terms and quarreled often; and it is said that it had not been so before. Whether it was owing to the intrusion of some third party, being a matter of a man's private life, I did not care to pry into. Once I saw with my own eyes Brother Yixin with one hand gripping his wife's hair, and with the other, fist clenched, striking her hard, while she wept and cried aloud, her voice carrying out of doors, till the neighbors on the same floor all opened their doors to ask what on earth was the matter.

This would not do; for if once you met with some meddlesome busybody who telephoned to report it to the police, would it not be inviting disaster to your door, courting trouble for yourself? Yet, out of regard for feelings, I was too abashed to say anything, and beyond a little consolation could think of nothing to be done. But I had already conceived the notion of asking for someone else in his place.

Afterward, the inner-duty comrades of the office reported that Wang Yixin's beating of his wife was no longer a single occurrence, and that of late it was near enough a small quarrel every three days, a great row every five; a word out of key, and he would fall to blows. A state of things like this had already come to affect the progress of the work and the security of the organization, and, kept up, might really bring trouble upon us. It thus the more spurred my resolve to ask that he be transferred away.

Just as I was pondering whether to ask my superiors to send some familiar hand, and yet did not know whom to ask for, I received, all unlooked-for, a telegram from above, bidding me at once help both Qi Qingbin and Zhang Zuoxing with their traveling expenses and assist them to come to Chongqing as soon as might be. Only then did I learn that Brothers Qi and Zhang had already come to Shanghai—I was overjoyed, for we had been together since boyhood.

At the end of 1939 the Japanese invaders were making great sweeps at both Beiping and Tianjin, arresting anti-Japanese elements and slaughtering them without number; and many high cadres of our Juntong—such as Zeng Che, Wang Wen, Chen Ziyi, Zhou Shiguang—had already met their glorious end. The working conditions grew worse by the day, and those whose identity was already exposed had all been ordered to withdraw one by one.

Brother Qi Qingbin, former assistant secretary of the North China Office (the North China Branch) of the "Renaissance Society," and Brother Zhang Zuoxing, former chief of the Public Security Bureau (police bureau) of Jize County in Hebei, had, at my invitation in 1938 and with approval, joined the "North China Loyal and Patriotic Army," serving respectively as chief of staff and director of the political department. In about September and October of that same year the North China Loyal and Patriotic Army's general headquarters, set up at Baodi County in Hebei, was stormed and taken by the Japanese army, with grievous sacrifice; and Brothers Qi and Zhang both broke out valiantly and so escaped. Thereafter they slipped back to Beiping and took part in other work, while I had already been sent to Hanoi and then recalled to Chongqing.

At this time I lost no moment in meeting both Brothers Qi and Zhang, who were lodging in a small hotel; the moment they saw me they said that, had I not come, they should have been in a fine plight. This scene was exactly like the shabby figure we three had cut eight years before in Nanjing, when we ate one meal and fretted over the next.

They told me: "The telegram from above, transferring us to the great rear at Chongqing, had come long since; but at Beiping, what with the repeated wrecking by the Japanese gendarmerie and the shuffling of personnel, the general working had lost its regular order, so that it was over a month before we got the notice. Luckily we were not dragged in, which may be counted great good fortune."

They further said: "The man sent from the Beiping District said that at present they could not lay out any considerable sum for travel, and hoped we would first raise it ourselves and, on reaching Chongqing, report it for reimbursement. This was a hard thing; reckon it up—from Beiping by train to Tianjin, by ship to Shanghai, then by ship again to Hong Kong, buying an air ticket to Chongqing, and having besides to lodge in hotels awaiting the flights—how much money would it all come to! Neither of us two could scrape it together; and after much consulting and asking of instructions, it was decided that we two should each raise the fare to Shanghai, and that once we reached Shanghai someone would come to receive us and arrange our onward journey for us. What was quite beyond our looking-for was that the one who came to look after us should be none other than you."

Having heard them out, it came my turn to question them; and the first thing I wished to know was whether their work had been assigned yet. They said with one voice, "Not yet." Since it had not, the matter was open to arranging. So I made my wish known: "Now, I have been transferred to Shanghai to be District Chief, and have been in office but a few months; at present I am much in need of helping hands. I wonder whether you two would stay in Shanghai and work with me? If there is no difficulty, I shall wire Mr. Dai the moment I get back." I paused a little, then went on to sound them out: "I mean to recommend Brother Ruozhai" (Qi's alternate name) "as secretary of the 'Shanghai District,' and Brother Kexin" (Zhang's alternate name) "as radio inspector. How does that seem to you two?"

The two of them looked at each other and said nothing; and after all had been silent a while, it was again I who spoke: "No need to waver—stay on, then!" They nodded to signify their consent.

We three had settled the most important matter; and only then did we exchange the greetings of long parting, and talk a little of the feelings of our separation. I left money to cover the hotel and their needs, and asked them to move to somewhat cleaner lodgings and await the reply from above. I reckoned it would take four or five days at the very fastest; and should Mr. Dai chance to be away from Chongqing, perhaps a week or so—there was no saying.

As for the inner circumstances my superiors would have to weigh—the transferring away of the present secretary, and the newly created post of radio inspector—these I did not tell them. For Zhang Zuoxing was quick of tongue and straight of heart, and it did not matter; but Qi Qingbin was another thing—an upright, foursquare, scrupulous man, and had he known the present secretary's post was already filled, nothing would have made him take it.

On the third day after the telegram went out, the Bureau headquarters wired to inquire into the origins and careers of Qi Qingbin and Zhang Zuoxing. I saw at a glance that this was because the personnel department in charge was too unacquainted with them. True enough, Qi and Zhang, save for a short spell of service in the "North China Loyal and Patriotic Army," had never formally taken part in the Bureau's field or inner work at any station or group; and though the Loyal and Patriotic Army was connected with the Bureau, its organization was after all of a separate system, so that on the Bureau's personnel cards there were simply no names of theirs. As for recalling them to the rear, that was probably a separate matter; and so they could find no personnel data on the two men, and could not endorse an opinion on my telegram—which was in the nature of things. I could only blame myself for not being seasoned enough; had I said a little more in the wire, there would have been none of these side-issues.

At this time I also thought: should it not be granted, I must fight it out to the last!

After the reply telegram had gone, several more days passed, and Mr. Dai's ruling came, which read: "All that is asked is granted." This included: one, that comrade Wang Yixin be transferred to other work; two, that Qi Qingbin be approved to succeed as secretary of the District; three, that comrade Zhang Zuoxing be assigned work by the District at its own discretion.

Over Brother Wang Yixin's transfer to other work I felt some regret, but no compunction; in truth it too was a thing that could not be helped. In this measure there was, it cannot be denied, some selfish motive of mine; and yet it was wholly for the furtherance of the work, and by no means a wish to make trouble. Indeed, to choose a capable aide is a small matter, of no account, and may as well be passed over. After Brother Yixin left, what he did I heard nothing of for many years; it is known that after the victory he once followed Mr. Dai to Shanghai; and when the mainland was given up he did not come to Taiwan, and thereafter what became of him is not known.

Qi, Zhang, and I shared hardship together in Shanghai for a good six years and more, right up to the victory in the War of Resistance before we parted, and there are many stories of that time to be told. To speak of the deep roots of our history together, it goes truly very far back, and is a long tale indeed. In the sixth chapter of Part One, "Rooting Out Traitors in the North," I once wrote a little of it in brief; and from now on I may say without the least reserve that it was as good as the three of us jointly presiding over the work of the "Shanghai District." So let me write a few more paragraphs of our past.

Zhang Zuoxing had in his early years been a stoker on the locomotives of the works at Changxindian on the Beijing–Hankou railway; and by his own self-schooling, and leaning on a little personal connection, he entered the Fourth Class of the Whampoa Military Academy, in the First Regiment, Third Battalion, Ninth Company, as a probationary recruit. In December 1925, at that same time, I—being two years under the required age—was sent up by the Qingming Middle School of Beiping and went to Guangdong, enrolled in the First Regiment, Tenth Company of the Fourth Class of the Academy as a probationary recruit. During our probation, though we ran on the same drill ground, slept beneath the same row of straw sheds, garrisoned in the same ancestral hall, and stood watch on the same stretch of city wall, yet we did not know one another. In the several months we were quartered in Huiyang County, of an evening, when there was no duty and the discipline was a little slack—because some of us were northerners—we would gather in groups of three and five to chatter idly, and turn and turn about it was still those same two topics. When the talk warmed, we would now and then get a dog to slake our craving, use a kettle for a wine-pot, and pool a few coppers to buy a little liquor to drink. In this set were both he and I, and it was thus that we first came to know each other.

The probation over, one had to pass an examination before being promoted into the Academy proper; and Zhang and I were both held back a year. Some months later he was assigned to the political branch, and I to the infantry branch, and so were listed with the Fifth Class—yet we had no chance to meet.

We met again in the spring of 1929, at the house of his brother-in-law Wang at Beiping. He was working at the Hebei provincial party headquarters, while I lodged with relatives, convalescing, with no income; and he was forever helping me out. Once he asked me to a plain meal, and there came along with him a tall, lean, lanky fellow, on whose introduction I first came to know Qi Qingbin. This little gathering left the deeper impression on me, not only for the friend the more I had made, but for the cheapest meal I ever ate. We three ate at a Shanxi house with private booths on Liangshidian Street—knife-shaved noodles with a topping (small squares of red-braised pork with the gravy); the bowls were small, three fen three apiece, and for one of no great appetite three bowls were enough to fill, coming to not even a dime; each of us added a few other things besides, and the whole came to just five dimes—truly cheap past forgetting.

Qi Qingbin, of the sixth class, political branch, was Brother Zuoxing's colleague, both serving at the Hebei provincial party headquarters, at a monthly salary and allowance of no more than forty or fifty yuan, and seemingly not much in luck. It was just because the two of them were so much at leisure that we were often together, and it was from this that our friendship was formally struck up.

Both Brothers Qi and Zhang, unable to bear to see me idling on so long, found me a post: to go to Luotun Town in Qian'an County as drill officer of the local militia, at a monthly wage of twenty silver dollars, with board and lodging. When I got there, it turned out they wanted me to lead a party against the bandits; and having come, I could not turn tail and run at the first word of a fight, so I could only steel myself and give it a try. But who could have known the bandits of that region were so formidable—forever coming and going within and beyond the Great Wall, knowing the ground, seasoned, and fierce; a raw little officer like me was no match for them, and at the first brush I was very nearly seized and flayed alive. The town elders, seeing how poorly I did, no longer wished to hire me; and the work being wearisome too, I made my way back to Beiping.

We three took counsel together, and thought it best to make a trip to Nanjing to seek fresh prospects; so, settling all our affairs, we set off together. Reaching Nanjing, Qi and Zhang passed the examination and entered the gendarmerie-police class of the Central Military Academy for further study, while I was picked out for training in the Special Research Class of the Central Military Academy. Our courses over, each went his own way; and it was not until 1938 that we three came together again at Beiping after its fall. Of what came afterward, Part One, "Rooting Out Traitors in the North," has a full record, and I shall not dwell on it further.

After Brother Qingbin took over as secretary of the "Shanghai District," he brought his family, left behind at Beiping, and found a new place to settle them. Where he lived also became the Third Office of the "Shanghai District." In the division of business, Brother Qingbin's dwelling served as our policy-making body, and the most secret and most important matters were all handled there; I went there nearly every day. It was on Seymour Road in the International Settlement.

Avenue Haig remained as before, and after the former secretary left, Brother Yuanshen presided there. Qingbin and I too went there to work.

There was also a Second Office—I forget on which road it stood—only that it was a garden villa, sun-drenched and very bright. The assistant secretaries Hu Shangwu and Gui Difei both worked there, and Qingbin and I went every day.

I asked Brother Zuoxing to take charge as radio inspector, dealing solely with the liaison of the three sets that regularly transmitted. This was a newly created post, without precedent, meant to link the District headquarters with the wireless sets, and carrying too the function of supervision and management. He did it very successfully; the District and the sets got on capitally, and the work ran most smoothly. At this time Brother Zuoxing too brought his family over, and his dwelling served as a relay station between both the District and the sets, adding one more layer of security.

Brother Zuoxing's abilities went well beyond this; the reason I did not ask him to shoulder more was that he had pockmarks on his face—one of the least fitting features for secret work. Its greatest drawback was that a man was too easily remembered and picked out by others.

Thus the "Shanghai District" headquarters had as many as four office premises, to be flexibly deployed and used; and I thought it a very good thing. Yet the funds thus laid out were no small sum, and the chief accountant Brother Mao Zongliang, who kept the money, knit his brows sorely. Though he did not object to my outlays in the work, he lowered his voice and, leaning close to my ear, said to me: "It is not that we grudge the spending—it is truly that there is no money to spend!"

Brother Zongliang too was a man whose identity was exposed in Shanghai, and being besides a relation of Mr. Dai's, he was a large target and ill-suited to stay long; and my superiors, seeing this, transferred him away in good time. The new chief accountant sent by Bureau headquarters to Shanghai, surnamed Zhao—forgive me, I cannot recall his given name—was a tall man by nature, and we called him Long Zhao. He had not the mettle of Brother Mao Zongliang: for instance, he would not disburse before approval was had, and if a thing was not passed one had to find some other way. And no wonder—Zhao's work seniority was less than Mao Zongliang's, and he could not but first guard his own post, or he truly could not bear the responsibility. This being so, I found myself somewhat hampered.

When I was chatting one day with my friend Brother Hu Yongquan, I let fall, all unmeaning, the state of our straitened funds, and told it him—merely to unburden myself, with no other intent. But he took it to heart, and, out of a friend's regard, actually set about thinking up a scheme for me. He made a trip to Hong Kong; whom he saw there I truly did not know at all; and when he came back to Shanghai and met me, he said: "Think of a name, and I will open an account for you at a bank, with the signature and seal as you please; up to five hundred thousand yuan you may draw at any time, and so you can set to work with a free hand." I thanked him from my heart; but there is no such easy thing in the world, and of course I had to ask him to make it plain. When he had explained the whole to me, it turned out this too sprang from a heart of loyal service to the country—admirable and moving. And precisely because this man too was a friend of Mr. Dai's, it had of course to have leave asked and granted first. So I sent a telegram to Mr. Dai, and his reply did not give consent. I have the document at hand, and its original text is as follows:

"Most urgent, to Shanghai, in cipher, for the hand of Brother xx to decipher: Your telegram of the fourth is received. The various difficulties of your work in Shanghai I well understand; and if you have any difficulty as to money, pray wire me at any time, and however hard my own case, I shall surely contrive a way for you. The worth and character of men like us today are not to be sold for hundreds of thousands, nor for millions of yuan. The victory of the War of Resistance is even now at hand; as the saying goes, 'to raise a mound of nine ren and fall short by one basketful'—every comrade of ours must understand the deep meaning of this word. Yongquan is very small of vision, and cannot after all cast off the tradesman's habit; the loftiness of men like us—how should he understand it? What has Yongquan done for you in Shanghai? Pray reply in full. Your younger brother Yuannong, respectfully; sent on the day, in the tenth month, from Chongqing."

The first half of the telegram, at once earnest and courteous, was moving to read; the latter half, its edge turning, opened with a scolding of another.

In truth, "the victory of the War of Resistance is even now at hand" had no direct bearing on the need of funds in the work; and as for "to raise a mound of nine ren and fall short by one basketful," we ought of course to strain every nerve to bear it out and win through our crisis—it was not that we were so poor we could hold out no longer. Turning it over again more closely, I could only blame my own artlessness and childish ignorance of the ways of the world: at the outset, having heard out Hu Yongquan's explanation, besides thanking him for his kindness I ought sternly to have said I could not accept, and there had been no need at all to wire for instructions; and had I been a little more seasoned, I might well have reported the whole to Mr. Dai for the record and shown that I had civilly declined—would that not have been very handsome? As it was, I got my nose rubbed in the dust, and had brought it on myself; whom was I to blame?

Brother Hu Yongquan was an outsider whom Mr. Dai had detailed to assist me when I was station chief at Tianjin; he never took part in our work, held no title, drew no pay. I felt that such men were not too many; and, aided by him more than once, I always kept a heart of gratitude, and never once thought he would use our name to feather his own nest—nor do I believe he would do such a thing. Mr. Dai held that "Yongquan is very small of vision, and cannot after all cast off the tradesman's habit; the loftiness of men like us—how should he understand it…" True enough, he was after all a man of the marketplace; that he could, whole-heartedly and in greater or lesser measure, help us, was already no small thing, and one need not, it seems, have set too high a hope on him.

Writing to this point, looking back on the past, I shall be a little unrestrained and say a few more words. Mr. Dai set great store by face; and to guard his own dignity and the pure honor of the body, in his domain there was never any such thing as "there is no way." To say before "outsiders" that funds were short, and to accept another's aid, was not only a loss of face but a breach of decorum too. I dimly recall that in our "founding" days something of the like seemed to have happened; but the circumstances were different—one time is one thing, another time another, and they are not to be spoken of in the same breath. Before I drafted my request for instructions, I truly gave it too little thought; had I but thought a little more, there would have been no such unhappy consequence.

I replied to Mr. Dai, and set forth truthfully that Brother Hu Yongquan had indeed helped me greatly, and begged him to set his mind at rest. As for the matter of the funds, I said not a word.

Being but a few months in office, and by heedless reckoning having come short both in public and in private, I suppose the District accountant in charge of finance, and even the chief accountant sent by headquarters, must have reported it to my superiors. In other words, they had already reported this state of things up. Not long after Mr. Dai's telegram declaring plainly, "If you have any difficulty as to money, pray wire me at any time, and however hard my own case, I shall surely contrive a way for you," I received another telegram from Brother Zhang Gunfu (Guanfu), in charge of finance at Bureau headquarters, which read in brief: "The several sums you have disbursed are, by order, approved to be written off in full." The exact figures I can no longer recall clearly, but it was, roughly, a private overdraft above a thousand and a public shortfall over ten thousand.

A state of things like this had, since I entered the work, happened more than once; and it was for this very reason that I fell into the bad habit of "spend first and speak later," and never troubled my head over where the money was to come from.

### Reunited in a Strange Land: A Winding, Wondrous Exploration Reopens

It was autumn; having eaten my supper at home, in the deepening dusk I moved a chair out onto the little balcony to take the air and let my eyes wander idly.

Of a sudden I saw a man pass before my door from east to west; the light was not strong, yet I could see clearly—was this not Fan Xing? Looking again more closely, who else could it be but he? By instinct I was just about to call out his name, when all at once I came to myself—no; a hail like that would let him know where I lived, would it not? I hurried back into the room, threw on a coat, put on my shoes and laced them; quick as I was, this brief delay was enough that, when I ran downstairs, opened the door, and hastened a few steps, there was no shadow of him to be seen. I strolled a turn round the block, but where was there any meeting him now? I could not but stand disconsolate a good while.

Fan Xing, also named Fan Jiman, a Sichuanese, called himself of the sixth class of the Military Academy; of his affairs there is a long passage of record in my humble work "Rooting Out Traitors in the North." When I was station chief at Beiping I took him into the work as a "directly-subordinate courier," dealing solely in "international intelligence," at which he was most excellent. In 1935, during that spell when I fled my post in dread of guilt and went far off to Inner Mongolia, he had for a time acted as chief of the "Beiping Station." When I slipped back from Suiyuan to Beiping to gather news, I once sought him out at his lodging in the YMCA hostel; and, not disdaining me, he even opened his purse and helped me with a sum of money. After I returned to Nanjing to ask for my own punishment, served over five months in prison, and was then sent back to Tianjin to take over as station chief, I often went back and forth to Tianjin; and it was understood that by then the "Beiping Station" had changed hands, that Fan Xing's whereabouts were unknown, and that none knew where he had gone—what it was all about, one could not find out.

This man was not to be lightly regarded; he was, of all I met in my life, the most legendary and mysterious figure. Here I must first make plain: both his thought and his conduct were much in question. In 1933 I had, at Beiping, asked in person for instructions of Mr. Zheng Jiemin, who presided over the North China work; and he laid down for me several principles for dealing with the matter: "If it is money he is after, we can meet him accordingly—what should be spent, do not grudge. Supposing he has some political background or international connection, that is just the work-lead we could most wish for; we may as well fall in with his plan, and carry on a testing sort of 'intelligence war' and 'political struggle.' The doubtful points you have found, do not hasten to get answered at once, for up to now we have suffered no loss whatever. From now on you should observe coolly, and on no account provoke him in word or act, or let slip any flaw; and it would be best if you could build up a private feeling between you, which will have a steadying effect. As for the intelligence data he 'obtains' or 'supplies,' handle it hereafter with care, keep the originals, so that they may be gathered and reviewed, and compared one with another before and after."

Thereafter I always kept touch with him in accordance with Mr. Zheng's instructions. To my lasting regret, through a dereliction of my own, the connection was broken off in the interval, and the line at last was cut. Now, having by chance found him in Shanghai, I must by all means contrive to find him again—for this was a work-lead of great value.

By my reckoning, since he came this way on foot, perhaps he lodged near by; or, if not, there must in this quarter be some place he had cause to go; and, given a little time, I might well see him again. I had thought too of advertising for him in the paper—but that was both inconvenient and no real solution; and even should he see the paper, he would not necessarily answer it and come out. To watch from the balcony was easy enough, but I feared it would still be a case of seeing yet not reaching; and to keep watch in the street—should ill luck bring us to a narrow path where I did not wait him out, but was myself waited out by others, would that not be more troublesome still? Turn it as I might, I could think of no most effective and most proper method; and in the "Shanghai District" there was not a soul who knew his face—there was, save myself, truly no one to help.

Some seven or eight days later, on an idle evening, near nine o'clock, it being too early to sleep and dull to sit, a sudden whim took me; and, dressing anyhow, I made up my mind to go out for a stroll—it was only at such hours that it was at all suited to my moving about freely.

Turning right out the door, not far off, there was a Doumer Theater, which showed only second- and third-run foreign films. Though I often passed by it, I had never had the least notion of going in to see a picture. But this evening I was caught by the bill posted on the board—it was, it turned out, a Western starring Wallace Beery; what the title was I cannot recall, but that is no matter, for what I like is just that rough-hewn quality of Wallace Beery's. I bought a ticket and went in; it had been showing some minutes already.

There were not many people in the whole house, and after the show they were all gone in a moment. I walked homeward alone, head down and steps unhurried; ahead of me a figure was also moving slowly, and by his gait, somewhat splay-footed, in a pair of old leather shoes worn crooked at the heels, he swayed the more from side to side. Looking higher, at the narrow shoulders, the bowed back, the lowered head—these were the more like him.

Coming to the fork where Geluoxi Road met Route Doumer, the man ahead turned, meaning to go off toward Geluoxi Road; and just then he too turned his head and glanced at me. From the half of his face I could see, I was already sure it was indeed he. At that moment, past my own governing, I cried out, "Jiman!" He started, and at once perceived too that the man walking behind him was I. We both stepped forward toward each other, clasped hands, and for a good while neither made a sound.

It was I who first opened, asking him: "How long have you been in Shanghai?" He did not answer me directly, but only said, "It is a long story." He pointed to an alley not far off and said again: "I live alone in that lane"; then, pausing, added with weight: "Just I, alone—if you do not mind, pray come and sit at my place, and we can talk more at leisure." I would of course not decline his invitation, though I still kept a little wariness. He walked ahead and I followed. The lane was not long, and the houses, one after another, were all of much the same plan. Past five or six households, he pushed open a half-height wooden gate, mounted the high stone steps, drew out a bunch of keys, and opened the first door of the house. Within was a narrow passage, black as pitch; groping along, he opened a second door—the very room he lived in.

He went in first and turned on the electric light, and I stepped in after him. Parlor and bedroom were all here. The room was not large, yet looked very roomy; for, save a camp bed made up, all the rest were odds and ends. Yes—in that corner by the window was a grease-blackened pressure stove, and on its frame a tin kettle for boiling water; and at the foot of another wall was a heap of clutter, covered with an old newspaper, out of which stuck what looked like a worn-out sock. And the only place to sit was the camp bed.

I asked him: "Peng Yaluo is not here?" He said: "She is in Shanghai, but we are not together." Peng Yaluo was Fan Jiman's lady friend, you might even call her his "beloved"; back in Beiping they had lived together. The reason I opened from the gentle side was to make the air a little easier, the better to talk on. A few idle words gone through, I told him, with nothing kept back, all that had befallen me since we parted at Beiping, down to my being sent to take charge in Shanghai—every word true, with no falsehood at all. All I held back was only that I did not tell him where I lived.

At first he squatted there and listened closely; later he simply sat down on the floor. He asked no questions; it seemed that however much I said, so much he listened, and what I did not say, he asked nothing of. At the same time, he was waiting for me to question him. At this moment what I most needed to know was whether he still had any working tie with the Bureau. This was a key question; for if he was even now still working for the Bureau, then what we had to talk of would be wholly different.

So I asked him straight out: "Do you still keep in touch with the Bureau now?" He answered: "Since I left Beiping it has been broken off." I did not follow with a single "Why?"—for I feared he would find it hard to hit on the aptest word to answer me. By our work there was never any such thing as leaving one's post of one's own accord; and by what he said, "since I left Beiping it has been broken off," it was plain he had quit the work and given it up. If so, there must be some grave cause behind it; would he tell me of it frankly? I would see, presently, how he explained.

Then I sounded him out, saying: "I very much hope now to have your help; I wonder whether, as at Beiping, you can gather for me some intelligence bearing directly on the War of Resistance, especially of a military and an international kind. If you are willing to resume the working tie, I will report it up for you; and if this is only a cooperation between us two, that too may serve—what do you say?"

He hesitated a good while, drumming ceaselessly with the forefinger of his right hand upon his left palm, and at last, putting his two hands together, answered me: "The lines are there, and I can do it; only, it were best it be a thing between us two alone, and on no account reported above. Of course, I understand that you cannot but report it; then pray use some false name, and this at all costs you must promise me—the two words 'Fan Xing' must never be brought up."

Full of doubts as I was, I could not but promise him; for I knew him deeply—should there be the least wavering in my look, tomorrow he would move house and be gone without a farewell. So better to hold him steady first; whatever the questions, sooner or later they would be answered. So I told him: "I can promise you—only, you must not…" I had got but this far when, not waiting for me to go on, he cut in: "Set your mind at rest, I shall not disappoint you." And thus a bargain was struck between us.

The night was already far gone—past twelve—and we agreed to meet again the next day at supper, about six in the afternoon, at his place. I rose to take my leave, and he saw me to the door. Out of the lane and round a turning, a few steps and I was home; I felt sure he would not trail after me.

When we met again, I brought two hundred yuan to give him, and said: "Of this two hundred, one hundred is a gift from me personally to buy yourself a few things; the other one hundred is a subsidy issued by the office, for the opening up of a work-line. Hereafter two hundred yuan will be issued each month, and if there be any special outlay, it may be put forward as an item for consideration." In addition, Fan Xing and I made several agreements:

That I myself should be in direct liaison with him, never through a third hand, and that this agreement be altered only by consent of both.

That we meet once or twice a week, each time appointing the time and place of the next; and, should any urgent matter arise, I would go to him. That, should he change his address, he must notify me—that is, he must without fail tell me where he lived.

That the things (intelligence data) he gave me must be marked with their true source.

That he should try to answer any question we put to him.

That after three months we should hold a review, and each seek to better his part, and settle any matters left unfinished.

I went over the points thus agreed once more, and he assented to them one by one. Only on the point of "marking the true source of the intelligence" did he wear rather a reluctant look, though he said nothing further.

The proper business done, I drew him out to a pocket-sized little Western restaurant at the mouth of the lane, and we ate a light meal—one soup and one dish—yet it cost no small sum; for who could have known this little eatery was very famous in Shanghai, a tureen of thick chicken-giblet-and-mushroom soup and a fillet steak being just its signature dishes, so that the dearness of the price may be imagined.

Over the meal I expressed my heartfelt thanks for the help he had given me in years past; and at the same time we had set up a working tie without precedent.

Afterward I truly did not report the true state of things, and did indeed use a false name in place of the intelligence-giver. That I dared be so "bold and reckless" had, of course, its reasons in me: first, the one who had at the outset best understood Fan Xing's inner story was Mr. Zheng Jiemin, and even Mr. Dai himself, though he might know of the matter, knew but the general outline. At present Mr. Zheng was busy with the affairs of the General Staff and rarely went over the Bureau's papers; to report to Mr. Dai by wire, again, would be hard to make clear; and should it be rejected, what then? As for the others, they neither understood this matter nor necessarily had the largeness for it, and might very likely raise complications and draw out needless trouble. Better, then, that I take the greater burden on myself alone. Happily its aim was the work, and so there was nothing much in it. I had, moreover, a plan in reserve: should I have the chance to see Mr. Zheng Jiemin, to ask him to put in a word for me before Mr. Dai, and then it would be much easier managed.

Herein was a problem that was never cleared up; in truth I had long wished to ask Fan Xing a question: "Under what circumstances did you leave Beiping and come to Shanghai? Why give up a good post as station chief and be willing to come here and drift?" But I could not ask; I was sure he would answer evasively and would by no means tell the true state of things. Then why not ask Bureau headquarters?—for at least I should learn half. But because, once I asked, I must first report the case truthfully, and should an order come down bidding me do thus and so with Fan Xing, how should I then dispose of it? For this reason I let it drift on; I judged there was for the present no urgency in seeking an answer, and there would be chances enough hereafter.

Of this matter the colleagues of the "Shanghai District" too were all in the dark; only to the new District secretary, Brother Qi Qingbin, after he took office, did I tell the whole truth and my own inner purpose.

Before long—within about two weeks—intelligence came from Fan Xing, most of it of the Japanese military, and now and then some of Japan's foreign dealings. At a glance every piece seemed important; weighed closely, they always ran to the large and vague; and the sources he marked out for the intelligence, though they hung together in reason, I dared not wholly believe. Still, one could not say they were without value for reference. My way of handling it was to note down certain doubtful points in the matter and require him to "re-check" them. What gave me some slight comfort, and at the same time left me unable to fathom him, was that he could actually come by fairly acceptable answers. Not every piece was so, but the proportion was not low; and this is a riddle I have never been able to unravel in all these years.

Another thing was that in reviewing and judging his intelligence we were forever on our guard, daring not the least carelessness, for fear of falling into his "counter-intelligence play." Yes—when Fan Xing and I chatted, I had, half by design and half not, dropped him hints in words; and he, for his part, feigned not to take my meaning, looked this way and that and talked of other things; and when I drew the thread back to the main point, he deliberately turned it aside again. Yet how should I not know that in a thing like this there is no morality or friendship to be relied on between the parties? Even a signed treaty could not bind it; and a bare verbal assurance in the air, or such gestures as thumping the chest and hooking little fingers, had even less real force. What was to be relied on was, after all, one's own power of judgment.

Was it not the evening after the day I first met Fan Xing that I drew him out to a little eating-house? Beforehand I had already set a man waiting there. When we parted, that man tailed him, to see what places he went to and what sort of people he met. I charged this man to keep up a regular watch and to submit written reports at all times.

By report, Fan Xing went alone, at no set hour each day, to a secondhand bookshop within a small arcade on Bubbling Well Road. This shop sold old Western-edition books and dealt as well in Chinese rubbings. It looked as though he might be the very owner of the shop. Now and then a thin, small woman, plainly dressed, with two big front teeth in her mouth, would come and sit a while, then presently leave. What puzzled one most was that, several days running, not a single customer came to the door.

By report, on one occasion a foreigner was seen to sit a while in this secondhand bookshop, acquainted with Fan Xing; they murmured together in low tones, their manner not at all furtive—of course one could not hear what they said. The man I had sent went a step further and tailed this foreigner, and tailed him to a firm on the Bund with the sign "Deer-Horn Trading Company." Following it up, the proprietor of the Deer-Horn Trading Company was a Latvian. Latvia, Estonia, and Lithuania are the three small Baltic states, and had already been swallowed up by Soviet Russia. At the time it was still not clear whether this Latvian was a refugee like the White Russians, or a docile subject under Red Russian rule; or else—perhaps he was no Latvian at all.

The man charged with the surveillance had not the means to probe deeper. But this much could be set down for certain: that Fan Xing himself never once went to that firm.

By my own analysis and judgment on the many signs already found:

One. The thin small woman who went to the secondhand bookshop was surely Peng Yaluo, for the two big buck teeth were just her mark. From this we may see that the two of them still kept some measure of tie; and this tie might well be beyond the private affections of man and woman.

Two. The Latvian who murmured with Fan Xing, whatever his nationality, was of course no pure merchant; not only had he to do with politics, he might well be the intelligence-supplier too—might even be a key figure of the double character, political and intelligence in one. The important point is that behind this man there must be a still higher controller or manipulator.

Three. Think not that Fan Xing was poor to the point of wretchedness; the substance of his life was by no means so simple. Tracing back from this point, his leaving Beiping was, as I see it, brought about by some irresistible and mighty pressure, and not of his own will.

Four. That he this time consented to supply us intelligence was wholly passive; money was not the sole condition, and yet, to his circumstances, one cannot say it was no help. Besides, toward the organization I represented—the Juntong—he harbored a strong dread.

Five. Whether his supplying us intelligence was a personal act, or done with the tacit leave or support of the "man behind," was at this moment hard to conclude. That not a single piece from first to last was true material does not mean one could be sure it would breed no counter-effect; for a thing of this kind is at bottom crafty and ever-changing.

The preliminary conclusion we came to was that this was a work-line worth carrying on; only, one should the more take care, and it was decided that at set intervals I should bring it up and hold a review with Brother Qingbin.

Of the story of Fan Xing, let this be set down for now as a stage. The tie between him and me was, in just this state, kept up right down to the end of 1941. For a year and more it was I alone who liaised with him in person; and each time we met, I always had to guard myself a little beforehand—not to the pitch of dread, yet unable to take it wholly at ease. In truth one cannot say there was no private feeling between us; it was just because the work I did was secret work that I could not let feeling rule me.

In the middle of 1940 we suddenly made a fresh discovery as to his doings: he and his lady friend Peng Yaluo were both no simple pair. When the time comes I shall set it down together with the related facts.

Many were the young men of hot blood who threw themselves into the ranks of the resistance to withstand the outer foe. The "New Group One" under the "Shanghai District" was just such a body, made up of these patriots. They were the fresh vigor of the vanguard, and were to mold for our secret-service organization a perfect type. And in every showing of the work to come, they displayed the more their all-round mastery of both pen and sword.

Such a secret unit—of the purest composition, of especial working efficiency, and able, moreover, to move freely at the enemy's front and rear—could only have been born of that age, in those circumstances. In the forty years since, intelligence work has still gone on without cease, and every kind of thing has made mighty strides; yet to look for another working unit like the "New Group One" is a hard thing to come by.

We did not risk ourselves for the sake of secret work, still less use the secret service to seek our own advancement; we loved home and country, and for the resisting of tyranny and the withstanding of the outer foe were willing to give ourselves in sacrifice. Just because of this, we laid stress that the secret service too must have its moral norms.

The writer solemnly avows: the manner of the "Juntong" was one that valued morality; and though it is hard to lay down for the secret service a set of moral standards, one ought broadly to accept the notion that "he who steals the enemy's intelligence is no thief, and he who slays a country-selling traitor—how is he a ruffian?" For their doings were all for the defense of the country and the welfare of the people. Whatever tales the outside world may tell—of other things I dare not speak, but in the field of work I came in contact with, we never did those things that outrage heaven and reason, that extinguish humanity, that go against morality.

To sanction a traitor is the very act, righteous and proper, of upholding the moral tradition; and to hate evil as a foe fully displays the lofty character of the loyal and brave. On the early morning of December 25, 1939, at half past three, the two men who had betrayed the organization and gone over to the puppets for glory, Chen Dirong (Mingchu) and He Xingjian (Tianfeng), were at last shot dead by our action men before the Weldon Dance Hall on Yuyuan Road in Shanghai. The whole course of it was most complex and winding, and some questions remain to this day unanswered.

Prompted by this case, we drew up an item, making ready to kill a few more armed Japanese.

Ever since I took office in Shanghai, hemmed in by circumstance and grown a degree more careful, I never as a rule brought documents home; nor were documents kept at home, so as to be ready for surprise searches and accidents. But this did not mean that the moment I came home I set the work quite aside and minded nothing. To give an example: thinking and sketching out are both work, and need not necessarily be put into writing. Besides, now and then I had to give some instruction over the telephone.

At the time I was renting a portion of another's house, and the telephone was set in the parlor; to make a call out I still had to wait my chance, or there were always some scruples in speaking. In those days there were as yet no public telephones on the streets or before the shops, and unless one could borrow a call while buying something, it was nothing like so convenient as now.

Since there was need to use the telephone, it was of course better to move and live on one's own. So the notion arose of finding a house and moving; but I had not yet begun to ask anyone to look for one.

One day, having slept very late and risen very early, I was making ready to draw up into an implementation plan the whole personnel-reorganization scheme I had conceived in the night, to propose it to my superiors for adoption. Unexpectedly, no sooner had I stepped out the main gate than I ran into Fan Xing, who had just come out of the lane; he plainly saw me, yet turned his head away and pretended not to. This, on the contrary, left the more of a mark; and whether or not there might be ill consequences, since my dwelling had been discovered, I must move at once.

The matter of finding a house I again put upon Brother Hu Yongquan. He had one great merit: he never asked me "why."

This house stood at the corner of Seymour Road and Sinza Road in the International Settlement, a Western-style flat with three bedrooms and two halls, on the ground floor of a four-story building, the first house on the right as one entered the lane. The first door was a glass door; pushing it open and going in, one more turn to the left and one was there. There was no doorkeeper or resident guard stationed, so that coming and going was as good as unwatched. Going round to the back, there was another back door, with a narrow stair by which one could go up to the roof terrace. The terrace was open, and one could come down again by another household's stair—for me, most convenient.

Inside the rooms was a ready full set of furniture, all brand-new, as if never used. One had only to move in with bedding and cooking things to keep house. Brother Hu Yongquan told me that this place had originally been the boudoir of the film star Gu Lanjun, done up for her by a certain man who called himself "X the Great Mountain Recluse"; as for whether anyone had ever lived there, that was not pursued further. For Brother Yongquan, patting my shoulder, said: "Live here with an easy mind; there will be no entanglement whatever."

What pleased me most was that there was a telephone already installed; and who could have thought that this telephone would, later, all but cost me my life.

To move in and out within the Shanghai Concessions, one need not register with the household office; one moved about at will, and no one at all inquired into it. Should one meet with one or two who came to "offer congratulations," a couple of coppers sent them off. In this quarter, it seemed, one had not yet seen any of those disreputable "petty shakedowns."

The house settled on, we moved at a word. My household, apart from what we wore and slept in, and the pots, bowls, ladles, and dippers of the kitchen, and odds and ends besides, had not a single heavy thing, so that moving was very simple.

On the day of the move I went off alone very early to the office. The hiring of a cart and the shifting of things were all done for me by Yongquan's younger brother Yong'an; and by the time I came home it was already a new scene. To say a word of no discernment—a house so richly splendid I had never yet lived in.

This new address, save the outsiders the Hu brothers, I told no one else; even the District secretary Brother Qi Qingbin and the wireless inspector Brother Zhang Zuoxing did not know where I lived. Apart from the few named above, I had not a single private friend in Shanghai; and though my wife had relatives long resident here, I did not favor keeping up with them. Seen thus, no wonder some say that men of our trade know not even their nearest kin. But who could enter into the bitterness of such solitude?

Many as were the new house's merits, one could not call it without a fault; being on the ground floor, and near the lane's mouth, by day one did not notice it, but come night—especially in the deep, still hours—there would often be a spell of clacking leather shoes sweeping past outside the window, and only when they had gone far off would I set down the heart that had been hanging. I often thought: sooner or later that day would surely come!

The day after I moved, my only friend in Shanghai, Brother Hu Yongquan, came to call, bringing all the receipts of this house-leasing and handing them over to me one by one. Among them were the lease contract, the receipt for the transfer fee, the receipt for the furniture assignment, the receipt for the first month's rent, and the fares and wages besides. He meant them for me to use in accounting. In the past, receipts of this sort the chief accountant and the District accountant always asked to keep; at present, a great many were already lodged with the District accountant. Though I thought it not quite proper—for should there be any stir in the accounts office, that would be past bearing—yet, given the real difficulties, we could not but keep books and account; and so it had all along been left a pending matter, never fitly resolved. This time it fell to me myself—what was to be done? I could not tell Brother Yongquan the inner state of things, and could only set them by for the time in my house, to await consulting with the District accountant Chen Xianrong before deciding.

In those days, to rent a house to live in within the Concession, there was already "key money"—that is, the sum of the "transfer fee" and the "furniture assignment" listed above. This key money was usually reckoned in "bars"; the greater part of it went to the former tenant, a small part being shared to the head landlord. Later the head landlords, to get a few coppers more, would no longer lightly agree to a transfer, so that there were also cases of subletting directly without notifying the head landlord. This bred a good deal of dispute. The transfer-fee receipt and furniture-assignment receipt Hu Yongquan gave me were just the whole of the "key money." To put it thus: the transfer fee was equal to a handling charge, and the furniture assignment, of course, cost several times more than buying new; yet what Hu Yongquan managed for me was much cheaper than the market rate.

Our talk of the house over, Brother Yongquan told me he must make another trip to Hong Kong before long: partly to render an account to that warm-hearted friend who had been willing to put up funds to help us, and partly to see to a little business of his own. I understood well enough—what he called "his own business" was just to make a bit of money. But I truly did not know how he made it.

As mentioned above, I had lately drawn up a "personnel-and-organization adjustment scheme," and proposed it to my superiors for adoption. This scheme Brother Qi Qingbin and I devised jointly; its gist was the making of action and intelligence into one, and the enlarging of the action establishment, and the like. Mr. Dai accepted this proposal, replying in brief: "The plan you have drawn up to expand the Shanghai District's action organization I have approved; pray seek out loyal and brave men and set it up at speed; and the weapons needed, pray contrive to buy in." By "pray seek out loyal and brave men" the reply meant "recruiting on the spot," for the cadres sent in did not necessarily suit the local environment, and the newly trained men were not enough to go round. And as for "the weapons needed, pray contrive to buy in," it was not that we lacked such weapons, but that their transport was arduous and cumbersome. On this I would especially explain: this was just a feature peculiar to the "Shanghai Bund"; had it been another place, one could not wholly have relied on "taking one's material on the spot."

The whole content of the "personnel-and-organization adjustment scheme" is too complex, and some of its terms rather technical; written out it is long-winded, and dull to read; so I shall simply not say much of it. But the essence of the "scheme"—that is, the matter of "the establishment and growth of New Group One"—cannot be lightly sketched over in a stroke; for in the two years' course of the work, "New Group One" brought forth "Nameless Heroes" beyond all counting.

In the first chapter I gave a brief account of the composition and personnel of "New Group One"; below I shall record more fully the whole of "New Group One." Let us first look at the array which the leader of "New Group One," Brother Bi Gaokui, styled "a newborn calf, an army of young men":

In June of this year, 1983, Brother Bi Gaokui came home from his post abroad to report on his duties, and we had chances to gather several times; present too were Brother Huang Zhiyuan, Brother Liu Yuanshen, and several old comrades of "New Group One" who to this day are unwilling to disclose their names. These few gatherings, quiet and free-spoken, were a true delight. The average age of us folk was about sixty-five; every one had drunk deep of trouble, and each was a survivor of the aftermath; and of things forty years past, glad as we were to recall them, none of us could stand the test of the years—either we could not think of them, or we could not remember them clearly.

Brother Gaokui reminded me that some old comrades might still be living on the mainland, and that when I mention their names in the book I must be especially careful, lest I bring them trouble undreamed of. Yes—most rightly said; we ought indeed to take some care.

The writer's work "Nameless Heroes" was meant to set forth the valiant deeds and the spirit of sacrifice of the "Nameless Heroes" during the War of Resistance; but, alas, to this day it is still hemmed in by certain factors, subjective and objective; and this is why the "nameless heroes" remain heroes without a name.

The "Shanghai District Directly-Subordinate First Group" was the first field unit set up after the "personnel-and-organization adjustment scheme" was put into effect, taking a mixed establishment of intelligence and action, and formed in the autumn of 1939. The leader Bi Gaokui and deputy leader Huang Zhiyuan and the rest were all sent from Chongqing; the full roster of the basic cadre I can no longer set out, but, prompting each other with Brother Gaokui, there were the following—

Leader, Bi Gaokui, foreign-affairs training class, now abroad; deputy leader, Huang Zhiyuan, Qianyang training class, now in Taiwan; member, Liu X X, Linfeng training class, now in Taiwan; member, Cheng Qinghe, staff training class; member, Shi Hecheng, Qianyang training class, now in America; member, Tao Lianfang, Qianyang training class, deceased; member, Dong X X, Qianyang training class, on the mainland; member, Yang Fulin, Qianyang training class, deceased; member, Chen X-fu, Qianyang training class, unknown; member, Qian X-xiong, Qianyang training class, unknown.

Member, Tan X-xiang, foreign-affairs training class, unknown.

Besides these, there were two basic members whose names I cannot for the life of me recall. Should one ask, why not look up the files? Because I know they cannot be looked up.

"New Group One," taking the several members above as its cadre, developed severally into many small units; of these, those already formed were an intelligence unit, an action unit, a labor-movement unit, a police-affairs unit, a Korean unit, and the like; while those still in nurture and planning, not yet fixed, were international relations, science and technology, and the like—so that it was a complete unit of the widest working face and the sturdiest strength.

The leader of "New Group One," Bi Gaokui, a native of Hangzhou in Zhejiang, a graduate of the Sino-French Institute of Shanghai, France-trained, a mechanical and electrical engineer, versed in English and French. Having thrown himself into the ranks of the resistance, he entered again the "Qianyang training class" and the "foreign-affairs training class" run by the "Juntong" to receive secret-service training. On a deep night in July 1939, while under training, he was granted an audience by Mr. Dai Yunong. As Brother Gaokui described the scene: "Mr. Dai, his two eyes red, his manner agitated, having ascertained my social connections and family circumstances in Shanghai, declared: 'Our organs in Shanghai have suffered accident upon accident; you are keen, responsible, and of strong ability—go at once to Shanghai and set up a new unit, and I will send men one after another to help you.' It was thus I was sent to Shanghai. Mr. Dai's words that deep night moved a young man of twenty-odd beyond bearing, never to be forgotten."

Indeed, comrade Bi Gaokui had a blazing revolutionary ardor and a fine, precise scientific head, and may be called of outstanding gifts; that he should lead "New Group One" was more than fitting, and he won achievement upon achievement, so that the "Shanghai District" too rejoiced deeply at having found the man.

The deputy leader of "New Group One," Huang Zhiyuan, cover name Kuang Zhihong, a native of Pudong in Shanghai, was, after graduating from the Qianyang training class, sent to Shanghai. Thick of brow and large of eye, alert and capable, keen in his work, bold to take responsibility, and moreover one who valued good faith and set store by his word, with the air of a man of benevolent chivalry. Being a native of Shanghai, he had not only good social connections, but was especially well versed in the local environment and the ways and customs of the people. And so, presiding over the action work in "New Group One," he showed to especially fine effect, and was truly a mainstay cadre not easily come by.

Others too—such as Liu X X of the first Linfeng class and Shi Hecheng of Qianyang—had excellent working qualities, and many times carried out action tasks in person, heedless of themselves, of outstanding showing, with many successful cases; these shall be told severally in the text below.

Let me select and mention once more the work-lines and intelligence sources of "New Group One":

Fu Shishuo's confidential secretaries Li Sijin and Zhao X-zhi—at the end of the twenty-eighth year the deputy director of the foreign-affairs training class, Mr. Xie Ligong, came specially from Chongqing to Shanghai for the sole purpose of introducing to Bi, and having them meet, Fu Shishuo's students—now serving as his confidential secretaries—Li Sijin and Zhao X-zhi, and handing them over to Bi for single-line liaison. That is, without recourse to any other hand, Bi himself was in contact with the two.

Fu Shishuo had once been president of Daxia University in Shanghai; after attaching himself to Wang, he held in succession the puppet posts of "minister," "governor," and "director of the Southeast Field Headquarters," and was fairly close to Wang. Our side hoped that both Li and Zhao, through Fu's connections, might do something; the highest aim being, of course, Wang himself.

Li and Zhao began with volunteer work, renting a high-grade flat in the French Concession of Shanghai as the liaison and work place of the three, Li, Zhao, and Bi, chiefly for the handling of the inside intelligence obtained.

Later, the matter being not close-kept, it was discovered by Fu; and Li, unable to keep his footing, went to Hong Kong; Zhao remained in place, but was of little use.

Penetrating Chu Minyi's circle—Bi Gaokui had originally been Chu's student, and while in France still had dealings with him, on good terms. On this trip to Shanghai he met with Chu, and planted a man at Chu's side, in the hope of going deeper still. After Chu became deputy president of the puppet organization's Executive Yuan, and then transferred to be minister of foreign affairs, the man who went with Chu to Nanjing and stayed at his side was the "New Group One" member, the trained comrade Tan X-xiang, for Tan, like Bi, had been a student under Chu. Only because Chu very seldom came to Shanghai, and comrade Bi Gaokui could not often go to Nanjing, Tan X-xiang, one hand alone clapping, made slow progress, and Wang was hard to get at; in a year and more it came to no more than gathering some inside intelligence.

Besides these, the connections given by our superiors, and those the group itself developed, were many; the great majority of them of the nature of volunteer service to the country—seeking neither fame nor gain, but only to contribute something to the resistance, and that without grudging sacrifice. Brother Gaokui gave the writer a list; and as I studied it closely, I found among them not a few men of note, and men of great wealth too in plenty. Reckoned up, they were more than twenty in all. At the outset, they were neither for fame nor for gain, and of course cared still less for the name of "hero." Now, though times have changed, yet to safeguard them, and out of regard for the circumstances of each, I have decided to keep their fair names back and not make them known. Only for the loyal patriotism of these worthy gentlemen do I here especially record it.

Among the many righteous and brave patriots listed above was one, the martyr Chen Sancai.

Of the martyr Chen Sancai's deeds there is a record in the book "The Whole Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi"; only concerning the hateful White Russian who sold him out was it left vague; and so, on the prompting of Brother Bi Gaokui, leader of "New Group One," who in those years was in personal liaison with Chen Sancai, I add as follows: "Because the martyr Chen Sancai was pressing hard to carry out the plan to remove Wang, he was unhappily sold out by the White Russian Ivanov whom he employed—who informed on him to the enemy and puppets—and so was arrested. The martyr Chen had learning and aspiration, and gave his all, even to sacrificing his all; after his glorious death, Gaokui grieved deeply, and thereupon made a vow, swearing to avenge the martyr Chen Sancai. Happily heaven has eyes; and at last, on the eighteenth of September in the thirtieth year of the Republic, our group shot dead the one Ivanov who had sold out the martyr Chen, at the mouth of Meicun Lane on Moulmein Road in Shanghai, to comfort the martyr Chen Sancai's soul in heaven, and the more to warn those who do much wrong!"

The group's showing in the work of action and sabotage was especially fine; and as to how it came to reap such fruit, the leader Gaokui and deputy leader Huang Zhiyuan explained: "For the execution of every case there was beforehand a thorough preparation and a fine, close working-out—such as the explaining and fixing of the target before setting out, the disposition of manpower on the scene, the carrying and transport of gear, the choice of the set position of execution and the changes that might arise, the dividing and control of time by stages, the agreeing of the various signals, and the route of withdrawal and its cover after the task was done, and so on." And according to the "Xin Shen Bao," the puppet-run paper published in Shanghai, in its issue of November 28, 1941, tracing back from September 1939, in this space of two years and two months the group had sanctioned traitors and enemy officers upward of twenty times; had wrecked, in the fringe zones of Shanghai—Yangshupu, Pudong, Dachang, and other places—enemy army warehouses, factories, ships, airfields, and the like upward of twenty times over; and had burned enemy military stores, airfield installations, and craft great and small past all counting.

What I had at first wished to record in full was the whole of "New Group One"; but, looking at what has been set forth above, it is no more than a silhouette. For the group's business was manifold and its scope broad, no longer to be told wholly in a few brief words; and ever since the leader of the "Shanghai District Intelligence First Group," comrade Zhu Xiaogu, unable to keep his footing in Shanghai, was transferred elsewhere, the group's leader Bi Gaokui was further appointed to hold the "First Intelligence Group" concurrently; so that, one man holding two posts and two groups merged into one, it grew the harder to tell them apart. Therefore the original "First Intelligence Group" part—especially the matter of the personnel relations and their use in the police affairs of the two Concessions—is separated out for a record apart.

### From Iron Discipline and the Power of Life and Death to Moral Conscience

Among the field units under the "Juntong," the "Shanghai District" was the largest in establishment and the most numerous in men, and so had of itself a representativeness not to be overlooked. Of its inner working I have spoken in outline in the first chapter above; now, gathering up its several traits, I shall divide them into the headings of "manner and temper," "iron discipline," "the power of life and death," and "moral conscience," and so open one half of the muffled gourd, to unriddle the various guesses the outside world has made these several decades about the "Juntong." Rest assured that I shall hold throughout to what is objective and fair, and shall by no means pick out only the pleasant to tell. And as for that other half—that too I cannot open.

In what follows I shall not divide it under fine headings, but write on in a flowing style, wherever it may go; and call it, if you will, a string of true stories.

The director of the "Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Military Affairs Commission" was He Yaozu, who held the title but did not attend to affairs; a very great part of the high cadres had never so much as met him. When there was a mass gathering—a memorial assembly, a mourning meeting, and the like—he never took part either. So that those who had worked in the Bureau did not necessarily every one of them know there was such a director. The man who was actually in charge of the Bureau's affairs was, as all knew, Dai Li, Dai Yunong. In fact, right up to his death by air-crash on March 17, 1946, he was still major-general deputy director, and was raised to lieutenant-general only posthumously. For a man of major-general's rank to command, in the later phase of the War of Resistance, nearly three hundred thousand officers, men, and working cadres—including among them a group of major-generals and several lieutenant-generals on the establishment—is truly a marvel of history; and I believe that, should China ever again have a war with a foreign power, there will never again be such a state of things.

The working comrades of the "Shanghai District," whenever they spoke of Mr. Dai, all called him "the Boss," and sometimes bluntly said "the Boss" without the surname. Nor was it so only in Shanghai, but everywhere alike. "The Boss" is the "shopkeeper" of a business; the fraternities of the greenwood call it the "keeper of the door"; in our "Juntong" it ought properly to be "the leader" or "the helmsman." The comrades liked to call him so—affection, reverence, and awe together in it, with a flavor besides of the "head of the family," and never the least contempt. Yet I myself was never much used to it, and, in public or private, to his face or behind his back, always alike said "Mr. Dai." As for the students of the various training classes he had trained, to his face they called him "Director," for the commandant of the special-police class was Generalissimo Chiang, and the class director was Mr. Dai. In the earlier years, a few old comrades of the founding days would now and then call out "Brother Yunong"; but by the time of the War of Resistance one no longer heard anyone so address him, which showed that his prestige had grown ever loftier and was no longer what it had been. The common view held that all this was wrought by Mr. Dai's outstanding leadership and by the flesh and blood of thousands upon thousands of comrades.

Mr. Zheng Jiemin must be reckoned the man most courteously treated by Mr. Dai in the whole "Juntong"—before and after, this one man alone. Chiefly this was because Mr. Zheng had once been secretary of the "Lixingshe," and because he held another charge besides at the General Staff. As for what post Mr. Zheng held concurrently in the "Juntong," or under what title, I do not clearly know. In sum, however one puts it, his standing in the "Juntong," and the weight set on him from above, was second only to Mr. Dai's. Yet one never heard anyone call him "the Second Boss"; and it was only after Mr. Dai's death, when Mr. Zheng took over as director, that even so he did not inherit the appellation "the Boss."

Coming down a further step, let me speak of the "Shanghai District," for which I was responsible. Because over the years, besides intelligence work, our superiors laid especial store by the effect of action work, the "Shanghai District" too had some "renown" in the Shanghai region; and though people did not understand what sort of organization it was, both the enemy-and-puppet side and society at large called it the "Blue Shirt Society," and knew broadly how "formidable" it was. What most baffled men was that outsiders could not make out its true face. In this organization of ours I was the Shanghai "chief"; and you would imagine that should be a man of power and place. But said plainly, it is laughable: as regards the head of a single working unit, he is no more than a transmitter of orders, an executor of policy. As for myself—tense within, ordinary without—set me where you please, I truly had no "prestige" to speak of. To say nothing else: even walking along the street I was a most timid man, forever dreading to meet with some trouble that would expose my identity. Even to leave a little extra tip at a small eating-house I had my scruples, for fear the waiter's loud "Thank you!" should draw an extra glance from the guests at the tables.

In handling documents at the office, though it was not done by stealth, one dared not do it in grand style either; and if by chance someone pressed the wrong doorbell, however calm I might be, I could hardly help a flutter of the heart. At times, too, I would in a fluster hurry the papers in hand into the "trap" for hiding; and if an outsider had seen this sorry sight, there would be no talk of any "authority" at all.

To change the subject, let me speak a little of the "manner" of our "Shanghai District." By "manner" one may here as well understand "way of working": you may do it this way, another may do it that; every road leads to the goal, and how one steps out, how one marches on, is each man's own affair. The ground it covers is fairly narrow, yet unless spoken of, few ever touch on these things; and among them are some that are not quite as the tales have it.

We who worked behind the enemy's lines were ill-suited to holding meetings; and neither I nor the secretary Brother Qi Qingbin liked to hold them. No meeting of any form—work conference, cadre discussion, and the like—was ever held; and even occasions of three or more gathered to talk over a problem were few. As for the problems that came and went, usually the secretary and I would consult and reach a decision, then hand it over to be carried out; those that bore some responsibility, that might draw criticism, that used money which could not be accounted for, I settled on my own authority; only those I could not decide had of necessity to be referred up. In sum, we never used meetings to solve problems or to study them. Was it then the same with every working unit in the fallen zone? So far as I know, not necessarily.

The comrades of the "Shanghai District," high and low, inner and field, all took a broad and clear view of things; and of course, from the special and solemn nature of the work itself, all bore a strong sense of honor and of mission, so that never did they fall into the profitless and harmful business of forming cliques for private gain, or of factional strife. We often hear it said: "Whose man is so-and-so; they are of one gang." The very words show that such a thing exists. In reason, with a great enemy before us and all facing outward together, no such thing ought to arise; but the weakness of human nature leans too much to selfishness, and so in some quarters it is, after all, hard to escape. That the "Shanghai District" was not infected with it had to do, no doubt, with the inborn insulation of an intelligence organization and with the Juntong's constant revolutionary manner; but it may also be said to have rested on the comrades' own self-awareness and on the fruit of my own even-handedness. I once said, as "District Chief," these few words broadly to the working comrades of the "Shanghai District": if any man ask me whose man I am, I shall answer him with a good conscience—I am a citizen of the Republic of China; I am a working comrade of the "Juntong"; and I am also a tool used by the government.

Next let me speak of the question of reward and punishment, of leniency and severity. In my heart I know well that, had my superiors not been generous with me, I should either have died of old age in prison or gone long ago to the Land of Bliss for further study; and so I have always held that toward one's subordinates, where one can be lenient, one should be lenient. Let me first tell a small story by way of example. When my predecessor as "Shanghai District" chief was in office, there was a working comrade in charge of general affairs, named Zhang Weixian, a Shanghai native, only twenty-odd years old; a graduate of some training class run by the "Juntong," he had been assigned to Shanghai and posted for the time to the District headquarters to handle general affairs. Being young, and reckless with money, he once ran short on funds for buying goods and could not square his account; not daring to come back, and at his wits' end, he could only put up at a hotel and think what to do. Meanwhile the others of the "Shanghai District," not seeing Zhang Weixian return, were all frantic with worry, for fear he had been seized by others.

To go on—Zhang Weixian stayed two days at the hotel, and not only could think of no way out, but could not even pay the hotel bill; and the hotel pressed him for money. In his helplessness he had no choice but to telephone the District headquarters and ask that someone be sent with money to redeem him. This was in itself no great matter, and the public funds fallen short were not many; to deduct them month by month, chide him a few words, and bid him not do so again, would have settled it. But who could have known this lad was so foolish—and he had been through training, too—that, truly bewitched, he said over the telephone two words not very pleasant to hear, something like: "If you do not redeem me back, I shall expose my identity." In truth he had no intent to threaten; it was only that, in his extremity, he spoke without choosing his words. Yet, taken at the worst, it did indeed make unpleasant hearing. I do not know in what terms the report was afterward made to our superiors; but the upshot was that he was recalled to the rear and handed to military-law trial, and was actually made out to a capital charge. Though it had warrant in law, I for my part always felt this too was over-severe.

Having told the sad story above, let me go a step further and speak of our discipline.

The discipline of the "Juntong" was very strict. So far as I know, the statutes cited in enforcing discipline, besides the criminal code and the military criminal code, included a set of "house rules"—that is, the Juntong's own internal by-laws, put into force by leave of the higher authority. What its name was, I—though in May and June 1939 I acted as chief of the Third Section of Bureau headquarters, in charge of just "action" and "judicial affairs"—truly never came into contact with this "house law," let alone knew its whole content. Yet I did know that its sentences were far heavier than the penalties of the military criminal code.

In the "house law," besides the "law" there was the "code of conduct"—such as no marrying in wartime, and the like. This was no offense against human feeling, nor a robbing of human rights, but rested on the sense of the saying "the Xiongnu not yet destroyed, why should one make a home?"—a thing forced by the nation's peril; and it was a stopgap measure for keeping the work smooth and drawing out the whole body's latent power, or it would not have been accepted by men.

What the "house law" stressed most was the "keeping of secrets." This too is an iron rule of secret work, the same in all ages and lands; and so secret work is also called "secret work." Of this too there is an instance to be cited, though I can no longer recall the names of those in the case. The case ran thus: in the rear, two colleagues lived next door to each other, both with their families, and both engaged in the "Juntong's" intelligence work. Once, one of them was sent to the fallen zone to carry out a task, and his wife did not know where her husband had gone. Being a woman, and her husband vanished, she asked here and inquired there—which is only human nature; and it chanced that the gentleman next door knew where her husband had gone. Though he knew well it could not be "divulged," yet, being a comrade and next-door neighbor, and seeing her pitiable distress, in a small failure of forbearance out of human feeling he secretly told the lady, charging her again and again not to say who had told her. But the lady half-disbelieved it, and, thinking herself clever, set about finding proof; and so the Bureau came to know of it, and, tracing it down, found that someone had "divulged a secret." "Divulging a secret" broke a great taboo; and though there were extenuating grounds, it could not be pardoned—it is said the man was sentenced to seven years.

Strict though it was, it had its more "open" side too. First, where the work had need, however heavy the sentence passed, it could all be struck off at a stroke, that the man might atone by merit. The procedure, roughly, was that a sentence passed by military law required a course of petition for "transfer to labor service," while one passed by "house law" was restored to freedom by a single slip. When I was in "lawful custody" at "Site B" (a place of detention) in Yangpi Lane at Nanjing in 1936, it was thus that I was let out. At the time I was tormented by not knowing how long my term was; yet I was held only five months and seven days—it was too cheap.

In my two years and more as District Chief of the "Shanghai District," I never once invoked the "law and discipline" to punish any comrade. In that time there was one such affair, which, after all, brought on no tragedy; the chief actor's name I shall withhold, and the course of it was broadly as follows.

There was a "group member," trained, of whom I do not know what shock his spirit had suffered, so that his will was cast down and he no longer wished to carry on. Under the rule forbidding long leave, he simply quit his post of his own accord and hid himself away. Such conduct, in the wartime fallen zone, was of course not to be borne by law and discipline. In an organization behind the enemy's lines, a working comrade's "escaping control" is a most grave thing: one can neither set it aside and let him go, nor make a false and easy report that one man the fewer is one man the fewer. So it had of necessity to be reported truthfully, and the higher authority at once sent down an order to sanction him and report the manner of execution. This put one in a hard place: to obey and carry it out seemed rather cruel—how could I bring myself to it?—but not to execute it was to defy an order; and for a moment I truly did not know how to deal with it. The upshot of consulting with Brother Qingbin was that we resolved first to find some way to find the man, and then that I would meet him in person—by no means luring him in by a trick to seize the chance and carry out the order, but meaning to counsel him face to face, hint to him the gravity of the case, and at the same time urge him to hurry back to Chongqing at once and ask for his own punishment, giving him a verbal guarantee of safety. This we did. Happily we found him, and happily he was not a man past all reason, so that he went off full of remorse. On the "Shanghai District's" side, after he had set out, we put in a few pleas for him and further begged our superiors to deal lightly. It is said he was held only a few months in detention, then was posted to other work, safe and sound. Here I must point out especially that in the early years the one thing least allowed in secret work was "escaping control"—the same the world over, in China and abroad. That I dare affirm this so is experience, is a thing tempered in the fire, and words spoken from the heart.

Of the matter of discipline let this be enough. Now let me turn the topic to the "power of life and death." On this point I have already touched in the "Opening Confession" of "Nameless Heroes," to this effect: "Many too misunderstand, and think Mr. Dai Yunong must surely have wielded the power of life and death. To this I can answer definitely: no—absolutely not! He was the leader of this part of our work, and the executor of matters bearing on national policy; all his measures had something to follow, and were purely rational. If it is said that he had more chances than others to counsel his superiors, that is a fact; but it was by no means so overbearing as the outside world tells and imagines." 

In the Shanghai of the War of Resistance, the outside world still spread tales without cease—how Mr. Dai did thus and so in Chongqing, thus and so in Hong Kong, thus and so again in the fallen zone—as though he had a boundless authority and a limitless power; and they painted it so vividly it seemed truly to have been so. Though the tales all had some ground, yet, so far as I know, they were in fact all at variance with the truth. Let me cite a further example by way of illustration:

Some days ago—in August of this year, 1983—Brother Liu xx, who was in those years chief inspector of the International Settlement police, told me with his own lips: "That year (probably the twenty-ninth year), the famous Shanghai lawyer Jiang xx asked me to see the venerable Yu Qiaqing aboard ship to Hong Kong, meaning to fly on thence to Chongqing to explain himself in person to Mr. Dai. For someone had accused the venerable Yu Qiaqing of conduct harmful to the War of Resistance, and an order to sanction him had been issued; and this trip was just to get the sanction of him canceled."

I was astonished beyond measure to hear it, for the "Shanghai District" had simply never received any such order; had there been one, I could not but have known, for Mr. Yu Qiaqing was a man of standing in Shanghai, who had done many public benefactions and made contributions to state and society, and I could not have failed to recall it or have confused it. Among the old papers there is a telegram Mr. Dai sent me, one passage of which directs: "The use of the workers is of the greatest importance, for today's secret service needs many deeply penetrating lines and the cover of every common trade in society. It is my thought that a Labor Movement Committee should be organized; and I ask that Messrs. Yu Qiaqing, Zhao Zigang, Liu Shaokui, Liu Junqing, and Jiang Futian be its members, and that our Shanghai District too may pick men to take part in its work; whatever monthly funds are needed, the Center will surely undertake." From this telegram we may see: to call Mr. Yu Qiaqing "the venerable Yu" showed respect; to list Mr. Yu first was of course to honor him, and to invite him to be a member of the "Shanghai Labor Movement Committee" then to be set up; and if he were not trusted, and his social standing not leaned upon, then for what? Therefore, apart from the "Shanghai District" for which I was responsible, I could by no means believe that any other unit received an order to sanction him.

Again, since no sanction order had ever been issued, of course none could be canceled. The rest of it I do not know.

Besides this, I have heard others too talk of the affair, and the content is much the same. I wonder—could it have been a piece of "psychological warfare"? "Psychological warfare"—"psy-war" for short—is one of the items of secret work, a thing of the highest craft; used ill, its effect turns clean the other way. Suppose the situation to be thus: we had already received intelligence that a certain man had turned traitor and had dealings with the enemy; and though it could not be verified, one could not but guard against it; so one deliberately spread rumor to warn him, and if by this one could get him to declare his heart, or rein in at the cliff's edge, would it not be to solve a hard problem without bloodying the blade? Let me say once more that this is wholly a supposition; Mr. Dai never directed me to carry out any "psy-war."

There is another matter I may as well mention in passing. But first let me declare: I have no wish to wrangle with anyone; over old topics of this sort, stale grain and rotten sesame, it is best that you have your say and I mine—that we may cross-check them is perhaps the more interesting. As for whether they are to be believed, that too may be left to each as he pleases.

What I would talk of here is, in the whole case of the puppet "chairman of the Shanghai General Chamber of Commerce," Yu Yefeng, the part concerning Mr. Dai Yunong's promise made to his face to "let his later conduct be the proof"—which bears on the "power of life and death."

Yu Yefeng, a Zhejiang man, ranked in Shanghai as a second-rate notable, his social standing below that of Du and Zhang (Zhang Xiaolin). Because he had become puppet "chairman of the Shanghai General Chamber of Commerce," and had organized the enemy-aiding body the "New Asia Peace Promotion Association," he was on record as ordered to be sanctioned. Of this affair there is a passage in what Mr. Wan Molin says in the first volume of "Bygone Days in Shanghai," under the subtitle "Aiding the Enemy with Goods and Making a Great Profit," from pages sixty-five to sixty-six: "On this trip of Yu Yefeng's to Hong Kong, he not only saw Mr. Du, but, taking the chance of Mr. Dai's passing through Hong Kong, again and again begged Mr. Du to help put in a word so that he might be admitted to see Mr. Dai. He declared to Mr. Dai that he would repent bitterly of his past and turn over a new leaf, and begged Mr. Dai to be magnanimous and not hold his past against him. It was clever of him to think up this trick of hoodwinking heaven and crossing the sea; and only when I received Mr. Du's letter did I suddenly understand—the purpose of Yu Yefeng's trip to Hong Kong had been, all along, to make his 'repentance' before Mr. Du and Mr. Dai, and to beg a 'reprieve from death' merely to save his own life. Yet from the time Yu Yefeng made this trip to Hong Kong he fancied he had already gulled both Mr. Dai and Mr. Du—especially, with Mr. Dai's one word of 'let his later conduct be the proof,' it was as good as having a 'guarantee,' and the working men on the Shanghai side would never make things hard for him again."

This course of things Mr. Wan relates I did not myself know; and I am sure that men not near enough to the matter would understand it still less. But suppose Mr. Dai truly had promised Yu Yefeng's request to his face—whether or not he could rightly do so, he would surely have let the "Shanghai District" know a word of it; there is no reason on earth that he should on the one hand issue an order to sanction, and on the other promise to "let his later conduct be the proof." Should Yu Yefeng really and sincerely mean to "repent of his past and turn over a new leaf," and we then carried out the sanction and brought it off, would that not have wrought a grave wrong? What I would have it understood is this: before January 14, 1940, the "Shanghai District" for which I was responsible received no fresh instruction from Mr. Dai on the case of sanctioning Yu Yefeng; to speak exactly, there was no instruction of the kind "stay execution," "suspend execution," or "investigate his activities and report at speed for approval"—none at all; and so on the night of January 14, at the Gengxin Stage on Newchwang Road, Ji Zhencang, Chen Mo, and others of the Second Action Brigade brought Yu Yefeng to justice. Of the particulars of sanctioning Yu Yefeng there will be a separate record in the next chapter.

Afterward our superiors wired their commendation of the meritorious and issued a reward by way of encouragement. This proves that we had made no error—that is to say, that Mr. Dai's promising him "let his later conduct be the proof" is open to doubt.

To this let me add a few words more: since I entered the work I have received sanction orders past counting; but save where "the situation had shifted" or the object had "vanished of itself," I have never once received a second order "remitting a sanction." I do not understand law; but roughly, "issuing a sanction order" is the same as "passing a death sentence"; and from "passing a death sentence" to "remitting the execution" there ought still to be some legal procedure.

There is yet another matter over which the outside world guesses much—especially in spy fiction, where a woman spy must all but be woven in somewhere. Now let me speak of the women working comrades of our "Shanghai District."

Perhaps it is too subjective; but because during my term I never dared entrust heavy tasks to the women comrades, I very seldom put them to important work. I held that women comrades in general, though they had certain advantages in carrying out a task, would often, at the critical moment, spoil the business; and examining the crux of it, the main thing was their tenderness of feeling and the frailty of their will. To say so may seem a little abrupt, and yet one cannot wholly blot out the facts. In the past, in the intelligence history of China and abroad, some women workers did indeed leave a few brilliant records, valiant and full of color; but that is no common or frequent thing—there is no knowing how many ages must pass, and among how many persons, before by chance one such appears.

The "Shanghai District," inner-duty and field together, had about forty women comrades in all; and save one who had for some years been leader of the Fourth Group, and was older than the general run, the rest averaged around twenty-two or twenty-three, and were all "liaison officers" and "couriers," with a very few "cipher clerks." Truly it did them a wrong.

Early in 1940 Mr. Dai sent another woman comrade, who came from Chongqing to Shanghai bearing a letter in Mr. Dai's own hand. This comrade was named Wang Qiufang, cover name Wang Fang, twenty-four or twenty-five, a northerner, who spoke fluent Beiping dialect and fluent English besides. She was a graduate of the Bureau's foreign-affairs training class. Though she was good-looking and dressed very smartly, from all her movements one could see she was quite without working experience. Reading Mr. Dai's letter closely, it was written rather delicately, like a letter of introduction, and besides bidding me assign her some suitable regular work, hinted between the lines that, if need be, I might as well direct her to some more effective work. I understood—this was to depend on whether the work had need. So I designated her for the time to courier work at the District headquarters' Second Office, under the direction and dispatch of the assistant secretaries Hu Shangwu and Gui Difei.

I asked Wang Qiufang privately: "Has Mr. Dai any other verbal message for you to bring me?" Wang said: "No; Mr. Dai charged me to obey your orders, and not to ask the reason." I asked again: "Have you any other task?" Wang thought a moment and answered: "Yes; he told me to make contact with a woman, one Tian Shujun." I asked further: "Is there any ready work-line in Shanghai?" She took up the thread: "No; that must wait till I have seen Tian Shujun, and see whether anything can be developed from her." She added a word besides: "This too was Mr. Dai's instruction."

The Tian Shujun whom Wang Qiufang spoke of was a "member of the household" of the former Shanghai garrison commander Yang Hu; she had a friendship with Mr. Dai, and would often address him as brother. This person was of the right stuff for intelligence work, though hard to control. Over the affair of Wang Qiufang and Tian Shujun, Mr. Dai sent me a telegram deciphered by his own hand, which directed: "The warrant against Tian Shujun must be a piece of surface show by the puppets; it is heard that Tian has dealings with Li Shiqun, and I ask you to watch it closely. Has comrade Wang Fang met Tian of late? Bid comrade Wang take great care in her movements, for the puppet secret service is fairly skillful in its shadowing."

For clearness let me add a word of explanation: Tian Shujun had, at Mr. Dai's charge (for she had no worker's status, so one cannot say "sent"), "penetrated" the Wang puppet organization; and now the Wang puppets had again put out a warrant for her, yet she had rather close dealings with the puppet secret-service chief Li Shiqun—so that Mr. Dai suspected there was some fraud in it. That is, Tian Shujun had made her feigned play real, or, one might say, her false surrender had turned into a true one. And because Wang Qiufang was in liaison with Tian Shujun, Mr. Dai feared besides that the puppet secret service might "tail" her back to the office, and so earnestly charged her to take great care, lest all be lost. From this we may see how a work-leader takes all into his over-all care, missing nothing great or small.

This affair had an inborn complexity; and though its distant prospect was fair, we never got a grip on anything; and, as I saw it, nothing would come of it. So I bade Wang Qiufang cut off her liaison with Tian Shujun, and at the same time put to Mr. Dai the proposal to give it up.

After a period of observation, Wang Qiufang's abilities proved none too strong; and since she was content with courier and message work, I had no mind to lay heavier duty on her. In truth, apart from a fine outside, in the matters of character, nerve, adaptability, and quick wit she was in every way unsuited to this kind of work.

Here is a blood-soaked true story, likewise a tragedy played out of a girl's tenderness of feeling. The thing happened at Qingdao in Shandong, in the early phase of the War of Resistance; and the chief actors in the case were Fu Shenglan and Ding Wenhui.

Ding Wenhui, a woman, a native of Rizhao in Shandong, young and comely, of a pure nature, a graduate of the Linfeng training class. About the end of the twenty-seventh year she was assigned to inner-duty work at the "Qingdao Station" under the Bureau; and the station chief was Fu Shenglan. Not long after, Fu Shenglan turned traitor, and Ding was coerced away with him, and at the same time fell into an attachment with him. To sanction Fu Shenglan, Bureau headquarters got into secret touch with Ding and charged her to carry out the sanction as opportunity offered. Though she had many chances of contact with Fu, and it would have been easy to do, yet she was after all a girl, and, feeling working within her, shoot or stab she could not bring herself to. In the end she had to change to poison, and was to put a certain drug, colorless, odorless, and tasteless, into the thermos Fu commonly used, so that he might be poisoned unawares. But after the drug was handed her, there was long no word. Of course Ding was loyal to country and to the organization; in truth she was now in a war of heaven and man, sunk in anguish—to do it she could not bear; not to do it, the task lay on her, and Bureau headquarters pressed her hard. From the conflict in her heart her distress showed on her face, and her every act could not but fall out of true; and all this had long fallen into Fu Shenglan's eye. Fu was an old fox, and had been trained by the Soviet "GPU," so that he heightened his guard and warded her at every turn. At last one day, just as she was placing the poison, she was caught by Fu on the spot, and was thereupon strangled in the bedroom. Pitiable Ding Wenhui gave her life for the country, yet all to no gain; and this too shows that women working comrades, tender of feeling and frail of will, cannot be charged with heavy duty—yet another instance.

Some pass judgment that those in the secret service come rather close to evil; and I for one do not oppose the saying, only one must draw the line clear: "close to evil" is by no means "the same as evil," nor is one to mistake the secret service itself for "being evil."

Just because secret work is "close to evil," it naturally comes under the rule of moral force; and this is not a thing law and statute can wholly hold in check, but is owed to the nurture of China's traditional culture.

The manner of the "Juntong," broadly, was one that valued morality. How so? Because it is hard to lay down a set of "moral standards" for the secret service; take only "stealing"—everyone knows this is immoral; but suppose it be "stealing the enemy's intelligence," and it is wholly another thing; so there ought to be a definition, and that is, "he who steals intelligence is no thief"—which is the "broadly" I spoke of above.

Whatever tales the outside world may tell, in the field of work I came in contact with, we never did those things that outrage heaven and reason and go against morality. I did secret work my whole life, and what I take most "comfort" in is just this.

The so-called "using any means" is used most, most widely, and most cruelly by the Communist countries; and when it comes to their secret agents, they go to every last extreme. As for the Western democracies, and Japan during the War of Resistance, though they cannot be spoken of in the same breath, it is still but a case of fifty steps to a hundred. The most despised on record are the German Nazis of the Second World War: they flouted morality and extinguished humanity; and even could they snatch some small advantage, it is not to be taken for a model. What we ought to do is kill more of the Japanese who launched the war of aggression.

There is one matter I ought first to make plain—not that I mean of set purpose to plead anyone's cause, but wholly to speak to the point, to say a fair word and keep the truth: since I took office at the "Shanghai District," and now for three months, I have never once received a single piece of intelligence that Wang Tianmu bore any intent or act harmful to my work. It was understood—we learned from intelligence—that Wang's surface movements had already gone far from secret-service activity, and were seeking a way forward in the very direction of how to get close to Wang Jingwei. Though he had his home in Shanghai, he went now and then to Nanjing; and by one account he had been picked by Wang to be a central supervisory committee member of the "Chinese Nationalist Party" (Sixth Congress), a piece staged and played by Wang himself. Looking into the yearbook printed by the Wang puppet regime, in the list of "supervisory members" of the "Sixth Central Executive and Supervisory Committee of the Chinese Nationalist Party," the name of Wang Tianmu is indeed set down.

The other man who had turned from the "Shanghai District," Chen Dirong (Mingchu), was another matter altogether; he had by now taken on the title of First Section chief of the puppet "Special Operations Headquarters" (No. 76), and day and night sought to strike at his "old friends." Though he did not amount to a grave threat, sitting there he was, all the same, a sore.

Between October and November 1939, Mr. Dai on a special task dispatched Brother Wu xx to Shanghai, handing him to the "Shanghai District" to be responsible for liaison and to be given proper support.

Brother Wu xx has appeared several times in Part One, "Rooting Out Traitors in the North." His post at the time was leader of the Tianjin directly-subordinate action group, and he had merit in turning the two Zhangs (Zhang Qingyu and Zhang Yantian) and their forces of the security corps of the puppet "East Hebei Autonomous Government" back to us, and further in bringing them into the ranks of the resistance. He now dwells abroad in America, and has for many years kept clear of outside affairs. In 1981 he once came home for a short stay; and, against all common feeling, he avoided my invitation—no doubt because he did not wish to speak of the past again. And so in this case I hide his name, that I may not vex him.

On this coming of Brother Wu xx to Shanghai, whether he and I ever met I truly cannot recall; what I remember most clearly is that through a "special liaison station" we had our relay line between us. The head of the "special liaison station" was Fu Bingchen, of the fifth class of the Military Academy, a Hebei man. During my time working at both Beiping and Tianjin he served on the Tianjin–Pukou railway trains under the name of "inspector," charged with "special-duty courier" work, and, of course, with other tasks besides. In 1935, on the road when Brother Lian Mou escorted me back to Nanjing to receive my punishment, we met Fu Bingchen again on the train. At the time he must have thought it very strange: had not this fellow bolted? Why did he dare sit brazenly on a train bound for Nanjing? We could not conveniently talk, and he only gave me a smile. Fu, being newly sent to Shanghai and unacquainted with the place and its people, was for the time asked to bear with his present post. In truth, to preside over a "special liaison station," of the highest secrecy, cannot be called unimportant.

Brother Wu xx's task on this trip to Shanghai was, chiefly, to move the three action members who had gone off with Wang to "return to the ranks," and, as chance offered, to counsel Wang Tianmu to "turn back." Should the situation allow, it was hoped too that the three action members would use the chance, while they could still get near the "traitor," to make some showing.

There was one point more, and this depended on the will of the three action members: as I incompletely understand it, the gist was to press Wang Tianmu to make some showing, or else forcibly to have him take common action with the three action members. Herein, of course, there was much I could not fully know, and so I dare not rashly draw conclusions.

These three action members had all been action-team men when Brother Wang Tianmu was chief of the "Tianjin Station"; having followed Brother Wang Tianmu for years, they kept always a "brotherly" bond of superior and subordinate, and naturally had a feeling not easily shaken. Their true names were Ma Hetu, Yue Qingjiang, and Ding Baoling.

Ma, Yue, and Ding were all Henan men, and rather than say they were of military origin, one had better call them stout fellows of the greenwood. Ma Hetu was the head of them; besides the fine marksmanship he had trained up, in resource too he was by no means to be taken for a mere roughneck. Yue Qingjiang and Ding Baoling were, one may say, both docile and respectful to him, more so by a few degrees than to Brother Wang Tianmu. Yue Qingjiang seemed a few years older than Ma Hetu, a "hothead," who could not go three sentences without a "damn his granny," and who at a word out of key would want to come to blows, yet who kept nothing in his heart. Ding Baoling was the youngest, of striking looks, a model handsome lad, whom to call a "powder-faced warrior" would not be amiss.

Turning it over in my memory again, whether I ever met Brother Wu xx—if I say I never once saw his face, that too seems against reason, for at the time there were security scruples, and it should be that we met only once or twice, and both times at Fu Bingchen's "liaison station."

As for whether Brother Wu met with Wang Tianmu, or was limited to contact with Ma Hetu, that is very hard to say for certain. It may be that Brother Wu never told me, and it may be that he told me and I have forgotten. Happily the crux does not lie here, and a small omission is no great matter. To speak further of the details—where did Brother Wu xx lodge? Where did he and Ma Hetu meet? At the meeting was it Ma Hetu alone, or the three, Ma, Yue, and Ding, together? Was the Wu–Ma meeting kept from Wang Tianmu, or told him openly? These I never inquired into, and so do not know; what I most cared about was the progress of the matter.

The most important point: on the "Shanghai District's" side, we never once received from our superiors any order to sanction Wang Tianmu, nor of course ever put forward such a proposal. As for whether Brother Wu xx's task on this trip carried any related instruction—because it was not convenient to ask, and all kept the deepest silence, no one turned his thought to this question, as though there could be no such thing at all.

To speak again of Mr. Wang Tianmu himself—what after all was his mind or his aim? We never could make it out.

There is yet another account: that Wu xx carried a letter in Mr. Dai's own hand to Wang Tianmu; what was written in it, of course cannot be known; and, in truth, whether there was such a letter at all cannot be verified. If there was, then Brother Wu xx very likely met with Wang Tianmu.

This affair seems the more one tells it the more muddled; at the time and for some years after it stayed clouded and dim, never coming clear. Writing here, a strange fancy strikes me: that if now—that is, at present, in 1983—the three chief parties bound up with this case, Mr. Wang Tianmu, Mr. Wu xx, and I myself, are all still alive and well in the world; and though Wang and Wu are both over eighty, they are singularly blessed, not only sharp of ear and clear of eye but able to walk as if flying; suppose there were someone all three sides got on with, to "bring us together," that we might open our hearts and, with nothing hidden, talk it all out to our content—would that not be a joy? Could it be? A strange fancy hardly makes fact; so I go on to record the outline of what I know.

Mr. Wu xx had already been a month or so in Shanghai, and still kept in touch with the "Shanghai District" without cease through the "special liaison station." By report, Wang Tianmu's three "bodyguards," Ma Hetu and the rest, had already agreed to "return to the ranks"; but as to when it would take concrete shape, they must still await a favorable chance. So it was said, yet in fact one supposes it was not so simple, and there must have been many subtleties and mysteries in it that he did not care to speak plainly. For consider: Ma Hetu, Yue Qingjiang, and Ding Baoling—if they wished to "go," they were neither hindered nor barred; they need only order a car to drive into the Concession, find a place to settle, and from then on not go back, and that was all; a thing so easy could be done at any time and any place, and there was really no need to wait for a chance. Their reasons for still "waiting" on were two: one, how to deal with Wang Tianmu; the other, that they could not go back "empty-handed."

After a spell of nurturing and brewing, the chance came at last: on the very early morning of December 25, at half past three, three-thirty—that is, on the "peaceful night" of Christmas Eve—Wang Tianmu's bodyguards Ma Hetu, Yue Qingjiang, and Ding Baoling, before the Weldon Dance Hall on Yuyuan Road in western Shanghai, fired several shots (by one account six in all) at the two men, Chen Dirong (cover name Chen Mingchu) and He Xingjian (also named He Tianfeng); Chen and He fell at the sound, lying stiff in their own blood. At the same time, unlooked-for, a stray shot struck a passerby named Liu Heng—a most unhappy chance.

Christmas, a night of revelry, just the time to take one's pleasure while one may. Chen Mingchu, He Xingjian, Wang Tianmu, Wang's concubine Wu Jiangyue, and Ma Hetu, Yue Qingjiang, Ding Baoling, and others whose names are unknown—more than ten in all—took several motorcars and drove in grand array to the several dance halls of the western Shanghai district to make merry. This district belonged to the "roads built beyond the boundary" of the International Settlement, and was at present within the sphere of the Wang puppets, with Japanese armed gendarmes (of the Japanese western-Shanghai gendarmerie) stationed there as well; and the good folk of the place called this ground the "Badlands." Their party went to several dance halls, and, perhaps still keeping some wariness, near enough all did but sit a little and dance a few dances and then move on to another; and by the time they got to the Weldon Dance Hall, the night was deep and their spirits flagging. Because several who were in the way had one by one taken their leave, and the police and detectives on guard had grown slack besides, it was thus that Ma Hetu and the rest got their chance to strike—cool indeed.

On Yuyuan Road in western Shanghai there had been a gambling house, which in ordinary times, to draw in gamblers and to fend off outside attack, had put itself forward under the name of the Weldon Dance Hall; and because the Japanese too went there in droves to gamble, it had been closed down, and then, after the gambling house (which had ties with Wu Shibao and that lot of No. 76) had again and again worked its connections, was allowed to reopen. The gambling house and the dance hall were joined by a little passage, so that lovers of gambling and of dancing flocked to it like ducks.

Chen Dirong (that is, Chen Mingchu, who had been an assistant secretary of the "Juntong's Shanghai District," and after his defection to the puppets had by now been appointed First Section chief by the puppet "Special Operations Headquarters"), gravely wounded on the spot, was at once rushed by car to the nearby Tongren Hospital for emergency aid, but died of his wounds.

The other wounded on the spot, He Xingjian (also named Tianfeng), was afterward borne by an ambulance sent by the Japanese to the Fumin Hospital on North Sichuan Road in Hongkou for treatment; but being wounded in the head, within a few minutes of admission he breathed his last. He Xingjian had been a commander of the Second Column of the Loyal and Patriotic Army, and was afterward transferred to be special commissioner for the reorganization and training of Loyal and Patriotic Army units in the Pudong district. In 1939 he went over to the puppets (a separate case from Chen Dirong's), and became commander of the "Second Route of the Anti-Communist National Salvation Army" of the puppet forces, styling himself "lieutenant-general."

Both these men were on record as ordered to be sanctioned; and in the operation to move Ma Hetu and the rest back to the ranks, to sanction Chen Dirong as chance offered, and so cut off future trouble, needs no saying; only He Xingjian was not a target within this plan. People often use "heaven's net is wide" to describe such things—true enough; and to say he was one who "chanced to be thrown in for good measure" would not be amiss either.

As for that innocent man who met with disaster, on inquiry his name proved to be Liu Huan, a silk merchant, living at No. 5 Jinshou Lane on Shandong Road; struck in the chest by the stray shot, he was already at his last gasp on the spot, and was borne by an International Settlement police ambulance to the Tongren Hospital on Avenue Haig for emergency aid, but died soon after arriving. This was truly a calamity out of the blue. Recording it here, with a heart of guilt and shame, I, on behalf of the "Shanghai District," respectfully offer my grief to the dead Mr. Liu Heng, and pray that he may live happy in heaven.

Now to speak of the International Settlement's handling of it: on hearing the news, the International Settlement police sent out a large party of detectives to the scene; and, besides taking the wounded Liu Heng to the Tongren Hospital, seeing that the other two wounded had already been carried off by car by the Japanese and the "western-Shanghai ruffians," they withdrew. Afterward the police department of the International Settlement declared that it would make no investigation of the case, on the ground that it had occurred in an "extramural area." I add a note: the scene was a street of the "roads built beyond the boundary," originally not within the bounds of the International Settlement; yet the British had "reached across the line" and treated it as their jurisdiction—whatever brought them profit they must have a hand in, a typical act of aggression. Over this our government had again and again lodged protests, all to no result. And now, the moment they saw Japanese and the puppet organization involved, the matter being thorny, they fell back on some plea of an "extramural area." Truly despicable! Pray do not mistake my meaning: this was by no means that they of set purpose left the net open on one side, but that they dared not meddle.

At the moment the thing happened, the central figure of the case, Wang Tianmu, was still safely on the scene and had not left—this is certain. But the other questions are complicated.

At the report of the shots, the two, Chen and He, met their doom, and the three, Ma Hetu, Yue Qingjiang, and Ding Baoling, thereupon quit the scene. They carried their weapons together to the landing-place Brother Wu xx had arranged beforehand, stayed a little, and then, with Brother Wu xx, made their way back to the rear by such routes as could be traveled. Let that be; but Mr. Wang Tianmu, though he came to not the least harm, yet did not keep step with Ma Hetu and the rest—then why did he not go? This is just the crux everyone wishes to know.

First a word in passing: Mr. Wang Tianmu had lived through war and upheaval, and would never be struck dumb by a burst of gunfire; his not going must have had some other cause.

The first thing to be probed is: in Ma Hetu and the rest doing this, did Mr. Wang Tianmu know of it beforehand or not?

If he knew—did he tacitly allow it? or disapprove? or had he counseled Ma and the rest by no means to act rashly, lest, pulling one hair and moving the whole body, it upset his set plan? If he knew, and yet decided not to go, then he must have had some ground he relied on. But could he really not have considered that such conduct would rouse suspicion in the enemy and the puppets? Besides, he must surely have thought too that, once Ma Hetu and the rest were gone, he would of necessity be left alone and weak, and set besides in a place of suspicion—and from then on, could he still seek "advancement"? If it be said he knew nothing at all beforehand, then, given the personal bond between Ma and the rest and himself—sharing life and death, comrades in hardship for years, in anything at all no one hiding a thing from another—it is a most puzzling thing. And there is yet another account, that at the moment of the affair Mr. Wang, being quick-witted and seasoned, at once ducked into the ladies' lavatory and so got clear of the peril.

Then what after all was the truth of it? I truly do not know. In all these years I have never talked over this affair with Mr. Wang Tianmu, and he has never let slip half a word of it. Brother Wu xx too keeps utter silence on it. Ma Hetu, Yue Qingjiang, and Ding Baoling I had known long before at Beiping and Tianjin, but after coming to Shanghai I never had contact with them again. This time, after the three of them sanctioned Chen Dirong and He Xingjian, we never met again, and so there was no chance to put a single question. This is all the plain truth.

To change the angle, and from the standpoint of the work make this comment: that Brother Wu xx dared meet Ma Hetu face to face rested wholly on force of spirit and courage; had Ma Hetu but refused to own the bargain, the consequence would have been past imagining. Nor was that all; once met, he had to work his words to move them, and a single sentence out of key would have swayed the whole; and Brother Wu xx was not skilled in this, yet he brought Ma Hetu round—truly no easy feat. And there is one thing more to be prized: that, the thing done, he claimed no credit for it.

The three, Ma Hetu, Yue Qingjiang, and Ding Baoling, deeply understanding the great cause, had much of the spirit of the brave man who severs his own wrist—praiseworthy indeed. Afterward Bureau headquarters issued them a reward, and forwarded a request that our superiors confer a decoration by way of encouragement, as was only right and proper.

Only Mr. Wang Tianmu had offended the god of fortune: after the Chen and He affair, the puppet "Special Operations Headquarters" at once placed Mr. Wang under detention. It is said the Japanese and puppets all held that, since Chongqing was sanctioning traitors, Mr. Wang ought to have been the prime target; and so "why did Ma Hetu not kill you?" became the great question; and Wang, though he had a mouth, could make no defense—say what he might, they none of them believed him. So he was held, then released, released and held again, dragging on several years. So racked, it was as good as his whole store of martial skill undone, never to be plied again.

On the "Shanghai District's" side, to have sanctioned Chen Mingchu, cleared away an obstacle in the work, and at the same time set the organization's discipline in order, was of course a cause for satisfaction. Yet before long word came that the puppet "Special Operations Headquarters" had again set the traitor Wan Lilang to succeed Chen Mingchu as First Section chief, dealing specially in wrecking the Juntong's activities. One ghost gone and another wraith come—this was quite beyond our reckoning.

Before the case of Chen Mingchu and He Xingjian, on December 8, 1939, in the bustle of a crowded market—before the Sun Company in the International Settlement—men dispatched by the Third Brigade leader Jiang Anhua of the "Shanghai District" sanctioned Zhao Gangyi, leader of the Third Action Team of the puppet "Special Operations Headquarters." And again, on February 26, 1940, Chen Mo and others of the Second Sub-Team of the Second Brigade of the "Shanghai District" sanctioned, on the spot, in the Ciro's Dance Hall on Bubbling Well Road, Qian Renlong, deputy chief of the confidential office of the puppet "Special Operations Headquarters." This was no act of revenge, but ought to be reckoned a stern warning, in the hope that those blinded by gain and shortsighted might rein in at the cliff's edge and turn back from their straying. This course of action, reviewed afterward, produced a considerable effect.

Yet at this time the "Shanghai District" became the more the target of all the arrows of the enemy and puppets. Originally the Japanese gendarmerie and No. 76 were our full-face foes; but after a spell of hacking and killing, there arose an odd, illogical thing—those who fought us at close quarters, flesh against flesh, were Chinese compatriots within the puppet organization, while the Japanese, who had launched a war of aggression against the Chinese race, sat high above and directed by remote control. This is truly a thing painful to tell. It was, no doubt, one set in the whole bag of tricks of "using Chinese to control Chinese"!

And so it stirred up in us the heart of shared hatred against the foe, and we were bound to deal a punishing blow. I thought: since the Japanese armed gendarmes could change into plain clothes and go into the Concession to arrest Chinese anti-Japanese elements and slaughter them, why could we not, in plain clothes, go into the enemy-and-puppet spheres at Hongkou and western Shanghai and attack armed Japanese soldiers?

This was but a notion, not yet ripe; and so I consulted several times with the District secretary Brother Qi Qingbin, and we drew up a few main points:

One. Japanese soldiers in military uniform to be the objects of killing, whatever their rank, high or low, whatever their office, great or small—strike them wherever met.

Two. It must be carried out in the district under Japanese rule or in occupied territory; save in the belt of the roads built beyond the boundary, no action to be taken in the two Concessions.

Three. To reckon beforehand the positive influence of the action, and the counter-effect it might produce—for example, the harm to the ordinary inhabitants of the district where the affair was set off.

Besides these three points, I separately exchanged views on the feasibility of this conception with the Third Action Brigade leader, my schoolfellow Jiang Anhua, and with comrade Bi Gaokui of the Newly Formed First Group. They both held that this measure was of no ordinary significance, and very well worth the trying. In the doing it was not only easy, but of small sacrifice and great effect, and a spur besides to our action men. Were it appointed to be carried out at Hongkou, western Shanghai, and such places, then, even should it bring on the Japanese army's martial law, blockade, and search, besides bringing inconvenience to the local inhabitants, it could yet reap the effect of "disturbing the social order in the enemy's rear and dealing a blow to the enemy's morale."

Inner-duty and field alike held the case feasible, and so a plan was drawn up and reported to our superiors for ruling. This was early in 1940. After the wire went out, we reckoned that approval could be had in about a week; but, unlooked-for, there was long no reply, which meant either that it was still under deliberation, or that it had been referred up to the summit for ruling.
