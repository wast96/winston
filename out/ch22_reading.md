## The Tiannan Teahouse

A guest came into the Tiannan Teahouse. Few knew his name; only a handful knew that his surname was Xiao. Like Lin Shi, he worked for the Central Liaison Bureau—a most secret unit, whose very existence was known to few even within the Chinese Communist Party. When it could not be helped and the thing had to appear in Party documents, it was screened under some such name as a rural work committee. As senior confidential couriers, the two of them were now inspectors sent out by the Liaison Bureau to the various regions. Old Xiao was quick-witted and very fast to react, and a fine shot besides. He was stationed at Ruijin, ready at any moment to take on a task assigned him.

He had brought off many a task that could scarcely be brought off, and this time too he had struck a hard one. Lin Shi had not appeared in Guangzhou as arranged. The top-secret message he was to deliver bore on the safety of a comrade of the Central leadership. It was now eleven days since Comrade Haohan had last made radio contact with Ruijin. The Central Committee was withdrawing from Shanghai by plan, but what had befallen Comrade Haohan was a thing unforeseen: someone had turned traitor, a secret organ had been broken up by the enemy, and several couriers, of the inner and outer lines both, had been arrested.

Before his emergency move, Comrade Haohan had wired Ruijin to report the situation. The Provisional Central, only just removed to Ruijin, resolved that Comrade Haohan must go to ground at once, sever every working connection, and wait for a contact signal. The signal would be published in Shen Bao on the fourteenth of the first lunar month: an advertisement for the purchase of old paintings and calligraphy; and should anything go amiss, the same advertisement would run in the Chinese-language Da Mei Wan Bao, which came out that same afternoon—the one and only fallback plan for making contact.

A secret letter of this kind, even where the radio could be used as normal, was not safe enough. Transmitters were monitored, ciphers were broken, and a decoded message, passing through courier after courier, was all too easily leaked. And but a little while before, a Southern Bureau secret radio set up in Kowloon had been destroyed. The British police were exceedingly cunning; they had tried to go on using that set to keep up wireless contact with the Shanghai underground Party—it was as well the thing was discovered in time. And so Comrade Shaoshan had sought him out, and charged him to deliver the message to Lin Shi in person.

He turned it over and over and gave up the notion of asking Ruijin for instructions by radio. Even to go through Mo Shaoqiu to the Guangzhou underground Party, and have them ask the Southern Bureau's secret station to send a wire, would mean a long wait—and was quite as unsafe. He made his decision: since Lin Shi had not come, he would make the trip to Shanghai himself. He had found a small cargo steamer leaving port that night, one that would take a few odd passengers, with several berths still free.

At twelve o'clock noon he had gone to the Xingchang Apothecary, and Mo Shaoqiu had said the two comrades from Shanghai had not arrived. The appointed time had passed, and still they did not appear. He could not wait forever at the liaison station, and so he told Mo Shaoqiu that when they came, they should meet him at the Tiannan Teahouse.

The second-floor balcony faced south, onto Jianglan Street; from this seat every stir in the street was gathered in at a glance. To the right, across Yang Lane, was Shibafu Street—joined to Jianglan Street, though Shibafu bent a little to the south—and where the three streets met they formed an open space, neither large nor small, a little square for the surrounding neighborhood: sugar-figure molders, olive-sellers, lenders of picture-books, street entertainers, tellers of fortunes by written characters and by faces—every kind of person, with rickshaws threading to and fro through the gaps in the crowd.

Red lanterns hung in the teahouse; the railings and pillars were sheathed in brass, and the floor was of patterned porcelain tiles. The tea-tables were of carved lacquered wood, and on them stood a cup of tea and two dim-sum plates apiece; some had even gone next door to buy the Shuangying chicken from the Shuangying Restaurant. The tea-drinkers had turned their chairs sideways, all of them facing toward the stage.

On the north side of the second floor was set a little stage, where a female xiaosheng was just singing the line, “I am of the Miu surname, styled Lianxian.” Old Xiao had chosen the second floor on purpose: it was crowded and confused, whereas at this hour the third and fourth floors held but a scattering of guests.

Why had they not come? Uneasy at heart, he watched Jianglan Street; on the stage the song had reached, “like the men and women who fled Qin into the Peach Blossom source”...

He noticed something not right under the arcade across the way: two or three idlers stood there talking, and one of them kept lifting his eyes toward the teahouse. Their shoulders were oddly askew, as though the right shoulder were troubled with rheumatism. That, he knew, was because beneath their clothes, under the right armpit, hung a pistol.

Without a flicker, Old Xiao rose, bending a little at the waist as though he meant to have water added to his teapot. With his heel he lightly pushed the chair aside, left the table quickly, and made for the stairs. He did not go down.

He meant to go up to the third floor. Before entering the teahouse he had noticed that the windows on the west of the third floor generally stood open, and that below them was the roof of the house next door—the Guang'an Pharmacy. Running along the pharmacy's tiled roof to the north end, one came to a row of windows in the gable, behind which was a storeroom that few, presumably, ever entered. He could climb down from the eaves and the sill, and no one would see.

The staircase was of wood, its well narrow and deep; from below came the sound of rough footsteps. Standing by the railing one could see the movement in the stairwell: on the third floor a man was craning his head to look down, one of the same company as the idlers in the street—Old Xiao knew him at a glance. There was no going up to the third floor now; his only course was to break out.

Beside the stairwell railing stood two great flower-vats, planted with little peach trees, thick with leaf and in full blossom. Standing on the stairs, one could, with a reach of the hand, tuck a small cloth bundle into the crack of a vat—one need only lean a shade to the right on the way up, and no one would catch the motion. He had done just this as he came up. In the bundle was a pistol, a Browning, with the figure of a pistol engraved on the left of the barrel. He was very fond of this gun; people all called it the pistol-brand pistol, but he would often say that by rights it ought to be called the pistol-brand pistol-pistol.

He came down with the bundle under his arm, keeping a little to the left of the stairs, his steps unhurried, a look of mild satisfaction on his face, as though he had just told the waiter a joke. He let his eyes rest on a spray of peach blossom poking up between the railings, like a guest whose mind was elsewhere.

At the foot of the stairs stood two men, heads tipped back, making signs up the stairwell to the third floor; they seemed in no hurry, meaning, most likely, to search the place floor by floor from the bottom up, these two being only there to hold the stairs. One of them was coming up, and as he brushed past Old Xiao, Old Xiao muttered under his breath: “Full house upstairs—packed in like sardines.”

He reached the ground floor; a few more steps and he would be at the door, yet still he did not quicken his pace. A waitress came near, carrying a hamper of dim sum—the Tiannan Teahouse was a proper place for tea, and its waitresses were simply waitresses, not the sort of hostess-girls found in certain Guangzhou teahouses. She passed by; three or five guests came in at the teahouse door; he judged he might now leave a little faster, and just then—

The man on the stairs suddenly shouted: “That's him!”

Old Xiao broke into a run. As he ran he pressed the bundle to his breast with his left hand, drew the pistol out of it with his right, thrust it inside his coat, and with the same motion flung the cloth away behind him.

The men in the teahouse came running out after him, and someone fired a shot into the air. At the sound the people in the street scattered and fled, tradesmen and peddlers abandoning their stalls to take cover under the arcade. Those men in plain clothes, carrying pistols, were most of them agents of the detective squad. Guangzhou's Municipal Public Security Special Detective Squad was the unit Chen Jitang kept expressly for hunting down the Chinese Communist underground. How had he been found out by the enemy? Could the Xingchang Apothecary have been exposed?

Old Xiao ran to the street corner and turned north into Yang Lane at a run; to the north lay all the little streets and lanes of Xiguan, running every which way. He squeezed into the gap between two rickshaws and fired two shots into the air. At this the street grew wilder still, the crowd fleeing in all directions. He mixed into the throng, turned into a small alley, ran straight to its eastern end, took a few steps to the right, and saw another straight lane leading east. This lane, it turned out, was very long, running east all the way, and bending right several times along its course.

In one of the bends he was stopped by agents. Two of them, one before and one behind, had hidden in two doorways, showing themselves only after he had passed the first, so that he was cut off front and back. Old Xiao stood side-on, looking ahead, then behind. Two guns were leveled at him. The lane was narrow; he had no room to maneuver.

Old Xiao gripped his pistol, held under the right side of his coat; but with it he could bring down only one man at a shot. Standing side-on, looking at the two agents before and behind him, he reckoned the angles of fire, and the time it would take, after firing left, to swing the muzzle round and fire right, and judged it impossible to drop both agents at once.

The agent at the eastern end had marked something amiss about him: “Take your hands out. Slowly.”

They held their muzzles on Old Xiao, watching his right hand; at the least suspicious movement they would fire.

Just then, from the western end of the alley, came the ringing of a bicycle bell. The ringing was urgent, yet the bicycle came on very slowly, and only after a good while did it turn out of the bend. Old Xiao looked at the man on the bicycle, and the two agents, too, could not help turning their eyes to look.

Old Xiao recognized the rider. The bicycle drew slowly nearer; the man on it did not look at Old Xiao, but said with a smile to one of the agents: “Got him?”

The words were not out before the shot rang. The rider was none other than Yi Junnian. He fired and shot one agent dead; and when he looked back, Old Xiao and the other agent had both been hit and fallen. The two had fired on each other almost in the same instant, and taken their wounds and gone down in the same instant, both gravely hurt, yet both still alive, struggling on the ground to raise their guns.

Yi Junnian rode up to the agent and fired one more shot into him, then watched the man until he breathed his last, the anger and bewilderment in his eyes slowly fading away.

The bullet had struck Old Xiao in the belly. Yi Junnian carried him out of the straight lane on his back, went north again, and at the crossing where Shibafu met Xiajiufu stopped a rickshaw, having the puller take them to a landing on the Xihao Creek; there he got down and carried Old Xiao onto a small boat. Old Xiao had held out until now, guiding the way, but once aboard, when the two of them had hidden under the awning, he let out his breath and at last sank into unconsciousness.

It was a Tanka sampan. The boatwoman worked the scull, and the little craft threaded along the creek; after a while—there was no telling how long—it moored beside a row of stilt-huts at the water's edge. A man came out of the huts and, seeing that Old Xiao had not woken, looked at the wound, knew that he could not be moved just then, told Yi Junnian he would go first to fetch a doctor, and left at once.

Yi Junnian knew well enough that this was a secret contact point of the Guangzhou underground Party, yet he gave it no particular heed. His whole care was for those few sentences Old Xiao carried in his head; the whole afternoon he crouched in the boat's awning, watching the unconscious Old Xiao.

The night before, on a sudden impulse, he had killed Ling Wen in that house on the Rear Street of Tianguan Li. Had it been only that Ling Wen had found out something of his past, he might not have done it so soon.

Before that he had even thought that, given time, Ling Wen might little by little forget Long Dong, and that then he might very likely bring her round. Failing that, she could be sent to the Reflection Institute in Nanjing and shut up there a while. The Special Operations Headquarters had certain Communist turncoats serve there as instructors, giving lectures to those not yet willing to recant, holding discussions and debates; some there had slowly changed their minds, and he had hoped that Ling Wen, sooner or later, would too.

But he had had no choice. Otherwise he would have had no chance to make Old Xiao tell him the secret. By instinct he could guess there was a gold mine down below; to dig out that vein might deal the Chinese Communist underground a devastating blow. It was a chance in a thousand years—this would be, since the year he had dug out Long Dong's intelligence network, another vast success. That earlier success had made him the ace of the Special Operations Headquarters, and from then on he had borne the code name “Xi Shi.”

He truly had not foreseen that Ling Wen would remember the photograph. When he showed it to her, he could not have foreseen that she would one day have the chance actually to go to that house. Even now he could no longer recall why he had shown her the picture in the first place, and told her it was the place where he had secretly joined the Party.

The photograph had been taken after he swore his oath. He had indeed sworn there and joined the Communist Party, with Long Dong as his sponsor. But by the time the picture was taken, Long Dong had been killed by him—the picture was taken after the killing. He had done a great service; he had not reckoned that a few days later Ye Qinian would have him slip into the Shanghai underground Party under the identity of Yi Junnian.

He never doubted Teacher Ye's designs. He was Teacher Ye's true disciple, taught at his own knee; the likes of You Tianxiao, who had come out of some training class and had “Teacher” forever on their lips, he could not but find faintly absurd. For it must be understood that as far back as Republic Year 13, when the two parties, Nationalist and Communist, had only just begun to cooperate at the start of the year, when the Northern Expedition manifesto was but a few days old, when Feng Yuxiang had not yet made a prisoner of Cao Kun and Sun Yat-sen had not so much as dreamed of going north—already, in those days, Teacher Ye had said to him: between the Nationalists and the Communists there must surely come a war.

Teacher Ye in those days was only a university professor of modest note, whom everyone took for a believer in anarchism, a scholar of Esperanto. No one guessed that what Teacher Ye carried in his mind was history.

Teacher Ye had told him that there was a calling which let a man stand always on the commanding heights of history. Teacher Ye liked to arrange his students' futures. He said that the coming struggle between the two parties would be exceedingly cruel, and that when it came, the Nationalist Party would need a special secret organization, holding a secret force in its grip.

The Nationalist Party? For the first time he grasped that Teacher Ye's anarchist stance was quietly turning; books by Dai Jitao had begun to appear on Teacher Ye's desk. By what means he did not know, but Teacher Ye soon came to know that author, and took part in the secret gatherings Dai Jitao organized within the Nationalist Party, where all were of one mind that the Communists must be exterminated root and branch, that on no account must one nurse an ulcer and leave a bequest of woe. But Teacher Ye had little interest in empty theorizing, nor did he believe the tide could be turned by writing a few articles of propaganda; the Nationalist Party, he held, must found a secret-service organization.

In earlier days, as an anarchist, Teacher Ye had been a man of action too. He had traveled through many countries, making a special study of the assassinations carried out by anarchists, and of the secret-police organizations of various governments and colonies. His conclusion was this: the world to come belonged to the secret agent.

And so Teacher Ye sent him to Guangzhou, for he was a Cantonese. Once there, he first sat the examination into a steamship company, and then joined a workers'-movement training class run by the Communists. For his zeal he was drawn into the workers' picket corps. After the great strike, by Teacher Ye's arrangement, he passed smoothly into the Public Security Bureau, served a few months first at the Hexi sub-bureau, and was then swiftly transferred into the Special Detective Squad. In the summer of Republic Year 14, someone assassinated Liao Zhongkai in the streets of Guangzhou—a matter which he had always suspected might have something to do with Teacher Ye.

All the while he still wore his short workman's jacket and sat in a back corner of the trade-union study class, waiting to be discovered. Everything fell out as Teacher Ye had foreseen, like playing chess from a printed manual. The man who came to him did not come by way of his old comrades in the picket corps, but sought him out at a fried-noodle stall on Weixin North Road; they became friends, and often met to take tea together. By Teacher Ye's method, he only let drop a scrap of intelligence now and then in idle talk, blending together the daily reports of the Special Detective Squad, the gossip picked up at the constables' social club, and the rumors of the streets, and dressing it all up with a little embroidery for his friend's ears. The Communist underground took a good long while to vet him; he waited two years, and at last Long Dong came.

The scull stirred the river water, and through the churning waves a larger wooden boat passed slowly by, laden with a full load of firewood lashed down. Yi Junnian looked at the firewood and roughly guessed where they were. But he had no interest in the underground Party's odd little contact points; his only interest was in the secret in the head of this man lying unconscious before him.

The night before, on leaving Haoxian Street, he had gone at once to see the chief of the Special Operations Headquarters' Guangzhou station. Before his arrival Ye Qinian had already wired orders that the station's head cooperate with his operation. The moment he got there he had a telegram sent to Director Ye, and, his consent obtained, had the Guangzhou station chief muster men from the Public Security Bureau's detective squad. The station chief was only a deputy in the detective squad; the captain was Chen Jitang's man, and that “King of the Southern Sky” was keenly on guard against the penetration of Guangzhou by Nanjing's forces—so the station chief was in a difficult position. To arrest Communists was no trouble at all; to make a pretense of arresting Communists was not so easily arranged.

“You must find a few trusted men, and set them among the operation's personnel, to lead the rest in playing out the piece.”

Yi Junnian knew that at the level of the heads of the Headquarters' various regional sub-stations, though they had no right to know of “Xi Shi,” they had all heard something of him: Director Ye's treasure, the Headquarters' greatest meritorious servant. For a trifle like this he did not care to send telegram after telegram asking Teacher Ye. He told the station chief in a low voice that he was “Xi Shi” (a thing that must never be let out): if matters were handled as he required, he would answer for any trouble; if they were not, then any trouble would be the station chief's to answer for. The station chief grudgingly agreed.

When it came to the point, the station chief balked again—for Yi Junnian had said he might have to kill one or two detective-squad men, so as to win the other side's trust.

The station chief thought a long while, then said: “If ever the word got out, the charge that the Special Operations Headquarters slaughters its own—why, not even Director Ye could bear the weight of that!”

This was a threat; he said nothing, and looked at the man.

“I had meant to send men without our captain's knowing. If, after all that, we not only fail to take any Communists but lose two of our own besides, the responsibility would be hard to shoulder. Things in Guangzhou are not as they are elsewhere: in the Public Security Bureau, from the commissioner on down, they are all Chen Jitang's men. There have always been those in the bureau who talk in private of my having dealings with the Special Operations Headquarters. Should another such affair occur—they are ruthless, cruel men, and I could not begin to guess how the King of the Southern Sky would deal with me.”

“Then let it be your own trusted men who die for nothing.”

The station chief thought a long while, seemingly unable, after all, to harden his heart to it. Yi Junnian, for his part, thought little of the scruple—though the course was a great risk to himself as well: should a single shot fail to kill, and they fire two back at him, then this “Xi Shi,” won in Guangzhou, would be lost again in Guangzhou.

The doctor came—a Western-medicine man. The underground Party was formidable indeed; wherever you went, they had a full complement of people, Yi Junnian thought. The wound was cleaned, the bullet taken out, and Old Xiao was given a saline drip. The doctor said the wound was grave; he could not promise the patient would wake—it all rested with the man himself. In other words, it lay in the hands of Heaven. And at this point Yi Junnian sincerely hoped he would wake.
