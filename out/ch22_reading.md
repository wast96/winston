## Chapter Twenty-Two. The Hour of the Dragon, First Half (7 a.m.)

{v} Watching Zhang Xiaojing in the misery of his dilemma, torn this way and that, Xiao Gui savored it to the full.

{v} With an effort he shifted his body over, pressed close against his ear, and in a low voice spoke a single sentence.

{d} The third year of Tianbao, the fifteenth day of the first month; the hour of the Dragon, first half.

{d} Chang’an; Chang’an County; Anye Ward.

Amid the urgent, hurrying beat of the street drums, Li Bi caught up the hem of his robe, strode swiftly forward a few paces, and came straight to the foot of the Self-Raining Pavilion. He raised his head and, without a trace of fear, fixed his gaze upon the man within the pavilion—the most powerful man in all the great Tang save the Son of Heaven himself, and his own greatest enemy. The other was watching him in the same moment, but, standing on his dignity, did not open his mouth.

Behind Li Bi came a clamor of disordered footfalls as the soldiers of the Lüben Guards pressed up together after him. Swiftly they formed into an arc, hemming the whole of the Self-Raining Pavilion tightly about. The guard at Li Linfu’s side arched a brow and made to draw his blade and step forward, only to be lightly checked by his master.

Li Bi brought his two hands together in a respectful clasp and said in a clear voice: ‘I pay my respects to the Right Minister.’

‘Deputy Director Li does me courtesy.’ Li Linfu returned coolly, with an air of authority that awed without anger. He was tall and spare of build, his face lean and clean, the white hair on his crown combed without a single strand astray—for all the world like a tall, long-legged crane.

Li Bi marked that the other had addressed him by his commissioned post, ‘Deputy Director of the Jing’an Bureau,’ rather than by his substantive office, ‘Hanlin academician-in-waiting’—a sign that Li Linfu had already judged Ji Wen’s seizure of power to have failed, and had accepted that outcome.

All this day the Right Minister had set himself against the Jing’an Bureau, and now at last he was showing weakness, owning defeat. At the thought Li Bi could not help a lift of his spirits. In all his long years as minister, Li Linfu had seldom shown weakness—that he should give ground like this, surely it was because Li Bi had struck him at a vital point?

And it stood to reason: this hidden hand behind it all, at the very moment he came closest to victory, had been run to earth by the Jing’an Bureau in his own most secret residence; that his composure should waver was only to be expected. So thinking, Li Bi said with a smile: ‘This Self-Raining Pavilion is at once exquisite and grand; none but a man of such deep design as the Right Minister could have contrived it.’

Li Linfu stroked the three long wisps of beard beneath his chin and raised his eyes: ‘The pavilion’s design is fine enough, true. When I have retired from office, I ought to learn the trick of it myself.’

From the reply Li Bi sensed the other’s weakness. He shook his head, drew a hand-declaration from the breast of his robe, and held it out: ‘The Right Minister makes light of it. Your servant has ascertained the matter clearly—is this not, after all, your concealed-holding residence?’

The aphids had once lodged in this house; so long as he could fix fast who its master was, then by no means could the man wriggle free of complicity. With the situation at the Xingqing Palace still unclear, Li Bi had to drive the nail home and clinch it, to seize the gravest hidden danger and hold it fast by the throat—only so could he win the greatest advantage for the heir apparent.

Li Linfu took the hand-declaration, ran his eye briefly over it, gave it a shake, and said with a cold laugh: ‘Because the two words “Longxi” are written here, this becomes my property? You are too rash by half, Changyuan.’ Li Bi had long foreseen that he would deny it flat: ‘If this is not the Right Minister’s outlying residence, then be so good as to explain—the spring banquet at the Qinzheng Wuben Tower was not yet over; why did you rise midway and leave, to hide yourself away in this place?’

He had supposed Li Linfu would go on hunting for excuses and quibbling, but the other’s response fell far outside his expectation: ‘Was it not you yourself, Changyuan, who called me here, saying there was a weighty matter to discuss?’

Li Bi was startled, and at once his face darkened: ‘I have been hard at work all this while in the Jing’an Bureau—when did I ever trouble the Right Minister? And besides, given my station, how could a single word of mine call you away from the spring banquet? The Right Minister rates me far too high.’

‘In the ordinary way, of course not. But today there were first the Türk Wolf Guards, and after them the aphids; all Chang’an was in alarm and unrest, and where the Sage’s safety might be at stake, I could not but be careful.’ Li Linfu produced from his breast a rolled slip of paper, on which stood a single line of ink; its purport, roughly, was that the Son of Heaven was in some unforeseen peril, and that the reader should come with all speed to a certain residence in Anye Ward to meet, and breathe no word of it to anyone. The signature was that of the Jing’an Bureau.

Li Bi said: ‘The Right Minister has planted so many eyes and ears within the Jing’an Bureau—how could you fail to know that at that hour Director He lay senseless and could not be roused, and I myself had been carried off by the aphids? How could anyone have sent a letter over in the name of the Jing’an Bureau?’

‘It is precisely because I did not know who had written it that I could not treat it lightly.’ Li Linfu tapped the back of the slip, where a round stain of seepage had been left. ‘This slip was not brought by a runner—it was pressed beneath my wine-cup.’

Li Bi started, for the two letters the heir apparent had received on the floor of the spring banquet had likewise been pressed, by no one knew whom, beneath a wine-cup. He had reckoned at first that this was the Right Minister deliberately drawing the heir apparent away, so as to cast him under suspicion of murdering his own imperial father; but now that the Right Minister had, of all things, received the very same letter, the whole affair was on the instant thrown into a bewildering tangle.

To draw both the heir apparent and Li Linfu away from the spring banquet at one and the same time—what in the world could be the reason for it?

No—that was wrong! Li Bi warned himself inwardly. There could be no such thing; between the heir apparent and Li Linfu, one of them must surely be lying. He clenched his fist, gave up the feints and evasions of his questioning, and said straight out:

‘Does the Right Minister know that, but a moment ago, the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower blew apart?’

Li Linfu’s face went grave; hurriedly he turned to look toward the Xingqing Palace. But the night was heavy and dark, the dawn-light only just risen, and he could not make out how things stood over there. They had heard the sound of the blast a while before, yet had not thought to connect it with that quarter. Now that Li Bi had spoken, Li Linfu grasped at once the gravity of it.

‘What has happened?’ this Secretariat Director of the great Tang asked in a heavy voice, his brows knotted tight together.

Li Bi secretly admired the man’s playacting, and said: ‘What has happened, the Right Minister should know better than I. You have long coveted the Jing’an Bureau, planted your spies within it, let the wolf in at the door—was it not all for this very moment?’ Li Bi had thrown caution to the winds now, and spoke plainly and sharp. He waved a hand, and the Lüben soldiers about him at once raised their crossbows, lest this mighty minister should make some sudden move.

Minister for so many years, Li Linfu turned it over in his mind and understood in a moment why Li Bi had come storming up to hem him in. Several guards, greatly alarmed, moved without thinking to shield their master behind them. Unruffled in the face of the change, he pushed the guards aside, drew himself upright, walked to the edge of the pavilion, and said coolly: ‘Changyuan, this is a plot.’

All at once Li Bi felt a great urge to laugh: that Li Linfu—honey on the lips and a sword in the belly—should call this a plot, what an ironical thing it was.

‘Has the Right Minister then no coveting heart toward the Jing’an Bureau? Does he not, day and night, dream of bringing down the heir apparent?’

A sinister, hawkish light showed in Li Linfu’s eyes, and the corner of his mouth lifted faintly: ‘You are not wrong. But in this matter—had I laid my schemes beforehand, then the one who ought now to be dead is you, Changyuan.’

‘Because in your reckoning, I was to have been dead long since!’

Li Bi held no longer to any courtesy; he stepped forward and seized Li Linfu by the sleeve. Li Linfu sighed and slowly shook his head: ‘Though you and I stand on different ground, I have always admired your gifts. A pity that your conduct now should so disappoint me.’

‘The Right Minister might as well come back with me to the Jing’an Bureau, and there we can sift and lay the matter bare at our leisure.’

Li Bi took him only for a man at the end of his road, babbling nonsense. The thread of the whole affair he had by now made out entirely: Li Linfu was the hidden hand behind the aphids and the Türk Wolf Guards, and had besides planted an inside man within the Jing’an Bureau. The two, working in concert from without and within, had crippled the Bureau and carried Li Bi off. Then the Right Minister had, on the one hand, seized the chance to set Ji Wen on to grasp power, and on the other loosed the aphids to launch their attack. He himself, to keep clear of the splash, had left the Qinzheng Wuben Tower early and hidden away in this house; and at the same time he had the aphids use Li Bi to draw the heir apparent, Li Heng, away. So contrived, the world would be led to think that this attack was the heir apparent’s own doing, a bid to murder his imperial father and seize the throne—and thereby he would be brought down once and for all.

Who had the power to set the Türk Wolf Guards and the aphids in motion? Who knew the particulars of Chang’an, within the walls and without, so intimately? Who had the power to move every last piece on the board into just its fittest place?

The whole plan was linked ring within ring, close-wrought and fine, by no means a thing an ordinary man could master. Reason it out by motive, by power, by style, or by the many signs already brought to light—only Li Linfu could have played such a game.

There were two variables in this plan: the first was Zhang Xiaojing, the second Li Bi. After the aphids had angled Li Heng out, they had meant to silence Li Bi for good; but never in the world had they foreseen that he would, of all things, make his escape with Zhang Xiaojing’s help. And so the whole conspiracy, just like that, was laid wholly bare the moment Li Bi laid hold of the residence in Anye Ward.

This talk of a slip from the Jing’an Bureau, of his not being the master of this residence—it was all empty falsehood. Li Bi could not be bothered to refute it point by point; he trusted that with Li Linfu’s discernment the man could see that, before so clear a chain of evidence, to hole up and struggle on was without any meaning. Gripping Li Linfu by the arm, he came out from the Self-Raining Pavilion, crying aloud: ‘Business of the Jing’an Bureau!’

The guards tried to bar the way, but the Lüben soldiers at once closed their formation around the two of them.

At this Li Linfu’s voice sounded once more: ‘Changyuan, you are so clever a man—how is it you cannot think through even this one point? This affair—what profit is there in it for me?’

The words were spoken low, yet in Li Bi’s ears they fell like a thunderclap. His steps froze where he stood, and he turned his head to look at this arch-culprit. The other’s expression was composed—there was even, in his eyes, a trace of pity.

Li Bi perceived that he had made a mistake—a very great mistake, a huge mistake born of certain surmises he had all this while striven with all his heart to avoid.

Yao Runeng lowered his aching arms, carefully set the purple lantern down upon a holding-frame shaped like an upturned saddle, and only then leaned his body against the parapet at the top of the great watchtower and let out a long breath—though no ease showed in his eyes.

Li Bi had promised to furnish him with resources, but there were far too few who understood the watchtower signaling, and so he could only do the work with his own hands. By now the street drums of the six avenues had begun to sound, and the city gates on every side were shut fast. The task Li Bi had given him was, for the time being, as good as done. To restore the old signaling power in full would take some days yet, but for the present, at the least, it would not hold up the great matters in hand.

Since being let out of the gaol, Yao Runeng had gained a rough grasp of the whole state of things in Chang’an. So bizarre had the course of events been that it left him agape. Several of the elders of the Yao house had risen from the ranks of officialdom, and from his boyhood had told Yao Runeng all manner of strange and curious cases. Yet all their tales put together were not so uncanny as this single case now before him.

Yao Runeng felt an unbearable tightness in his chest. This calamity before them might plainly have been averted; had it not been for the drags and hindrances of every kind, it would surely have been settled long ago. So simple a thing—why had it been made so tangled? Now Zhang Xiaojing was nowhere to be found, Tanqi’s whereabouts were unknown, and Xu Bin had even been murdered in the very heart of the Jing’an Bureau—all of it, plainly, needless.

Could this be what Zhang Xiaojing had meant by ‘unless you become a monster like it, you will be devoured by it’?

Yao Runeng clenched his fists in grief: if a man did not keep faith with his first resolve, then what meaning was there in holding fast at all! A few shichen before, atop the great watchtower, he had flung out in anger his vow of ‘no retreat’—for the very reason that he would not become a monster foundered in the world as it was, however heavy the cost. He believed that Commander Zhang too must be somewhere, striving to resist the corrosion of Chang’an.

Yao Runeng had sent signals out to every watchtower, asking after Zhang Xiaojing’s whereabouts, but not one tower had given a satisfying answer. The last time Zhang Xiaojing appeared in the watchtower records was at the first half of the Rat hour, in Zhiye Ward; after that he had vanished utterly, and been sighted no more.

Yao Runeng was just wondering where Zhang Xiaojing might be when an assistant beside him called out: ‘The Xun position, second tower—a message coming in!’

With the great watchtower for its hub, the surrounding country was parceled into eight regions, each named for one of the Eight Trigrams. All the watchtowers, far and near, stood raised upon the axes of these eight regions. Xun lay to the southeast; ‘the second tower,’ then, meant the second tower along the southeastward axis from the great watchtower.

These hastily gathered assistants could do a few simple things, but they did not understand the sending, receiving, and reading of the signals; that work had to be Yao Runeng’s own. He hurried to the southeastern corner of the great watchtower and, watching the distant purple lantern rise and fall, called out the numbers aloud so the assistants could set them down. When the signal was fully through, Yao Runeng bent his head, drew a few strokes, and swiftly deciphered it.

‘Runeng: Commander Zhang summons you urgently. Come alone. Imperative.’

Yao Runeng’s brows drew tight together. Commander Zhang? Why did he not come back, but instead hide away upon some far-off watchtower to send a message? Was he wounded, or was there some trouble he could not speak of? Stranger still, this message was sent to him alone, and not to the Jing’an Bureau.

He glanced at the assistants; they were wholly in the dark about the numbers, and had no notion of what the message read when it was rendered out.

Yao Runeng quickly folded the roll of paper and gripped it in his palm. This move of Zhang Xiaojing’s was understandable enough. He had, after all, been suspected again and again, had even been hunted through the whole city; that he should be full of wariness toward the Jing’an Bureau was only to be expected.

Commander Zhang must be caught now in some hard strait; for one reason or another he could not openly ask for aid, and so had no choice but to send a signal back by an outlying watchtower. He must know that Yao Runeng was the only man who could now read the signals, and the only man in the Jing’an Bureau he could at present trust.

At the thought a warmth kindled in Yao Runeng’s breast. He charged the several assistants at his side to keep watching the lantern-messages round about, and then hastened down the ladder from the great watchtower.

The traitor within had not yet been caught, and so the Jingzhao Prefecture and the environs of the former Jing’an Bureau were still under strict lockdown. But Yao Runeng had already been cleared of suspicion, and the guards, after a few simple questions, let him out.

The second tower of the Xun position lay in Xinghua Ward, to the southeast of Guangde Ward. This ward held two watchtowers in all—a first tower at its northwestern corner, and a second at its southeastern, set catty-corner to each other. Yao Runeng came at a jog-trot to Xinghua Ward, where he saw many of the common folk yawning as they made their way back, while the ward-soldiers already stood guard at the gate, urging the residents to get home as fast as they could, for the gate was about to be shut.

Yao Runeng flashed his waist-tally and went straight into the ward, making directly for the second tower. That watchtower stood beside a great livestock pen, penned full of pigs and sheep, chickens and geese, the reek of dung thick in the air. Holding his nose, he ducked his head and passed through the pen, and soon saw the long wooden ladder standing at the foot of the watchtower.

Intent only on his errand, he did not mark a low, grim clank of metal that rose from the pen beside him. Yao Runeng tilted his head back, reached up and gripped a rung of the wooden ladder, climbed two rungs, and set his feet upon it in turn. Soon his body hung in mid-air, wholly off its guard.

A pig in the pen all at once set up an uneasy grunting, and the chickens and geese too beat their wings and cackled loudly. From behind them a crossbow-lock reached out, leveled at Yao Runeng’s wholly unshielded chest.

Bang, bang, bang, bang, bang.

Five reports came in succession, the sound of crossbow-bolts loosed, and then one shrill and piteous scream.

Yao Runeng’s eyes went wide; his whole body froze upon the wooden ladder, unable to stir so much as an inch.

From his high vantage he could see clearly some ten-odd Lüben soldiers come charging out of the alley beyond, each with a short crossbow in hand, and behind them a civil official following after. Swiftly they surrounded the whole vicinity; and within the pen a figure lay toppled on the ground, still gripping in his hand a crossbow-lock that had not yet been fired.

‘What—what is going on here?’ Yao Runeng did not know whether he should climb up or climb down.

The civil official tilted his head up and called out: ‘Young master of the Yao house, you have had a hard time of it—come down.’ The voice struck Yao Runeng as familiar; he looked hard, and found it was indeed someone he knew—none other than Adjutant Zhao, with whom he had had dealings in the Right Xiao Guard, and who was now lending his help to the Jing’an Bureau as well.

‘But...’ Yao Runeng cast a glance upward—perhaps Zhang Xiaojing was still up there after all. Adjutant Zhao saw through his thoughts: ‘It was a trap. Did you truly believe it?’

Yao Runeng did not believe it; he climbed on to the top and looked, and sure enough there was no trace of Zhang Xiaojing within—only two constables lying toppled inside, their breath already gone. He climbed back down the ladder, his face turned very ill, and asked Adjutant Zhao what in the world had happened.

‘Do you remember, Deputy Director Li told you that the traitor within the Jing’an Bureau had had some dealings with you?’

Yao Runeng nodded; he remembered clearly Li Bi’s very words: ‘From our analysis, we judge that this traitor must have had some dealings with you, and must at some point have let slip a flaw. Think it over carefully; if anything comes to you, tell me at any time.’ At the time he had thought it strange that Deputy Director Li should insist so flatly that he must know something of the traitor.

Adjutant Zhao, with a touch of self-satisfaction, tapped his own head: ‘That was not said for your ears—it was said for the traitor’s.’ Yao Runeng was a blunt, forthright man, but no fool; hearing this, he understood at once.

Deputy Director Li did not in fact know with whom the traitor had had dealings, and so had deliberately let off a smokescreen in front of Yao Runeng. The traitor, hearing it, would surely grow anxious, and would try to silence Yao Runeng for good, to keep his own identity from coming out.

But the Jingzhao Prefecture, within and without, was under total martial guard, and Yao Runeng hung apart, alone atop the great watchtower; there was no getting at him from inside. So this traitor had turned to account the very nature of watchtower signaling—that it passed messages without any man being seen—and angled Yao Runeng out beyond Guangde Ward, to strike when the moment served.

And Adjutant Zhao had long since had his instructions from Li Bi’s own lips, and kept a close watch on Yao Runeng’s movements. The moment he found him going out, he had fallen in behind him at once—and sure enough it had answered.

Yao Runeng’s face stiffened somewhat: Deputy Director Li had made a bait of him. Had Adjutant Zhao been half a step late, the traitor would indeed have been exposed—but Yao Runeng himself would not have escaped death. Adjutant Zhao clapped him on the shoulder and said they had best take a look at the quarry first.

Yao Runeng roused himself with an effort and looked over toward the pen. The livestock had all been driven off, and one could see a dark shape lying face-down in the filthy mire, a hand-crossbow flung to one side. Two bolts had struck him in the back; but from the faint twitching of the line of his spine, one could tell that he was still alive.

Alive was well and good. This fellow had opened the water-channel in the rear court of the Jing’an Bureau, done to death half the Bureau’s staff—Xu Bin among them—and had a hand, at one remove, in bringing on the eruption of the Que-le Huo-duo. Reckoned up strictly, he was one of the greatest criminals of the night; he could not be let die so easily as this.

Yao Runeng stepped forward, into the pen, the rank and stinking muck splashing up beneath his feet. He reached out and turned this traitor over. By now the sky had grown dimly light, and in that faint illumination Yao Runeng saw the features of the man’s face, and could not help his shock.

‘How can it... be you?!’

In the instant of Yao Runeng’s stunned surprise, the traitor sprang all at once up out of the mud, flung out both hands to spatter filth into Yao Runeng’s eyes, and then, bearing his arrow-wounds, turned and ran off in the opposite direction.

Adjutant Zhao was in no great hurry; he had men posted all through this quarter. Struck with arrows as he was, the fellow could not possibly get away. Zhao called his men to close in from every side, forming a tight, unbroken line, drawing in by degrees upon the pen.

But when they had drawn in to a very small compass, they found—the man was gone!

Beside himself with vexation, Adjutant Zhao ordered a thorough search. Soon it yielded a result: beneath this pen, it turned out, there ran an earthenware drainage pipe, sloping down aslant straight into a covered channel below. When the pen was cleaned in the ordinary way, the dung and filth of the beasts was drained off through here and washed away with the water.

The lid of the pipe had been flung open and cast to one side; its inner bore was fairly wide, and plainly the traitor had made his escape down this very way.

Adjutant Zhao roared at them to give chase, but the soldiers, seeing the pipe fouled within and without with black-brown filth and giving off a putrid, rotten stench, all hung back, and were a beat slow to move.

Only one man was the exception.

Yao Runeng was the first to rush forward, and without a backward thought bored his way into the pipe.

The outer rampart of Chang’an stood some four zhang high, rammed twice over from the finest yellow earth, and in solidity it rivaled the Tongwan City that Helian Bobo had raised in his day. At its four corners, and about its twelve gates, it had been especially strengthened with a facing of brick. And along the foot of the outer wall there ran a moat, three zhang broad and two zhang deep, girdling it about.

The water of the moat came from the three great canals—the Guangtong, the Yong’an, and the Longshou; in winter the water ran low, but it kept always to a depth of a zhang and more. The people of Chang’an, at a loose end, would come down to the bank to fish and the like. The garrison did not forbid it; only they would not allow bathing or the washing of clothes, lest the envoys of the outer domains should see it and be offended in their view.

Seen from afar now, the whole moat was like a sash of dark silk, studded all along with countless flecks of glinting golden light—the several hundred water-lanterns set out upon the ice.

These water-lanterns were of the simplest make: a board or a sheet of oiled paper for a boat, and upon it a single candle set upright—this was in origin a custom of the Ghost Festival, for ferrying the dead across, but the common folk felt that at the Lantern Festival too they could not forget their departed kin, and must set out at least a few. Still, it was, when all was said, a shadowy rite of sacrifice to ghosts, and unlucky within the city walls; and so everyone came to release them by the moat outside the city, where the gates in any case stood unshut the whole night through. The one inconvenience was that the water’s surface had frozen, so that the lanterns could not float, but could only shine where they stood.

Now, above that surface glittering with golden light, a dark mass was plunging swiftly downward. Those faint fires on the ice, that might gutter out at any moment, lit up—together with the light of dawn—two despairing silhouettes.

Zhang Xiaojing had his arms locked about Xiao Gui, and together with that one signal-flag the two of them were tangled in mid-air into a single knot, dead fast—the very scene of years ago before the beacon-fort played out once again, only this time the bond between the two men was utterly changed. Xiao Gui glared viciously at Zhang Xiaojing, while Zhang Xiaojing kept his single eye tightly shut and made no exchange with him at all.

The speed of their fall was too great; they had no leisure to open their mouths. As the wind went whistling past their ears, their bodies swiftly neared the ground. First came a crunch, and the thin ice split, overturning a great heap of little water-lanterns; then came a splash, the water flying up, the candle-flames of the ghost-ferrying all round guttering out at once, as the two men crashed straight down into the moat and threw up a high surge of waves.

Water a zhang and more in depth was not enough to cancel out entirely the force of their descent. The two of them sank straight to the deepest part and struck heavily against the bottom, mud and silt flying up in a swirl, so that all at once the water turned turbid.

Zhang Xiaojing felt only a wild dance of golden stars before his eyes, his whole body as though struck a savage blow between the shoulders by some great hand. His five viscera and six bowels, in one instant, congealed into a single lump, and in the next flew apart in every direction. The violent shock of that stretching and wrenching all but shook his three souls and seven spirits clean out of their fleshly shell. For the space of a breath, Zhang Xiaojing truly saw his own back—and saw it, moreover, drawing slowly away from him. In the same moment, a great flood of ice-cold water poured into his lungs, and set him coughing and choking in agony.

Had it been in the fullness of his powers, Zhang Xiaojing could have swiftly gathered his wits and striven to save himself. But he was too weak now; a whole day of running and fighting had wrung every last measure of strength from his bones. Slowly Zhang Xiaojing spread his four limbs, let his muscles go slack, and the last thought in his heart was that to die just so would be well enough.

But all at once a violent churning sounded at his ear, and his body, in spite of itself, bobbed upward. Zhang Xiaojing turned his face aside and saw Xiao Gui struggling hard with both arms, floundering up toward the surface of the water. The irony was that the signal-flag had by now been soaked and rolled into a single cord, one end wound about Xiao Gui’s ankle and the other about Zhang Xiaojing’s waist. Wet and drawn tight, the flag could not easily be loosed, so that it looked for all the world as though Xiao Gui were hauling on a rope to drag Zhang Xiaojing upward with all his might.

Zhang Xiaojing did not know whether Xiao Gui truly meant to save him, or had simply had no time to loose the flag; but he had no strength left to think it through, and let the other flounder as he would. Xiao Gui’s strength was a good deal greater than Zhang Xiaojing’s, and after a struggle of ten-odd thrashings the heads of both men broke the surface at once, rasping and gasping for breath.

From the bank of the moat came several alarmed cries: ‘Hey! It looks like someone’s fallen in over here!’ And then footsteps came near.

These people must be common folk who had been releasing water-lanterns nearby, all clad in white shirts, lanterns in hand. Seeing that the ice of the moat had split open a great hole, with two men’s heads bobbing inside it, they all gave a start; and looking harder, they saw that one of the two was still floundering. Several lanterns were held high, lighting the bank bright as day, and a few bold young fellows stepped out onto the thin ice, edging fearfully toward them.

Someone had brought several long bamboo poles of the kind used for setting out lanterns, and laid one under each of Xiao Gui’s armpits. Several men heaved with a will and, in one breath, hoisted the two of them clear of the water, and dragged them by a dozen hands to the bank.

Zhang Xiaojing’s sight was blurred; dimly he felt his two cheeks being slapped hard, and then a finger reached in beneath his nose, and a voice cried out loud: ‘This one’s still breathing too!’

‘Still breathing too? Then Xiao Gui is alive as well?’ Zhang Xiaojing’s consciousness now hung together not at all; he could think only in broken fits and starts. Below his neck he felt almost no sensation—even pain, cold, ache, all such feelings had vanished, leaving him wooden and numb, as though his head had been joined onto a stone statue.

In a little while, another honest, simple voice reached his ears: ‘This—is this not Chief Zhang?’

The voice sounded faintly familiar; Zhang Xiaojing forced his eyes open and saw an honest, kindly face with a lion’s nose and thick lips. It came back to him a little—this was Aluoyue, a man of Linyi who bred camels in the East Market, whose greatest dream was to raise up the finest of all ‘wind-footed wild camels.’ Aluoyue had once been bullied by a petty clerk who insisted that the camels he had toiled to rear were stolen; in the end it was Zhang Xiaojing who upheld justice for him, and so saved the fruit of his labors.

Finding it was, of all people, his benefactor, Aluoyue’s face lit with gladness: ‘It really is Chief Zhang!’ He bent down, pressed his hands to Zhang Xiaojing’s chest, and bore down to knead it. That pair of rough, big hands was uncommonly strong; Zhang Xiaojing opened his mouth and, with a retching gush, brought up a great heap of water, and his body came at last to a little feeling.

Several heads round about pressed in, and one by one they too made out who he was, so that a chorus of ‘Chief Zhang,’ ‘Zhang the Yama,’ ‘Zhang Xiaojing’ rose up. These people Zhang Xiaojing remembered as well: all of them residents of Wannian County, who had had dealings with him in greater or lesser degree.

He wanted to warn these people, to have them look up toward the city wall. Hanging there was a rattan basket, and within it the fainted Taizhen, while nearby lay the present Son of Heaven himself, senseless and beyond rousing. But Zhang Xiaojing worked his mouth and found that his vocal cords could bring out no sound at all.

It was likely that the shock of the fall into the water had numbed him for the time, and it might take a while yet before his voice came back.

Seeing that Zhang Xiaojing had come to himself, Aluoyue was greatly glad. It occurred to him that another man lay nearby—a friend of Zhang Xiaojing’s, no doubt—and so he went over and kneaded at him a while too. Just then a companion of his said suddenly: ‘Did you hear the drums?’

Aluoyue was taken aback; he halted and listened, and sure enough the all-too-familiar street drums were sounding within the city. He could not help wondering: ‘It’s near sunrise already—what sort of street drums are these?’

‘Aiya, listen again!’ his companion said in a fluster.

Aluoyue listened again, and found that there was still another kind of drumbeat coming from the two directions of north and south. This drumming was shrill and urgent, wholly unlike the long, drawn-out manner of the street drums. His face changed: this was the gate-tower’s closing-drum, which meant that the gates to the north, at the Chunming Gate, and to the south, at the Yanxing Gate, were about to be shut.

By custom, at the Lantern Festival, both the ward gates and the city gates stood unshut the whole night through. That was why these people had first wandered the lantern fair inside the city a whole night, and only as the Dragon hour drew near had come out of the city to release water-lanterns by the moat. Now what had happened? Why, with day almost broken, were the city gates to be sealed instead? Could it have to do with that blast a while ago before the Xingqing Palace?

Aluoyue and his fellows had not gone to gawk before the Xingqing Palace, and had no clear notion how great a thing had happened over there. But they knew well how severe the closing-drum of the gate-tower garrison was. If a man had not got into the city before the drums fell silent, he could forget about getting in at all. They had brought neither food nor a copper coin; to be shut out of the city would be a great trouble indeed.

‘Let’s be off, quick!’ A companion tugged at his sleeve, urging him on.

‘But Chief Zhang and his friend—we can’t just leave them to shift for themselves...’ Aluoyue’s voice was hesitant. He glanced at the fish-belly white of the far horizon, and then at the lanterns on the gate-tower of the Yanxing Gate, and set his teeth: ‘Go on, the rest of you! I’ll stay.’

‘Eh?’

‘The gates won’t stay shut forever, in any case—at worst I spend a day out here. Chief Zhang did me a kindness; I can’t watch him die and not lift a hand.’ Aluoyue had made up his mind. He added one more charge: ‘Mind you feed my camels for me.’ His companions answered with a word and went hurrying off, one and all, toward the city gate.

Aluoyue was strong of frame, and hoisted Zhang Xiaojing up onto his shoulder with the greatest ease, then set off outward. Beside the official road, some two hundred paces out from the city wall, there stood a tiny roadside shrine of the god of journeys, where the people of Chang’an, seeing off a traveler, would always come to make a bow. Aluoyue laid Zhang Xiaojing down within the shrine, a rush mat spread beneath him, then went out and carried Xiao Gui over as well, and the two of them lay side by side, shoulder to shoulder.

Earlier, for the releasing of the water-lanterns, this band had kept a fire-source alight on the bank. Aluoyue fetched it over, and with a broken jar from the shrine heated a little hot water and poured it down the two men’s throats. Before long, both of them slowly came back to their senses. Well pleased, Aluoyue said he would go out and scare up something to eat, and off he went with his bamboo pole, leaving in the shrine only Zhang Xiaojing and Xiao Gui, the two of them.

Zhang Xiaojing slowly turned his head aside and found that Xiao Gui’s hurts were far graver than his own: a great patch of his chest had caved in, and blood-froth foamed at the corner of his mouth. Plainly, in the fall into the water, he had gone down face-first, taking upon himself the greater part of the impact in Zhang Xiaojing’s stead.

Seeing him in such a state, Zhang Xiaojing knew that he was as good as past saving. A fierce grief, like a bolt of lightning, clove into Zhang Xiaojing’s stone-stiff body. The last time he had known a like feeling was on hearing of Wen Wuji’s death.

At this Xiao Gui opened his eyes.

‘Why?’ In that one word lay a boundless weight of question and of rage.

Zhang Xiaojing worked his mouth, but still could bring out no sound.

‘Why, of all people, did it have to be you—you, to betray me?’ Xiao Gui seemed to grow agitated, and the blood-froth at his mouth thickened a little more. He knew, no doubt, that he was done for, and paid not the least heed to the wound in his chest, coughing as he spoke: ‘No—that’s wrong! Cough, cough... From the very start, you never truly meant to help me, is that it?’

Wordlessly, Zhang Xiaojing gave a nod.

‘Who would have thought it—to win my trust, you actually made an attempt on Li Bi’s life. Zhang Big-Head, oh, Zhang Big-Head—should I call you ruthless enough, or sinister enough? Cough, cough!’

Only now did Xiao Gui perceive at last that this flawless plan had fallen short by a single basketful of earth for the sake of this one old comrade. His own boundless trust in Zhang Xiaojing had become, instead, the sharp blade that hewed at himself.

‘I do not understand—why would you betray an old comrade who shared life and death with you? Why help the imperial house? I cannot think of a reason—not one reason can I think of.’ Xiao Gui clutched desperately at Zhang Xiaojing’s hand, his eyes full of bewilderment.

He was not in an agony of grief, nor in a wild fury; he bore now only a deep incomprehension. An old comrade who had been so tormented and so abused ought, by every reckoning, to have stood on his side; yet Zhang Xiaojing, of all things, had not, but had instead braved death for the very men who tormented him, holding his own life as nothing.

But Zhang Xiaojing could bring out no sound just then; Xiao Gui stared at his lips: ‘You do not hold with what I have done?’

Zhang Xiaojing nodded.

‘Are you then so loyal to that Son of Heaven?’

Zhang Xiaojing shook his head.

Xiao Gui drove a fist against the slender pillar beside the little shrine, all but roaring: ‘Then why, in the end? If you are not loyal to that Son of Heaven, why protect him! Why do you not hold with what I have done! Doing this—can you answer for it to the brothers who died?’

Silently Zhang Xiaojing met his gaze. All at once Xiao Gui remembered: on the roof of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, they had had a dispute over the ‘weighing of human lives,’ and Zhang Xiaojing had seemed to feel strongly upon the point, insisting that a man’s life could never be weighed so.

‘You think I did wrong? You think I stopped at nothing, slaughtering the innocent? You think I should not, to be rid of the emperor, have made so many victims of it?’

This time Zhang Xiaojing nodded, and nodded with the firmest resolve.

Xiao Gui, past all anger, laughed instead: ‘After all you have been through, you are still so weak, so childish... cough, cough... Whom is it, in the end, that you mean to protect? The officials who brought death on my sister’s whole family? The Prince Yong who did Wen Wuji to death? Or the court that cast you into the death cells and tortured you time and again?’

This time Zhang Xiaojing did not answer; grave-faced, he cast his gaze outside the shrine. By now the dawn-light had little by little driven off the dark, and the outline of Chang’an’s city wall was growing slowly clear. Today would be another fine day.

Xiao Gui followed Zhang Xiaojing’s gaze. They had, after all, been partners who braved death together, and a single glance was enough for each to read the other’s mind: ‘Ten years a soldier in the Western Regions, nine years a chief in Chang’an—you don’t truly take yourself for the guardian of this city of Chang’an, do you?’

With an effort Zhang Xiaojing raised his right arm and scraped the water from the socket of his eye; that single eye was uncommonly solemn.

The corner of Xiao Gui’s eye twitched; he could scarcely believe it: ‘Big-Head, you truly are the most naive, the most foolish fellow in all the Eighth Company.’ Zhang Xiaojing, with every ounce of his strength, raised his right arm and struck himself a heavy blow on the left shoulder. This was the salute-cry of the Eighth Company, and it meant: ‘Nine deaths, and no regret.’

Seeing it, Xiao Gui was silent a moment, and then burst into a great laugh: ‘So be it! So be it! A man must answer for his own choices in the end. I trusted you, you betrayed me—it is all no more than I deserve. Well and good; to die at the hands of my own brother is no loss either. I have thrown Chang’an into an uproar, blown apart the lantern-tower, smashed the palace hall, held the emperor himself hostage—from ancient times till now, how many rebels have had such glory as mine!’

His laughter was shrill and piercing, and more fresh blood ran from the corner of his mouth.

With an effort Zhang Xiaojing turned on his side, meaning to reach out and wipe it away for him. Xiao Gui knocked his hand aside without ceremony: ‘Get off! When we reach the underworld, we’ll let the brothers of the Eighth Company decide which of us was in the wrong! Cough, cough, cough, cough...’

After a violent fit of coughing, the sound broke off all at once, and the roadside shrine fell into a dead silence. Zhang Xiaojing thought him already dead, and was about to lean in for a closer look. But unexpectedly Xiao Gui all at once sat up straight again, a fierce light blazing in his eyes like the last flare of a guttering lamp:

‘Though they have slipped this once through the net, I will not let Chang’an rest easy either. Cough, cough—Big-Head, let me tell you a secret.’

Zhang Xiaojing knit his brows and did not draw near, not knowing what medicine the fellow had in his gourd. Xiao Gui’s face was hung all over with a mocking smile: ‘Do you not want to know how we aphids were able to raise so great an uproar in the city of Chang’an?’

At these words Zhang Xiaojing’s heart drew violently tight. He had long harbored the doubt: this plan of the aphids’ was too vast, its demands upon every link too high; by Xiao Gui’s handful of discharged old soldiers alone, it could never have been carried this far. Behind them, there must be some further power lending its support.

Now that Xiao Gui of his own will meant to speak this secret out, Zhang Xiaojing found himself half afraid to hear it. From the fellow’s look of excitement, this would be a secret to throw the whole of Chang’an into turmoil. Yet to seize the true culprit was the duty of the Commandant of the Jing’an Bureau; he could not but listen.

Watching Zhang Xiaojing in the misery of his dilemma, torn this way and that, Xiao Gui savored it to the full. With an effort he shifted his body over, pressed close against his ear, and in a low voice spoke a single sentence. Zhang Xiaojing’s body could not stir, yet his single eye all at once glared open so wide it seemed about to burst from its socket.

Xiao Gui’s head drooped, his body slowly canted to one side, and his brow came, all unmeaning, to rest against Zhang Xiaojing’s chest—and so he died.

Within the Qinzheng Wuben Tower now, it was more chaotic still than at the moment of the attack a while before.

The imperial guards of the several commands, beside themselves; the panic-stricken guests who had escaped with their lives; the guards and yamen-runners hurrying in from Wannian County and the Xingqing Palace to the rescue; the bondservants, the musicians, the dancing-girls, all like flies with their heads off—a countless throng ran this way and that amid the rubble and the smoke, some fleeing outward, some charging in, some shouting, some weeping aloud, every last one of them at a loss what to do.

When the several commands of the imperial guards learned that the Son of Heaven had been carried up the tower by the outlaws and had vanished without a trace, they grew more fearful and unsettled still. The commanders of the Longwu, the Yulin, the Left and Right Xiao Guard, the Left and Right Qianniu Guard, and the rest each gave his own orders and sent men searching in all directions; and since the military commands issued from no single source, they could not but hinder one another, so that they fell to quarreling, and even to open conflict.

Above all, the guests trapped on the sixth floor soon threw themselves into the confusion as well. Not a few of them were hurt, though few had died. Every one of these persons was of noble station—if not of the imperial house, then a great minister—hot of temper and fond of giving orders, and each felt he ought to be tended to first. The soldiers who had gone up the tower first did not know whom they should heed, and could afford to offend no one, and so were wholly at a loss.

For a while, above and below, the whole tower was aflicker with moving figures, for all the world like an ant’s nest whose queen has been dug away.

The one comfort was that, with so many relief troops crowding up, the fires kindled here and there by the wreckage of the lantern-tower were swiftly beaten out; at the least, the Qinzheng Wuben Tower would not be destroyed by fire.

In this uproar of clamoring voices and cries that rose to the heavens, a man and a woman were making their way outward without a flicker of expression. In front went a broad-browed man, walking with a limp, wounded, it seemed, in the attack; close behind him pressed a Hu woman, her cloud-coifed hair likewise in disorder, her face all smoke and grime, but her look grave and composed. Watch closely, and one would find that the man’s eyes were blinking without cease, and that the woman behind him kept her right hand always pressed against the small of his back, all but driving the man forward before her.

The wounded and the dead within the tower were far too many; no one would pay any particular heed to this pair of lightly hurt ones, still less mark such small details. And so they walked slowly outward, none questioning them, none barring their way.

They were, of course, Yuan Zai and Tanqi, who had stayed behind in the Qinzheng Wuben Tower.

Earlier Zhang Xiaojing had charged Tanqi to wreck the ‘tower within the tower’ and then leave at once. She had carried out the task without a hitch, but instead of going away, she had turned back and hauled Yuan Zai up by the scruff.

Yuan Zai had supposed the relief troops were coming, and that he was about to be saved. But no sooner had he risen to his feet to call out than Tanqi struck him a blow on the shin, so that beads of sweat sprang out with the pain. Before he had time to ask her why she had laid hands on him, he felt something hard and stiff pressed against the small of his back. He knew without looking that, if it was not a blade, it was at the least some sharp thing keen enough to pierce flesh and blood.

‘Walk out with me, and you’re not to speak with anyone.’ Tanqi said coldly.

‘Miss, there’s no need for you to...’ Yuan Zai tried to argue, but at once a stab of pain took him in the back, and in fright he hastily shut his mouth.

And so, holding Yuan Zai hostage in this way, Tanqi slowly withdrew from the Qinzheng Wuben Tower and came to a thicket near the Longchi of the Xingqing Palace. The blast a while before had frightened off every bird of the place, leaving only a dense, black stand of trees. The palace watch of the Xingqing had all rushed off to the tower just now, and this quarter was for the moment unpatrolled.

‘Can it be... that you mean to kill me, Miss?’ Standing in the open ground amid the trees, Yuan Zai turned his head in some alarm.

‘That is so.’ In Tanqi’s two great eyes a deep killing-intent flickered. ‘To let you live would be a danger to Commander Zhang.’

The matter of Yuan Zai’s earlier framing of Zhang Xiaojing she had already questioned out of him clearly. Tanqi feared greatly that, were she to let this fellow go back, the Jing’an Bureau would surely take a doubled revenge upon Zhang Xiaojing (she did not yet know that Li Bi had retaken command of the Bureau). The lecher, burdened with so many slanders, was fighting on still; she must do something to help him, even at the cost of staining her own hands with blood.

Things being come to this pass, she could no longer spare a thought for herself.

From Tanqi’s expression and her breathing Yuan Zai could tell that the girl was in earnest. She might never have seen blood, but once she set to it her will would be firm. Setting aside his own safety, he rather admired this decisiveness in the killing stroke—she was, in truth, no unworthy product of Li Bi’s training.

Tanqi bit hard upon her white teeth and was on the point of bearing down with her hand when Yuan Zai said sharply, all at once: ‘Whether you kill me or not, Zhang Xiaojing dies all the same!’

At these words the sharp thing gave a tremor, and did not, after all, drive in further. Yuan Zai seized the chance: ‘As you came down the tower, you heard those people talking of what Commander Zhang had done, did you not?’

‘And what of it?’

As they had come down the tower, they had happened upon an official who had by good luck escaped injury, running down and, in his agitation, telling the guardsmen the whole of what had passed on the seventh floor, words and gestures together. Only then had they learned that Zhang Xiaojing, after going up the tower, had of all things joined hands with the aphids—had not only knocked Chen Xuanli senseless, but had openly carried off the Son of Heaven and Taizhen.

Tanqi and Yuan Zai understood, of course, that this was a stratagem of Zhang Xiaojing’s; but in the eyes of everyone else, Zhang Xiaojing had become a villain who had done every wicked thing.

‘With the whole court, civil and military, looking on with a thousand eyes—even should you, Miss, hack me into ten thousand pieces, the stain on his name will not be washed clean.’

‘I can go and bear witness!’ said Tanqi.

A flicker of a contemptuous smile showed on Yuan Zai’s face: ‘Everyone takes him for your sweetheart; not a soul will believe a word you say.’ Yuan Zai was an Evaluator of the Court of Judicial Review, and knew all too well the logic by which his superiors handled a case.

‘But I have proof that he is innocent!’

‘Holding the Son of Heaven hostage—that is a crime no washing will ever whiten. To speak plainly, I do not well understand why Zhang Xiaojing should choose so thankless and toilsome a road; for him it is nothing but a dead end.’

‘You...’ The tears were already turning in the rims of Tanqi’s eyes; she knew that Yuan Zai spoke the truth, and it was for that very reason that she was the more incensed. Tanqi bore down with her hand to drive the sharp thing in. Yuan Zai flinched instinctively aside, turned his ankle, and fell to the ground: ‘Wait—don’t strike—hear me out. You cannot save him, but I can.’

‘Did you not say he was at a dead end?’

‘If you kill me, then it truly will be a dead end.’ Lying on the ground, Yuan Zai cried out: ‘The only one who can now undo the charge against him is I. I am an Evaluator of the Court of Judicial Review, and I hold a post in the Jing’an Bureau besides; my word they will believe.’

Tanqi said with a cold laugh: ‘Why should I trust you? Before this you did him no small harm. If I let you go now, who is to guarantee that you will not turn round and sell me out?’

‘You need not believe in my good faith—only believe that the thing is to my advantage, and that will serve.’ Though Yuan Zai lay wretched in the dirt, a confident smile showed on his face.

‘What?’ Tanqi did not follow him at all.

‘When I framed Zhang Xiaojing before, I too did it at another’s bidding, promised a rich reward for it. But I have just been reckoning it out with care: as things now stand, to help clear him of suspicion would bring me a greater advantage still—for you must understand, human nature is ever to run after gain and shun harm. A man may betray loyalty and righteousness, honor and virtue, but he will never betray his own interest. So long as the thing profits me, Miss, you need have no fear that I will betray you.’ The more Yuan Zai talked, the more fluent he grew, slipping visibly back into his familiar rhythm.

This dissection of human nature Tanqi had heard before from her young master too—that at court all is a contest of interests. But that Yuan Zai should speak it out so nakedly left her truly a little disconcerted, and she could not help spitting: ‘Shameless!’

Yuan Zai scrambled wretchedly up from the ground; seeing that beyond her scolding Tanqi made no further move, he knew that the girl was already shaken. He brushed the dirt from his gown, his face wreathed in smiles.

‘And what advantage could there be in it for you? I cannot see it.’ Tanqi still kept her face set hard.

‘If by chance Zhang Xiaojing truly brings the Sage out safe, then he is a great hero. And when the Son of Heaven looks into it—why, here is a loyal, upright official who saw it first and saw it clear: at a time when all the world took Zhang Xiaojing for a traitor, he alone labored to wash the wronged hero clean of his disgrace. The advantage in that is to be measured by the cartload and the peck.’

‘This is a gamble of yours—and if he fails to bring him out?’

‘Then Chang’an and the whole court will fall into great disorder—and who will have leisure left to trouble over him?’ Yuan Zai lifted his right hand and worked his fingers back and forth, as though he held a die in them. ‘So whether the Sage be safe or in peril, to whiten Zhang Xiaojing’s name is, for me, the most profitable course of all.’

Watching this fellow speak of such rank irreverence toward the throne in so offhand a way, for all the world like a tradesman talking over a piece of business, Tanqi felt a chill rise straight up through her. Yet his words were without a flaw, and had all but convinced her; the hand that gripped the sharp thing sank, in spite of her, to her side.

What Tanqi did not know was that Yuan Zai had one small calculation more that he had not spoken aloud. Before this, at the gate of Chao Fen’s house, he had been frightened out of his wits by Zhang Xiaojing and had let that god of slaughter go free. Should his superiors pursue the matter, he too would bear a heavy share of the blame—might even be beheaded on a charge of ‘conniving at a violent criminal.’ And so, come what may, he must clear Zhang Xiaojing’s name. In a manner of speaking, the two of them were already grasshoppers tied to a single string.

Renown shows itself only after bitter toil; riches and honor are sought in the midst of peril. Yuan Zai wiped the sweat from his broad forehead. His good luck had not yet wholly deserted him this night; it was worth a hard throw of the dice.

Tanqi asked: ‘Then what are we to do?’

‘First of all, we must find a certain man.’

‘Who?’

‘A man who hates Zhang Xiaojing to the very marrow.’

That last remark of Li Linfu’s plunged Li Bi as into a pit of ice.

‘What profit is there in it for me?’

Whether in an ordinary judicial inquiry or in a plot of the palace, one most basic principle held: ‘He who profits most is suspect.’ The one who stands to gain the most is forever the most to be doubted. Li Linfu had not entangled himself with Li Bi over the twigs and branches of the thing, but had gone straight for the root, and bidden this Deputy Director of the Jing’an Bureau con over that piece of elementary common sense.

Since taking the office of Secretariat Director in the twentieth year of Kaiyuan, Li Linfu had, for close upon ten years, held the Son of Heaven’s trust and regard alone, the imperial favor toward him never flagging—a thing without precedent in the present dynasty. Should the Son of Heaven pass away, he would become a tree without root, a stream without source; even were he to set some other infant prince upon the throne, what he gained by it would hardly be so rich as what he had now. In other words, this conspiracy aimed at the Son of Heaven was, for him, all harm and no gain—scarcely a jot of profit in it.

Li Bi, reasoning out Li Linfu’s supposed disposition of the plot from sign after sign, had a seemingly flawless explanation—yet he had forgotten the one most fundamental thing. For Li Linfu to have gone to such painstaking lengths to raise so vast a commotion would only shake his own position; and he was no fool.

But, following out this same principle, one was driven straight to set the heir apparent in the place of greatest suspicion.

Ever since he had succeeded to the Eastern Palace, the heir apparent had suffered the Right Minister’s oppression again and again, and had been doubted, moreover, by the Son of Heaven, so that day and night he lived in dread, ill at ease in his heart. Should the mountain tomb, by ill fortune, come to collapse, the heir apparent would succeed as a matter of course—above, inheriting the line of the great throne; below, ridding himself of the Right Minister’s menace—and might be said to gather every glory to himself alone.

‘No—it cannot be. You deliberately drew the heir apparent away, to make him bear the suspicion of regicide and parricide, so that he could not take the throne.’ Li Bi tried to argue it out.

‘Regicide and parricide? Among the emperors of our great Tang, when have such things ever been wanting?’ A thick note of irony was in Li Linfu’s tone. ‘Let me ask you this: of all the other princes, was there any one who rose and left midway?’

Li Bi shut his mouth and said nothing.

‘Had I arranged this affair, I should by now be keeping some prince safe in hand, marshaling the guards of the Southern and Northern Commands, sending crack horsemen out on every side to pick off you and the whole Eastern Palace faction one by one. I should not be sitting alone in a great courtyard like this, wagging my tongue with you.’ Li Linfu smiled faintly, but in the smile there was a thread or two of self-mockery and helplessness.

‘We have both been played for fools.’ the Right Minister sighed of a sudden.

At these words Li Bi’s frame swayed, as though struck a mighty blow. It was true: a usurpation turned upon a thunderbolt stroke that brooked not a moment’s hesitation. A man as deep-laid and far-seeing as Li Linfu would surely have had his reckoning ready long before, his further measures coming on in an unbroken stream; he would never have been so sluggish as this.

Could it be... that this was truly the work of the heir apparent, biding in the physic-garden of the Eastern Palace? That he had deceived even me?

In Li Bi’s heart there came first a wave of bitter desolation, then anger, and then there rose a strange kind of clear understanding.

Things being come to this, to press the question of blame was without any meaning. Li Bi knew that in politics there is no right and wrong, only the contest of interests. As the chief strategist of the Eastern Palace, even though he had been kept in the dark beforehand, even though there was little reason to be found in it, he must yet contrive to win the heir apparent every further advantage he could.

Here now, within this secluded residence, the heir apparent’s greatest enemy, Li Linfu, had but a scant few guards at his side, while the Lüben soldiers Li Bi had brought were fully ten times their number... As Li Bi thought on and on, his gaze slowly changed, and his arm began to rise.

Since ancient times, Mount Hua has had but one road up: he had already done one deed against morality for the heir apparent, and would not mind doing it once again.

Li Linfu saw the killing intent rising in the young man’s eyes, and only smiled. In his view Li Bi was a raw, unpolished child: his conduct had method enough, to be sure, but it left too heavy a trace, and wanted seasoning.

‘Have you not stopped to think—what if the Son of Heaven comes to no harm?’ He said only this one light sentence.

Li Linfu’s words, like a cold gust of wind, blew out without a stir the murderous light in Li Bi’s eyes. Just so—what if the Son of Heaven were safe and sound? Then for him to strike at this moment would be not only meaningless, but a source of endless trouble to come.

Li Bi did not know to how dire a pass things had come at the Xingqing Palace; but since Zhang Xiaojing was over there, perhaps he might yet work a wonder and truly bring the Sage out safe. And all at once Li Bi found that, for one flickering instant, he had actually hoped that Zhang Xiaojing would fail.

This was, in truth, the most ironical thing of all this day.

Between the truth and his promise to the heir apparent, Li Bi must now make a choice.

The moment Yao Runeng bored into the pipe, a rank and fetid stench came stabbing at him fierce as a long spear, as though it would lift the very crown from his skull. He held his breath with all his might, leveled out his body, and, with a single slithering hiss, went sliding downward.

The inner wall of the pipe was coated with layer upon layer of a yellow-brown crust of dung, greasy to the touch, so that Yao Runeng slid down fast. He had to thrust out both hands against the walls to check his speed of descent. His fingers raced across the brittle crust, throwing up flake after flake of flying grit that fell upon his body, his head, and his face.

In the ordinary way, the fastidious Yao Runeng would long since have been sick. But now he cared nothing at all for such things; his whole mind and heart were fixed on the pitch-black mouth of the hole ahead.

Who would have thought the traitor should turn out to be him! It was wholly beyond anything Yao Runeng had looked for. And yet, thinking harder, it fitted every detail to perfection: apart from him, there could be no one else!

This vile wretch was a great enemy of the Jing’an Bureau; even at the cost of his own life, he must be caught. For the sake of Chang’an, Commander Zhang had braved death again and again—and I can do the same! Such was the cry that echoed on and on in Yao Runeng’s mind.

As he neared the way out, Yao Runeng saw a round opening ahead, and could hear the babble of a water-channel. All at once his father’s teaching came back to him—his father had been an old thief-catcher, who used to say that the instant of closing on a criminal is the most dangerous of all, and that one must be careful, and careful again.

A strong presentiment came over him, and so he braced both feet against the two sides with all his might to slow his slide. The instant he slid out of the pipe, Yao Runeng heard a rush of wind at his ear. The traitor had, of all things, been bold and fierce enough not to flee first, but had lain in wait at the mouth of the hole, and with a brow-high wooden pole of the kind used to clear blockages from the pipe, smashed down savagely at his head.

It was well that Yao Runeng had slowed himself beforehand, so that the pole did not come down upon his head, but struck heavily instead against his lower belly. Bearing the fierce pain, Yao Runeng—his right hand having early closed upon a lump of hardened dung—flung it sideways at the man. The traitor’s movement was checked for half a beat, and Yao Runeng, seizing the moment, caught the man’s sleeve-hem with his right hand and, using the force of his fall, wrenched with all his might, so that the two of them rolled down together into the covered channel.

This covered channel was for the drainage of the ward; within it, besides the livestock pens, the wine-shops, the eating-houses, the shops, and the great households would each build a drain-run to pour all manner of kitchen slops and foul water into the channel, relying wholly on the force of the water to sweep it away. Day upon day, year upon year, all kinds of rotting filth silted up in the channel, foul beyond compare, its reek so strong a man could scarcely keep his eyes open.

The two of them fell with a plop into the channel; the place was cramped, the smell stinging, and every art of combat was rendered useless. The traitor had no wish to grapple with him, and was struggling to swim clear when, unlooked-for, Yao Runeng lunged over and reached out to wrench sheer out of his back an arrow that was lodged there. The crossbow-bolt was barbed, so that the wrench tore away with it, at once, a great gobbet of flesh and blood.

The traitor let out a piteous, agonized cry, turned about, and drove a fist into Yao Runeng’s face; at once Yao Runeng’s nose gushed blood, and with a splash he toppled into the foul water. The traitor was on the point of turning to flee when, unlooked-for, Yao Runeng rose again out of the water with a great splashing, matted and filthy, for all the world like a water-demon. He spread both arms and clamped them tight about the other’s body, and however the traitor struck at him, he held on for dear life and would not let go.

The traitor had not looked for Yao Runeng to hold his own life so cheap. His back-wound was by now most grievous, and soaked besides in such filthy dung-water that it would surely be slow to heal. The traitor could delay no longer, and could only smash fist after fist against Yao Runeng’s spine, in hopes he would let go. But Yao Runeng, though he was battered till he spat blood, still would not release him; his whole body had turned into a stone lock, binding the traitor fast within the covered channel.

The traitor began with a single hand, and then took to both fists clasped together, smashing them down with savage force. There came a sharp crack, and a small patch of Yao Runeng’s back suddenly caved in—it seemed a length of his spine had been broken through. The young man let out a wail of agony, yet the locking grip of his two arms did not slacken in the least.

The traitor too was almost spent; he gritted his teeth and was about to smash down once more. All at once, behind him, came a run of splashing plunges into the water; he knew it boded ill, and heaved his body with all his might—but Yao Runeng, already sunk into a half-swoon, kept his ten fingers locked fast the whole while, and would not let him stir.

Those who had fallen in were several Lüben soldiers, driven to leap in one after another at Adjutant Zhao’s compulsion, a bellyful of gloom in every one of them. Seeing now this arch-culprit, they itched to stab him dead on the spot and drag him off. Luckily Adjutant Zhao had given orders that he be taken alive, and so they took up their scabbards and beat at him with a will.

The scabbards of the Lüben Guards were of hard leather sheathed in bronze, and their power to wound was startling. Beset on every side, the traitor had no room left for any resistance; beaten till his nose and face were bruised black and blue, he soon toppled sideways into the water and was taken, his hands bound.

By now Yao Runeng had sunk into a dead faint, but his ten fingers were locked so tight that the soldiers could not, for all their trying, pry them apart at once, and so had no choice but to carry the two of them up together out of that filth-harboring hell, and bring them up to the ground.

One look, and Adjutant Zhao saw that the two of them were filthy past all recognition, their faces not to be made out; he ordered clean water fetched and dashed over them. When several buckets of well-water had been thrown across, the traitor’s face showed at last—honest, simple, and familiar.

Adjutant Zhao leaned close to look, and blanched with shock: ‘This—is this not that runner of the Jing’an Bureau?’

***

Aluoyue had fair luck: outside he had brought down several skylarks, and though they were small of body, they made a dish of meat of a sort. He strung the skylarks onto a single skewer and brought them back to the shrine, where he found the other man lying still upon Zhang Xiaojing’s breast, not stirring at all. Zhang Xiaojing’s look was agitated, his chest heaving without pause.

He supposed Chief Zhang was grieving at his friend’s death, and went over to lift Xiao Gui’s body away; but Zhang Xiaojing all at once caught hold of his hand, his wide mouth opening and closing, his throat straining as though to cry out some word.

But Aluoyue heard only a few empty rasps, and said to Zhang Xiaojing, somewhat helplessly: ‘Best you make no sound, and rest here. When the gates open, I’ll get you a camel and you can be off as quick as may be.’

He supposed Zhang Xiaojing must have got mixed up in some great crime, and that this was why he was so desperate to leap down from the city wall and flee out of Chang’an.

But Zhang Xiaojing let go his hand, drew a splinter of bamboo from the rush mat beneath him, and began to scratch and draw in the dust of the ground. Aluoyue said he could not read, so it was no use writing; but then, looking down, he found it was not Chinese characters at all, but a gate-tower, and a city gate. With the bamboo strip Zhang Xiaojing drew, further, an arrow pointing in through the gate, then pointed at himself, and looked up at him.

Aluoyue understood in a flash: ‘You want to get into the city? To go in this instant?’

Zhang Xiaojing nodded.

At this Aluoyue was thoroughly baffled. Through a thousand toils and hardships the man had just leapt out from the city wall—why should he now want to go back in? He gave a wry laugh: ‘There you have me stumped. I went to take a look just now, and the gates are truly sealed—sealed in the very strictest fashion, too. The whole of Chang’an has become a locked wooden box now; no one is to think of getting in or out.’

Zhang Xiaojing gripped him by both arms, straining and grunting, that single eye glaring perfectly round.

‘Or will you wait a while yet? The gates can’t stay sealed forever, in any case.’

Zhang Xiaojing shook his head desperately. Aluoyue guessed that he must get into the city no matter what, and get in at once. What in the world had happened, to drive this buliang chief to such a pitch of urgency, he could not tell.

‘But there’s nothing I can do, either—force our way in, and the garrison will shoot us dead on the spot...’ Aluoyue spread his hands and said helplessly.

Zhang Xiaojing bent again and drew a letter, leading it by an arrow to the mouth of the city gate. Aluoyue hazarded: ‘You mean that, so long as a single letter can be got in, that will serve?’

‘Mm-hm.’

Aluoyue knit his brows, knowing that this too was hard. If men were not let in, still less would the garrison allow some strange thing to be carried through. Chang’an was under a ban and a seal now; no person and no goods were to think of coming in—without exception.

Without exception, without exception, without...

Aluoyue folded his arms and muttered it over a while, and then all at once his eyes lit up. He hurried to the door of the shrine to look at the color of the sky outside, then turned back and said with delight: ‘I’ve thought of a way—maybe I can get you in after all.’
