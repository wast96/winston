## Chapter 22

Dr. Philip's clinic was the place where Stephen and I first became friends; since that day I had never once come back. Now I came for the second time.

It was about half past four when I registered at the door, filled in the patient's slip, and went in. There were still six people in the waiting room: two men, three women, and a white child of twelve or thirteen leaning against a woman of nearly forty. Two of them were leafing through magazines. I sat down on the sofa beside them. In about the time it takes to smoke half a cigarette, someone came out from inside, and a nurse, a Chinese girl in white, came holding a case-record slip and called the next person in.

I took a magazine from the rack and turned the pages idly, but my heart was too uneasy to read. In the end I went back to my old place and sat waiting in silence.

About the time the third patient was being seen, another elderly patient came in from outside. He sat down almost across from me; his color was very bad, and he seemed anxious besides. When I had come in I had felt all along as though I carried some grave mission, yet in this waiting I felt as if I too were only a patient. Then it struck me all at once: could these patients perhaps all be workers of the navy, come here to await their assignments? Or were some of them on the same errand as I? I began to study their faces and their bearing, but I could make out nothing. I waited like this a long time, watching those in the seats go in and come out, the ones who came out leaving, the ones in the seats going in, the waiting room emptying more and more, until at last it was my turn; but the name the nurse called was not, of all things, my own, and I watched the old man across from me answer to it and go in.

A quarter of an hour later the old man came out; he slipped away quietly, and then the nurse came out and called me. At Stephen's house I had met Dr. Philip several times. He was a gentleman of perhaps forty-odd, a moustache on his upper lip, his manner grave and refined in the extreme. As I went in he smiled and nodded, and when I had sat down beside his desk he shook my hand, though not warmly. He wore a white coat; on the desk lay my blank case-record slip and prescription book. The backs of his hands were covered with fine hair; in his right hand he held a pencil, tapping it lightly against his left, and when he spoke his voice was low but forceful. He said:

"Feeling unwell?"

"Yes," I said. "Neurasthenia, I think."

He began to look at me: a pair of sapphire-blue eyes, giving off a strong, forceful light.

He seemed to look at people seldom, but each time he did it was with this piercing radiance. I avoided his eyes.

He drew over another stool from the side, went and washed his hands, then sat down facing me, his knees pressed against mine, and told me to close my eyes gently, then to open them; then he pulled the stool away. He told me to take off my clothes, and again sat down facing me; he listened and listened, tapped and tapped, then put away the stethoscope, stood up, and told me to dress. He went back to the desk and began to write a prescription. By now it was as if I had really come to be treated; the mood of a patient rose in me, and I asked:

"Is there anything wrong with my lungs?"

"No." He did not look at me, and said indifferently: "Neurasthenia." He handed me the prescription and seemed to have nothing further to say to me. Naturally I was conscious of my mission, but he had already risen and gone to wash his hands, so I stood up too, and asked:

"Nothing more?"

"Sleep more, use your brain less, rub yourself down often with cold water; you know all this, I expect." Drying his hands on a dry cloth, he smiled, his eyes seeming to see me off.

I nodded to him, my eyes on his face, and moved slowly toward the door; he had given me not a single instruction, and I was so astonished I did not know what to do. Could it be that Mrs. Stephen had not arranged it with him after all, or that they had meant to use me at first and then decided I would not do?

In this state of doubt I opened the door and came out. When I had gone in, the waiting room had been empty; but now there was a woman, standing at the window. Just as I stood there not knowing what to do, she turned her head.

It was Mei Yingzi!

"Ah, it's you?" Showing her small almond-colored teeth, Mei Yingzi rose smiling and said:

"It's been a long time."

"A long time, and you are as radiant as ever." I went over and shook her hand. The nurse stood in the doorway holding a case-record slip.

"Are you ill?" I asked.

"An injection," she said. "Wait here a moment; I'll be out directly." She left a lovely smile behind her and went in. I sat down and smoked. I began to grasp that the person Mrs. Stephen had spoken of, who would come to make contact with me, must beyond doubt be Mei Yingzi. So Mei Yingzi was one of their company after all; no wonder she had been keeping up with the Japanese all along.

I threw away my cigarette end and walked to the window. The rain had stopped; there were red and yellow evening clouds on the horizon, and a red glow shone through beneath the white wisps of cloud above. It was very beautiful.

"Sorry to keep you." Mei Yingzi was already at my side.

"How have you been lately?"

"Thank you," she said. "Well, will you keep me company at dinner?"

"You've no engagement?"

"The greatest honor would be to dine with you." She laughed. "I ought to forget all my other engagements."

"The honor is mine."

She took my arm and we went out, rode down in the elevator; at the door was her red car. I said:

"The Sarofan?"

"No," she said. "The Benner Inn."

"The Benner Inn?" I asked.

"You don't know it?" she said. "That's why I want to take you there." She drove west along Bubbling Well Road.

It was dusk; the road was crowded with people, the trams jammed to bursting, but there were very few motorcars, for Shanghai's gasoline had long been under Japanese control and cars were liable to be requisitioned at any moment; yet Mei Yingzi could drive about as she pleased, which showed her standing in Japanese circles. And she was a worker for the American navy? It suddenly occurred to me that perhaps she was not the person Mrs. Stephen had meant, the one who would make contact with me over the work, but had truly run into me only by chance.

Over toward Bubbling Well Temple the pedestrians were thicker still and the car slowed; just then a car came around a turn, three Japanese officers with a splendidly dressed woman seated among them. It rounded the corner and sped off east. Our car crossed Avenue Haig into Great Western Road, and Mei Yingzi suddenly asked with a smile:

"Didn't you see?"

"See what?"

"Our lovely Bai Ping." I thought at once of the woman seated with the Japanese officers. I asked:

"Was that really Bai Ping?"

"You don't even recognize Bai Ping now?"

"It's a long time since I saw her."

"A long time since you saw her?" she asked in surprise.

"Why?" I asked in return.

"Bai Ping is a true Sai Jinhua now!"

"You mean..."

"I mean she's important, and busy!"

The pedestrians on the road grew sparse; the car's speed rose to forty-three miles an hour; then, crossing a desolate stretch, it suddenly slowed again. I asked:

"Here?" I wondered that there could be a restaurant in such a place.

"We're almost there," said Mei Yingzi.

I saw a row of short green wooden palings, and the car turned in; ahead was a three-story foreign house, its windows bright with light, lawns all around, planted thickly, it seemed, with trees; since it was winter, I saw very few leaves. The car stopped before a path flanked by two neat rows of holly. I jumped down and saw the street lamp opposite, which might as well be called a gate lamp. On the lawn behind the holly to the left, a spherical white porcelain shade bore the words Benner Inn; we went in by a little path, and I could see no other sign on the house until I reached the door, where on a brightly polished brass plate I saw the same words at last. Mei Yingzi rang the bell, and a waiter in white came to answer it. In the corridor Mei Yingzi hung up her overcoat, and I put away my coat and hat as well. Mei Yingzi took me to the drawing room, then excused herself and went off. The drawing room was fitted out in the genuine English style: two writing desks with small racks of notepaper and envelopes on top, a round table in the middle of the room, small sofas set end to end all around, arranged as if in groups, with little side tables behind them or beside them. In the corner across from the window was a suite of sofas around a light little oval table. The room was very warm with steam heat, the windows all closely curtained. When I went in there was no one there, but presently two middle-aged men came in speaking German, which I did not understand. I sat in a corner, and, like a bird that has flown into a room, I felt intensely uneasy in the strange surroundings; yet at the same time I felt by instinct the mystery of the place.

Mei Yingzi came in; she had washed and dressed her hair afresh and changed into evening clothes, charming beyond words.

"So Mei Yingzi lives here," I thought. Bai Ping had once lodged at Mei Yingzi's rooms, and had mentioned it in passing at the time; I had not asked in detail, but it seemed she had never spoken of the Benner Inn, so Mei Yingzi must have moved here recently?

Light and graceful, Mei Yingzi came up before me, then turned and sat on the sofa at my side. She said:

"It's not bad here, is it?"

"Very quiet," I said. "You live here?"

"Yes."

"For long?"

"No," she said. "Not a week yet."

A waiter came in and told Mei Yingzi that dinner was served. Mei Yingzi and I went to the dining room.

The dining room had yellow walls, on which hung two brightly colored still lifes; there were perhaps ten tables or so in all, five or six of them with people at them. Mei Yingzi exchanged brief greetings with them, and we sat down facing each other at the two ends of a laid table. Mei Yingzi called for wine.

I kept thinking Mei Yingzi must by now have some instruction to give me, but no; she smiled faintly and knit her brows, and said very little. There were plenty of people in the room, yet all was utterly still; the wireless began to broadcast a quiet nocturne, and Mei Yingzi seemed to be listening. I too melted slowly into the imaginings of the music, and for a moment even forgot the mission I was supposed to be awaiting.

It was a long, long time since I had had so sweet an enjoyment: good music, good company, good food, spending a dusk and half a night in a lovely, spotless room. It could give my soul the freshness of a new birth and make my work far more effective. But tonight I could not lose myself in such enjoyment; around my spirit there hung an atmosphere I cannot name, that made me long to strip away the riddle the sooner. But Mei Yingzi kept silent, and in the room there was only the occasional small sound of knife and fork.

Not until the meal was over, as a piece of music came to its end, did Mei Yingzi say:

"Would you like to come and see my room?"

"..." I made no sound, but nodded. She rose, and I rose with her, followed her out of the dining room, followed her upstairs, followed her into the room, where at once a scent she often wore came over me. Outside seemed to be a very small sitting room, a suite of sofas and a writing desk set sparsely about; a great yellow floor-length velvet curtain hung across the doorway, and from where I was I could see the bed, could see the lamp table; this was without doubt her bedroom, but I never went in. Mei Yingzi settled me on a sofa; with a light smile she brought out her low voice and said:

"Any regrets about your promise to Mrs. Stephen?"

"None, of course," I said. "I'm not a child."

"But this is no joke," she said. "I'll give you now one last chance to draw back."

"Set your mind at rest," I said. "I won't regret it, and there's no need to draw back."

"Truly?" she said. "But the work is nothing but obedience and courage."

"I know."

"Then," she said, "I'm going to ask you now to do a thing." She sat down beside me and took up a cigarette; she smoked only now and then, but always with a very practiced air. I lit it for her, and she began to watch the smoke she breathed out, and said, grave and calm:

"How many days is it since you were last with Bai Ping?"

"Already a long while."

"Ah!" She gave me a glance, then said calmly: "The work now is to ask you to fetch, from Bai Ping's, a package in a white sealed envelope."

"Bai Ping?"

"Yes," she said. "I've hinted it to you before."

"You mean she..."

"Yes," she said. "But you needn't ask further." Then she gave a slight smile: "The envelope is the size of a twenty-four-mo sheet of newsprint, printed with the words of the Japanese Navy Ministry, unopened, with a seal of sealing-wax on the back."

"It's certainly at Bai Ping's?"

"Certainly," she said. "But you must be quick, tonight, tomorrow, tomorrow," she reckoned, and added: "Before noon tomorrow I'll be here; the morning after, at seven o'clock, I'll wait for you in Jessfield Park."

"..." I could say nothing; I was sunk in thought.

"Otherwise I'm afraid the thing will no longer be in her hands," she said. "You must get it tonight, at once; failing that, don't leave her side tomorrow, for tomorrow there's still one chance."

"Very well," I said firmly.

"I hope it will all go smoothly. Don't quarrel with Bai Ping, and don't let her find out that it was you who took it."

"But afterward, how am I to cover it up so she doesn't notice?"

"I need only one night; the next day I'll ask you to take the thing back and put it where it was."

"Mm."

"From now on you must keep up regular relations with her, but without letting her grow suspicious of your purpose. If through her you come to know the Japanese soldiers connected with her, and can do it without making those soldiers jealous of your friendship with Bai Ping, then you will have wholly succeeded. You must keep a detached air while you are with Bai Ping."

"All right, I'll try."

"You must on no account drop her any hint, or take it into your head to persuade her to mend her ways."

"Why?" I was astonished, for this was precisely what I had wanted to do. A person like Bai Ping, if she had been bought by the Japanese, it was entirely because she was extravagant, because she needed money, because she had given herself up for lost. She was Chinese after all; if she were given money, could she not just as well be one of us? Yet Mei Yingzi had forbidden it in advance. What did this mean?

"It bears on your whole work; it bears on your life," she said coolly.

"I don't understand," I said. "She's a person of ability, isn't she?"

"Yes." Mei Yingzi smiled slyly.

"She's Chinese, isn't she?"

"Why can't you use her?" I said. "I'd have thought using her would be better than using me."

"Yes; but how do you know I haven't tried?"

"And I'm not to be allowed to try again?"

"But your work is only to steal that document, and to keep on good terms with her." She suddenly stood up and walked away, and spoke in a cool, stern, commanding tone.

"Then I'll obey," I said.

"Thank you." So saying she rose, went to the writing desk, opened a drawer, and took out a check, came lightly over and handed it to me, saying: "This is money."

"Money?"

"Take it," she said flatly. "When you have any special need, tell me."

I took the check; it was on the Fuyuan native bank, for the sum of twenty thousand yuan. I put it away. She said: "Now you may go. My telephone here is xxxxx; of course nothing can be said over the telephone, so unless it's necessary, better not to call. Before noon tomorrow I'll be here. If you don't come, the morning after, at seven o'clock, I'll wait for you by the pond in Jessfield Park."

"Goodbye, then."

"Goodbye." She shook my hand, seeing me off with only a lovely smile. The door closed softly, and when I turned my head again I heard the sound of the lock going home.
