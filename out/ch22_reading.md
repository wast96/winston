### A Vicious Manhunt (Part 2)

### The Secret Abduction of Ding Ling

On May 14, 1933, the woman writer Ding Ling—then Chinese Communist Party group secretary of the League of Left-Wing Writers in Shanghai and editor of the magazine Big Dipper—was secretly abducted by Kuomintang Zhongtong agents from her home at No. 2, fifth floor, No. 7 Kunshan Garden Road. Taken with her was the philosopher Pan Zinian, who had come to see her. As they were being forced into the car, the writer Ying Xiuren, who was calling on Ding Ling, and another comrade fought a hand-to-hand battle in her room with the agents who had come for her. Ying Xiuren was killed in the struggle, falling from the building; the other comrade got away by luck and reported to the organization the news of Ding Ling's and Pan Zinian's arrest.

This despicable abduction was carried out by men under Lü Keqin (real name Shi Jimei, another alias Ma Shaowu), Gu Shunzhang's favorite pupil from the "agent training class," on Gu Shunzhang's orders. A month later, on the evening of June 15, this same Lü Keqin was killed at Xiaohuayuan on Zhejiang Road by the Central Special Section's "Dog-Beating Squad": Old Kuang (that is, "Big Cantonese," Kuang Hui'an) led two squad members and shot him dead. On the 16th the Shanghai Da Mei Wan Bao reported the words of Wen Hong'en, the Kuomintang's Shanghai public security chief: "Lü [Keqin]'s appointment as supervisor of the Bureau's special-service group was made by the central authorities" (the writer's note: meaning the Nanjing Kuomintang Central Party headquarters). The paper went on: "On learning of it, the Center, holding the case grave, sent Gu Shunzhang of the People's Training Committee to Shanghai last night to investigate everything; Mr. Gu reached Shanghai this morning, called on public security chief Wen Hong'en around nine, and then went on to call on Mayor Wu [Kaixian]." A dispatch in the Tianjin Ta Kung Pao of June 19 also said: "Ma [Shaowu]'s appointment as supervisor of the special-service group of the Shanghai Public Security Bureau's inspectorate was made by the central authorities."

Ding Ling was abducted by Kuomintang agents within the International Settlement. The agents first took her by car to a small hotel at the south end of Shiliupu by the Huangpu River and held her there a night, then next morning put her on a train under guard to Nanjing, "to a rather high-class hotel entirely of the old Chinese kind," and "the next day moved her to another hotel." After that she was shifted to a Zhongtong agent's residence and kept in confinement.

Ding Ling was by then a well-known left-wing writer. Introducing her early life, the Xinhua News Agency said: "Comrade Ding Ling, originally named Jiang Bingzhi, was born on October 12, 1904, in Linli county, Hunan. Reared by a mother of democratic ideals, she early conceived a strong anti-feudal consciousness, and while at middle school in Taoyuan, Changsha, and elsewhere she came under the influence of the May Fourth movement. In 1922 she went to Shanghai to the Common People's Girls' School founded by Chen Duxiu and Li Da; in 1923 she entered the Chinese department of Shanghai University, founded by our Party. In late 1927 she began to publish fiction; works such as The Diary of Miss Sophie boldly laid bare the dark reality of old China and voiced the pain and yearning of the young intellectuals awakened after May Fourth, and stirred a strong response in society. In the revolutionary literary movement Ding Ling was a writer of great influence under Lu Xun's banner. In May 1930 she joined the League of Left-Wing Writers. In early 1931 her companion and comrade-in-arms, the noted left-wing writer and Communist Hu Yepin, was killed by the Kuomintang reactionaries; in the face of the savage White Terror she bravely took up the editorship of the League's organ, Big Dipper; and in March 1932 she joined the Chinese Communist Party. In the latter half of that year she became secretary of the League's Party group. She drew together well-known writers on a broad front, nurtured young authors, and did a great deal to beat back the Kuomintang's cultural 'encirclement' and to develop revolutionary literature. The works she created in this period, Wei Hu, Water, Mother, and others, showed the real achievement of left-wing revolutionary literature and won the approval of Lu Xun, Qu Qiubai, Mao Dun, and others."

When word got out of Ding Ling's and Pan Zinian's abduction, it at once drew the concern, the rescue efforts, and the protest and outcry against the reactionary Kuomintang government from every quarter of society, above all from the literary and art world, and drew the notice of world opinion as well. Famous figures at home and abroad—Song Qingling, Cai Yuanpei, Lu Xun, Yang Xingfo, Liu Yazi, and Barbusse, Vaillant-Couturier, Romain Rolland—launched protests and rescue efforts. The League of Left-Wing Writers was first to issue a "Declaration on the Arrest of Ding and Pan and Against the Kuomintang White Terror," followed by a "Declaration for the Rescue of Ding and Pan" from the literary world's Ding-Pan rescue committee; thirty-eight literary figures, among them Zou Taofen, Ye Shengtao, and Yu Dafu, jointly sent a telegram of strong protest to Wang Jingwei, president of the Kuomintang government's Executive Yuan, and Luo Wengan, minister of justice, demanding the swift release of Ding Ling and Pan Zinian. The China League for the Protection of Civil Rights spared no effort in the campaign for Ding and Pan, and its American members Agnes Smedley and Harold Isaacs took active part in the rescue too.

At this time Yang Quan (Xingfo), secretary-general of the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights, though threatened many times, was neither cowed nor afraid, and firmly kept up the effort to save the Communists Ding Ling and Pan Zinian. The Shanghai Public Security Bureau flatly denied taking anyone, but Yang Xingfo had by now got hold of important evidence. On the one hand he denounced the Bureau on solid grounds and negotiated with the Municipal Council in the concession; on the other he wired the Kuomintang government demanding the release of Ding Ling and Pan Zinian, "who stand high in the writing world." Sadly, one month and four days after Ding and Pan were abducted, Yang Quan was brutally assassinated by Kuomintang agents.

Over Ding Ling's and Pan Zinian's abduction the Kuomintang's Shanghai Public Security Bureau, guilty and afraid, kept up a flat denial and a deep silence, the papers' reports contradicted one another, and all sorts of rumors spread. Some even put it about that Ding Ling had already been killed, and the tale grew wilder in the telling. As an article in one magazine put it: at the time "many said she was no longer among the living. For a certain span, Ding Ling's death seemed to become an accepted fact in society, and the event the public cared about most."

Lu Xun had always held Ding Ling's firm stand and her writing gifts in high esteem. Interviewed on May 22, 1933, by the Korean journalist Shen Yanjun, he was asked: "In the Chinese literary world today, who do you think is the representative proletarian writer?" Lu Xun answered: "Miss Ding Ling is the one true proletarian writer." He was therefore beside himself with anger at the Kuomintang agents' crime of abducting Ding Ling. Lu Xun was not one to credit rumors lightly, but he knew that Chiang Kai-shek and his Fascist bandits were cruel and merciless, poison past all others, capable of any monstrous deed. In one letter he wrote: "Protest over the Ding affair is useless; the authorities will spare it no thought. As things stand her life or death is still unknown. In truth people vanish often in Shanghai, and only because they have no name does no one speak of them. Yang Xingfo too was one who worked hard to save Ding, but he has been assassinated …" (June 26, 1933, to Wang Zhizhi). In another letter he wrote: "As for Ding Ling, there is no news; to my mind she has already been killed, and some publications still spread many rumors about her—truly lower than beasts" (August 1, 1933, to the Science News Agency).

On June 28, with fierce hatred for Chiang Kai-shek's dynasty and deep, tender remembrance of Ding Ling, Lu Xun wrote the poem "Mourning Ding Ling":

{p} Night air heavy as a great stone presses the storied towers, / a willow-cutting spring wind ushers in the ninth month's autumn. / Dust settles on the jasper zither, its clear lament stilled; / alas, no woman left to shine upon the high hill.

On September 21 Lu Xun sent this poem to Cao Juren, saying in his letter: "An old-style poem; I wonder if The Sound of Waves might print it?" (the writer's note: the poem is "Mourning Master Ding," carried in The Sound of Waves, vol. 2, no. 38, on September 30 that year) (September 21, 1933, to Cao Juren).

Taken under guard to Nanjing, Ding Ling was moved the next day to the Xindu Grand Hotel on Hubu Street and held there. In this hotel, Wang Shengdi—former propaganda chief of the Communist Jiangsu Provincial Committee, who had turned after his arrest—came again and again to press her to surrender, and she cursed him out each time. Later Zhang Chong came too to press surrender, and she drove him off as well. After that she was moved to a private house where a Zhongtong agent lived. Xu Enzeng, the Zhongtong chief and head of the Kuomintang Central Party headquarters' investigation section, came several times to press surrender. Xu Enzeng, Zhang Daofan, and the renegade Gu Shunzhang tried in turn to threaten, tempt, and deceive her, hoping to use her fame for their own ends, but she firmly refused to work for the enemy, to write for them, or to show her face in public.

When Xu Enzeng came the first time at the end of June, the guard said only, "Section Chief Xu has come to see you." Who Section Chief Xu was, she did not know. He played the "Smiling Tiger," feigning innocence and lying to her face: "That we brought you to Nanjing is truly a 'misunderstanding.'" In fact it was because Ding Ling was a Communist that he had had Gu Shunzhang abduct her here in secret. After she was brought under guard to Nanjing, the first renegade Xu Enzeng sent to the Xindu Grand Hotel to press her, Wang Shengdi, had said to her: "You are a Communist—there's no wriggling out of it!" Now this "Smiling Tiger" said to her, on purpose: "But you're not a Communist!" Xu Enzeng then talked in a long roundabout way before coming to the point of pressing surrender. Ding Ling recounted: "He went on: 'And you don't know where the other Party members live, and you can't help us catch anyone, so you're of no use to us. You understand too that we weren't out to catch you in particular; we only wanted to wreck the Communists' apparatus and happened to run into you. Only, now that you're here, it's hard to let you go. We don't fear being called savage, brutal, kidnappers, and the rest; whatever Cai Yuanpei, Song Qingling, whatever the Civil Rights League, whatever the writers—none of it troubles us. All we fear is stirring up a protest from the foreigners. We caught you in the concession. Where you lived is the concession, and this has already brought a protest from the concession police, who say we violated their extraterritoriality. We don't want more trouble, so we can only hold to our denial. That is how things stand.' He said all this in one breath, stopped, and looked at me, watching. I understood: this was a man far more formidable than any who had come before, perhaps the most formidable adversary I would meet in my whole life—a 'Smiling Tiger,' a true enemy. I only held fast to the thought that, since I cared for nothing and did not even fear death, what could a 'Smiling Tiger' do to me? The 'Smiling Tiger' said again, with a faint smile: 'We won't release you because we're afraid a reporter will ask how you came to Nanjing—that would give the foreigners a handle. If you'll agree not to see reporters, and move to another place, away from them, to live freely, it will be much more convenient.' He paused, then added: 'Peng Xuepei can fund a trip abroad for you; he is willing to give you sixty thousand yuan to go abroad …' He paused again, watching me. I was not moved, and answered him plainly: 'I cannot take Peng Xuepei's money; we are not friends, we have not the slightest connection. As for going abroad, it is a thing I have never once thought of, and I do not wish to go abroad now. There is no need to speak of it further.'"

After that Xu Enzeng came several more times to press surrender, and Zhang Daofan, propaganda chief of the Kuomintang Central Party headquarters, came three times as well, and all three times ran into a wall. The first two times he suggested Ding Ling write a play, which she refused; later he said he had written a play himself and asked Ding Ling to "have a look," "offer an opinion," "or would I (Ding Ling) take up the pen and revise it for him." Ding Ling said she did not understand the theater and had no interest in it, and he left crestfallen. Time and again they came shamelessly to plead, meaning only to have her surrender and betray the Party, and to use her name for their own ends. Xu Enzeng later said in his own memoir: "She has an outstanding gift for writing, and I very much hoped she would become a forceful cultural worker for our Party in time." This one-sided fancy came to nothing in the end.

With all the pressure from Xu Enzeng, Zhang Daofan, and the rest to no avail, two months later Gu Shunzhang appeared again in the room where Ding Ling was held. He, the very ringleader of her and Pan Zinian's secret abduction, came to play the cat weeping over the mouse, even lying that "my own situation is much like yours"—without freedom. Ding Ling saw through his scheme at a glance and drew a sharp likeness of this traitor's sinister, shameless, hypocritical face. Ding Ling recounted that in the autumn of 1933, after an illness during which she "lay a week hidden behind the bed-curtains":

It was early September. I cannot recall which day, but about the time I had just risen, a guard suddenly brought in a few gifts—fruit, pastries, and the like—saying: "These are from Mr. Wang out back. He'll come to see you in a while." Mr. Wang? I remembered that when I first came here there had been a Mr. Wang who wanted to see me, but nothing more had come of it. I had paid it no mind then, taking it for a bit of idle nonsense; what did I care whether it was a Mr. Wang or a Mr. Zhang! Now, sure enough, there was a Mr. Wang. He had first sent gifts and would then come to see me—who on earth was this Mr. Wang? What was he coming for?

After a while the guard brought a man in. He was of short, sturdy build, quick in his movements, with two round eyes full of spirit. He put on no airs and was very free, speaking like an old acquaintance who came and went often: "I've wanted to come see you for a long time and only found the time today. Ah! You've been shut in a good while. I imagine you must feel it dreary." Seeing that I did not know how to answer, he introduced himself: "My own situation is much like yours—on the surface I can go out for a walk in the street, but really I too am not free. I've come today with no other purpose than to help you pass the dull hours; set your mind at ease." What sort of man was this Mr. Wang? What part was he playing in coming here? He was a figure such as I had never met in my twenty-odd years of life, and I watched him warily. Seeing that I would not speak, he said no more, took his leave casually, and left the feeling that he would come again. Who on earth was this Mr. Wang?

It was the guard who told us afterward, and how could I not be startled? He was Gu Shunzhang—a rather famous great renegade of the Communist Party, a great agent of the Kuomintang! I had heard something of him before, and the newspapers had trumpeted him too; he was a complex man, not to be understood by a simple person like me. But I knew clearly in my heart that he was a great villain, and I understood, of course, that these guards, this pack of executioners who killed without a blink, were his clique and his subordinates, and that his coming now showed the Kuomintang would by no means let me be.

Gu Shunzhang did indeed come again, and seemed wholly unaware of my coldness toward him, as if he had only come to find an old friend to chat with, telling of his old days keeping an antiques shop, of touring the ports with a magic troupe. An antiques shop, to be sure, is a trade fairly easy to hide behind—who would guess that the people coming to buy and sell paintings and pottery were Communists? So too with magic. In the old society, doing magic and sleight of hand was generally taken for a wandering way of scraping a living; who would think to link it with revolution and the Communists? Gu Shunzhang came again, each time putting on the air of an old neighbor dropping in, doing tricks before me: he took a few ping-pong balls and turned one into two, three, four, five … then a few strips of red and green silk, red into green and green into red, something into nothing and nothing into something. He played with cigarettes too, swallowing a lit one and bringing it back up. He came often, and whether one heeded him or not, however cold one's manner, he seemed not to feel it, not to mind it; he would come and talk a little social news, of which he had a sharp grasp. He talked business and buying and selling and seemed shrewd; he did his magic with clean hands. He talked of his own past too, glossing himself over, saying he had not sold out the Communists—that the Party had fallen into an irreparable misunderstanding about him. He put on a look of neither resenting Heaven nor blaming men. He spoke of his future too, saying he would in time withdraw from the political stage, go to the countryside, start a farm, take up practical industry, and build from small to great. I only hinted to him that I did not believe him. I could see he was watching me too. One day he hypnotized a guard in the outer hall; I do not understand hypnotism, but that guard was truly put under, wholly obedient to him, doing at his command what he could not do in the ordinary way, what an ordinary man cannot do. Afterward the guard was so exhausted for three full days that he could not stir, and knew nothing at all of what he had said or done under the trance. It was a thing to make one's flesh creep. But I said to Gu Shunzhang: "You cannot put me under, because I do not believe you. Your whole method, the tricks you display—I simply will not listen." He could only say: "If that is how you are, there is nothing I can do. To hypnotize a man, he must first believe me, listen to me." I had heard before that at times, when Kuomintang agents seized our comrades, besides the ordinary instruments and the electric torture they even used hypnotism to get confessions. But I reckoned that, for all that I worked for the Party at peril, I knew none of the Party's important secrets. Should I fail to withstand the hypnosis, lose my senses, be unable to hold myself, the most I would say was that I was a Communist, secretary of the League's Party group. And had the Kuomintang not long known that? They had even meant to clear me, saying "you're not a Communist after all." Say I was, it was but one death; say I was not, it was still one death. So I met it calmly. A few days later, chatting, Gu Shunzhang said: "It does the Kuomintang no good to kill you—it isn't worth it. Now the foreigners are in a lawsuit with the Kuomintang over you. The Kuomintang dares not release you either, afraid a reporter will find you; you yourself are a piece of evidence, taken in the concession. In truth they've taken more than you alone in the concession—they've taken plenty. The foreigners hold it a violation of their extraterritoriality and have long had a grievance. And your case has got out, the foreigners know of it, some foreign writers and notables have wired against the Kuomintang's taking you and demand your release. You have a name, the thing has got out, and the foreigners lose face too, so they mean to settle accounts with the Kuomintang; there's a spell of haggling to be done …"

I need not have pressed him—I had heard all this before. Naturally the words he spoke, that "it isn't worth it for the Kuomintang to kill me," did move me; whether the Kuomintang arrested me, killed me, or held me, they could get nothing they hoped to get—that, I thought, was so. But did the Kuomintang kill only those whom it profited to kill? What need was there to kill Hu Yepin? What need to kill the five League martyrs? Chiang Kai-shek had said long ago: "Better to kill a thousand by mistake than let one go." In any case, in another's hands, one has no sense of safety. When Gu Shunzhang said these things, he was not negotiating with me, nor asking my opinion; he only let them fall lightly, like idle talk, and passed on.

A few days later Gu Shunzhang came back to this point and added a few words: "There's nothing to be done, shut in here. My situation is much like yours. I think, if only I had a thread of freedom, I could move, could get clean away—why should I be penned in here?" He seemed to be speaking truly of himself.

I knew well in my heart that a traitor and agent like him could indeed never win freedom under the Kuomintang. His talk of starting a farm and so on was nothing but a fraud on others, or only on himself. But I was wholly unlike him. I could go back to the Party without shame. If only I had a little freedom, I truly could get clean away. To be long sealed up in this bandit's lair, neither living nor dead, neither clear nor plain, was indeed without hope. I turned over how I might change the form of my confinement. I asked to be put in a proper prison, thinking that in jail I could win the open, could make contact with the comrades inside and the Party outside. But they turned a deaf ear and paid it no heed. This too, it seemed, could not be managed. Then—might I find some way to make them keep me under a looser guard? If I could only win a thread of a crack, and through that crack let in a wisp of fresh air, a ray of bright sun, only a little, a shoot might yet grow. Even a moldering thing can sprout; then could not I find the chance to make my life put out a tender shoot? Everything needs a bud; with a bud it can grow, with a bud there is hope of growing. Sealed now in a stoppered jar, without a breath of air, I had not a spark of life in me. So all day I turned it over: I dreamed my mother had come to Shanghai, I dreamed "they" were in a lawsuit with the Kuomintang and surely raising a public protest, crying out for my rescue as for the arrested and imprisoned Noulenses, for Liao Chengzhi, Luo Dengxian, and the rest. Would they engage a lawyer for me and try to get me out on bail? Such things had happened to other comrades before. At times I was full of excitement, at times cast down. I understood it was all a dream, for I was not in prison. The Kuomintang had never dared admit openly that it had arrested me, afraid of stirring a dispute, afraid of offending the foreigners; they would deny it to the death, and so they would neither release me nor send me on to a prison. Was I then truly only to die in vain in this cold, gloomy hall? I turned it over and over, and though the two purple weals on my neck had by now healed, the wound in my heart seared me with pain every day.

From May 1933 to September 1936 Ding Ling was held captive in Nanjing. Whether it was the "Smiling Tiger" Xu Enzeng or the great renegade Gu Shunzhang, their threats and inducements she threw back; her whole heart was set on finding the Party, and at every moment she longed to escape the demon's lair of Nanjing. She knew the Party Center had reached northern Shaanxi after the Long March, and that far-off region became the holy ground she yearned for day and night. Her wish was at last carried to the Party organization through a friend of the Chinese people, a member of the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights, the American progressive writer Agnes Smedley. "In April 1936 Lu Xun told Feng Xuefeng that, by Smedley's account, Ding Ling had wished to find her ties to the Party. Smedley told Feng Xuefeng too. In September Feng Xuefeng, through Zhang Tianyi, made contact with Ding Ling, and under Feng's arrangement she escaped from Nanjing to Shanghai, and was then sent on to Xi'an and thence to northern Shaanxi." In November Ding Ling reached Bao'an in northern Shaanxi as she had longed to do, and returning to the Party's embrace, was welcomed at a meeting the Party Center's Propaganda Department held in a great cave dwelling, at which the central leaders Mao Zedong, Zhang Wentian, Zhou Enlai, and others were all present. Ding Ling later recalled: "This was perhaps the happiest, most glorious moment of my whole life." It was a special honor the Party gave Ding Ling, and showed too the Party's regard and trust for her. After the meeting Mao Zedong asked her what she meant to do, and Ding Ling answered: "I want to be a Red Army soldier." Ding Ling stayed at Bao'an only twelve days before going to the front as a Red Army soldier, following Yang Shangkun, director of the Political Department. She marched with the troops day and night, and after reaching Qingyang moved with the First Corps of the First Front Army. At Qingyang, Ding Ling received the ci poem Mao Zedong sent her by telegram, "To the Tune 'Immortal by the River': For Comrade Ding Ling" (when Ding Ling returned to Yan'an in early 1937, Mao Zedong wrote out the whole text in his own hand and gave it to her):

{p} On the wall the Red Flag flutters in the setting sun, / the west wind sweeps over the lone city. / The people of Bao'an are new for the hour; / in the cave a banquet is spread / to welcome one come out of prison. / A slender pen—who can match it? / Three thousand Mauser troops. / The battle array points east of the Long Mountains. / Yesterday a delicate Miss of letters, / today a general of the field.

Sadly, grave setbacks followed, and Ding Ling long suffered unjust treatment. In the "Cultural Revolution" she was framed and persecuted still worse and held for five years. At last, after an investigation by the Party organization, the matter was cleared up and she was fully rehabilitated. The Organization Department of the Party Center held that "during her imprisonment Comrade Ding Ling was threatened, tempted, and deceived by the enemy, who sought to use her fame for their own ends, but she refused to work for the enemy, to write, or to show her face in public; she did nothing to endanger the Party organization or the comrades' safety, and afterward, making her way between Nanjing and Shanghai, she at length found the Party organization and, with its help, escaped Nanjing and reached northern Shaanxi." The Organization Department of the Party Center therefore, on July 14, 1984, notified the whole Party:

Comrade Ding Ling is an old comrade of our Party who, in more than half a century of revolutionary struggle and of literary and art work, did much useful work and created many fine literary works of great influence at home and abroad, and rendered service to the Party and the people. After 1957, through more than twenty long years, though in adversity, she bore herself well throughout. After her return to work in 1979 she upheld the line, principles, and policies laid down by the Third Plenum of the Eleventh Central Committee, wrote on with vigor despite her age and frailty, upheld Mao Zedong's thought on literature and art, taught young writers, and made several trips abroad, all to good effect. The facts show that Comrade Ding Ling was a Communist loyal to the Party and to the revolution. It is now decided that Comrade Ding Ling's reputation be fully restored; the friends and comrades implicated by her wrongful conviction and wrongful classification shall likewise all be set right, every false word overturned and its effects erased.

### A Hand in the Assassination of Yang Xingfo

On June 18, 1933, Yang Quan—secretary-general of the Kuomintang's Academia Sinica and executive member and secretary-general of the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights—was killed before the door of the Academia Sinica's International Exchange Bureau of Exhibits at 331 Avenue du Roi Albert in the French Concession of Shanghai; and Gu Shunzhang had a hand in it.

Yang Quan, courtesy name Xingfo, also called Honggu, was born in 1893 in Yushan county, Jiangxi. A left-winger of the Kuomintang, he was a close friend who consistently supported the Chinese Communist Party. In his youth he passed into China College in Shanghai, then a meeting-place of revolutionaries. He joined the Tongmenghui and went to Wuchang to live through the 1911 Wuchang Uprising. After Sun Yat-sen set up the provisional presidential office in Nanjing, Yang served as head of the receiving-and-dispatching group of the presidential secretariat. Later, with Sun Yat-sen's approval, he was sent by the Bureau of Rewards to study in the United States, at Cornell and Harvard. Home again, he served as professor and head of the engineering faculty at Nanjing Higher Normal School (later renamed Southeast University). In 1924, with the cooperation of the Kuomintang and the Communist Party, he had dealings at Southeast University with Yun Daiying and many other Communists. Sun Yat-sen had by then set up a revolutionary government in Guangzhou, and Yang Quan went to serve as his secretary; in November 1924, when Sun Yat-sen came to Beijing to convene a national assembly, Yang Quan went with him. After Sun Yat-sen's death, Yang served as secretary-general of the preparatory office for Mr. Sun Yat-sen's funeral. On May 5, 1925, when the Shanghai Communist organization decided to hold a meeting at Shanghai University marking the 107th anniversary of Marx's birth, Yang joined it with the Communists Yun Daiying and Ren Bishi. Yang Quan gave a report at the meeting, introducing the Marxist doctrine of class struggle. After the May Thirtieth Incident, on June 10, he founded the National Daily in Shanghai, writing an editorial daily to strike at the crimes of British imperialism and expose the servile, surrendering behavior of certain warlord politicians and society notables. For this the imperialists and reactionary warlords hated him, and the paper was ordered shut after only sixteen days. In October 1925 he and the Communists Yun Daiying and Hou Shaoying founded the China Relief Society. After Chiang Kai-shek launched the "April 12" counter-revolutionary coup, the China Relief Society went underground and carried on. Right into the 1930s Yang Quan kept his ties to it, using every avenue to rescue and relieve arrested Communists and progressive youth. In 1926 he was made propaganda chief of the Kuomintang's Shanghai Municipal Party headquarters. When the Guangdong National Revolutionary Army set out on the Northern Expedition, Yang Quan took charge in Shanghai of the work coordinating with the Expedition, setting up a secret radio station to wire military intelligence daily to the Expedition's front command. Found out by the warlord Sun Chuanfang's forces, he was arrested in league with the French Concession police and was to be handed to the Chinese city for execution; only after Song Qingling engaged a lawyer to negotiate with the French police was he released. Yang Quan at once rented the French garrison's radio station and went on transmitting. In March 1927, when the Chinese Communist Party led the Shanghai workers in their Third Armed Uprising, Yang Quan warmly approved the Communists' plan for armed rising, worked hard to bridge the views of the two parties, and helped the Shanghai workers' rising along. After the rising's victory, the Shanghai Provisional Municipal Government was formed by election, and Yang Quan was elected a standing committee member and head of the land bureau. In the "April 12" bloodbath, when Chiang Kai-shek washed Shanghai in blood, this newborn revolutionary regime was strangled. Yang was deeply dissatisfied with the Kuomintang right's crimes, and afterward kept in contact with Yu Zehong, sent by the Communist organization.

In October 1927, when Cai Yuanpei became president of the University Council, Yang Quan was invited to be director of the Council's educational-administration office and later its vice-president. In June 1928, when Academia Sinica was founded in Shanghai, Yang Quan was engaged as secretary-general at the invitation of its president, Cai Yuanpei. With his Western democratic ideals and modern scientific knowledge and a strong capacity for work, Yang Quan became Cai Yuanpei's ablest helper in building Academia Sinica. At the same time Yang Quan, Cai Yuanpei, Song Qingling, He Xiangning, and others supported anti-Chiang activity on every side. In July 1931, on the strength of what he had learned on an inspection tour to Jiangxi, he wrote "The Present State of the Chinese Communist Party," published in the Shanghai North China Daily News, giving an objective account of the Chinese Communist Party and the Red Army, and drew Chiang Kai-shek's hatred. After the "September 18" Incident he called on many occasions for an end to the civil war and firm resistance to Japan.

In December 1932, Song Qingling, Cai Yuanpei, Lu Xun, Yang Quan, and others organized the China League for Civil Rights (the "League" for short) in Shanghai, forming a central executive committee of seven, with Song Qingling as chair, Cai Yuanpei as vice-chair, and Yang Quan as secretary-general. It was a political body built to oppose Chiang Kai-shek's Fascist rule and to win the people's democratic freedoms and rights. Its published manifesto said: "The purposes of this League are: (1) to struggle for the release of political prisoners within the country and for the abolition of unlawful detention, torture, and killing. The League will devote itself first of all to the great numbers of the nameless prisoners society takes no notice of; (2) to give political prisoners within the country legal and other aid, to investigate prison conditions, and to publish the facts of the oppression of civil rights within the country, so as to rouse public opinion; (3) to assist the struggle for freedom of association and assembly, of speech, and of assembly and association."

The moment this League was founded, Song Qingling laid bare to the press the Kuomintang agents' crimes against the Communists and the people: "countless compatriots unlawfully arrested and imprisoned," "the medieval survival—the existence of secret military courts." She said: "The League's first concern is to aid the great mass of nameless, voiceless political prisoners crammed in the jails." The "League" would organize legal safeguards for political prisoners, give them what legal aid it could, and see that their cases were brought to open trial. "We must demand the immediate abolition of every law and ordinance that restricts citizens' rights by any means, such as the Emergency Law for Crimes Endangering the Republic. Another task is to destroy the secret courts that try political prisoners; in such places not even the barest justice, but the very basic rights of a person, are toyed with and trampled. We must also struggle against the imperialists' methods of secret flogging and secret 'framing to convict' in the concessions …"

Even before the "League" was founded, Yang Quan, Song Qingling, Cai Yuanpei, and others had rescued the Noulenses, secretary of the Pan-Pacific Trade Union Secretariat's Shanghai office and a Comintern worker in China. After its founding, the League did a great deal to safeguard the people's democratic freedoms and rights, to rescue political prisoners, and to oppose the Kuomintang's unlawful detention and killing. In December 1932, when the progressive professors Xu Deheng, Hou Wailu, Ma Zhemin, and more than twenty other patriotic teachers and students in Beiping were arrested, Song Qingling and Cai Yuanpei on hearing of it wired Chiang Kai-shek a protest demanding their immediate release, and under the pressure of opinion and the rescue efforts of Song Qingling and others they were freed one after another. In April 1933, as the atmosphere of terror grew graver still, Soong, Cai, and Yang, with the lawyers Shen Junru, Wu Kaisheng, and others, went to Nanjing to visit the imprisoned political prisoners Chen Geng, Luo Dengxian, and the rest, and secured a meeting with Wang Jingwei, president of the Kuomintang Executive Yuan; besides demanding the release of all political prisoners and the abolition of such special laws as the Emergency Law for Crimes Endangering the Republic, they proposed an immediate end to the civil war and Kuomintang-Communist cooperation for united resistance to Japan. Song Qingling and others also rescued Chen Duxiu, arrested in 1932, and Ding Ling and Pan Zinian, arrested in 1933. As Deng Xiaoping said in his eulogy at Song Qingling's memorial meeting: "In the 1930s, engaged in revolutionary activity in Shanghai, she forged a deep revolutionary friendship with the great Communist Lu Xun, and with Lu Xun, Cai Yuanpei, Yang Xingfo, and others organized the 'China League for the Protection of Civil Rights,' waging a head-on struggle against the Kuomintang reactionaries, protecting and rescuing great numbers of Chinese Communist Party members and anti-Chiang patriotic democrats, and making a unique and major contribution to the cause of the revolution."

As a founder and executive member of this League, Lu Xun too took active part in its work. Song Qingling once wrote of it: "Lu Xun and Yang Xingfo had both held posts in the Nanjing provisional government in 1911, but only after both joined the China Relief Society in 1927 did the two come to know each other. In the summer of 1932, when Yang was secretary of Academia Sinica, he asked Mr. Lu Xun to join the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights, and that autumn Lu Xun, Cai Yuanpei, and I were all elected executive members of the League. The White Terror was then very fierce. Lu Xun lived in the Hongkou district of Shanghai, in a hard position, for there were many Kuomintang reactionary agents and police watching him there. Each time the League met, Lu Xun and Cai Yuanpei both came on time. Lu Xun, Cai Yuanpei, and we discussed together, with heat, how to oppose the White Terror and how to rescue the imprisoned political prisoners and arrested revolutionary students, and to provide them with legal defense and other aid."

The League's work to rescue political prisoners had already made Chiang Kai-shek furious. In March 1933, spurred on by the Chinese Communist Party, Song Qingling, Yang Quan, and others, together with part of Shanghai's patriotic and progressive cultural circles and workers' bodies, organized a revolutionary body of still wider scope and clearer aim, the National Salvation Association Against Foreign Aggression, which the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights's Shanghai branch joined as a body member. On March 8, Yang Quan, speaking for the League at the Association's founding meeting, declared: the Association's aim was to unite the masses in resisting foreign aggression and saving themselves, to win China's independence, unity, and territorial integrity, and to strengthen support for the righteous armies fighting Japan. Not long after, the Shanghai concession authorities searched the Association's headquarters and arrested its speaker in Pudong. Afterward Yang Quan gave a statement exposing the concession authorities' collusion with China's reactionary forces in crushing the people's anti-Japanese patriotic movement, and said too that "the Association's central task is to buy arms and arm the masses." This of course touched Chiang Kai-shek's raw nerve; he would by no means tolerate it, and so on the one hand searched out and banned, and on the other resolved to cut the grass and pull up the root.

In March 1933 Chiang Kai-shek personally ordered Yang Quan killed. This criminal task was first put to Xu Enzeng's Zhongtong agents, and Xu Enzeng handed it to the renegade Gu Shunzhang, now an action-squad chief, to carry out. It is said the Zhongtong sent some twenty agents to Shanghai for it. Lin Chengyin, an agent just graduated from the second class of the agent-training school Gu Shunzhang ran for the Zhongtong, was assigned by Gu Shunzhang to go to Shanghai with the renegade Cai Fei (nicknamed "Old White-Face") and take part. After the country's liberation Lin Chengyin said: "One day in early March 1933 Gu Shunzhang suddenly called me in and said to me: 'You are still young, and though you've had three months' training you have no practical experience, so this time I'm sending you to Shanghai with Cai Fei to carry out the assassination of Yang Xingfo—do everything as Cai Fei directs. If you fail, don't come back to see me.' Hearing all at once that Gu Shunzhang was sending me to kill a man frightened me stiff; I dared not refuse, still less resist, and only fear filled my whole soul. I walked out of Gu Shunzhang's room in silence, and the whole day I could neither eat nor sleep. The next day I had to steel myself and go to Shanghai with Cai Fei. Who Yang Xingfo was, I had no idea, and only learned later that he was an executive member and secretary-general of the 'China League for the Protection of Civil Rights.' … Cai Fei was a Shanghainese and had done secret-service work with Gu Shunzhang in Shanghai all along, so Shanghai was very familiar to him. After we got off the train, Cai Fei lodged me in a downstairs room at No. 74 Joffre Terrace on Avenue Joffre (now Jinling East Road) in the French Concession, and then left. I began by staying indoors all day and seldom going out; Cai Fei was out and about all day and seldom came here to sleep. … They knocked about in Shanghai a while, the assassination of Yang Quan came to nothing, and the Zhongtong agents who had come to Shanghai all went back to Nanjing." "So the Zhongtong's plot to murder Yang Xingfo was dropped. Yang Xingfo was, however, killed by the Juntong in the end."

Lin Jinsheng, Gu Shunzhang's bodyguard, also spoke of Gu Shunzhang's hand in the case: "The assassination of Yang Xingfo, secretary-general of the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights, was long premeditated by the Zhongtong. Gu Shunzhang had ordered Cai Fei to take agents and carry it out. They found a house near Yang's residence on Avenue du Roi Albert in Shanghai and watched Yang's movements day after day, waiting their chance, but never brought it off; in the end Yang Xingfo was killed by agents Dai Li sent." At that time Ding Ling, secretly abducted by Gu Shunzhang and taken under guard to Nanjing, was shown a photograph of Yang Xingfo by a young Zhongtong agent guarding her, who secretly took it out "and said the Kuomintang meant to kill him. Their investigation section sent twenty men to Shanghai, and he went too, but they did not succeed. All were called back, and each had a month's pay docked. Now the Renaissance Society (the writer's note: that is, the Juntong) people have gone again."

Shen Zui (alias Chen Lun), then head of the French Concession intelligence group of the Juntong's Shanghai special section and a party to Yang Quan's assassination, said: "Dai Li was ordered to kill Yang in April or May 1933. … The one charged with carrying out the killing was Zhao Lijun, chief of the East China action group, aliases Zhao Lijun and Tao Shineng, a Sichuanese, a graduate of the fifth Whampoa class, who had directed Juntong action work in Shanghai for years and served during the War of Resistance as chief of the Action Section of the action department of Juntong headquarters. Zhao himself lodged above the Defeng Russian Restaurant in a lane along the middle stretch of Avenue Joffre in the French Concession. The deputy group chief, Wang Kequan, and several action men lodged in a three-story building on Rue Maresca in the French Concession, where the group office was also set up. The six assassins in this action took an oath beforehand to 'succeed or die trying'—should any be caught, he was to kill himself at once and not leak anything, on pain of severe punishment." Shen Zui said further: "The agents first watched Yang's daily movements and learned that Yang himself lived above Academia Sinica; Yang was fond of riding, kept two fine horses on Great Western Road, and every free morning would ride about the Great Western Road and Zhongshan Road area for an hour or two. The agents reckoned this the best and surest chance to shoot Yang. But just as they were preparing, Chiang Kai-shek would not agree to act in that area, for to assassinate Yang outside the concessions would neither achieve the aim of intimidating Soong (the writer's note: meaning Song Qingling) nor spare the reactionary government trouble, and, fearing censure from every side, would force the case to be solved; so he insisted it be done near Soong's residence in the French Concession, which would both display the agents' strength and let the case go unsolved without blame. Dai Li had to change the plan and decided to set up near Academia Sinica, ready to strike when Yang went out for a walk or on his way to Soong's residence." At 8:15 on the morning of June 18, 1933, Yang Quan was shot dead before the door of Academia Sinica. Shen Zui said: "This was the first bloody crime the agents under Dai Li committed within the Shanghai concessions, and for years after it was the 'masterpiece' the Juntong agents loved best to dwell on."

This assassination shook the whole country, and Shanghai opinion was in an uproar. The next day every paper carried the news in a prominent place. Shen Bao ran a long report setting down the scene of the agents' crime: Yang Quan had gone out for a drive at 8:15 the previous morning with his eldest son, Yang Xiaofo. "Father and son got in, and no sooner were they seated than the driver, Xiangdu, started the motor. The car had just come out the gate of Academia Sinica when four short-jacketed ruffians rushed out from beside the road, drew Mauser pistols, ringed the car on both sides, and fired, the bullets falling like rain. The driver Xiangdu took two shots first in the chest, but being of very strong build, though wounded, at once threw open the door and dashed out, sprang onto another car, and was rushed to the Red Cross Hospital on Haige Road for treatment. In that desperate instant, Yang, in his love for his son, threw his whole body over Xiaofo to shield him. The two ruffians, one on each side of the car, went on firing at Yang; three shots struck vital spots, and he fell in the carriage and died at once. Xiaofo, shielded by Yang, took only one bullet in the right leg, a light wound—a lucky thing. Seeing their aim achieved, the assassins scattered and fled. Then, 'shots crackling, the neighbors round about all heard the alarm and came out to look, and watched the assassins escape at their leisure, none daring to go to the rescue. A Russian named Peike, an employee of the Liuhan Motor Company at No. 362 opposite Academia Sinica, ran down from upstairs and, amid the flying bullets, threw himself forward to the door of Yang's car and looked in, and saw Yang already fallen in a pool of blood in the carriage, his son beneath him. Peike asked Yang what on earth had brought this about, but Yang, too gravely wounded, could only be heard gasping and could not answer, his state already most critical. Peike, heedless of all, shut the carriage door, drove the car himself, and took it to Guangci Hospital on Rue du Père Robert. … On reaching Guangci Hospital, the doctors there, Bokishi and Wu Can, examined Yang and found him already dead, washed and bound him, and sent him to the hospital mortuary. Young Master Xiaofo, wounded in the right thigh where the bullet had only grazed, was not badly hurt; Dr. Luo Zhong at once treated him and sent him to a ward. By Dr. Wu Can's account to reporters, Yang was struck by three bullets in all: one entered from the left ribs and passed clean through the right ribs; one struck the left waist; one struck the tip of the heart in the chest—it was this bullet that killed Yang.'"

The Kuomintang government's savage assassination of Yang Quan roused the fury of every member of the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights and of progressives at large. Lu Xun, like Yang Quan a founder and executive member of this League, had likewise long been on the Kuomintang agents' blacklist, a target of their manhunt too. In the terror-stricken air where each feared for himself, he ran a very great risk and went in person, with Song Qingling, Cai Yuanpei, and others, to take part in the encoffining of his dead friend Yang Quan at the International Funeral Parlor on June 20. Feng Xuefeng recalled in Remembering Lu Xun: "On the day of Mr. Yang Xingfo's encoffining, the Kuomintang Fascists again spread threatening word that they would that very day assassinate others of the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights, above all Mr. Cai Yuanpei and Mr. Lu Xun. But Mr. Lu Xun went to see him off without the least hesitation." Xu Guangping said: "… Lu Xun was set on going to the encoffining; many urged him not to, fearing danger, but Lu Xun was resolved to go, and handed me all his keys, to show that if he met with harm he would not come back." Coming home from the funeral parlor, with boundless indignation and deep, deep grief, Lu Xun wrote from the heart the moving poem "Mourning Yang Quan":

{p} Where is the high-hearted feeling of old days? / Flowers open, flowers fall, and both take their course. / Who would have thought I would shed tears in the Jiangnan rain, / and weep once more for a stout son of the people?

Not only was Lu Xun already on the Kuomintang agents' blacklist—the agents had set about assassinating him. Half a century later, Shen Zui, the noted Kuomintang Juntong assassin who had been ordered to carry out this criminal work in those days, told this "secret never disclosed" to Zhou Haiying. In his book Lu Xun and I Over Seventy Years, Zhou Haiying set down a talk Shen Zui had with him in 1992: "He said that in about 1933 he received an order from above to form a surveillance group to assassinate my father. In the end they watched from the building opposite for many days, and he went several times too, only ever seeing my father writing at his desk—you were still small, playing in the room—and could see nothing out of the ordinary. It was because of my father's prestige that they held off and withdrew. Otherwise, he said, I would have wronged you, and it would have made an irreparable tragedy." From this one can understand still better the peril of Mr. Lu Xun's position at the time and his high, righteous nobility of character.

Song Qingling was chair of the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights, and that May she and the vice-chair, Cai Yuanpei, and the secretary-general, Yang Quan, all received many ultimatum-like threatening letters: if they did not disband the Civil Rights League and stop "interfering" with the Nanjing reactionary government's arbitrary arrests and killings, they would be put to death. They laughed it off, and the League worked on as before. Israel Epstein, in his book Song Qingling: A Great Woman of the Twentieth Century, said: Chiang Kai-shek and his kind resolved to assassinate Yang Quan, whom his intimidation had not cowed, "so as not only to destroy the Civil Rights League but also to warn Song Qingling that she would be the next target. To drive the warning home, they even sent her a 'classic' token symbol—a bullet." Shen Zui, the Juntong agent who took part in this assassination, also said Yang Quan was killed on Chiang Kai-shek's personal order: "Chiang Kai-shek's chief reason for deciding to kill Yang then was to intimidate Miss Song Qingling. From early 1933, when the Civil Rights League Soong had founded was set up and Yang Xingfo became its executive member and secretary-general, the two of them were hated by Chiang. Since to strike directly at Soong carried too many misgivings, he decided to kill a 'suitable' man to terrorize Soong. Add to this that in the spring of 1933 Yang made another trip to north China and the like, not only trumpeting everywhere the sacred task of the Civil Rights League but waging firm struggle against Chiang Kai-shek's every lawless contempt for human rights, and strongly urging an end to the civil war and united resistance to Japan. This ran wholly counter to Chiang Kai-shek's policy, and was the chief cause of his death."

In the face of Chiang Kai-shek's shameless intimidation, Song Qingling's answer was to issue a stern statement striking back with force:

These men, and the assassins they hire, imagine that by violence, kidnapping, torture, and murder alone they can strangle the faintest struggle for freedom. This is the weapon by which they rule the people, and it lays bare the very face of their whole regime. The China League for the Protection of Civil Rights stands for just such a movement for freedom, and Yang Quan was cruelly killed for nothing but his activity in this body.

But we, far from being crushed, are made only the firmer by the price Yang Quan paid out of sympathy for freedom to struggle on, again and again, until we reach the goal we ought to reach. Let the executioners who killed Yang Quan understand: political crimes will surely bring them the punishment they deserve.

Many years later Shen Zui recounted in detail how the Juntong agents killed Yang Quan. He laid bare not only the inhuman savagery of the Kuomintang agents but also the dark inner workings of mutual deceit and mutual slaughter among them: "The agents had meant to act on the morning of the 17th, but the assassins, arriving near Academia Sinica, ran straight into a French police patrol car, and then a relief squad of police passed by, so they dared not act and slipped back separately. At past six on the morning of the 18th Zhao Lijun again led the assassins Li Ada, Guo Decheng, Shi Yunzhi, and others there. Zhao himself sat in the car, which was parked at the corner where Avenue du Roi Albert turns into Rue Massenet. Li Ada, Guo Decheng, and two others, four in all, spread out near Academia Sinica, with one man keeping watch at each end. Around eight they saw Yang Xingfo come with his son Xiaofo into the yard to get into the car, and the agents drew near the door. After Yang and the others had got in they came down again a moment later, and the agents, thinking Yang had noticed, were about to rush in when Yang led the child up onto another car. As the car drove slowly out the gate at No. 32 Avenue du Roi Albert, four pistols fired at once into it. At the sound of the guns Mr. Yang knew at once they meant to kill him. Since more than a month before he had received several threatening letters and a bullet the agents sent him, warning him again and again, and he had not only ignored it but grown even more active than before, though he had not foreseen that the agents would truly dare do him this deadly harm. At this life-and-death moment Yang knew he could not escape, but in his love for his son he at once threw his body over Xiaofo to shield him. So the agents, firing more than ten shots, killed only Yang and the driver, while Xiaofo, hit only in the leg, was spared. Seeing their aim achieved, the assassins dashed for the car parked nearby and scrambled aboard. At the sound of the shots Zhao Lijun had already ordered the driver to start. Just then Guo Decheng, having run the wrong way in the confusion, was still far off when he turned back to chase the car, running and shouting 'Wait for me.' Seeing him several yards short, and with police whistles now shrilling all around, Zhao could not stop to wait; and fearing besides that Guo would leak information if caught, he fired a shot at him from the car, missed a vital spot in the haste, and drove off at full throttle. This assassin, having just done the job, was shot by his own side against all expectation. Still he struggled to flee for his life, but as the police closing in from every side drew near, knowing he could not escape, he had to raise his pistol and kill himself. One bullet passed through the side of his chest; in agony he fell, yet did not die, and was taken by the police and sent at once, with Yang and his son, to Guangci Hospital on Rue du Père Robert for emergency care. Mr. Yang died soon after reaching the hospital, too gravely wounded to be saved.

"Guo Decheng, after emergency treatment, could speak by that afternoon. Questioned by men the police sent, he gave his name as Gao Dechen (his alias after joining the Juntong), a Shandong man come to Shanghai to seek relatives, and so on, but dared not yet tell the truth. Dai Li, hearing that Yang had been shot dead, was delighted, but on hearing that Guo Decheng had been caught and had given his alias, he was furious, and at once notified Fan Guangzhen, a Juntong agent serving as a Chinese detective at the French Concession police station, telling him to bring a packet of poison and approach Guo through his police connections. That night this assassin too was made into a case of 'wounds too grave to treat' and died. The next day, when the papers ran the story of Yang's murder, they named only the assassin Gao Dechen, and said that in shooting Yang, the four assassins had fired at one another and Gao was caught only after being hit by a comrade's shot, and so on. On the evening of the 20th, Dai Li returned to Nanjing well pleased, to report to Chiang Kai-shek and claim his reward. Over Guo Decheng's death, besides showing a hypocritical grief, Dai Li gave his family a solatium, and Guo Decheng's son was raised at the Juntong's charge all along. During the War of Resistance, Dai Li even wished to use this 'spirit of Guo Decheng'—'unable to escape after finishing the task, killing himself to keep faith'—to teach his agent-trainees, and time and again, in his 'spirit talks' to the trainees at Chongqing and elsewhere, he praised him; and later, when roads were built within the grounds of SACO in Chongqing, he named one of them 'Guo Decheng Road.'"
