## The Life of Zhang Xiaolin

{d} By Zhu Jianliang and Xu Weizhi

Zhang Xiaolin was one of the leaders of the Shanghai gangs, ranked together with Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng as the "three big bosses" of Shanghai. His original given name was Xiaolin; his milk-name was Ah Hu; he later changed his name to Yin and took Xiaolin as his style-name. He was born in 1877 (the third year of the Qing Guangxu reign), a native of Cixi county in Zhejiang. His father was a carpenter who died young, and the whole family lived by the mother's labor, in hardship.

In 1897, when Zhang was twenty, finding it hard to get by in the countryside, the family moved to Gongchen Bridge in Hangzhou prefecture. Once in Hangzhou he entered a Hangzhou weaving workshop together with his elder brother Zhang Dalin to learn silk-weaving; but he was idle and shiftless, would not work in earnest, and kept company only with hooligans and local ruffians. Before long he quit the trade and tested his way into the Hangzhou Military Preparatory School, where he became close friends with a fellow student, Zhang Zaiyang, and others — the origin of his later dealings with the warlords. Afterward he took as his "teacher" a foreman (a detective-head) named Li Xiutang at the Hangzhou prefectural yamen; and from then on, besides running errands for Li, he made his living around Gongchen Bridge by picking fights, extortion, and gambling. At that time there was another gambling sharp in the Gongchen Bridge area, nicknamed "West Lake Treasure," who came to hate Zhang on the grounds that Zhang was forever luring away his gamblers. Once, "West Lake Treasure" gathered the gamblers under him to fight Zhang Xiaolin; outnumbered, Zhang could no longer stay at Gongchen Bridge and had to hide elsewhere with his fellows, making a living by manufacturing and selling loaded dominoes, lead-weighted dice, and other rigged gambling gear.

Zhang Xiaolin not only gambled and cheated people of money in the city of Hangzhou; each year, at the spring silkworm market and the autumn rice harvest, he would go out into the Hangzhou-Jiaxing-Huzhou region to entice peasants into gambling and swindle them of their money. Of the country peasants taken in by his cheating, some lost until they had pawned and sold everything they owned, and some, having lost, drowned or hanged themselves; popular indignation ran very high. In the Xuantong years at the close of the Qing, the people of the Hangzhou-Jiaxing-Huzhou area once submitted a written complaint against him; but Zhang Xiaolin, relying on his acquaintances in the yamen to tip him off, managed to stay beyond the reach of the law.

In 1912, when Ji Yunqing, a well-known hooligan of Shanghai's British Concession, went to Hangzhou to engage star opera performers to come and perform in Shanghai, he met Zhang Xiaolin; the two hit it off at once and became bosom friends. Zhang thereupon followed Ji to Shanghai, where around Mantingfang on Fifth Avenue (today's Guangdong Road) he lived off shaking down the gambling dens and taking cuts from the brothels, and around Daxing Street on Fourth Avenue (today's Fuzhou Road) he set up tea gatherings, dealing exclusively in pimping, rigged gambling, the trafficking of people, the forcing of decent women into prostitution, and the like.

To gain a firm footing in Shanghai and build up his hooligan power, Zhang Xiaolin took Fan Jincheng, of the "Da" generation of the Green Gang, as his "old man," becoming a member of the gang's "Tong" generation. He then took on disciples wholesale and expanded his power. Because he was burly of build, thick-armed and strong, vicious and ruthless, he took to likening himself to Zhang Zuolin, the warlord of the Fengtian clique, and his disciples too called him "Marshal Zhang."

At that time the hooligans of the Shanghai Bund each lorded it over his own patch, contending with one another for supremacy. Once, to seize the right to move fruit at the docks, Zhang Xiaolin came to blows with the Guangdong-gang hooligans; after a full day of melee, Zhang at last wrested away the transport monopoly. Later he, together with the Nanshi hooligans Fan Kaitai (nicknamed "Ebony Kaitai"), Fan Huichun (nicknamed "Ivory Huichun"), Su Jiashou, and others, fell to blows with Jin Tingsun and other hooligans of the French Concession over the right to move opium. Jin Tingsun was the favorite disciple of Huang Jinrong, at that time chief inspector of the French Concession police, while Zhang Xiaolin had private ties with the Zhejiang governor Zhang Zaiyang and the military governor Lu Yongxiang. The Songhu Defense Commissioner of the day, He Fenglin, had originally been a subordinate of Lu Yongxiang, and so Zhang Xiaolin struck up a connection with him. Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng, wanting to make use of Zhang's warlord connections, had no wish to fall out with him; and Zhang, wanting to expand his power in the French Concession, had no wish to sour things with Huang and Du. So they invited the "Da"-generation elders of the Green Gang in Shanghai to come forward and mediate, and reached an agreement to the effect that "ill-gotten money is to be shared out fairly," with all the concessions and all the gangs cooperating and running opium jointly. On one occasion Huang Jinrong was imprisoned for having offended Lu Xiaojia, son of the warlord Lu Yongxiang; Zhang, drawing on his warlord connections, joined with Du Yuesheng in working to rescue him and secured Huang's release, for which Huang was deeply grateful. From then on the three men — Huang, Du, and Zhang — became sworn brothers.

In 1920, Huang, Zhang, and Du formally went into partnership running opium in relay. Zhang relied on He Fenglin's men to move the opium from the mouth of the Wusong River down to Shiliupu, whence Du Yuesheng's "Little Eight-Gang" band contracted to carry it into the French Concession, where Huang Jinrong issued the "passes." In this way the three hooligan bosses — Huang, Du, and Zhang — became the heads of Shanghai's notorious drug-trafficking ring and grew immensely rich on the traffic. The Sanxin Company was the headquarters from which they retailed opium, and it brought enormous income to the gang bosses, the warlords, and the concession authorities alike. In 1924, Huang Jinrong took two mu of land he held on Rue Wagner (today's Ninghai West Road) in the French Concession and made a gift of it to the Zhang and Du households, where two Western-style houses were built; Zhang lived in the east house and Du in the west, the two families coming and going through a single main gate, as close as one family.

In early April 1927, Chiang Kai-shek, preparing to betray the revolution openly in Shanghai, directed the Shanghai gang bosses to appropriate the name of the "Mutual Progress Society" from the days of the 1911 Revolution and organize a "China Mutual Progress Society," marshaling Shanghai's Green and Red Gangs to serve as the shock troops of counter-revolution. Zhang Xiaolin was one of the three chief leaders of this body. On April 12, Huang, Zhang, and Du directed hooligans disguised as workers to set out from the concessions and attack the Shanghai workers' pickets; and with the cooperation of the Kuomintang army, they brought about the April 12 massacre that shocked China and the world. Afterward, to reward them, Chiang Kai-shek appointed Huang, Zhang, and Du major-general councillors of the General Headquarters of the National Revolutionary Army; and so Zhang Xiaolin struck up connections with the new warlords as well. Huang, Zhang, and Du also swore brotherhood with the Kuomintang's Wang Bailing, Chen Qun, Yang Hu, and others, by way of cementing the tie.

In 1930, Zhang Xiaolin "returned in glory to his native place," causing a stir for a time throughout the city of Hangzhou. Local bullies and bad gentry, reactionary politicians, township and village heads, and local ruffians and hooligans threw banquets to welcome him home; and a shameless literary man writing under the pen-name Mei Wusheng presented Zhang with the name "Yin" and the style-name Xiaolin — Yin because Zhang's zodiac sign was the tiger, and Xiaolin, "a tiger roaring in the depths of the forest," to advertise his ferocity. Thereafter the bandits, thugs, local ruffians, and hooligans of the Hangzhou-Jiaxing-Huzhou region flocked to take their place in Zhang's following. Zhang built a villa on the shore of Hangzhou's West Lake, opened the Tianran Hotel on Hubin Road, and bought up great quantities of good farmland, cruelly exploiting the peasants.

In 1931, Zhang and Du Yuesheng and others jointly opened a gambling house at No. 181 Avenue Foch (today's Yan'an Middle Road), using it to rake in money. They also invested the huge sums got by illegal means in banks and in industrial and commercial enterprises, transforming themselves at a stroke from hooligans into "industrialists." Members of the Kuomintang ruling clique, such as H. H. Kung and T. V. Soong, likewise had dealings with them. Some among Shanghai's financial, industrial, and commercial circles — factory owners and shopkeepers — sought them out as backers and asked them to lend their names. In 1932, on Du Yuesheng's recommendation, Zhang became a supervisor of the Shanghai Chinese Merchants' Cotton Yarn and Cloth Exchange. Zhang Xiaolin's "fiftieth-birthday celebration," the completion of Huang Jinrong's "Huang Family Garden," and the completion of Du Yuesheng's "Du Family Ancestral Hall" all became sensations of the Shanghai Bund of the day.

After the 1930s, Du Yuesheng's power in Shanghai, and his ties with political circles, both came to surpass those of Huang and Zhang, which made Zhang intensely jealous. In 1935, when Chiang Kai-shek brought in the legal-tender policy, H. H. Kung and T. V. Soong tipped off Du Yuesheng in advance; Du did not pass the news to Zhang, but stirred up the exchange on his own account and made a great fortune, at which Zhang was deeply resentful. What was more, because Zhang was a miserly man, whereas Du had a gift for being generous with what belonged to others, Zhang's disciples one after another changed their allegiance and went over to Du. On top of this, after Zhang's son Zhang Fayao came home at the end of 1927 from studying in France, Zhang asked Du to present the young man to Chiang Kai-shek, but he never won Chiang's regard, and Zhang blamed Du for being unwilling to help. For all these reasons, friction began to grow between Zhang and Du — outwardly harmonious, inwardly at odds — and Zhang's relations with Chiang, too, gradually cooled.

After the War of Resistance against Japan broke out in 1937, on the eve of Shanghai's fall Chiang Kai-shek directed Du Yuesheng to have Huang Jinrong and Zhang Xiaolin go with Du to Hong Kong. Huang, being old and sickly and no longer troubling himself with outside affairs, decided not to go. Zhang reckoned that with Du gone to Hong Kong and Huang withdrawn from affairs, here was just his chance to lean on Japanese power and lord it over Shanghai alone, and so decided to stay. When the August 13 Shanghai fighting began, Zhang was summering at Moganshan, and the Japanese secret-service organ at once sent men hurrying there to hold secret talks with him. After Shanghai fell, Zhang returned to the city and set his disciples to acting as traitors.

Around 1939, at the instigation of the Japanese secret-service organ, Zhang Xiaolin organized a "New Asia Peace Promotion Association," gathering his disciples to go out into the surrounding countryside and buy up and haul for the Japanese army such urgently needed supplies as coal, rice, and cotton, thus serving Japanese imperialism in the provisioning of military materiel. With the backing of the Japanese invaders, Zhang's business grew larger and larger, and he seized the moment to make himself hugely rich.

At the end of 1939, Zhang Xiaolin and the Japanese secret-service organ were secretly scheming to set up a puppet Zhejiang provincial government, with Zhang to serve as puppet governor. When Kuomintang agents got wind of this, they resolved to have him killed.

On January 15, 1940, Zhang Xiaolin's trusted confidant Yu Yefeng — the man in charge of the cotton-supply-to-the-enemy work within the "New Asia Peace Promotion Association" — invited Zhang to the Gengxin Stage to watch a play; Zhang, with urgent business come up, did not go, and in the event Yu was assassinated while watching. Zhang, though he had the luck to escape the plot, was frightened out of his wits. That same year, on one occasion when Zhang went out by car, his automobile had just reached the vicinity of Avenue Foch when a red light suddenly barred the way, and a volley of bullets came flying at his car, punching three holes clean through the glass; his driver stamped hard on the accelerator, ran the red light, and Zhang Xiaolin thus escaped death.

After these two incidents, Zhang shut his door and did not go out, and hired more than twenty bodyguards of consummate skill and deadly aim, with Japanese military police standing watch around his residence day and night. Later, Kuomintang agents bought over Zhang's personal bodyguard Lin Huaibu with a heavy bribe. On August 14, 1940, while Zhang Xiaolin was on the third floor discussing matters with Wu Jingguan, director of the puppet Hangzhou Tinfoil Bureau, Lin Huaibu deliberately fell to quarreling loudly downstairs with Zhang's driver; when Zhang stuck his head out of an upstairs window to demand what the matter was, Lin drew his pistol and shot him, putting an end to the evil life of this local ruffian, hooligan, and traitor.
