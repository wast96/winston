### Pheasant Universities

In recent years every sort of enterprise has been caught in the tide of the slump, showing everywhere the signs of retrenchment and decline; only the founding of this or that university and this or that college goes on sprouting up lustily like bamboo after rain. Just look: every time the enrollment season comes round, open a newspaper and glance through it, and the universities and colleges are so many you cannot record them all, cannot count them all.

The universities being so many, it might seem on the surface a proof of how our country's education flourishes, of how many now seek advanced learning; but under the skin it is not so simple, for onto this sort of no-good university people tack the two words "pheasant," and it becomes a "pheasant university." Now a university is the highest seat of learning, holy and grave, the eyes of all the people upon it — how can it be sneered at as a "pheasant university"? Is that not a crime? The truth is that the men who run this sort of university are, in their aims, exactly like a stock exchange, business being their whole object: they take the greatest pains to make the buildings handsome and roomy, they set the fees extravagantly high, and toward the students they adopt the plain principle of laissez-faire, so that both discipline and instruction are done anyhow, boarding students may go and lodge outside, roll-call at lectures may be answered by a stand-in, and how a student fares in his studies is no concern of theirs. When the course is done, they hand him — anyhow again — a mortarboard and a diploma, and reckon their duty discharged. Such is the whole of their inner workings, and no wonder people call them "pheasant universities" — a thing to sigh over indeed! Though of the universities and colleges of all Shanghai, those excellent in their results and strict in their management are certainly not few, and it is by no means all of a piece — a point the writer wishes to make plain.

One day, in the "tea-talk" column of the Xinwenbao, a cub reporter's piece titled "Save This College Student" opened with the words: "The universities of today are truly a pit one falls into." Two lines that may be called spoken with feeling indeed.
