### Smoked Fish and Crisp Candy

The shop-signs of the city's confectioners used, in former days, all to read "Daoxiangcun" or "Yebiqi"; nowadays they read "Lao Dafang" or "Tianlu," with only a mark added above to tell one from another. Of the wares a confectioner sells there are, at the least, a good few hundred kinds, yet upon two of them—smoked fish and crisp candy—they lavish a particular care: over and above writing "Flavour-Through Smoked Fish" on the hanging sign, they set up beside the counter a gilt-lettered, vermilion-lacquered board bearing the two great characters "Smoked Fish" (some write "Crisp Candy" instead); from which one may see how much store they set by these two things.

But why should they neglect their other wares and lavish care on just these two? There is a reason. It is because the Shanghai man puts all his effort into the one word "eat." Smoked fish is savoury, and does as well with a bowl of gruel, with wine, or with a meal; buy two *mao* or four *mao* of it and it will gladden the palate—so the confectioner, to fall in with his customer's fancy, cannot but lavish care upon his smoked fish. Crisp candy, being sweet, will neither wash down wine nor go with gruel; but the addicts of Shanghai are many, and every one of them loves his sweets: lying on the couch, having taken his fill of opium, he eats a few pieces of crisp candy, the bitter tempered with the sweet, and finds the savour of it endless—and so the crisp-candy trade in the confectioners' shops flourishes wonderfully too. They have set up the great gilt-lettered, vermilion-lacquered board with the very aim of spurring the addicts on to come and do more and more business.
