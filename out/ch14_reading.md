## Born at Last

The first work Hijikata Toshizō and Kondō threw themselves into, once they had come up to Kyoto, was the killing of Kiyokawa.

An assassination, of course.

If it leaked out who had done the killing, then the plan that was to follow—the forming of a new party by a secret compact between the Kondō and Serizawa factions—would be made difficult.

The eight of the Kondō faction strolled the Mibu quarter idly, day after day, and spied out Kiyokawa's comings and goings.

The five of the Serizawa faction lent great help in this as well; but their leader, Serizawa Kamo, was a coarse, rough man, ill-suited to such fine-grained scouting work.

"Kondō,"

{j} Serizawa would come tramping into Kondō's room almost every day, and,

{j} "tiresome work, this,"

{j} he would say. He was short of temper.

He was forever with the smell of drink about him.

As he talked, it was his habit to slap his knee, whack on whack, with a great iron war-fan. The fan was carved with a phrase:

{j} —To spend one's loyalty to the last, and requite the nation.

{j} It was a slogan that had lately come into fashion in Mito.

"Better yet, what do you say,"

{j} Serizawa pressed eagerly.

"By some dark night we burst into that Kiyokawa Hachirō's lodging and, allowing him no word one way or the other, hack him down at a stroke—"

"Now, that is—"

"A capital scheme, is it not,"

"A hero indeed you are, Master,"

Kondō, with desperate effort, spoke the flattering words. To have Serizawa Kamo act rashly now would smash everything to pieces.

"But you must restrain yourself. And by the way, the reply from your honored elder brother seems slow in coming."

"Ah—the matter of making a connection to the Protector."

"Just so."

"I went again yesterday. My brother says the answer ought to come any day now. Once we have it, Kondō, the capital is yours and mine to command."

"I am nothing but a blockhead,"

{j} Kondō paid the compliment with a pained face.

"But you, Master, will become the first patriot of the capital."

"Flattery will get you nowhere,"

"Do you take me for a man who deals in honeyed words and fair faces?"

"There is that, too,"

{j} and Serizawa's face loosened.

Painful as it was, Kondō had to lay on flattery to the utmost. This was the design drawn up by Toshizō, the man behind the curtain. Until the thing was done, the man Serizawa Kamo was, come what may, necessary.

—Look here, Kondō.

{j} Toshizō had impressed it on him.

—If Serizawa tells you to, you must lick the very soles of his feet. For now the one thing is to keep the man in good humor.

As was told in the last chapter, Serizawa's blood kin were of good standing: his own elder brother was a retainer of the Mito Tokugawa house and, conveniently, served as the domain's liaison officer in Kyoto—a diplomat posted to the capital. Through that brother they hoped to be put in touch with the liaison office of the Kyoto Protector, the Aizu Middle Captain Matsudaira Katamori, and so to obtain a secret sanction:

—You may put Kiyokawa Hachirō to death.

{j} Such was the thing they wished for. That, at this juncture, was the Kondō faction's moment of truth.

By Toshizō's reckoning, the shogunate's own council would surely be incensed at Kiyokawa's uncanny turning of coat. (In this he was right: just as Toshizō had guessed, the senior councilor Itakura Suō-no-kami had secretly charged Sasaki Tadasaburō, a shogunal retainer and instructor at the Kōbusho, with Kiyokawa's assassination.) And so the Kyoto Protector, the shogunate's watch-office in the capital, would naturally take no pleasure in Kiyokawa. Of this there could be no doubt whatever.

(The secret sanction will come, without fail.)

{j} So judging, Toshizō, while setting Serizawa to work the connection, pressed ahead with his plan to kill Kiyokawa.

Sure enough, it fell out as drawn.

It was the next day.

There came to Serizawa, from a shrewd man—Toshima Kihee, a liaison officer to the Kyoto Protector Matsudaira Katamori—a single word.

—I must see you.

So the message ran; and with it came the careful caution that, since there were eyes about, it be kept private.

"With this, the thing is as good as done,"

{j} Toshizō said to Kondō. That nameless rōnin swordsmen should gain a connection to the Kyoto Protector—an authority greater than the shogunal council in Edo—was, in itself, no small harvest.

"So it is,"

{j} The blood had risen in Kondō's cheeks as well. From sheer joy, perhaps,

{j} "Toshi, this we ought to send word home about. We surely should,"

{j} he said. Their mutual sworn friend, the Hino headman Satō Hikogorō, would be glad of it. Even since they had reached Kyoto, Hikogorō, saying "your expenses must run high," had been sending money by express courier. A good friend is a thing worth having.

The next day they set out. To the corps they gave out, for form's sake, that it was "to see the sights of the city."

The party turned east out of Mibu. Its company was—

Kondō Isami, Hijikata Toshizō.

Serizawa Kamo, Niimi Nishiki.

—Four men in all.

That these four would, some months hence, become the men who set Kyoto trembling, not even the four themselves can have guessed.

By the time they reached the Aizu headquarters at Kurodani, the sun was leaning a little past noon.

"Ho,"

{j} said Kondō, looking up.

A gate like a castle's, studded with iron bolts, reared up before them. Aizu headquarters though it was called, it was in fact the Konkai Kōmyō-ji, a head temple of special rank of the Jōdo sect. Yet rather than temple architecture, with a hill at its back, it resembled a fortress. More than resembled—there was a reason in it.

In the early Edo years the Tokugawa house, foreseeing the chance of a rising in Kyoto, had built—besides the formal castle, Nijō—two disguised castles. These were the Chion-in on Mount Kachō and this Kōmyō-ji at Kurodani. In these times, the shogunate's "should the worst come" had come. For that, they had garrisoned the Aizu Matsudaira domain in Kyoto. Its headquarters was that disguised castle, the Kōmyō-ji. The foresight of the Tokugawa ancestors, two hundred years and more on, had proved of use, one may say.

"Master Serizawa, a splendid headquarters, is it not,"

"Well—so it is,"

{j} Serizawa had no interest in buildings and the like.

They were shown into the great abbot's hall.

Before they had waited long, a middle-aged samurai with a sharp, keen eye appeared and, from the lower seat, made a bow.

"I am overwhelmed that you should come so far. It is I, Toshima Kihee, who serve as liaison officer. I beg you will know me from this day on,"

{j} he greeted them, in the old, formal manner proper to an Aizu samurai.

After that came the wine. Toshima Kihee, a man of taste for all that his face belied it, kept the company going single-handed—telling harmless jokes, and, in his cups, giving out an Aizu country ballad in an unexpectedly pretty voice. But Kondō, new to a gathering of this kind, had gone altogether stiff with nerves.

Toshizō too only rolled his eyes about, and never once smiled.

When it came time to take their leave, Toshima Kihee saw them as far as that same gate, and,

{j} "A merry evening, this,"

{j} he said, slowly stroking his face; then, dropping his voice all at once,

{j} "Master Kondō,"

{j} he said.

"Yes?"

"The matter of the letter K—kindly see to it."

{j} That was all he said. "K" was a covert word for Kiyokawa. With this, the Kyoto Protector had, in effect, secretly ordered Kiyokawa's assassination.

***

Kiyokawa Hachirō went out every day. He set off in the direction of the Palace.

At the Palace there was—

{j} "the Gakushūin,"

{j} a newly established office. There the ablest heads among the court nobles were chosen and set to work, to study and debate policy toward the shogunate. Yet the nobles, stripped of governance for the seven hundred years since the wars of the Genji and the Heike, had no political training whatever and not the least power of judgment of their own. In sum, it was an office that merely danced to the arguments of the "Revere-the-Emperor rōnin" who came and went there.

Kiyokawa, too, was one of these puppet-masters.

Toshizō had Kiyokawa's route studied.

(Kiyokawa the swordsman indeed.)

{j} What drew his admiration was that Kiyokawa's going and coming took a different road each day. He was guarding, no doubt, against an assassin's ambush.

From their scouting they found the one place he was sure to pass every day.

It was the corner where, south of the house of the Kujō regent, the Marutamachi avenue, running east and west, crossed the Takakura street, running north and south.

The corner was a townhouse, and stood empty.

(This is convenient.)

Toshizō put it to Kondō and Serizawa, and it was resolved to hide their men there.

"The killing must be by night, without fail,"

{j} Toshizō said to Serizawa.

"And it must be settled at a single stroke. Dawdle, and our faces will be known."

"Understood. You are a strategist,"

"And the men—keep them few,"

"I know it. I need no directing from you."

In both the Kondō and the Serizawa factions, none but the four named above knew of this plot. And so the killing, too, could only be done by the four of them.

The four were split into two pairs.

Kondō Isami, Niimi Nishiki.

Serizawa Kamo, Hijikata Toshizō.

These two pairs would lie hidden in the empty house by turns. That the pairings were deliberately not made within a single faction was because, should Kiyokawa's head be taken, it would otherwise become the one-sided credit of one faction or the other. To that length ran Toshizō's care.

The plan was put into effect.

But Kiyokawa was no fool either. Whenever he went out, he kept several stout, trusted men at either hand.

And after sundown he did not walk abroad.

"A man with not a single opening,"

{j} Even Kondō cried off at last. Day after day they lay in wait, but it was too bright all around.

Serizawa, for his part, ground his teeth.

Peering through a knothole in the board fence, Serizawa would be on the point of springing out at any moment, and Toshizō held him back with all his might.

At last the chance came.

It was the turn of the Serizawa-and-Toshizō pair. Sundown came, and still Kiyokawa had not returned from the Gakushūin.

"Tonight, it seems, our luck is in,"

{j} said Serizawa, loosing a thick stream of piss at the foot of the board fence. The spray caught, without mercy, at Toshizō's hems, but Serizawa paid it no mind.

Toshizō grimaced.

(A loathsome fellow.)

As he moved to step clear, the scene on the road, glimpsed through a gap in the fence, changed.

A cluster of paper lanterns was coming.

There was chatting and laughter.

"It's Kiyokawa,"

{j} said Toshizō.

"Here, here—let me see too,"

{j} and Serizawa peered out. As he peered,

{j} "Four of them,"

{j} he laughed. The trusted men were Ishizaka Shūzō, Ikeda Tokutarō, and Matsuno Kenji—every one of them able, sword in hand, to keep a town dōjō of his own.

"Hijikata, let me be the one to cut Kiyokawa down,"

"Then I will take on the underlings. But—one stroke, mind. And raise no battle-cry."

"You harp on it,"

{j} Serizawa, unhurried, put on the mask he had made ready. Toshizō too covered his face with black cloth, leaving only his eyes.

"Hijikata—we go,"

{j} In a flash he was out from the board fence.

Serizawa broke into a run with his sword already drawn. Toshizō too, as he ran, drew the Izumi-no-kami Kanesada.

—What's this?

{j} and the cluster of lanterns halted. From ahead, two jet-black shadows came running.

One of the shadows had absurdly loud footfalls—a showy tread, as if to set the ground ringing.

(That Serizawa...)

As he ran, Toshizō was galled by the man's carelessness.

But Kiyokawa's side, on the contrary, were reassured by this all-too-open manner of running, and,

{j} "Is there a fire somewhere, perhaps,"

{j} Ishizaka Shūzō said, so easy was he in his mind. But their leader, Kiyokawa Hachirō, saw well enough that this was no ordinary thing.

"Gentlemen, gather the lanterns and set them on the ground. Yes—two or three steps back. Wait there."

{j} he said.

Kiyokawa's handling was not wrong. Assassins spring in aiming at the lantern-light.

First, Serizawa came charging in.

He leaped the cluster of lanterns on the ground.

As he leaped he swung his sword up into a bold overhead guard, and the instant his feet touched earth, brought a single stroke down upon Kiyokawa.

Kiyokawa fell back two steps.

"Who goes there,"

{j} he said. Unshaken.

Serizawa, who would surely have liked to name himself with a flourish, held his tongue. Still silent, he pressed in two steps, three, and brought down another stroke.

Kiyokawa was taking them, blade on blade.

The "single stroke" of Toshizō's ordering had failed.

(That Serizawa—not the man his mouth makes him.)

Toshizō, springing this way and that, was cutting in a dizzying flurry at Ishizaka, Ikeda, and Matsuno; but no more time could be spent—sooner or later someone would take notice.

The moment he slipped Ishizaka Shūzō's blade, he took it as his cue and broke into a run.

Serizawa ran too.

Down the Takakura street they went south, then west along the Ebisugawa, through the Ainomachi, east along the Nijō avenue; and only when they had come near the Kyoto residence of the Kawagoe domain did they at last shake off the enemy.

"Master Serizawa—we've bungled it,"

"Hm,"

{j} Serizawa was breathing hard. Toshizō, seasoned in brawls, was in speech and bearing not one whit changed from his ordinary self.

(Full transmission in the Shintō Munen-ryū, they say, with pupils of his own raised up besides—yet nothing so very much.)

{j} Some such feeling of Toshizō's must have carried across, for Serizawa Kamo turned sullen.

"The fault is yours,"

{j} he said. Toshizō bridled.

"And what does that mean?"

"Two more passes at it and I'd have cut Kiyokawa down. But you took to your heels, and so we let the big fish slip clean away."

"That is a mistaken view. The plan from the first was: fell him at a stroke, or else withdraw. That was the agreement."

"You are a clever man,"

"I'm an unlettered one,"

"No—a clever man. Strategy, strategy, you say. In the end it is proof of a want of courage."

"What—"

From the wall of the Kawagoe mansion, the shadow of a red pine spilled down.

"Whether I want courage or no—Master Serizawa, let us put it to the test. Draw, if you please."

"You'll have at it, then?"

{j} Serizawa too drew his sword.

Just then figures rose beyond the wall.

Several came running, pell-mell. Kiyokawa's men, no doubt—so both Serizawa and Toshizō judged.

(No good.)

Shoulder to shoulder, the two of them took to flight.

The next day.—

Kiyokawa called the whole rōnin corps to assemble once more at the Shintoku-ji in Mibu.

"Gentlemen, I have joyful news for you,"

{j} said Kiyokawa.

"Our long-cherished purpose of expelling the barbarian has reached the Imperial ear, and we have received an Imperial decree besides. Our coming up to Kyoto in force has borne its fruit. But now the Namamugi affair, as you know—"

{j} he said. The Namamugi Incident was this: not long before, at Namamugi on the Tōkaidō, between Kanagawa and Tsurumi, Englishmen on horseback had cut across the procession of Satsuma's Shimazu Hisamitsu, whereupon the domain's samurai had cut one down and gravely wounded two others; and over this a diplomatic quarrel between the shogunate and England had grown tangled.

It would come to war, men said, and about Yokohama there were townsfolk who bundled up their household goods and moved away.

"It is at an impasse. Should England open hostilities, we are to be the vanguard that beats them off. Word to that effect has come from the government, and so we return to Edo in haste."

That was the shogunal council's stratagem.

Schemer though he was, even a man of Kiyokawa's cunning fell for it. After this, once back in Edo the rōnin corps was named the "Shinchōgumi," and its promoter Kiyokawa was assassinated at Akabanebashi by Sasaki Tadasaburō and his men.

At the gathering in the Shintoku-ji at Mibu,

{j} "We decline,"

{j} those who rose to say it were the faction lodged in the Yagi Gennojō house—Kondō, Serizawa, Toshizō, Niimi, and the rest. At once they walked out.

It was on the thirteenth day of the third month of the third year of Bunkyū that the rōnin corps under Kiyokawa's command left Kyoto and set off once more down the Kiso road for Edo; their stay in the capital had been a bare twenty days.

Kondō Isami's faction, eight men.

Serizawa Kamo's faction, five men.

Thirteen men in all, and only these, stayed on at their lodging in the Yagi house.

They had broken away.

"Broken away" sounds well enough, but they were now a mere band of rōnin, with no more stipend from the shogunate and no guarantee of standing whatever.

"Toshi, what are we to do,"

{j} said Kondō, at his wits' end. There was no money even for food. Rice, at least, they had wept and borrowed from the Yagi household, but they would not be fed for nothing forever.

"The Kyoto Protector,"

{j} said Toshizō. For him it was all as he had long been thinking. Through that elder brother of Serizawa's, they would work the connection to the Kyoto Protector. Once they were "rōnin in the charge of His Lordship the Aizu Middle Captain, the Kyoto Protector," they would have a proper backing, and money would come down. Above all, the legal ground for their garrison at Mibu would be established.

"A capital scheme,"

{j} Serizawa said, delighted.

"Only—Kondō—I am the supreme commander, mind."

"Of course; so I intend it,"

And no wonder. The whole approach was possible only through Serizawa's brother; and, more, the name of Serizawa Kamo of the Mito Tengu Party was one the world knew. For now there was nothing for it but to hoist Serizawa up as their signboard.

When they made their approach through that same liaison officer, Toshima Kihee, their petition was—surprisingly—granted the very same day, and the corps' name, the "Shinsengumi," was officially sanctioned as well.

"Toshi—it's like a dream,"

{j} Kondō gripped Toshizō's hand. Toshizō gently gripped back, and,

{j} "The real work begins from here,"

{j} he said. In his mind's eye was Serizawa's face.
