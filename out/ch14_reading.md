## Chapter Fourteen. The Hour of the Rat, First Half (11 p.m.)

{v} Taizhen, seeing Tanqi, was overjoyed. Long shut up in the palace, she seldom had the chance to meet an old friend from bygone days, and she caught Tanqi’s hands in hers. “Why, it has been an age since I saw you, little sister — how have you been?”

{d} The third year of Tianbao, the fifteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Rat, first half.

{d} Chang’an; Chang’an County; Guangde Ward.

Yuan Zai came back once more to the gate of the Jingzhao Prefecture, a little crestfallen.

He had gone to no small trouble to lay hands on Wen Ran, never expecting Wang Yunxiu to blunder in upon them — still less that the two of them turned out to be old acquaintances, and on the warmest of terms.

The Wolf Guards who had meant to seize Wang Yunxiu had seized Wen Ran by mistake; the Bear Fire Gang who had meant to seize Wen Ran had seized Wang Yunxiu by mistake. Two crossed blunders, each the work of some perverse turn of chance, had put the two women through their several panics and frights.

Yuan Zai understood the whole tangled business only too well, and it gave him a headache. If he forced Wen Ran away with him now, he would have to explain himself plainly to Wang Yunxiu; and any such explanation would tear a hundred holes in the story that “Zhang Xiaojing has abducted Wang Yunxiu.”

For it had to be remembered that, ordinary young woman though Wen Ran was, her affairs could be traced all the way from the Bear Fire Gang up to Prince Yong.

Wen Ran was a mere makeweight; it was Wang Yunxiu who lay at the very heart of the interests he served, and the plan aimed at the latter could on no account go wrong. Weighing the one against the other, Yuan Zai could only let Wen Ran go for the moment, and let Wang Yunxiu carry her off back to the Prince’s mansion with her.

To make sure nothing further went awry, Yuan Zai climbed into Wang Yunxiu’s carriage as well. Wen Ran was very frightened; Wang Yunxiu, though, was quite pleased — a single word from her and Yuan Zai had agreed at once, which showed that her opinion counted for much in his eyes.

Yuan Zai saw them all the way to the gate of the Prince’s mansion before turning back. He was not without a pang of regret: this perfect night had, in the end, come out with one small blemish upon it, its full success just short of complete.

“Now, the only one left is Zhang Xiaojing.”

He stepped down from the carriage deep in thought, turning over how best to lay his snares and take the most savage madman Chang’an had known since the founding of the city. Coming toward him, two men were walking out of the main gate of the Jingzhao Prefecture, and one of them looked somehow odd. Yuan Zai, ever a careful observer, narrowed his eyes and made out a Persian — who, of all things, had put on the blue-green robe of a physician.

Chang’an’s physicians’ halls had always been staffed by Tang men. Hu foreigners seldom practiced medicine, and even those who did kept private surgeries; they would never wear the blue gown of a licensed physicians’ hall. Besides, Deputy Director Ji had already issued the order to expel the Hu — how could this one still be here?

“Could it be... he is one of the attackers, slipped into the Jingzhao Prefecture?”

The thought made Yuan Zai suddenly wary, and he kept watching. The longer he looked, the more his doubts multiplied. Why was there no physician’s satchel at the man’s waist? Why did he wear a pair of rush boots rather than the leather shoes a physician favored? Most suspicious of all was the placing of the stains on that blue gown. For one had to consider: when a physician treated wounds of this kind, he generally had to bend to his work, so that the front hem was the first to be fouled with filth — yet this man’s front hem was clean, while the stains sat lower, below the chest, which was all but impossible. Unless the gown had never been his at all, but belonged to a man of shorter stature.

Yuan Zai looked again at the man’s companion. He seemed to be a patient, and there was nothing strange in his dress; only his face was smeared all over with soot, so grimed that his features could not be made out. But his gait gave Yuan Zai a shock: at nearly every step the distance was exactly the same, and the whole man moved with great steadiness.

Only one kind of man walked like that: a soldier.

Yuan Zai put it together. More than one person had said that the ruffians who stormed the great hall of the Jing’an Bureau seemed to be of military background — could these be the very men?

He gave no alarm. There were only these two here; taking them would gain little. Better to pay out the line and see whether he might land a bigger fish. Yuan Zai’s heart leapt with pleasure — his luck tonight was almost too good to be true. Might he not, in passing, roll up the Pifu’s very nest as well?

Quietly Yuan Zai called over a buliang man, whispered a few words in his ear, and secretly gave him his instructions.

***

Zhang Xiaojing and Yisi walked all the way out of the Jingzhao Prefecture, unhindered by anyone, and were quietly thankful for it.

Once outside, Yisi asked what they would do next. Zhang Xiaojing gave a shake to the sack full of broken bamboo shards and said they would go find an expert to examine them. At this Yisi jutted out his chin, unwilling to be outdone: “And who could have a keener eye than mine?”

Zhang Xiaojing raised his head and, gazing at the black smoke rising over the great hall, said with feeling: “In the great hall of the Jing’an Bureau there once stood a miniature sand-table of the whole of Chang’an — a thing wrought fine past all telling, the work of a supernatural hand. The man I am looking for is the artisan who made that sand-table.”

Zhang Xiaojing had heard something of it, in outline, from Tanqi. When Li Bi was setting up the Jing’an Bureau, he had required that a great sand-table be built in the hall, true to the whole face of Chang’an. It was a task of the highest difficulty, and not a few famous craftsmen had shrunk from it; in the end a craftsman named Chao Fen had completed the masterpiece.

The curious thing was that Chao Fen was no man of the Central Plains. He was a native of Izumo in Japan, come to Chang’an with a Japanese embassy to study the arts of the great Tang. The man had extraordinary gifts; after ten-odd years in Chang’an his skill had been honed to a consummate perfection. His master was none other than the famous Chao Heng, Vice-Minister of the Court of the Imperial Regalia — himself a Japanese.

Chao Fen lived in Zhiye Ward, not far from where they were. If anyone in all Chang’an could read the secret of this bamboo-work, it could only be Chao Fen.

The two left Guangde Ward and plunged back into the surging sea of people, and before long reached Zhiye Ward. It lay hard against the west side of the Vermilion Bird Avenue, likewise a first-rate quarter where mansion stood ranked beside mansion; at its gates the lantern-frames were close-set as fish-scales, and the bustle was tremendous.

For some reason the flower-lanterns here were fashioned with a touch more life in them than elsewhere. The scales of a golden dragon-lantern, for instance, would lift a little when the wind blew across them, so that the dragon looked as if it had come alive; the Star of Longevity held up his peach of long life, and the peach’s leaves would sway up and down, lifelike to the eye. Set against ordinary flower-lanterns these changes were in truth slight, but they showed the utmost ingenuity, with the marvel of the dot that brings a painted dragon’s eye to life.

And so the lantern-viewers about Zhiye Ward were unusually many too. Yisi fretted: “Look at these flower-lanterns — surely they must all be from the hand of that master craftsman. How could he be sitting quietly at home at such a time? Beyond doubt he prizes his own worn broom, and is off admiring them everywhere.”

Zhang Xiaojing had long given up trying to correct Yisi’s misuse of set phrases. Frowning, he said: “We do what men can, and leave the rest to Heaven.”

The two of them parted the crowd and went into the ward. Inside, many small flower-lanterns had been set out, string upon string hung down both sides of the streets, wonderfully pretty. Chao Fen counted as a man of note in this ward; a little asking soon turned up where he lived.

It was an ordinary gateway at the northeast corner of the crossroads, plain and unadorned at the door. Were it not for a lantern hung there bearing the words “the Chao Residence,” no one would ever have dared believe this was the dwelling of the master craftsman who had modeled the sand-table of the whole city of Chang’an.

Zhang Xiaojing stepped up and knocked at the door-ring. Very soon someone with the look of an apprentice opened it and said the master was inside. When they had gone in, they could not help being brought up short.

The whole courtyard was strewn with all manner of bamboo, wood, stone, and clay, till there was scarcely anywhere to set a foot. Half-finished bronze cups and wooden figures, iron kettles and porcelain pillows were heaped into little hills. To one side of the yard rose a kiln of yellow brick, blazing away, and a short man with a tiger’s back and a bear’s waist was watching the mouth of the kiln with his whole soul. Sweat beaded on his taut, bronze-colored muscles, glittering in the firelight.

Yisi was astonished. Today was the Lantern Festival, after all — for this fellow not to go out and enjoy himself, but to hole up in his own yard and work, was surely too much the eccentric.

Zhang Xiaojing took a step closer and cleared his throat. The short man paid him no more heed than the empty air, not so much as turning his head. The apprentice at his side explained in a low voice: “Once the master fixes on the kiln, he’ll go days on end without sleep or rest, and take no notice of anyone...”

Zhang Xiaojing was in no mood for such leisure. He stepped forward: “I am Zhang Xiaojing, Commander of the Jing’an Bureau, come this night to ask the master to appraise a certain thing for me.”

At the three words “the Jing’an Bureau,” Chao Fen at last turned his head. “Appraise what?” he said, indifferent.

“Broken bamboo-ends.” Zhang Xiaojing pinched the sack and gave it a shake before his eyes.

“No interest. Kindly leave.” Chao Fen refused flatly. Again the apprentice explained under his breath: “The master is just so. Lately he’s grown besotted with firing porcelain, and anything that isn’t porcelain he can’t even be bothered to look at.”

Zhang Xiaojing said: “This touches the safety of the whole city of Chang’an; the matter is as urgent as fire. You must give it your eye. This is not a request — it is an order!”

But dragging out the city of Chang’an did not move Chao Fen from his indifference. His gaze stayed fixed on the kiln-mouth, as though nothing in all heaven and earth mattered so much as the thing firing within.

In ordinary times one could not but praise such a craftsman’s single-heartedness; but now time was precious, and there was no allowing the fellow his willfulness. Zhang Xiaojing reached out to haul him around — only for Chao Fen to fling back a hand and wrench his palm clean away. Zhang Xiaojing, who prided himself on the strength of his grip, could not last a single exchange before Chao Fen.

In his many years in Chang’an, absorbed in the craftsman’s art, he had long since forged himself a pair of iron arms.

Yisi saw it and grew anxious too: “The Jing’an Bureau was stormed, the dead and wounded more than half, the deputy director carried off, the great hall burned — this is the only lead we have...” At these words Chao Fen suddenly swung his thick, fleshy neck around, a pair of tiger-eyes glaring this way: “Say that again!”

“The Jing’an Bureau was stormed, the dead and wounded more than half, the deputy director carried off—”

“The next line!”

“The great hall was burned.”

Chao Fen’s two hands shot out and seized Yisi, and Yisi felt as though he were gripped in a pair of iron pincers, utterly unable to stir. Chao Fen said in a heavy voice: “The great hall burned — then what of my sand-table?”

“Burned to ashes with it, of course.”

said Zhang Xiaojing. He had by now taken this man’s pulse. Chao Fen was a man possessed by one thing; outside the objects in his hands he cared for nothing, and to move him one had to prick him where it hurt him most.

Sure enough, the moment Chao Fen heard the sand-table was destroyed, his two bushy brows knotted together, and he grieved over it more than if the real city of Chang’an had been in peril. He let out a sudden low roar, his two iron arms released Yisi, and he brought them smashing down on a wooden board beside him — with a crack the fine cypress plank broke clean in two.

“That was lent by me to the Jing’an Bureau! I was to take it back to Japan one day, and build another Chang’an from it! Destroyed, just like that? Who — who was it that did this?”

Zhang Xiaojing seized the moment: “These bamboo-ends are an important clue to catching the culprits.” Chao Fen thrust out a great hand covered over in old calluses, his eyes blood-red: “Give them here!”

Yisi handed over the sack. Chao Fen poured out all the broken bamboo-ends and made them out one by one, while the apprentice hastily trimmed the candle-wick brighter. Chao Fen’s fingers, though short and thick, were wonderfully nimble; those fine bamboo scraps rolled and turned between them, and not a single piece fell. Then Chao Fen fetched a smoothed, transparent piece of jade, squinted one eye, and studied them.

“These fragments came from the hands of twelve different craftsmen. Each hand had a different strength to it, and the cut-marks on the bamboo run deep and shallow, no two alike.”

Yisi clucked his tongue in wonder; he prided himself on his sharp eyes, yet even he was not so formidable as Chao Fen. Chao Fen went on: “This way of paring bamboo is not of the Chang’an school — it should be somewhat farther north. Northern bamboo is slender, the knifework drawn inward; and not a few of the fragments have two layers of break at the edge, which comes of cutting amiss and having to add a second stroke — likely the work of craftsmen from about the Shuofang country.”

He was a master craftsman indeed, reading these fragments through at a glance. But Zhang Xiaojing felt a slight disappointment: none of this was of any help against the Que-le Huo-duo.

“And this one, then?” He handed over as well the bamboo piece that Yuchang had let fall.

He gave it the briefest glance, then held forth at once, fluent and assured: “Eight angles without, a groove within — see, the bamboo is flat and narrow, and there are scorch-marks too; this is the typical handiwork of the Fang school of Lingnan, with something borrowed besides from the fine touches of the Lin school of central Sichuan...” The regional traits of every craftsman in all the great Tang, Chao Fen had studied with care; before him such things could find nowhere to hide.

“Is there any connection between this one and those broken bamboo-ends?”

“All I can say is that, taken together with those loose bamboo scraps, they are all offcuts pared from the making of some large implement.”

“Can you tell who did the paring?” Zhang Xiaojing felt there was something in this.

Chao Fen shot him a look: “There are tens of thousands of craftsmen in Chang’an, and I am no fortune-teller — how should I tell?” Zhang Xiaojing was left with nothing to say, knowing his demand had indeed been too much. If the man could truly know a hand at a glance, he might as well set up as an immortal.

Chao Fen said slowly: “But I can tell you this much — what it was made for.”

He bade the apprentice fetch two lengths of raw bamboo, took up a strangely shaped long knife at random, and plied it with a will, chop after chop, swift as the wind. Zhang Xiaojing and Yisi watched: the broken bamboo pieces falling to the ground were much the same in shape as those they had brought. In no long time there was in Chao Fen’s hand a strangely shaped bamboo tube, both ends pared into a saw-toothed pattern, so that it could interlock with another such tube — and even turn.

From merely a few scraps and edges of bamboo, Chao Fen could reason back to the thing that had been made — it was to marvel at him as at a god.

“What can this do?”

“This is a qilin-arm. It can join beam to pillar, drive wheels and bear up frames — its uses are without end. So far as I know, in all Chang’an there is only one man whose designs call for a part so precise.” Chao Fen stroked the bamboo tube and said with feeling, “And he is the one man I have yet to surpass.”

“Who?”

“Mao Poluo’s son — Mao Shun.”

Mao Poluo had been a man of high attainments in the days of the Wu Zhou; skilled in painting, a master of carving, he held the post of Aide in the Directorate for Imperial Manufactories at court. When the Prince of Liang, Wu Sansi, sought to curry favor with Empress Wu, he joined with the chieftains of the four barbarian quarters in a memorial praying that a Sky-Axis of bronze and iron be cast and set up before the Duanmen Gate; and this Sky-Axis, the Tianshu, was cast by Mao Poluo.

Mao Poluo’s son Mao Shun surpassed even his father in the fineness of his art, and stood very high among the craftsmen of Chang’an. One had only to hear Chao Fen’s admiration to know the man’s measure.

Zhang Xiaojing too had heard the name, and his thoughts raced. What had puzzled him all along was this: what did the Pifu mean to do with the missing rock-oil? Now, hearing Chao Fen speak so, he feared this use was bound up inseparably with some design of Mao Shun’s. Lay hold of Mao Shun, and the purpose would stand plain as day. He asked at once: “In what design of Mao Shun’s does the master think this was to be used?”

Chao Fen said: “Mao Shun has Heaven’s favor and the means besides, and stands high in the Sage’s esteem. This year, for the Lantern Festival, he has presented a Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower, to serve in the rite of the lantern-floats. The tower rises above a hundred and fifty chi high and twenty-four bays wide; without, it is draped in colored silk; within are set lantern-figures; its structure is of the greatest intricacy, and once it is lit, it can turn without cease, blazing for li on every side — a wonder such as no age has ever seen. The Sage prized it greatly, and by edict granted him charge of the building of it. And now it wants only the touching of the flame.”

As he spoke, Chao Fen was full of envy — who would not wish to see his heart’s labor made solid and real? He did not notice that Zhang Xiaojing’s face had already changed color several times over.

“The qilin-arm — is it used precisely in this lantern-tower?” Zhang Xiaojing said in a trembling voice.

“Just so. That Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower has upon it twenty-four lantern-chambers, each with its own different scene of lantern-figures. If those figures are to move of themselves, there is no doing it but by joining them with qilin-arms.”

Zhang Xiaojing took the qilin-arm from Chao Fen’s hand and studied it closely, and found that it was hollow within. Chao Fen explained: “The Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower is too tall; timber and stone are all too heavy — only hollow bamboo is fit for the raising of it.”

“But then would not the qilin-arms be easily damaged?”

“Bamboo is very light, and can be changed out at any time. Besides, the lantern-tower is used for only three days — no great matter.”

Light broke in Zhang Xiaojing’s mind. Little as he understood of the craft, he could roughly guess what the Pifu intended. They had first pared the bamboo tubes into the shape of qilin-arms, then filled them full of rock-oil — so many small-sized fierce-fire thunders. When the time came, the Pifu, in the guise of craftsmen, would slip into the lantern-tower, and on the pretext of repairs would, before all those watching eyes, change them out for “qilin-arms.”

In this way the whole Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower would become one enormous fierce-fire thunder, and once it burst, all within a li or more around would likely be reduced to a shambles.

“Where is the lantern-tower built?”

“South of the Xingqing Palace, on the plaza before the Qinzheng Wuben Tower.”

This night, at the hour of the Ox, second half, the Son of Heaven would perform the rite of the lantern-floats in the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, the civil and military officials all about him in the tower, and envoys come from a myriad nations to pay court besides. And the Qinzheng Wuben Tower stood but thirty paces from the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower.

The Pifu’s ambition stood plain as day. They meant, it seemed, to take the whole court of the great Tang in one net, and turn the rite of the lantern-floats into a national calamity of mourning.

Amid his shock, Zhang Xiaojing turned the matter over again. The fierce-fire thunder had one peculiar property: before use it had first to be heated, so it could not be loaded onto the lantern-tower in advance. If the Pifu were to achieve their end, they must go to the site one shichen before the lantern-floats and change out the qilin-arms. The lantern-floats were at the hour of the Ox, second half; it was now the hour of the Rat, first half — less than one and a half shichen remained.

Those Pifu — even now, they must be in the lantern-tower, setting the things in place!

Zhang Xiaojing sprang suddenly to his feet. With no time to say another word to Chao Fen — with no time even to explain to Yisi — he broke for the doorway at a run. This was the last chance; if he did not get there now, it would be too late past all remedy.

But just as he was about to reach the door, the gate was flung open with a bang. A great press of Lüben Guard soldiers, crying “Down on your faces and you’ll live!”, poured into the yard, and in an instant hemmed the place so tight not a drop of water could have leaked through.

Yuan Zai stood far off behind the soldiers, watching with a face full of self-satisfaction as the “Pifu” were about to fall into his net.

***

The men charged this night with the outer cordon of the Xingqing Palace were the Longwu Army. As the palace guard most trusted by the Son of Heaven, they had long since gone over the plaza before the Qinzheng Wuben Tower once from end to end, posting sentries at every point and stretching out the spiked barriers, striving for a flawless watch.

This was, of all the year, the Longwu Army’s most miserable hour.

In one more shichen, the lantern-float carts chosen by the prefectures and counties of every region, and the throngs that backed them, would come driving into the plaza for their last contest of skill. Then the place would be packed with the people till not a drop of water could pass, folk standing even at the nearby street-corners and ward-angles and atop the walls. More troublesome still, the Son of Heaven would stand upon the Qinzheng Wuben Tower and receive the people’s mountain-shouted “Ten thousand years!” from the plaza. In the Sage’s eyes this was to share his joy with the people and bathe together in a flourishing age; in the Longwu Army’s eyes it was hazards past all counting.

Today was too extraordinary. The Longwu Army could not, as in ordinary times, wall off the idle and unconcerned with a heavy force of men; they could only hold a few vital points at all costs. Besides the Jinming, Chuyang, and Tongyang gates below the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, this year there was one more: the Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower.

The four words “Taishang Xuanyuan” were the honorific title conferred upon Laozi in the days of Gaozong and Empress Wu. The reigning Sage honored the Dao, and Lao Dan above all, so that even in building a lantern-tower he must hang this name upon it.

Towering and grand as this lantern-tower was, there was no fear of its being carried off; the fear was rather that some over-curious commoner might run up and, with itching fingers, snap off some drifting bead or phoenix-horn or the like. And so the Longwu Army had set three rings of guards, and none without an official craftsman’s bamboo tally might come near it at all.

Ten-odd firewood carts came slowly in from the east side into the south plaza of the Xingqing Palace. This was because traffic through the whole city district had all but seized up, so that they could only take the passage between the eastern wall and the ranked wards, and come round that way. The Longwu Army soldiers at the plaza’s edge had marked them long since, and raised a hand in signal. The convoy halted, and the man at its head came forward of his own accord, calling himself the foreman of the craftsmen’s guild, and passed over a string of bamboo tallies bound with fine cord.

“The lantern-tower, for the lighting of the lamps,” he said.

The guards had known well beforehand that craftsmen would come to take up post in the lantern-tower and work its lighting, and were not surprised at their coming. They took the bamboo tallies and looked them over one by one.

These bamboo tallies would set out each craftsman’s name, appearance, native place, master-lineage, the ward and shop he belonged to, and his authorizations, with the countersignature of a government officer on the back besides; there was nothing amiss with them. The guard-corporal set the tallies down, glanced over toward the convoy, and of a sudden felt something odd:

“And where is Recorder Zhang?”

By the rules, for so great a matter as repairs to the lantern-tower there must be an officer of the Forestry and Crafts Bureau in attendance. The foreman leaned in and said low: “Ai, don’t speak of it — Recorder Zhang was watching the lanterns on the bridge just now and got shoved into the water by the crowd; they still haven’t fished him out. We were afraid of holding things up, so we took it on ourselves to come ahead first.”

The guard-corporal, hearing there was such a business, was put in a hard place: “For craftsmen to take up post, they must be attended by a recorder of the Forestry and Crafts Bureau.” The foreman said anxiously: “It wasn’t I who pushed Recorder Zhang in! If he won’t come, what am I to do about it?”

“Rules are rules — else let the Forestry and Crafts Bureau send another man over,” the guard suggested. As one of the Longwu Army, charged with the Son of Heaven’s safety, he held the rules above all.

“Everyone’s out watching the lanterns — how am I to find one...?” The foreman grew more and more agitated, wringing his hands till they rasped. “There’s one shichen left to the hour of the Ox, second half. The least delay and we’ll have no way to finish the repairs in time. The Sage has set his whole heart on the lantern-tower blazing bright tonight, to proclaim a flourishing age to the four quarters. And if the lantern-tower should fail to light... all because the Longwu Army wouldn’t let us craftsmen near it?”

At these words the guard-corporal began to waver. Great as the rules were, they were surely not greater than the Son of Heaven’s mood. He glanced at that line of carts: “Very well, the craftsmen may go in — but what is being carried in these carts?”

“All replacement parts, for use in the repairs and changes.” The foreman threw back the cover-cloth and, with an open air, invited the guard to inspect. The guard-corporal waved a hand, and each of his men took a cart and searched it carefully. On the carts were indeed nothing but bamboo tubes, the two ends of each tube pared into a curious shape, much like certain parts up on the lantern-tower. Beyond that, there was nothing else.

These bamboo tubes, though, were hot to the touch, as if they had been heated not long before. The corporal knew nothing of the craft and guessed this was some secret method of the working. He set the bamboo tube down and raised one more doubt: “There’s but one shichen to the lighting of the lamps — and this many spare parts still needing repair?”

This time the foreman pointed at the carriage without ceremony: “That question you may go and ask of Director Mao himself.” The corporal raised his eyes: seated at the front of the carriage was a frail old man with a goatee, gazing up expressionless at the lantern-tower — none other than Mao Shun, the Director of Lanterns.

The corporal fell silent at once. What rank was Mao Shun’s — how did it fall to a mere Longwu Army soldier like him to question the man? His doubts all gone, he ordered the spiked barriers lifted aside and let the convoy drive slowly in.

Two rings of guards in succession had let them pass without trouble. Though these craftsmen had no warrant from Zhang Luo, and it was against the rules, the presence in person of Master Mao Shun was enough to overawe any and every cavil. And so the convoy drove smoothly up beneath the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower.

This lantern-tower was so tall that its base was built up of brick and stone into a Mystic Abbey, the yellow earth rammed hard all around, and only then did it bear up a huge, gourd-shaped great frame of bamboo. The way into the lantern-tower lay within that Mystic Abbey.

The craftsmen jumped down from the carts one after another, each gathering up several qilin-arms, and went into the lantern-tower along that passage. Here too the Longwu Army kept watch, but having had word from ahead, they raised no difficulty at all, and even came over to help carry.

The last down from the cart was Mao Shun, his movements very slow, as though his mind were elsewhere. The foreman went over and took him warmly by the arm. Mao Shun glanced at the foreman and said in a low voice: “This old man has brought you here as agreed — now you can let my family go, can’t you?”

“Director Mao, what a thing to say.” Long Bo laughed. “The remaking of the lantern-tower has yet to lean upon your learning.”

Tanqi had never dreamed she would run into Taizhen up on the Qinzheng Wuben Tower.

To speak of this woman — she was truly a figure of legend, relished on every tongue in the wards of Chang’an. Her name was Yang Yuhuan, and she had been the consort of Prince Shou, Li Mao. Tanqi had come to know her on an outing when the various princes went touring in spring: the Prince of Shou’s consort had carelessly fallen from her horse and wrenched her ankle, and Tanqi, being skilled in massage, had helped to treat it. The two of them had talked well together; the Prince’s consort had not looked down on Tanqi for her maidservant’s station, and they had soon become good friends.

Then, unlooked-for, not a few years later, the Son of Heaven had of all things called Yang Yuhuan into the palace, saying it was to pray for the blessing of the Empress Dowager Dou, and had her leave the world to become a Daoist, styled Taizhen... The twists and turns behind the powder-curtains of the inner palace are not for outsiders to tell, but all Chang’an knew how the matter stood, and for a time it passed as a marvel of gossip.

Come to think of it, she had not seen Taizhen in some years, and had never thought to meet her again tonight at the Lantern Festival spring banquet. One look at that lithe Daoist robe and Tanqi knew that, though she waited at the sovereign’s side, she had as yet no title, and so still went in the guise of one withdrawn from the world, not free to appear openly at the banquet — for the Prince of Shou was seated right there below.

Taizhen, seeing Tanqi, was overjoyed. Long shut up in the palace, she seldom had the chance to meet an old friend from bygone days, and she caught Tanqi’s hands in hers. “Why, it has been an age since I saw you, little sister — how have you been?” Tanqi’s hard-won resolve was cut short all at once, and for a moment she did not know how she ought to answer.

Taizhen only took her for over-excited, and drew her aside, warmly making small talk of home and family. Tanqi was in a fever of anxiety, answering at random with her mouth while her eyes stayed fixed on the far side of the beaded curtain, where a tongtian crown swayed again and again in time to the exquisite strains of the Rainbow-Feather Dance.

Taizhen saw that Tanqi’s mind was elsewhere, and was rather curious. She had glanced just now at the seating: the heir apparent was there, but Li Bi was not — could it be that Li Bi had made a gift of his household maid to the heir apparent? Yet this grimy dress of hers hardly looked fit for attending a banquet.

“How come you to be dressed so, little sister? Have you met with some trouble?”

Hearing this, Tanqi’s eyes flashed of a sudden.

That Taizhen practiced the Dao and prayed for blessings was purely the Son of Heaven’s screen to blind others’ eyes; in truth his favor upon her was beyond measure. Tanqi had heard that in the palace all called Taizhen “my lady,” treating her already as one of the imperial consorts. If she could get her to put in a word with the Son of Heaven, would that not be far more to the purpose than forcing her way in?

Tanqi’s thoughts turned like lightning. All at once she caught Taizhen’s sleeve and wept: “Sister, you must save me!” Taizhen hastened to help her up, saying gently: “What is it that so alarms you? Tell it to me.” Secluded lady Daoist though she was, there was in her tone a faint, calm assurance of high breeding.

Tanqi caught her soft, slender hands and said shyly: “I have secretly plighted my troth to a man; but he has been envied by treacherous men, framed and falsely accused, and is now wanted through the whole city. I have run about the whole night, and not one soul will lend a hand. Truly at my wits’ end, I could only risk my life to come to the heir apparent — but the heir apparent too...” and toward the end she was near to tears.

Tanqi knew Taizhen well: she was a guileless, innocent soul, and talk of Chang’an being destroyed and the like she would not understand. She loved only to hear tales of legend — the phoenix wooing his mate, the Rhapsody on the Goddess of the Luo, the Butterfly Lovers, Red Whisk fleeing by night — all of them matters of love between man and woman. To move Taizhen to help, one could only make up some romance between herself and Zhang Xiaojing.

Sure enough, when Taizhen had heard her out, her eyes brimmed with tears; she found the tale truly tender and sad. A troth secretly plighted, a beloved fallen into misfortune, a life staked to save him — every point of it touched her heart. In her early years she had been the Prince of Shou’s consort, and now she waited upon the sovereign; all along she had never been her own mistress, and toward such stories she had always cherished a little longing.

Taizhen embraced Tanqi’s soft form and found that even the nape of her neck was smudged with dirt — proof that this night had truly given her no rest, and it pained her heart past bearing.

“Be easy; I will go and say a word to the Sage. What is this sweetheart of yours called?”

“He is called Zhang Xiaojing.” No sooner had Tanqi said it than she shook her head: “A crossbow of a thousand jun is not to be sprung for a shrew-mouse. The Sage’s every move carries wind and thunder — how should he trouble himself with so small a matter? It would only make them think less of you, sister.” Taizhen was rather moved that even at this pass the girl still thought of her, and comforted her: “Rest easy. I am forever begging some grace or favor for my family, and the Sage never refuses me; a pardon-edict is an easy thing to ask.”

Tanqi said in a small voice: “To beg His Majesty for a pardon would draw in too much of the court; I cannot let it touch you, sister. If you have the heart, you need only have His Majesty ask one question about the Que-le Huo-duo, and it will be done.”

“And what is that?” Taizhen had not understood a word.

Tanqi smiled bitterly: “It is the matter my beloved is caught up in, kept from the Sage’s hearing by treacherous men. So His Majesty need only give it a little heed, and my love can be delivered from his peril.”

Taizhen thought a moment. This was simpler even than begging a favor, and left no trace; so she nodded her assent. Tanqi bent to sink down in a kneeling thanks, but Taizhen held her up: “Outside the palace, apart from a few sisters, you alone are an old friend — there is no need for this.”

Watching Tanqi’s shining tears, Taizhen felt of a sudden an uncommon sense of achievement. To bring about a marriage-bond with a single word was, in a way, to fulfill a long-held wish of her own. She soothed Tanqi with a few more words, then lifted the beaded curtain and went to the Son of Heaven’s side.

Tanqi stayed where she was, ill at ease in her heart.

Tanqi had already reckoned it through beforehand: whether to clear Zhang Xiaojing’s name, or to give the Jing’an Bureau back to the Eastern Palace, neither could be brought before the imperial presence. To the Son of Heaven these were all small matters. To startle the Son of Heaven, it must be a sharp and venomed needle, the kind that stings at a single prick.

And that venomed needle was the Que-le Huo-duo — the Que-le Huo-duo that would destroy Chang’an.

Just now the heir apparent wished to bear and forbear, and Minister Li wished to contend; both sides, whether by intent or not, had let the threat of the Que-le Huo-duo be overlooked. What Tanqi could do was to overturn the whole table utterly, and blow the matter up large. Let the Son of Heaven but once inquire, and everything would be laid out on the board.

Whether stirring up the situation so could save Zhang Xiaojing, Tanqi did not know, but it could hardly make things worse than they now stood. Yet she knew too that, by this one stroke, she would offend both the heir apparent and Minister Li at once, and the fate that awaited her after was like to be very wretched.

But she could spare no thought for such things now, fixing her whole mind on the far side of the hanging beaded curtain. She saw Taizhen’s yellow crown draw slowly near the tongtian crown, then tilt of a sudden, as if turning her head aside to speak. In a little while Tanqi saw two young eunuchs hurry in through the curtain and hurry out again toward the seats. The heir apparent and Minister Li together left their places and pressed forward to the imperial table. A traveler’s crown and a black gauze cap bowed low as one, seeming to make obeisance, yet stayed long unraised, while only the tongtian crown swayed now and again — giving them a dressing-down, most likely.

In the palace the bells and chimes, drums and music, played on as before, the din unbroken. Tanqi could not hear the talk before the imperial table, and could only lean against the cloud-wall, like a gambler who has staked his whole fortune, waiting for the moment the dice-cup is lifted.

At last the traveler’s crown and the black gauze cap rose together, one of them swaying rather more than the other, as if its wearer’s spirit had taken a blow. Tanqi did not know whether it boded good or ill; she swallowed, and without waiting for Taizhen to come out, slipped quietly back behind the heir apparent’s seat.

Li Heng came back with a face like iron, and at the sight of Tanqi his look turned all at once to sudden understanding: “Was it you who said that to the Taizhen woman?”

“It was.” Tanqi held her body straight.

“You—” Li Heng pointed at her, his finger trembling slightly, so angry he did not know what to say. “You treacherous slut, that eats within and crawls without! For the sake of one death-row convict, you’ve sold every last thing!”

Just now his imperial father had asked about the Que-le Huo-duo, and neither of them could conceal it. Minister Li had seized the chance to attack, charging that Li Bi had put his trust in the wrong man, employing a traitorous death-row convict and so bringing on the Jing’an disaster. Li Heng had no choice but to grit his teeth and argue it out with him. When Minister Li said the Jing’an Bureau was too incompetent to keep from being stormed, he charged the Censorate with grasping to seize others’ authority; when Minister Li said Zhang Xiaojing was in league with the Pifu, he brought out Zhang Xiaojing’s brave deeds in the West Market to rebut the slander.

The two of them, dragged by a mere little maidservant into a war for which they were wholly unready, could only quarrel to no effect, empty blow against empty blow. In the end the Son of Heaven grew impatient of listening and said, “The great enemy is not yet driven off — why all this wrangling!” He had no interest at all in what became of Zhang Xiaojing, but the Que-le Huo-duo was a thing that would destroy the whole of Chang’an. Li Heng and Li Linfu could only kowtow together and beg pardon, professing to cast off their old grievances and strive with all their might to keep Chang’an safe.

Tanqi, though she knew nothing of the inner matter, heard the words “for the sake of one death-row convict” and knew the Jing’an Bureau would not, for the present, keep its teeth clamped on Zhang Xiaojing. She could no longer be bothered to explain the misunderstanding to Li Heng; she leaned her body back against the wall and wearily closed her eyes. She heard footsteps come, and a savage grip seize her arm and drag her out.

What came after could be left only to the amorous rogue himself...

***

The soldiers poured into Chao Fen’s courtyard, and the first to react was Yisi. Without a word he leapt up onto the work-shed, tore away the thatch cover, and down came the thatch in a scattering flurry, veiling the Lüben Guards’ sight.

“Commander Zhang, go, quickly!”

Zhang Xiaojing knew the situation would brook no delay of any kind. With a knit of his brows he turned and ran the opposite way. But he soon saw that on the eaves opposite ten-odd archers had already planted their feet and were drawing their strings. To try to go over the wall now would be to make himself, on the instant, a live target for their feathered shafts.

Hastily he raised his head and called for Yisi to come down, but Yisi was busy up on the shed-roof pulling away the thatch, and did not hear. Then, out of the dark night, came the sharp hiss of arrows splitting the air; Yisi’s body stiffened, and he pitched headlong to the ground.

“Yisi?!”

Zhang Xiaojing started in horror and strode to go to his aid, but a squad of Lüben Guard soldiers had already rushed in and cut off the way between them. Then Yuan Zai too, hedged about by his guards, came into the yard. He glanced at Yisi lying fallen on the ground, and called over this way in high triumph: “The Jing’an Bureau is at its work! You are at the end of your rope — will you not yet submit and be bound?”

To heighten the effect, Yuan Zai took up a knife himself and stabbed it into the badly wounded Yisi’s thigh, drawing from him a loud and piteous scream.

The strange thing was, this time Zhang Xiaojing made no sign at all.

Yuan Zai was a little taken aback at his calm, but a glance around set his mind at ease. There was only the one entrance here, the courtyard gate, and the many soldiers were pressing warily this way with knives drawn. Beyond them archers and crossbowmen held all the high points besides. This was a net spread over heaven and earth; these Pifu could by no means escape.

Still, remembering how he had come near to being taken hostage by Wen Ran just now, he stepped back a few more paces and hid himself in the midst of the troop — proof, this time, against all mishap.

“Bring up the lanterns!” Yuan Zai felt this fine moment ought to be a little brighter.

At once soldiers hung lanterns on the corridor-pillars, and the whole small courtyard grew brighter still. Yuan Zai suddenly cocked his head and gave a “tsk.” He had made it out at last: this man before him seemed to be one-eyed, the left eye no more than an empty socket.

“Zhang Xiaojing?” Yuan Zai was at once startled and delighted; he had taken them for two Pifu spies, and never thought it would be so big a fish. It seemed the great credit of this day was fated to be his alone to take.

Yuan Zai edged a little forward and shouted in a stern voice: “Zhang Xiaojing! Your sins are heavy, past atoning with a hundred deaths! This officer is come here today — will you not yet kill yourself and answer for your crimes?” Seeing Zhang Xiaojing still made no move, he shouted again: “Your confederates — Yao Runeng, Xu Bin, Wen Ran and the rest — have all been taken, and go to the block; they wait only for your head to hold down the ranks!”

Yuan Zai had no wish at all for Zhang Xiaojing to surrender. Whether the abduction of Wang Yunxiu or the storming of the Jing’an Bureau, both these great black pots had best be hung round the neck of a dead man, where they were safest. And so he was goading Zhang Xiaojing: let the man but strike back, and he would have him cut down on the spot.

At Yuan Zai’s words, Zhang Xiaojing’s shoulders began to shake. The apprentice took him for afraid, but a closer look showed that he was, of all things, laughing. The corners of his mouth split open in a smile cruel and bitter, his two silkworm-brows arching high to either side, as though he had met with something to excite him past all bounds.

Zhang Xiaojing took up at random the long knife Chao Fen used for splitting bamboo, hefted it for its weight, tore a strip of cloth from his sleeve and wound the haft fast to his hand, then turned about and faced his pursuers head-on.

Seeing him take up the knife, Yuan Zai’s heart leapt with joy, though his mouth roared out in anger: “At death’s door, and still you’d put up a stubborn fight? Men — seize him for me!”

At the command the soldiers rushed forward as one to take this “chief of the Pifu.” But Zhang Xiaojing’s knife flashed once, and the man foremost in the charge fell to the ground, head parted from body, a fountain of blood spouting to the sky. Those behind checked their steps in fright, glanced left and right at their fellows, and at a flicker of the eye charged in together. Two more flashes of the knife, and on the instant two more pitched down.

Before the soldiers behind could make any answer, Zhang Xiaojing had already charged back into the midst of their ranks. Not a word did he speak; the knife-light flashed on and on, and the chopping blade in his hand was like the fetters of the Death-God, every stroke it swung carrying off a life. In a moment blood flew scattering and piteous cries rose on every side.

The apprentice had long been shaking with terror, and crouched with his head in his arms. Only Chao Fen himself sat steady before his stove, watching the flames leap on, turning a blind eye to this cruel and bloody scene.

Yuan Zai could not hold back a shudder; instinct told him something was badly wrong, and he shrank back without thinking, shouting for the soldiers to press on.

Zhang Xiaojing’s assault went on unabated; he was as though possessed by the Seven Killings itself. The Lüben Guard soldiers had never once fought an enemy so mad — that towering killing-intent, those blood-red furious eyes, in the dark like a savage beast’s, so that whoever he touched died. The courtyard was cramped, and the ground too littered with clutter; the Lüben Guards, packed close together, had no way at all to deploy their strength and hem him in, and could only bear in terror the assault of one man upon a whole army.

Were Feng Dalun at hand, he would have raised the alarm. The year before, when Zhang Xiaojing broke into the Bear Fire Gang to take his revenge, he had killed and wounded more than thirty of its members, the deputy chief and several enforcers dying wretchedly under his blade besides — it was just such a state of frenzy as this.

Zhang Xiaojing had, in truth, gone mad now.

Before this, however dangerous the straits he met with, he had always stayed his hand, unwilling to take more lives than he must. But Yisi’s taking of the arrow, and Yuan Zai’s provocations one upon another, had at last found for the wrath pent up in Zhang Xiaojing all along this road an outlet to vent itself.

His comrades struck down one after another, the enemy still pressing step by step forward, the stupid and greedy faces of the officials, the dying charge of an old brother-in-arms, a million living souls of the city of Chang’an — one pressure joined to another and another, till at last they squeezed out a violent force long lying hidden, and made of his whole person one dreadful killing-demon. Before his eyes there was no more weighing of this against that: met a god, he killed the god; met a Buddha, he killed the Buddha — let alone those frail Lüben Guard soldiers.

More terrible still, Zhang Xiaojing’s frenzy showed itself not as a mad, flailing slaughter, but as an extreme of cold — cold as a slab of rock. He made no wasted motion, no sound, harbored no scruple and no pity, not even any thought of preserving himself. He neither dodged nor gave ground, wholly a puppet with neither flesh-and-blood nor thought left in it, the one lingering intention being to kill. Every stroke was a killing blow.

In Zhang Xiaojing’s single eye, the ghastly scene before him, the ghastly scene of the Bear Fire Gang, and that vision of the Asura hell in the years when he held a city in the Western Regions — these three images overlaid one another. As the killing went on, Zhang Xiaojing had sunk already into a waking dream, believing himself still holding that little fort in the Western Regions, fighting a bloody death-struggle against the great host of the Türks.

A silent monster of this kind charging into their ranks made the silence more terrible yet. Amid the shouting and the piteous screams, almost every man was slain at a single blow. A few bolder soldiers thought to cut him off, only to find there was no stopping him at all. That strange knife in Zhang Xiaojing’s hand sheared through iron like mud, and was of an extreme toughness besides; buried in the bodies of so many men, still its edge had not turned.

One man, one knife — and he had killed till the Lüben Guards lay strewn as corpses across the ground, and soon, by main force, had thrust them clean out of the courtyard. Five Yamas — the Cruel, the Vicious, the Fierce, the Willful, the Ruthless — were famed through a hundred and eight wards. But the Chang’an of this night bore witness to a sixth Yama: the Mad.

Ten-odd lanterns still hung on the corridor-pillars, their candle-light flickering, casting up from the pools of blood spread across the ground that one savage and solitary knife-bearing black shadow.

Yuan Zai was quick to react, and had fled the courtyard at the first instant. He found his heart all but hammering through his breast, and his trousers hot and wet — he had, of all things, wet himself. That killing-god’s mad performance had torn Yuan Zai’s courage utterly to shreds.

Now at last Yuan Zai understood why Prince Yong and Feng Dalun so dreaded this man. This was no itch of scab or ringworm — this was a mortal sickness at the very heart!

No more than seven or eight men had got out of the courtyard in time with Yuan Zai; luckily there were another ten-odd in reserve on the perimeter, who now came hurrying up one after another. But at the sight of that wretched scene, they too were, every one, quaking at the knees.

“Get in there, quickly!” Yuan Zai urged the soldiers at his side, and found his own voice thin and dry, without a shred of authority to it. The Lüben Guard soldiers gripped their weapons tight, but every face was aghast, and none would step forward. They, like Yuan Zai, had had their nerve and spirit shattered by that one fight.

Zhang Xiaojing came walking step by step toward the courtyard gate, a desperate, awe-freezing air of death breathing from his whole body.

This strong and dreadful aura bore down on the soldiers and pressed them back and back. Behind them Yuan Zai cried out in terror: “The crossbows! The bows!” He wanted nothing now but to be free of this nightmare as fast as he could, yet his muscles were drawn tight as iron and would not stir at all.

The Lüben Guard soldiers, hearing the reminder, woke as from a dream, and the men in the rear ranks drew out their hand-crossbows one after another. However formidable that one man, he was still a thing of flesh and blood, and could never stand against these crossbow-bolts.

And at the very instant Zhang Xiaojing was about to step out of the courtyard and the soldiers to squeeze their triggers, those two courtyard gates, as though seized by a pair of great invisible hands, slammed shut of a sudden with a bang. Thud, thud, thud, thud — that whole volley of crossbow-bolts nailed into the door-boards. Then, with a clack, a crossbar seemed to drop into place.

Yuan Zai’s face twisted. If he did not see Zhang Xiaojing dead with his own eyes, then for the rest of his life he would likely be haunted night after night by this nightmare.

“Quick! Go and ram the gate!” Yuan Zai shrieked, heedless now of the reek of urine between his legs. But no one heeded him, as though that were the gate of the land of the Yellow Springs.

Inside the gate Zhang Xiaojing had likewise halted his steps; he too had no notion how those two doors had suddenly closed. He lifted his hollow right eye and found, behind the two doors, a whole contrivance of pull-ropes and bamboo poles, running all the way back into the courtyard.

Zhang Xiaojing had no interest in such things now; he wanted only to kill. Slowly he raised his arm, meaning to hack at the crossbar between the two doors. Just then a great callused hand closed over the hand that held his knife.

“Good. You are very good.” Chao Fen’s grip was extraordinarily strong; he wrested the knife straight out of Zhang Xiaojing’s hand.

The moment the knife left his hand, the light of reason came back into Zhang Xiaojing’s eye. He looked at the courtyard heaped with dead and wounded, his silkworm-brows drawn tight, without the least trace of triumph.

“Do you know what the most beautiful thing in this world is?” Chao Fen’s voice had changed altogether from its cold aloofness of before. “It is the utmost, the pure, the most thoroughgoing single-mindedness. I came from Japan to the great Tang to learn the crafts precisely in the hope of seeing such beauty.”

He turned the knife sideways and, with his thumb, wiped the bloodstains from the blade, till it shone cold and bright again.

“I have walked through many places and tried many things, yet always it fell just a little short. But just now, in you, I saw that realm I have sought so bitterly and so long — what beautiful killing that was, unmixed with any dross, pure to the very last degree.” Chao Fen’s eyes blazed as he spoke.

The apprentice at his side wore a look of disbelief — the household in such an uproar, and the master could still find it beautiful? Trembling, he got to his feet and took to his heels. Chao Fen made no move at all to stop him, only saying with disdain: “These men know nothing but the use of tools and clever tricks; in the end they can never grasp the Way.”

Zhang Xiaojing kept his silence; he had not yet fully come back out of that frenzied mood.

Chao Fen held the knife out to him again: “I gave up forging swords long ago; this is the last blade I made with my own hands. I had thought it fell short of what I asked of beauty, but now I see it was only that it had been given to the wrong man. I can hear it now, trembling, singing for joy — for you are the man it has waited for. Take it.”

To Chao Fen’s surprise, Zhang Xiaojing pushed the knife back, his tone bitter: “In my life I have killed past all counting, yet I have never once felt killing a man to be a happy thing. Just the reverse — every time I raise my hand it leaves me heavy with weariness and grief. To you, perhaps, it can be a thing of beauty; to me, killing is only a bitter torment I cannot escape.”

“Killing or torment, it makes no difference — so long as it is the utmost, it is beauty.” Chao Fen explained excitedly. “A pity only that the living cannot go down into hell; that is the place I have longed for in my dreams.” Once more he held out the knife.

“You will see it soon enough.”

Zhang Xiaojing did not take the knife. He turned to look at Yisi, lying in a pool of blood. He had taken two arrows; the good fortune was that neither had struck a vital spot, but the tendons of both his legs were severed, and hereafter — never mind cave-running — he would likely find even walking hard.

“Commander, I have not the strength; I am of no great use...” Yisi said with a struggle, a shocking trace of blood at the corner of his mouth. This scion of the Persian royal house had eyes gentle as ever, their light undimmed.

“I will send word to the men of the Persian Temple to carry you back,” was all the comfort Zhang Xiaojing could give him.

“...It is the Nestorian temple,” Yisi corrected in a low voice; he had no strength to spare, and could only look pitifully up at Zhang Xiaojing. This time Zhang Xiaojing understood; he drew the cross out from around Yisi’s neck and laid it against his lips. Yisi sighed a sigh of deep contentment, murmuring, and said a prayer for Zhang Xiaojing.

This was the only thing left him now that he could do.

Zhang Xiaojing had no words to spare. He got to his feet and said to Chao Fen: “Trouble yourself to call a physicians’ hall, and have him sent for treatment.”

“Where are you going?”

“The Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower.” Zhang Xiaojing’s voice sounded sharper even than Chao Fen’s knife.

“But there are still all those soldiers outside the door, waiting for you.”

“Either I get out clean, or I die where I stand. If it’s the latter, that would be the easier thing for me.”

Chao Fen drew the knife back: “Since you’ll not have the knife, then let me tell you a thing or two.”

***

The Lüben Guard soldiers that came up after gathered by and by into Zhiye Ward, their number swelling to more than thirty. Yet Yuan Zai still did not feel safe enough; he reckoned it would take two hundred men at least before he could kill Zhang Xiaojing with any peace of mind.

With their commander so craven, the men below were the less willing to spend their strength. They ringed Chao Fen’s dwelling round and round, so that not even a bird could fly out — but no one dared go in. That knife behind the door, and that killing-god, had drunk the blood of no few men; who knew how much more he would drink this night?

The master of this dwelling had already been found out — the famous craftsman Chao Fen, whose master-house was that Japanese, Vice-Minister Chao Heng of the Court of the Imperial Regalia — a high official of the senior fourth rank, upper class, not to be trifled with. So Yuan Zai changed his tactics, giving over the active assault and turning attack into blockade.

This courtyard had no hidden passage. If Zhang Xiaojing meant to come out of it, he must go by the main gate — and one step outside it he was a live target, with several tens of crossbows and long bows waiting for him there.

Sweat kept starting from Yuan Zai’s forehead, faster than he could wipe it away. His hands trembled slightly even now; he could not fathom why one man alone should bring so crushing a pressure to bear. At the thought that it was still hot between his legs, Yuan Zai’s shame and fury surged up by turns.

He must be killed! He must be killed!

But just then a messenger came hurrying up with a letter, said to be a three-feather dispatch from the Secretariat. To hear it was issued by the Phoenix Pavilion took Yuan Zai rather aback. He took the document and read, and could not but be dumbstruck.

This document named no addressee; it was being circulated broadcast through every ward and street-post. The messenger, happening to see a large body of Lüben Guards gathered here, and finding that it met the requirements for delivery, had brought it over first. The content of the document was very simple: the citywide warrant against Zhang Xiaojing was for the present to be held in abeyance, and all the wards were to bend every effort to seize the Pifu, and so forth. And the seals of authority at the foot bore, besides Li Linfu’s, that of Li Heng.

When had these two powers joined hands?

Whether Zhang Xiaojing was truly in league with the Pifu, Yuan Zai did not care. But his every scheme was built upon the one foundation that “Zhang Xiaojing is the Pifu’s inside man.” Once that was shaken, there was the danger of the whole thing collapsing across the board.

For the moment things were still well enough: the warrant was only held off, not withdrawn. But that wheel of fortune, unseen, seemed to have begun turning in an ill direction. It was a very bad feeling.

Just then the courtyard gate swung open again with a bang, and Zhang Xiaojing appeared once more in their view. The soldiers and Yuan Zai swallowed all at once, their bodies drawing a few degrees tighter.

This time Zhang Xiaojing held no knife in his hand. Facing so many men, wholly without dodge or dread, he came walking calmly forward. Yuan Zai knew that if he gave the order to loose arrows now, this nightmare before him would vanish for good.

Yet all along he minded those two signatures on the document.

Why had Li Linfu and the heir apparent joined hands? Did the holding-off of the warrant mean the Eastern Palace had resolved to protect Zhang Xiaojing at all costs? And what was the Phoenix Pavilion’s stand? It seemed reluctant, and yet to have yielded. He was suspicious by nature, keen to any faint sign or trace in matters political. Turning it over and over, Yuan Zai realized of a sudden: Zhang Xiaojing must not be killed!

It was a trap! The document had said plainly that they were first to bend every effort to run down the Pifu. If he killed Zhang Xiaojing here, it was as good as flouting the order from above. And should the Pifu bring off some great outrage, this would make a splendid pretext to hang the blame on him — “The villains had their way, and it must be your fault; who told you to defy the order from above?”

This was no idle guess; Yuan Zai reckoned that, were he in the other’s place, he would surely do just so. At the thought of it, another film of cold sweat broke over Yuan Zai’s broad forehead. He had been too pleased with himself tonight, and had come within a hair of a fatal carelessness.

What of taking him alive, then?

Yuan Zai soon put the notion away. One look at Zhang Xiaojing’s air of grim resolve told him it was out of the question: go, or die — there was no third possibility. After turning it over and over, Yuan Zai found that only by letting Zhang Xiaojing go was the risk least of all.

After all, this was an order from on high; I am but carrying it out.

Zhang Xiaojing walked straight ahead without a glance to either side, while the soldiers raised their bows and crossbows, their wrists trembling, waiting for their commander’s order. But the order was slow, slow in coming, and this only wound the strain in them tighter.

Zhang Xiaojing came ten paces nearer, near enough now that the ghastly single eye and the deep-gullied cheeks could be seen plain, and still Yuan Zai made no move at all. The Lüben Guard soldiers could not stir either — one move and the whole formation would fall into chaos. Zhang Xiaojing came five paces nearer, and now at last Yuan Zai spoke through gritted teeth: “Draw off the arrows — make way!”

The soldiers were on the point of squeezing their triggers when their fingers gave a start. What? Draw off? Had they misheard? Once more Yuan Zai shouted: “Make way! Make way! Clear off, quickly!” The Lüben Guard soldiers were well-drilled after all; though they did not understand, they carried out the order to the letter.

With one accord they lowered their crossbows and parted to either side, leaving a passage open. Zhang Xiaojing was taken aback; he had braced himself for a bloody fight to the death, yet the other side had of their own accord made way — what was this?

Zhang Xiaojing could not make it out, but his steps did not halt; he walked straight up beside Yuan Zai before he stood still. Yuan Zai was strained to the last degree, feeling himself watched by a venomous snake. He shrank back a little, so that if the other should burst out killing, there would at least be a guard or two to block the way.

“My friends’ account I’ll settle another day. Now — get me a fast horse.” Zhang Xiaojing said coldly.

Yuan Zai was somewhat vexed: you kill this many of my men, and getting away alive is more than you deserve — and yet you’d ask a thing of me besides? But meeting Zhang Xiaojing’s gaze, he drew in his neck, and lost every scrap of courage to argue.

A fast horse was soon led over. Zhang Xiaojing mounted it and, bowing his head, said to Yuan Zai: “If you’ve half a wit left among you, get to the front of the Xingqing Palace as fast as you can — the Pifu are all there.”

With that he wheeled his horse’s head about and galloped away.

***

The stretch between Zhiye Ward and the Xingqing Palace was, at this hour, the most clogged road in all Chang’an, with Wuben, Pingkang, Chongren, and the East Market all along the way blazing with lantern-light. This year the Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower reared high before the Xingqing Palace, more conspicuous even than the Wild Goose Pagoda, and made men’s curiosity past all curbing. Looking down upon Chang’an, one would have seen the plaza before the Xingqing Palace like a vast pool, drawing in the human tide of the whole city, as ten thousand rivers run home to the sea.

To ease the press of the crowds, the wards had opened their gates and main streets one after another, letting the sightseers pass through. But even so, the traffic was far from easy.

Especially once the hour of the Rat was past, the fever in the great streets did not abate in the least, but rather mounted higher and higher. The clamor of drums and music never left the ear, the smells of incense, candle, and cosmetic powder hung all about, the whole street a spread of silks and gauzes, pearls and kingfisher-feathers flashing their light. This all-pervading excitement gathered into one invisible great Lantern-Festival hand that swallowed up the lantern-viewers and made them a part of the very mood. Excited and bewildered at once, they pressed blindly forward with the crowd as if bewitched, leaping in time to the song and dance, so that even a streamer of colored silk flying through the air overhead would draw a burst of startled cries.

Zhang Xiaojing rode well, and the horse was a good one, but in a place like this it was of no use at all. Even to go round by the south was no good; the crowds from every quarter were all flowing this way, and there was no clear stretch of road to be had. Zhang Xiaojing pushed forward a few paces and soon saw that, jammed like this, he might not get across in a whole shichen.

That one shichen was, for Zhang Xiaojing — no, for the city of Chang’an — far too great a luxury.

Zhang Xiaojing simply sprang down from the horse and searched with his single eye for some other way to get there quickly. But he was disappointed: from here all the way to the Xingqing Palace the great road was one dense, packed mass of people, through which — never mind a mule — not even a mouse could have squeezed. He turned his gaze to the nearby ward walls. The ward walls were about two chi thick, and one could just manage to walk atop them. But alas, even up there now they were crawling with people, sitting or standing, like a row of ridge-beasts high and low.

Zhang Xiaojing swept his eye round a few times and could truly find no way to pass quickly. On foot it would take a full half-shichen at the least. Just then a high, clear woman’s voice came from far off, like a whip cracked across the sky, and in an instant it drowned out every other sound. The woman’s voice had scarcely fallen when the cheers and applause of a thousand and more people rose in wave upon wave, surging toward him, till even the candle-flames of the lantern-wheels along the street trembled a few times.

Zhang Xiaojing looked up and saw two lantern-float convoys again contending in skill in the middle of the street. One cart had been made over into the shape of a tiger, even the shaft-horses draped in tiger-striped brocade, its middle bulging up in a ring like a crouching tiger’s arched back. Three big men stood on the tiger’s back, each wielding a set of army cymbals and drums — plainly in imitation of the Dance of the Prince of Qin Breaking the Line. But the three of them now hung their heads in dejection, having clearly lost.

Their opponents, the victors, were a tall phoenix-tail cart. At the cart’s tail a thousand and more five-colored bird-feathers had been fixed into a fan-shape, arrayed like the tail-plumes of a phoenix, so that the sight of it was as a hundred birds paying court to the phoenix. In the middle rose a tall pole wound with colored silk, a narrow platform at its top. A woman singer in a rainbow-feather robe stood upon it, peerless and alone. That exquisite song just now, shaking the very clouds, had come from her lips.

The countless people all around shouted with one voice: “Xu Hezi! Xu Hezi!” This was the singer’s name, and the cheering went long unbroken. In the lantern-float contest of skill, what counted was that the side which drew the loudest cries from the onlookers won. This Xu Hezi could set a myriad throats shouting as one by the power of her voice alone — proof enough that the other side had lost, and lost utterly.

Xu Hezi, having won this bout, pointed a round fan of golden sparrow-design toward the Xingqing Palace, meaning that tonight she would take the first lantern. This early declaration of victory made the people more excited yet. Xu Hezi, all triumph, came down from the high platform and ducked into the carriage to rest. Not until she met the next lantern-floater would she mount the stage to do battle again.

The carriage began slowly to move, and many backers thronged about the phoenix-tail cart, calling her name and moving forward together with it. Their faith was firm indeed: with their own cheers they would help their goddess win the title of first at the Lantern Festival.

The most frenzied of these followers, by his dress still a young gentleman of some great house, had his head-cloth askew and his robe torn open at the breast; drunk with rapture, he was steadying himself against the carriage and just making ready to fling his scent-pouch up to her. All at once he saw a one-eyed fellow shoving through as well, and was about to bark at him — only, before he could, the fellow drove a savage elbow into his lower belly, and the young gentleman went down on the spot with the pain, sprawling on the ground.

The fellow plucked a small knife from the man’s waist as he passed, set a foot on his back, and with a light spring leapt into the phoenix-tail cart.

The carriage-box of the phoenix-tail cart was specially made, sealed all round without a crack showing, so there was no fear of some frenzied backer bursting in. But the fellow did not so much as glance at the carriage-box; in a few quick strides he came to the front of the cart and set the small knife against the driver’s neck.

“Drive straight ahead, and don’t stop along the way.” Zhang Xiaojing said, holding down his voice. The driver was frightened out of his wits and stammered that this was Lady Xu’s lantern-float — what were they to do if a challenge came midway? By the rules of the contest, whenever two carts met in the street there had to be a bout; the winner went straight on, the loser went round.

Zhang Xiaojing pressed the blade a little harder and said it again: “Drive straight ahead, and don’t stop along the way.”

The driver did not know what this was for, but the threat of the blade against his flesh was real and immediate. He could only shake the reins and put the shaft-horses to a quicker pace. The backers all about hastened their steps too, shouting the name of “Xu Hezi,” and the people around, hearing it, made way of their own accord one after another.

Mad as this move of Zhang Xiaojing’s seemed, there was truly no help for it. The roads were too clogged, and the one thing that could pass freely was a lantern-float cart. Everyone wanted to see its contest of skill, and no one would block its way; the fanatical backers would even clear the road ahead of it.

He had no other choice but to seize Xu Hezi’s cart before all those watching eyes.

As the people ahead scattered away, this phoenix-tail cart gradually gathered speed, till the backers were somewhat hard put to keep up. Swiftly it passed the two crossings of Wuben-Kaihua and Pingkang-Chongren, and made for the East Market.

Just then a burst of drumming broke out on its right, and a spring-ewer cart of strongly Western-Regions flavor came charging out from between the East Market and Xuanyang Ward, a great crowd of backers trailing behind it. The drums atop the spring-ewer cart went boom, boom, and a snake-waisted Hu girl climbed up onto the cart’s head and struck a bewitching pose — this was a challenge of skill flung at the phoenix-tail cart.

And just when all the people were looking forward with full hearts to a stunning duel, the phoenix-tail cart swung its head about and drove off toward the north side of the East Market, taking no notice whatever of the spring-ewer cart’s challenge.

This was an enormous insult. The spring-ewer cart’s backers burst out in loud curses. Only now did the phoenix-tail cart’s backers come hurrying up, and seeing their goddess reviled, at once began to curse back; and as they cursed, the two sides came to blows, and the crossing turned on the instant into a battlefield.

The phoenix-tail cart had not the least mind to slow; once round the East Market, the Xingqing Palace was there. Just then the carriage-box opened from within, and an old serving-woman put out her head.

It turned out that inside the carriage-box they had heard the challenge-drums too, but the cart had not once stopped, and so the serving-woman who looked after Xu Hezi had come out to ask what the matter was. When she saw that a ferocious, fiendish one-eyed man had appeared beside the driver, she was so frightened she cried out at once: “Disaster! Disaster! A lovesick pest has come!”

Every year at the Lantern Festival there would be a few over-besotted backers who did outrageous things: cutting themselves to make a vow, courting favor at knife-point, dogging her day and night, stealing her underthings — anything at all, and all of them called “lovesick pests.” Seeing Zhang Xiaojing force his way onto the cart, the serving-woman took him too for a lovesick pest.

Zhang Xiaojing turned his head and flashed his tally at the serving-woman: “The Jing’an Bureau is at its work; I’m requisitioning this cart for the time being.” Hearing that he was a government man, the serving-woman would still not let it go: “Lady Xu has staked a thousand strings of cash on this — you say ‘requisition’ as easily as opening your mouth, and if it spoils the great matter of the lantern-floats, who’s to pay?”

Zhang Xiaojing had no patience to jaw with her, and brought his knife down on the carriage-frame beside the serving-woman’s head, shearing off half the hairpin from her topknot besides. The serving-woman started back in terror and tumbled with a thud back into the carriage-box. Through the little door left open, Zhang Xiaojing saw a round-faced woman seated composedly within, holding a bowl of throat-soothing pear broth in her hands, her face serene, that rainbow-feather robe hung on a little rack beside her.

“Mama, if it’s the officer requisitioning it, do as he says.” Xu Hezi said calmly, without the least alarm or anger. Zhang Xiaojing cupped his hands: “I’ve held up the young lady’s lantern-floats, only that I’ve a pressing matter of my own and no choice in it. Forgive the offense.”

“A matter bigger even than the lantern-floats?” Xu Hezi asked, curious. Her voice was very faint — she was likely sparing her throat on purpose.

“As far apart as heaven and earth!”

Xu Hezi laughed: “That’s just as well, then; I get to take a little rest myself.” With that she lifted the broth-bowl and took another small sip. Her bearing now was placid and serene, with nothing at all of the fierce, overbearing air she had shown on the high platform.

“Aren’t you afraid, young lady?” He narrowed his single eye.

“Being afraid does no good anyway, does it?”

Zhang Xiaojing laughed aloud, and felt the vexation in his breast lighten a little. He cupped his hands to Xu Hezi once more and went back to the driver’s side.

By now the cart had drawn near the plaza of the Xingqing Palace. There was still some while to go before the lantern-floats, and the various entrances were still shut off by the Longwu Army. Not a few of the people had gathered here early to queue, waiting to go in. That Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower reared high not far off, candle-light glimmering faintly within it, and no few human shadows moving about too.

Zhang Xiaojing watched a while, then said: “All right — stop here.”

The carriage came to a halt at a corner some tens of paces from the entrance; before it was fully stopped, Zhang Xiaojing had jumped down. He was just about to go when Xu Hezi’s voice came softly from behind him: “Officer of the Jing’an Bureau — do your very best.”

Zhang Xiaojing halted his steps and put in a word of warning: “You had all best leave now — the farther from the Xingqing Palace, the better.” With that he hurried away.

When he had gone some distance off, the driver at last dared to rub his neck and curse bitterly: “That lovesick pest!” Xu Hezi set down her pear broth, her two dark brows drawing gently together: “I think we ought to do as he said.” The serving-woman clambered up from the floor: “Young lady, you’ve lost your senses — you’d believe the wild words of that knife-happy devil?”

Xu Hezi gazed at that far-off retreating figure and sighed softly: “I believe him. I have never seen such despair in any man’s eyes.”

Zhang Xiaojing knew nothing of these little interludes behind him, nor had any interest in them. He had already mingled with the queuing people, and was drawing slowly near the plaza.

At no great distance, music drifted down from the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, where the Lantern Festival spring banquet was still going on. Many common folk had come running to the plaza just to hear this music, and to smell the savor of the delicacies, which made them feel that they too had been asked to the feast.

Zhang Xiaojing alone had fixed his attention upon the Longwu Army. As he had foreseen, the plaza’s guard was loose without and tight within, most rigorous, dense with sentries open and hidden, and no ordinary person might get inside. The Pifu must surely have got hold of craftsmen’s warrants and slipped in disguised as craftsmen.

To break through the barrier head-on was out of the question — he would be cut down where he stood. Zhang Xiaojing had thought of going to warn the high officers of the Longwu Army, but he had no proof in hand. How a great Tang official would regard a death-row convict wanted through the whole city — Zhang Xiaojing did not yet know things had changed — no one knew better than he.

A sigh slid from Zhang Xiaojing’s lips. Li, Yao, Xu, Tanqi, Yisi — every one of them was gone now, and the watchtower system had collapsed. He now was a truly solitary man, alone. No one to back him, no one to believe him, no one so much as knowing what he was about; the one thing that had kept him company to this pass was only that bronze tally of the Jing’an Bureau at his waist.

Zhang Xiaojing put out a hand and brushed at his eye-socket.

He looked once more at the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, then slipped soundlessly out of the queue and walked off in the opposite direction, and soon flitted in through the ward gate of Daozheng Ward.

Daozheng Ward lay on the south side of the south plaza of the Xingqing Palace. When Xingqing Ward had been enlarged into a palace in earlier days, it had encroached on part of the ground of Daozheng Ward, so that the two lay very near each other. It was for this reason that the Longwu Army had stationed a body of soldiers here too, to keep any treacherous men from seizing the high points. But to the lower-lying ground they paid less mind, and they had not sealed off the whole district.

Once inside the ward, Zhang Xiaojing avoided every Longwu Army patrol and went straight east, passing through the mansions where the rich households dwelt, to come to a hollow of ground thick with a grove of scholar-trees. In the middle of the hollow was a fish-pond ringed with a paved apron for the runoff. The rain-gutters on both sides of the ward’s streets all flowed here, and then, by a narrow drain, discharged into the Longshou Canal.

Just now it was early spring, and the fish-pond had dried to its bed. Zhang Xiaojing felt his way carefully down the pond wall to the bottom, then groped his way forward along the narrow drain. As he neared the main current of the Longshou Canal, he crouched down and, at the edge of the outfall, found a long earthenware drainpipe. The pipe was very long, running parallel to the Longshou Canal, and at the last it led Zhang Xiaojing to a pitch-black opening beneath the canal’s embankment, where four dragon-scale water-dividing posts stood ranged.

This was the great secret Chao Fen had told him before he left.

Though the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower was Mao Shun’s design, at bottom all its changes rang upon one principle. Chao Fen had pointed out that, for the lantern-figures within the tower to move of themselves, water-power must be brought in. The Longshou Canal lay but a few tens of paces south of the Xingqing Palace; Mao Shun could not but make use of it. The likeliest way was to dig, beneath the Longshou Canal, a covert channel running at right angles to it, leading the water to under the lantern-tower to drive the pivot-wheels and furnish the power.

Chao Fen had reckoned that, with the bulk of the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower, the volume of water led in would of necessity be enormous, and that, to let craftsmen conveniently clear away silt besides, this covert channel would be dug very broad — broad enough to just admit the passage of one man.

In this way Zhang Xiaojing need not cross the plaza at all, but could go by the underground passage straight into the very heart of the lantern-tower.

The surface of these dragon-scale water-dividing posts was a layer upon layer of scale-shaped ridges. If a man tried to squeeze through the gap between two posts, he would be caught fast on the scales, unable to stir, unable even to draw back — and no use to smear himself with oil either.

But Chao Fen had made ready beforehand, and had given Zhang Xiaojing a straight-handled horse-tooth rasp. Zhang Xiaojing soon rasped through one of the dragon-scale posts and squeezed himself in. Sure enough, within was a brick-built conduit just large enough to admit one man moving bent-backed; the canal-water diverted from the Longshou Canal flowed into the tunnel, gurgling as it came.

Zhang Xiaojing soaked his whole body in the water, tipped his head back, and pressed a crossbow tight against the upper edge of the conduit, wading forward step by step. That crossbow too was Chao Fen’s giving: seeing Zhang Xiaojing would not take the knife, he had given him this specially made repeating crossbow instead, which could loose four shots in succession. Chao Fen had set his heart on Zhang Xiaojing’s working, once more, the “beauty” of the crossbow.

When he had gone some tens of paces, the conduit suddenly opened out, and ahead of him became an underground space shaped like a spirit-vault. The canal ran through the very middle of this vault, and on either side of the water stood three huge wooden wheels, turned ceaselessly by the water’s push, creaking in the dark. This must be the lowest level of the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower, the place that furnished the power for a hundred and more lantern-figures. Up on the domed vault there was besides a curiously shaped array of horse-mouths, to what use he could not tell.

The Son of Heaven of the great Tang had truly spent no small fortune on a lantern-tower that would be lit for only three days at the Lantern Festival.

Zhang Xiaojing climbed up out of the water, wrung the water roughly from the hem of his clothes, and peered carefully ahead by the faint light. He saw that at the far end of the spirit-vault was a rough wooden door, within which there seemed to connect a flight of stairs — this must be the way in and out of the vault. Above the door hung a torch, giving the whole vault its limited light.

At the outer edge of the torch’s light there seemed, moreover, to stand several human shadows. Zhang Xiaojing leveled the crossbow and stole toward them light-footed. As he drew near, his nose caught a strong reek of blood.

Zhang Xiaojing held his breath, and looking more closely, found that those few figures were not standing but leaning aslant against several wooden crates, every one of them iron-faced, their breath already stopped in death. These men wore brown short robes and waterproof rattan shoes on their feet — they must be the craftsmen charged with tending the water-wheels.

Beside them stood a lean, hard-muscled man in tight clothes, toying with a knife in his hand.

Zhang Xiaojing’s heart gave a jolt: the Pifu had indeed already got into the lantern-tower.

Just then a sound of footsteps rose from the other side of the water-wheels, and a tall, lean fellow came out of the shadows, his step easy, humming a little tune under his breath. But the light was too dim to make out his face. The lean hard man put away his knife and said respectfully: “Master Long Bo, this side is all cleared away.”

The tall lean fellow walked, unconcerned, past that row of corpses, clicking his tongue a few times, whether in regret or in praise there was no telling.

At that name Zhang Xiaojing’s heart stirred. Long Bo? This fellow the Jing’an Bureau had hunted so bitterly for had shown himself at last. At first they had taken Long Bo for no more than an inside contact of the Turkic Wolf Guards; now it seemed plain that he was the black hand behind it all, the very chief of the Pifu.

Zhang Xiaojing narrowed his eyes and arched his back, gathering himself to strike, waiting for the instant when Long Bo should draw near the door and step to the edge of the torchlight. First Zhang Xiaojing threw up a shot and brought the torch down off the door, then, in the instant of the shift from light to dark, of a sudden thrust off with his right foot and rushed forward at furious speed, the crossbow in his hand loosing a two-shot burst.

The lean hard fellow took a bolt each in the forehead and the throat and pitched to the ground. Zhang Xiaojing flung himself straight at Long Bo, bore him down to the earth, and jammed the hand-crossbow against his temple.

The torch rolled a few turns on the ground but did not go out. Zhang Xiaojing swung aside and, by the last of the torchlight, saw a gaunt, withered face and a hooked, eagle’s-beak nose. At the same moment, the other made out his face too.

“Aha, Zhang Big-Head, and keeping well, I see.” Long Bo split his mouth in a grin — and, of all things, laughed.
