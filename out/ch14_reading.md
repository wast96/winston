## Chapter 14

The lamps were lit, the piano sounding, and an air of elegance and sweet repose swept away the desolation and sorrow at the bottom of my heart. I said:

"Am I too late in coming?"

"Ah, everyone has gone, and only now you come," said Mrs. Stephen, rising from the piano bench.

"I have hurried here all the way from Hangzhou!" And so I told them of the Hangzhou trip.

The company had indeed thinned out, but Dr. Philip and his wife, Mrs. Gao and Miss Gao, and Mrs. Manfield and her daughter were all still there. There were several others besides whom I did not know, and after Mrs. Stephen had introduced me, I asked Mrs. Gao:

"And Mr. Gao?"

"He had business and left early."

Helen Manfield was looking at me, but when I looked at her she turned her eyes away. I said:

"Miss Manfield, how stupid of me not to have known you at the concert the other day."

"…" She smiled shyly and said nothing.

"Mrs. Stephen, have I broken in upon your music by coming?" I said, moving nearer to her, and went on:

"Will you now play a piece for Miss Manfield, so that I may have the luck of hearing her lovely voice again?"

"You ought first to ask Miss Manfield."

So I said:

"Miss Manfield—if my request is not too forward."

Miss Manfield was a little flustered, and looked at her mother; but her mother encouraged her, and she went to the piano. I clapped, and everyone clapped. We sat down and held our breath; Mrs. Stephen and Miss Manfield settled on a piece—by Schubert, I think—and Miss Manfield, her back to us, filled the drawing-room with her singing, and filled my heart as well. She was no perfect singer, but she had a voice so sweet and full that her gift astonished me beyond measure; in her training, too, she had room to spare to put her own self into the song—a quiet, unassuming character glinting in faint grey ripples—and it woke in my heart a sense of old acquaintance. What sort of feeling was it? I could not paint it.

When the song ended everyone applauded, and only then did I wake from that strange sense of old acquaintance, and applaud with the rest.

—

The guests began to leave one after another; Mrs. Gao's car had by now brought Mr. Gao back again, Dr. Philip had a car of his own, and most of the guests had a man to escort them. At last I said:

"Mrs. Manfield, may I have the honour of seeing you home?"

"Won't it be too much trouble for you?"

"It is a great honour," I said.

I called a car, and once we were in it we somehow fell to talking of Chinese food; it turned out they had been to only one Chinese restaurant, and so I said:

"If it is not too late to get home after, will you let me stand the two of you a dinner now?"

Having their consent, I had the car drive to the Jinxiang. There I felt to the full Mrs. Manfield's warm kindliness and Miss Manfield's quiet gentleness. It was as though I had come upon another beautiful world—a natural, simple air, without clash, without waves, that stilled my cluttered mind, as muddy water settles into clearness; a warm and peaceful ease that made me envy the two of them, mother and daughter. And so, seeing them home when the dinner was over, I was invited by Mrs. Manfield to a plain supper at their house the next evening, and gladly agreed.

I watched them go into the Bakou Apartments, then, smoking alone, strolled from Route Lafayette by way of Route Massenet to Avenue Joffre. It was early yet, but Route Massenet was already deep in quiet, the leaves of the street-trees looking the more tender under the lamps, and the waning moon overhead uncommonly clear; matched with the calm of my mind, it made me feel the world might yet have corners worth its song, forever adorning our lives.

And yet—a dinner engagement; tomorrow again a dinner engagement. Was this not a life carrying me far from my own world? What I had resolved that day was to go back to my own world; I had left the world of Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping and Stephen—and was I now to step into yet another person's world? But this, after all, was another kind of world, a world calm and peaceful, tender and clear; could such an air, too, disturb the life I ought to live?

And so I thought of Helen—her dreamy smile, her singing full of feeling, her gentle, unhurried movements—which made me think of the lamp, and of what Mrs. Stephen had said at the ball. Just then, because I had walked, I suppose, into a place the street-lamps did not reach, the moonlight came scattering down through the trees, and I saw my own disordered shadow, and saw of a sudden that silver girl in that silver room, and how I had put out the lamp on her stand and come softly out of her room; that silver air which had seeped into my breast was the very sense of old acquaintance that Helen's singing had woken in me just now. Now the feeling rose again in my heart; I looked up into the void—the blue-black sky, the faint white clouds, the scattered stars and the bright moon—and a hidden desolation and a faint sorrow, in one instant, congealed into loneliness and solitude. I quickened my pace, cut through to Avenue Joffre, and boarded a tram.

I must have been tired; back home, I fell asleep before I had read three pages. After a spell of good sleep such as I had not had in a long while, I rose, bathed, and began, with a mind refreshed, to do the things I ought to do. I went out at ten to call on a friend, and at eleven went to see Bai Ping.

Bai Ping was already up, lightly made up and dressed in black, sitting on the sofa I had sat in the day before, eating chocolates and reading. At her feet slept a pure-white Persian cat. Knowing I had come in, she laid the book on her knee, raised her head, and said with a smile:

"Have you really hurried over to have lunch?"

"I thought you ought to have slept a little longer," I said. "When did you get up?"

"Not so very long ago," she said.

"Is the illness quite gone?"

"The fever seems to have passed."

I went over and felt her brow; the heat seemed to have gone down. I said:

"Have you a thermometer?"

She called Ah Mei, who took a thermometer and some alcohol from a drawer and gave them to me; as I wiped the thermometer with the alcohol, I said:

"Why did you not sleep a little longer?"

"There was a telephone call," said Bai Ping. "It woke me."

"Couldn't you just have said you weren't at home?"

"It was Stephen," said Bai Ping. "I thought the two of you were already together."

I put the thermometer between Bai Ping's lips and, holding her hand, looked at her wrist-watch. Bai Ping bent her head, took up the book on her knee with her right hand, and seemed to go on reading where she had left off.

Bai Ping was indeed without fever now. I said:

"Very good; but you ought still to rest."

"But Stephen has arranged to come and see me at the dance-hall this afternoon."

"You are going to the dance-hall again today?"

"Yes," she said with a smile. "Did you not want me to tell him of the change in your life? I think I shall manage it very well for you."

"Did they come back on the night train?"

"Yes. He said he telephoned you, but you had gone out." Then, changing her tone, she asked me: "Where did you go?"

"To see a friend—he had come to see me the day before yesterday, and yesterday, and missed me both times," I said. "Well, didn't you ask Stephen to come and dine with us?"

"No," she said with a smile. "From now on I'll ask no one else here; you may come whenever you like, but you are not to come and see me at the dance-hall."

"Very well."

"But if you let me run into you at the dance-hall, I shall treat you as no more than one of my dancing-patrons."

"Very well; but what if I come just once in a while?"

"Only if you have some formal engagement."

"Very well. I shall keep to it faithfully."

"Then you may come often, and bring your manuscript too," she said. "I can even set up a bed in the drawing-room next door, so that if it grows late you may stay the night here."

"You expect too much of me."

"Perhaps," she said. "But I won't have you entertaining friends here."

"Only I may come."

"Only you may come—to work," she said gravely. "What I mean is that if too many friends come to see you at your home, you may come here."

"And can you stay in too?" I said.

"I have my own world; why should I not go out?" she said, with pride and depth. "But when I am not here you may still come and go as you please, without minding me. If now and then we happen to meet, we'll take a meal together here, drink a cup of tea, and talk."

"And if now and then I want to go out walking with you?"

"A play or a film excepted," she said, "nothing else is allowed."

"You are too good, Bai Ping." My heart was full of a gratitude past telling.

"Don't think me good," said Bai Ping, with a proud self-assurance. "I am only making an experiment. People say I've led many a man into nothing but pleasure and away from his proper work; I mean, for once, to see whether I can also keep a man at my side doing the things he ought to do."

Just as I was about to say something, Ah Mei came in and asked whether the meal might be served. Bai Ping asked me:

"Hungry?"

"Ask yourself that," I said.

"Serve it," Bai Ping said to Ah Mei, after a moment's thought.

I went to the washroom, and when I had washed my hands and come out, Bai Ping had risen. She said:

"You have not been in my drawing-room yet."

She walked ahead, the Persian cat following, and I following too. We went into the room next door: outside its door was a coat-rack, with a raincoat hanging on it, and within were two rooms each the size of her bedroom, a silver-grey velvet hanging between them—on one side a drawing-room, on the other a dining-room. The drawing-room walls held several scrolls of painting and calligraphy, by Qi Baishi, Wu Changshuo and the like; here and there on the floor stood pots of flowers, and a small Japanese-style folding screen; four low soft stools ringed a round brass tray like the one in her bedroom, with matches, an ash-tray and a cigarette-box upon it; there were several grey sofas, a grey carpet on the floor, and a low table by each sofa—only, of a proper table, there was none. In the dining-room stood a wine-cabinet and a square table, spread with a grey cloth worked with yellow flowers at the four corners, and on it a glass fruit-bowl heaped with oranges. There were four chairs with grey cloth cushions, and in the corner two stands of potted flowers, all of trailing dwarf bamboo. The furniture was all of unpainted white wood, and the floor laid with a silver carpet. On the wall hung a painting, a landscape by Ren Bonian, and on one side a clock of the kind common in Dutch villages. I said:

"Are you so fond of silver, then?"

"Are you not?" she said, setting some glasses straight on the wine-cabinet.

"I love silver dearly, but I do not like it."

"What do you mean by that?"

"I love the mood of silver, but there always seems a hidden desolation in it, that often wakes in me a faint sorrow."

"Then what do you like?"

"White—pure white."

"I love white, but I do not like it."

"You mean…"

"I love its purity, but find it not profound," she said. "Do you not feel silver more profound than white?"

"Yes. White seems empty within, and silver seems to hold something within," I said; "but what is it within? A hidden desolation that wakes in one a faint sorrow."

"Perhaps," she said, gazing at the bottles on the wine-cabinet, and seeming to pay my talk little heed: "Will you have some wine?"

"Yes; but you are not to drink."

"I'll have just a little too," she said. "What will you take?"

"Grape wine," I said.

"I'll drink crème de menthe."

She poured me a glass of red grape wine, and for herself a glass of crème de menthe topped up with soda-water. She set the two glasses on the table—one deep red, one emerald green—with a bowl of golden American oranges between them, a colour so tempting that I fixed my eyes on it. Bai Ping sat down.

Ah Mei brought in the chopsticks, spoons and dishes; the chopsticks were silver, the bowls and plates plain and unpatterned, all of them white—no fine porcelain, but all most charming. The fare was three dishes and a soup, very simple. Bai Ping said not a word of ceremony to me, but bade Ah Mei go and make some coffee, and then raised her glass and said:

"With this glass," she said, "I wish your newly settled life to be forever green and evergreen as this." "And with this glass," I answered, "I wish you forever bright and beautiful and radiant."

We each took a sip, and we both laughed.

The food was delicious, and I ate a great deal. I said:

"This is the most beautiful meal of my whole life."

"Truly?" she said.

"Am I the first to dine with you like this, just the two of us?"

"And has that anything to do with your sense of beauty?"

"No," I said; "only that I should like a little honour beyond the beauty of it."

"There is no honour in it," she said.

"But I should not wish to be like the Japanese who went with you to Hangzhou."

"Mei Yingzi told you that?" she said.

"Yes."

"Then which of the eleven Japanese, men and women, who travelled with us are you jealous of?" she said.

"Have you entertained them all here?"

"Do you suppose this room could entertain eleven guests?"

"In any case, a Japanese walking into your room displeases me as much as their army walking into our country."

"Do you really think my address is a thing I'd give to a person of any nation at all?" she said, knitting her brows.

"Then—then you have not deceived me?"

"And if I had?"

"Then my feeling, beyond the beauty of it, is one of insult."

"I do not lie," she said seriously; "but to you it may be an insult still."

"You mean…"

"I mean that when I dine at home alone, there is, every day, a Persian sitting in your seat to keep me company."

"And who is that?" I laughed, not knowing whether there was any tint of jealousy in my smile, and said: "Bai Ping, tell me."

"He is right beside us now." Without looking at me, she took a piece of fish with her chopsticks, laid it in the spoon-dish and pushed it over, and called: "Jimi."

"Miaow…" I saw the white Persian cat climb up from a chair.

I laughed; Bai Ping went on watching the cat eat its fish.

Ah Mei came in and took two knives from the wine-cabinet drawer, giving one to Bai Ping and one to me. I began to cut an orange; Bai Ping, still watching the cat, said to Ah Mei without even raising her head:

"Take the coffee to my room."

"Will you have some fruit?" I said.

"No." She raised her head and said, smiling: "Thank you."

Ah Mei gave us hand-towels, and Bai Ping stood up and said:

"Let us go and sit through there."

Jimi followed her, and I followed her, and I heard the clock just striking one—a very plain, curt sound that I did not care for.
