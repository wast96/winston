## 14

The news of Shanghai's liberation spread from the mouths of a band of routed Nationalist soldiers. After looting the town's rice shop, butcher, cake shop, and clothier, they shouted a few words about taking to the hills as guerrillas and hurried out of town, vanishing into the far edge of a plain woven thick with waterways. Xietang soon returned to quiet, hardly different from its days before. Lin Nansheng went to work as usual at St. Aloysius Middle School. He was by now the most popular history and English teacher there; like the many men and women stranded in this town, he had made the place his own home.

That day the headmaster burst suddenly into his classroom. "The comrades from the work team are here. They're waiting for you in the office."

The two who had come for Lin Nansheng were young men not much older than his students, in yellow army uniforms and yellow army caps. They had come to re-register the household rolls. The moment they met, one of them said, "Name."

"Lin Qiuming."

"Date of birth."

"The nineteenth of November, 1912."

"Native place?"

"Fuyang, Zhejiang."

"How did you come to be here?"

"Fleeing the war."

"Now that liberation has come, why not go back to your home village?"

"There's no one left at home." Lin Nansheng said, "In the bombing of 1937, the family was gone."

The young soldier softened his tone. "Marital status."

Lin Nansheng looked at the teachers in the office, lowered his eyes, and said, "Widowed."

In truth, over this past year and more it was not that no one had tried to make a match for him — what war leaves most of in this world is orphans and widows. But Lin Nansheng declined them all, one by one. To each he said the same one sentence: This suits me well enough; I won't go dragging anyone else down.

The people of Xietang all thought Teacher Lin set his sights high, that he looked down on uneducated women. But Lin Nansheng alone knew, in his heart, that his days in this world were few. His body no longer felt the heat in summer, nor, come winter, the cold.

One day just before New Year's that year, with no wind and no snow, it was strangely cold, and a thick sheet of ice had formed in the water jar at home. Lin Nansheng had climbed a bamboo ladder and helped the gatekeeper hang a red lantern at the school gate when he saw a military jeep come up trailing a road's worth of dust. Two hours later that same jeep bore Lin Nansheng out of Xietang, trailing the same dust, and after a full half-day on the road drove into the city of Shanghai just as the lamps were coming on at nightfall.

When Lin Nansheng saw Ji Zhongyuan in an office of the Shanghai Public Security Bureau, he said mildly, "Why go to such lengths to bring me here?"

Ji Zhongyuan wore a khaki serge uniform, yet look as you might, he still seemed the seal-engraver of former days. He smiled. "We've been looking for you nearly two years."

As he spoke he took a box of files from the cupboard, and said that when he had come back from Hong Kong at the end of 1948 to take over Old Pan's work, he had begun a secret search for Lin Nansheng. He did not believe that an agent like Lin Nansheng would have died crossing the blockade line.

Lin Nansheng said, "Could you not have taken me for truly dead?"

Ji Zhongyuan shook his head, opened the box of files, and let Lin Nansheng see for himself. These were the reports sent up from below, a record of his every move in Xietang. It turned out that Lin Nansheng had been under watch as long ago as half a year before. The one who had informed on him was a village head in the town, once an outer-ring man cultivated by the Baomiju, who had glimpsed Lin Nansheng once, from a distance, in Shanghai. Only, the local public security office held firmly to the belief that a major agent hiding in a small town must have some great conspiracy behind him. They wanted to pay out a long line and land the big fish.

"These materials only came into my hands recently." Ji Zhongyuan said, "We need you to come back."

Lin Nansheng said, "I was watched half a year and never sensed it. I am no longer an agent."

"But we won't forget your contribution," Ji Zhongyuan said. "You shouldn't be sitting in a small town as a teacher."

"I was a teacher to begin with. My dream was always to be a teacher." Lin Nansheng turned to look at the charcoal still burning in the fireplace, and before his eyes rose again the sight of Zhu Yizhen in her school uniform. In those days she had worn her hair in a child's bob.

"And if this is an order?" As he spoke Ji Zhongyuan rose, took a letter of appointment from the desk, and put it into Lin Nansheng's hand. "The revolution has succeeded, but our fight is far from over." Then he looked at Lin Nansheng gravely and added, "This is the trust the organization places in you."

Now Lin Nansheng's daily work was to put in order the files left over from those years, to pick out from them the long-broken threads, and in the end to find those people and fix and name their identities. Lin Nansheng began to drink again. Home from work, the first thing he did was pour a glass of strong spirits and drink it down mouthful by mouthful, until he was dull and reeling.

It was the only way left to him of falling asleep.

One day in May, hotter than the weather was the fervor of the people raising money for the Volunteer Army. The streets of Shanghai were everywhere hung with slogans and cries to Resist America and Aid Korea, to Defend the Home and Country — and yet, crowding onto a tram, Lin Nansheng suddenly collapsed.

When he woke he could no longer move. The pitch-dark ward held no sound, and no other patient; it was like lying in his own grave — the end he had foreseen countless times. In the darkness Lin Nansheng quietly looked back over his life, and found that in this world he had neither friend nor kin, and that all he had left was the memories in his head.

Early on the third day, after Ji Zhongyuan had visited him, he stepped into a shop on his way back to the office, picked up the telephone on the counter, dialed a number, and said, "Put me through to the Jing'an District Committee."

That afternoon Zhu Yizhen came into the ward with a paper bag of apples in her arms. It was the first time they had met since his return to Shanghai, though both knew their workplaces stood only a few blocks apart. Zhu Yizhen worked at the Jing'an District Committee and lodged in the municipal government's single-persons' dormitory. Many a night, when no amount of drink would let him sleep, Lin Nansheng would go out from home alone, walk to the foot of her dormitory building, stand a while, take one look at that lighted window, and then go home to drink on.

Zhu Yizhen sat by the bed and, without a word, peeled a whole apple, then fed it to him slice by slice.

"And your husband? Why didn't the two of you come together?" Lin Nansheng looked at the fruit knife in her hand, and suddenly smiled. "This may be the last time we see each other."

Zhu Yizhen did not answer. She only lowered her eyes, looking too at the fruit knife in her hand.

Not long after Shanghai's liberation, Meng Annan had submitted a report to the organization asking to return to his country and join the war of resistance against the French, under Ho Chi Minh. But the answer he received was an order to march with the Third Field Army to the Fujian front. On the eve of parting he said to Zhu Yizhen, "It's all right that you won't marry me. But you owe me the reason, at least."

Zhu Yizhen gazed calmly into his deep-set eyes, and it was a long time before she said, "I have a husband come back from the dead, and I have a lover whose fate is unknown. Tell me — how could I marry you?"

Meng Annan said not one word more. He watched Zhu Yizhen turn and go into her room and softly shut the door. The whole night through he sat at her door, leaning against his own field pack, and only at daybreak did he rise and leave without a sound.

There was a distraction about Zhu Yizhen the whole while. As she folded the fruit knife shut, she suddenly, for no reason, smiled, raised her eyes to Lin Nansheng, and said, "We were such fools."

Lin Nansheng tried to sit up, but his muscles would not answer him. He could only look, rigid, at the woman before him, and after a moment said, "Still — I got to see you after all."
