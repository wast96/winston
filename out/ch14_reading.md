## Chapter Fourteen. "Secret Number One"

### 1. The Central Archive

Chen Tao went by the foreign nickname "UvdenoB" and billed himself a half-baked revolutionary; he latched onto the revolution, played the rake, was the leading man in the West Lake hotel sex scandal, and was called a lecher; in his day he, Ah-Shuang, and Luosan passed for men about town. He loved to regale Luosan with a couplet a fellow provincial named Shu had once presented to a courtesan. (It ran: "A hundred men call her a marvel, a thousand call her a wonder; once she is at her dressing table they would gladly be her ox and horse; one glance of hers topples a city, a second topples a kingdom; they would keep her in a house of gold, and she would breed them dragons and serpents.") Luosan and Ah-Shuang praised it to the skies, and pronounced the author a man of matchless talent, worth recruiting as one of their own. (Luo Zhanglong, 2005)

Luo Zhanglong thought the world of himself and had spent a lifetime going his own way, with no time for anyone; so even in his last years, guttering like a candle, he was still naming names and giving no quarter, cursing them one by one, damning "the deserters and beaten generals of the Great Revolution," "the empty-headed young lords, all bluster and swagger," "the 'nine-tailed turtles' of the ten-li foreign quarter," "the vile hangers-on and kept men," all of them: Ah-Shuang, Luosan, Chen Tao, every one of them cursed till the blood ran down.

Ah-Shuang was Qu Qiubai, Luosan was Li Lisan, and Chen Tao was Chen Weiren.

Chen Weiren, said Luo Zhanglong, "played the rake and did evil without end," "forced himself on the mill girls, stopped at nothing," and was one of Xiang Zhongfa's and Li Lisan's "rich idlers of the underground court," a man who "cultivated and practiced a debauched and shameless way of life."

But the most moving thing about Chen Weiren's life is precisely that he was "frugal and careful, disciplined and devoted to the public good," and that on a broken constitution he kept the Central Archive safe for the Party.

By then Chen Weiren had been let out of prison "with his sentence served too hard on him, both legs numb, his tuberculosis flared up again" (Han Huiying, 2005). The organization arranged for him to rest and recover quietly, but he begged, over and over, to keep working, even if it meant going from a senior Party leader (Chen Weiren joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1921 and had served as secretary of the CCP's Northern Workers' Movement Committee, head of the organization department of the Northern Region Executive Committee, secretary and concurrently secretary-general of the Manchurian Provisional Committee, and secretary of the Manchurian Provincial Committee) down to an ordinary clerk inside a Party organ, tasked with nothing but keeping documents.

In July 1926 the CCP Center convened an enlarged Executive Committee meeting in Shanghai and passed the "Resolution on Organizational Questions," which laid down clear rules for "the Party's organs": it stipulated that "a Central Secretariat should be added, to take overall charge of the Center's technical work." This was an important rule. By insisting on "sound organization suited to need" and "a full and responsible staff," it guaranteed "the soundness of the Party's organs" and guaranteed that "the Party's political and technical duties" would be carried out through proper "organs." But from the birth of the Central Secretariat in September 1926, to the Center's reminder in the April 1930 "Letter to Comrades of the Central Departments and Committees on Secret Work" that all "documents not needed must be sent at once to the document depository for safekeeping," to the Center's April 1931 "Regulations on the Disposal of Documents," which set out by rule how files were to be gathered, sorted, arranged, catalogued, kept, and destroyed, the centralized custody of the Center's documents took time to develop. And in that process the parts played by Zhang Weiyi, Qu Qiubai, and Chen Weiren stand out above the rest.

At that time the Central Secretariat had five sections under it, and Zhang Weiyi was head of the documents section; its staff included Zhang Yuexia and Zhang Ji'en. This section handled the stencil-cutting, the mimeographing, the sending and receiving of documents, and the secret writing in chemical inks. Each of these jobs was done separately, and all of them were highly secret. The Center's documents and meeting minutes were made in three copies: one kept by the Center, one sent to the Comintern in the Soviet Union, and one sent by the Special Branch to be kept out in the countryside. (Maomao, 1997)

That passage comes from Maomao (Deng Rong), relaying an interview with Huang Jieran, who had been Chen Duxiu's secretary and secretary-general of the CCP Central Secretariat. He says "the Center's documents and meeting minutes were made in three copies," of which "one was sent by the Special Branch to be kept out in the countryside." Whereas the *Biography of Chen Weiren* (1997) puts it this way: "At that time the Center's archives were divided into three sets. One was kept in the Secretariat's document-security office; there were two more, one held by the Comintern's CCP delegation and later entrusted to the Comintern's keeping, and another that the Center entrusted to Gu Shunzhang."

The "Secretariat's document-security office" must mean the "document depository" that Zhang Weiyi ran, which is Huang Jieran's "documents section."

Zhang Weiyi, born in 1892, from Taoyuan in Hunan, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1927, and was known as "the Old Master."

In September 1930 the Center resolved to trim its own organs, abolishing across the board every "office" below the section level, in the name of "a new organizational method" that promised "faster organization over a wider range." So Zhang Weiyi set up the "Central Archive" at No. 1141 Gordon Road (that is, Hengjili, today No. 10, Lane 673, Jiangning Road), to hide away six trunks holding more than twenty thousand "precious documents and materials."

This trove of precious documents and materials, six trunks and more than twenty thousand items, held every kind of meeting paper of the CCP Center, such as the resolutions, decisions, and minutes of the successive Party Congresses; papers the CCP Center had issued publicly, such as manifestos, circular telegrams, and appeals to the workers, the peasants, and the soldiers; the cables passing between the CCP Center and the Comintern; and also the local Party organizations' reports and requests for instructions, their survey materials, their minutes, their internal journals, and the papers of the branches of students in France and Germany; the papers of the various revolutionary mass organizations; and some materials left behind by famous revolutionary martyrs. These documents reflect, fairly completely, the activity and the historical face of the CCP, and are among the most precious of its records. (Maomao, 1997)

The Central Archive was the first underground document repository in our Party's history, the "Secret Number One" of our Party's early memory.

Seeing that the Central Archive kept taking in more and more documents, with nothing but the simplest register at intake and no sorting or arranging by category, Zhou Enlai had Qu Qiubai draft a scheme, a few workable measures set down in writing. Qu Qiubai was meticulous to a hair, and quickly produced seven points, taking up in turn the scope of what to collect, the principles for classifying and cataloguing, and the format for registering and recording, down to the fine instruction that everything in the archive be "arranged by date," with a strict "note the year, month, and day; the fuller the better."

The "Regulations on the Disposal of Documents" that Qu Qiubai drafted is the earliest document in the history of the CCP's archives that can still be traced. Zhou Enlai wrote on the regulation in his own hand: "Give it a trial run, and see whether it works." Zhou Enlai and Qu Qiubai were of one mind. The "ideal" they wanted was an archive both convenient "for consultation (available for withdrawal, that is, and return)" and fit "to hand on to the future party-history committee (when the country is ours)."

Gu Shunzhang's defection nearly wrecked that hope of "handing it on to the future" in a single stroke.

### 2. Not One Scrap of Paper to Fall into Enemy Hands

In his interview with Maomao (Deng Rong), Huang Jieran said that the portion of the Center's documents sent by the Special Branch to be kept in the countryside was never lost, and was all recovered after the founding of New China. This is plainly mistaken. Back then Huang Jieran had helped Zhou Enlai with the emergency, dealing at speed with Gu Shunzhang's defection, and he ought to have known about that portion of the Center's documents. It was precisely because "the Center had entrusted them to Gu Shunzhang's keeping" that, the moment Gu turned, Zhou Enlai sent Chen Geng and others after them, to run them down at all costs.

In April 1931, Gu Shunzhang was escorting Zhang Guotao to the Soviet areas; on the way back to Shanghai he stopped over in Wuhan, was arrested, and promptly turned traitor. But for fear of implicating his father-in-law and family, he said nothing about the documents, and secretly sent someone to destroy them in secret. (Lü Fangwen, 1997)

Why did Gu Shunzhang "say nothing about the documents"? Because "he feared implicating his father-in-law and family." Why would that "implicate his father-in-law and family"? Because that portion of the Center's documents had been kept in trust by "his father-in-law and family."

On January 15, 1982, at a meeting in Shanghai, Li Qiang said in his remarks: "Hong Yangsheng burrowed everywhere and was busy everywhere, but there were plenty of things I knew that he couldn't tell you. For instance, Gu Shunzhang left a batch of documents with his father-in-law for safekeeping. Hong Yangsheng didn't know where they were kept. I did, because I'd been to the place myself."

On April 25, 1931, Gu Shunzhang, then the chief officer of the Central Special Branch, was arrested in Hankou and promptly turned. He had command of every Central organ in Shanghai and the addresses of the Central leaders, and knew the ways of secret work like the back of his hand. Zhou Enlai sent word to Zhang Weiyi at once to move all the documents immediately. Zhang Weiyi hired two rickshaws and, through the night, hauled the six trunks of documents in several loads to a freestanding little house in the French Concession on Rue Kraetzer (today Jinling Middle Road), which was his home. So "the Central Archive" escaped disaster once more. (Dong Shaodong, 2013)

Zhang Weiyi first moved the Central Archive back to his own home, then handed it to Ling Bing, a leader of the Hunan peasant movement, for safekeeping. After the "January 28th" Shanghai War, Ling Bing in turn handed the Central Archive to Chen Weiren. Once Chen Weiren formally took it over, he moved all the documents in secret to his home in Mingyue New Village on Kinnear Road (today Wuding West Road). It was a three-story house with its own entrance: the ground floor was a bedroom that doubled as a sitting room; the second floor a bedroom; the third floor was made over into a little loft, where a plank wall was built two feet from the inner wall, and the documents were stored in the space between.

From then on, Chen Weiren and his wife Han Huiying lived apart from everyone, joining no Party meetings, taking part in no public activity, striking up no friendships lightly. By day he was the proprietor of a Xiang embroidery shop; by night he bolted the doors and windows, sealed off every chink of light, and spent whole nights, night after night, in the third-floor loft, putting the Party's "Secret Number One" in order. To make it easier to hide and to move, he copied out every document afresh on thin paper in tiny characters, trimmed off the blank margins on all four sides, and filed them by category. Beside his desk stood a stove kept banked with live embers the year round, ready, at the first sign of trouble, to burn the loft and the house down: not one scrap of paper would be allowed to fall into enemy hands!

### 3. I Will Not Die; I Still Have Work to Do

In February 1935, a traitor's tip-off led to the arrest, one after another, of Zhang Weiyi and Han Huiying. Chen Weiren, "to guard against exposure and avoid a loss, moved house at once, took the alias Zhang Huigao, and with the whole 'archive' and his three young children moved to a two-story house at No. 15 Hexingfang, near the corner of Ferry Road and Connaught Road (today near Xikang Road and Kangding Road)." (Xu Qiang, 1980)

From this time on, Chen Weiren's contact with the Party organization was cut off, and he fell into terrible distress: on top of shouldering, alone, the heavy charge of guarding the Central Archive, he still had to feed and care for the three children at his side. On the day Han Huiying was arrested, there was nothing left in the house, and they got through the day on two meals of sweet potato. Chen Weiren, coughing blood from his lung disease, had to make do with raw turnip for fruit. To keep up his cover and fool the landlord downstairs, every time Chen Weiren had boiled the sweet potatoes downstairs he would lay a strip of dried fish across the top and carry them up (a sign that life was normal); then, as he neared the door upstairs, he would tuck that strip of dried fish away again, for fear the little ones, too young to understand, would see it and clamor for a bite. That one strip of dried fish lasted a full month that way. (Lü Fangwen, 1988)

In the autumn of 1936, when Chen Weiren made contact with Xu Qiang (in her *Autobiography*, Chen Weiren's widow Han Huiying wrote that he was "surnamed Jin, given name unknown") and, as required, delivered the last trunk of documents, he came home and could not stop the blood pouring from his mouth. The organization said: "So long as we can save Weiren, spend whatever it takes." But Chen Weiren flatly refused to let the organization spend a cent more on him. To the very last moment of his life, Chen Weiren, both fists still clenched and his eyes wide open, murmured to his wife, herself just out of prison: "Don't be afraid, I'm all right... I will not die, I still have work to do..."

At about nine on the evening of March 12, 1937, Chen Weiren was pronounced past saving, and died before his time.

From 1937 to 1939, the Central Archive was kept in turn by Xu Qiang, Li Yun, Zhou Tianbao, Wu Chengfang, and Liu Zhao.

In February 1939 the CCP Center established the Central Social Affairs Department. Pan Hannian took up the post of head of the department's second bureau, and together with Kong Yuan (real name Chen Tiezheng, aliases Tian Fu and Tian Xin, born 1906, from Pingxiang in Jiangxi, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1925, and had served as head of the organization department of the CCP Jiangsu Provincial Committee, head of the organization department of the Central Organization Bureau, and the Center's resident representative in the North) and Zeng Xisheng (formerly named Zeng Mian, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1927, and had served as secretary-general of the Military Commission of the CCP Yangtze Bureau, chief of the intelligence section of the Central Military Commission, chief of the reconnaissance section of the Red Army General Headquarters, and head of the Second Bureau of the Central Military Commission) took special charge of intelligence.

On August 25, the CCP Politburo issued the "Decision on Consolidating the Party," pressing further the demand that "the Party's secret work must be intensified," drawing a strict line between "the Party's open organs and its secret organizations" and between "open members and secret members," and holding that "open members should on no account double as staff of the Party's secret work."

On October 10, the CCP Center issued the "Resolution on the Struggle Against Spies and Traitors," requiring that "the central bureaus, regional Party committees, and provincial committees, wherever they have cadres politically firm and loyal and equal to the job, must set up a dedicated social affairs department." The Center held that "the enemy of the nation and the enemy of the class are now plotting harm against the Communist Party, the Eighth Route Army, and the New Fourth Army harder than at any time before," seen concretely in how "the traitor Wang's Han-traitors have lately, in Shanghai and elsewhere, used special-service work and assassination to carry on their capitulationist, anti-Communist activity and practice individual terror," and in how "the anti-Communist elements, since the Kuomintang's Fifth Plenum, have issued their so-called measures for curbing the dissident party, set up an anti-Communist special-service committee, and dispatched agents everywhere to carry on anti-Communist special-service work." Therefore "the whole Party and the whole army must raise their revolutionary vigilance to the highest degree."

It was against this backdrop that Pan Hannian became deputy head of the Central Social Affairs Department, charged with building the South China Intelligence Bureau and directing intelligence work in Hong Kong and Shanghai.

### 4. "The Little Boss"

In the spring of 1939, Pan Hannian was sent to Hong Kong and Shanghai to open a covert struggle against the enemy. That autumn the Center approved the founding of the South China Intelligence Bureau, with Pan in charge, assisted by Zhang Weiyi, Li Monong, and others; its leading body was set up in Hong Kong, and it worked across Shanghai, Hong Kong, and the South Seas. To assist Pan's covert activity and carry on intelligence work, the Central Social Affairs Department sent more than ten back-office cadres to Shanghai. (Liu Renshou and He Luo, 1995)

The South China Intelligence Bureau took charge at two ends: one end was Hong Kong, the other Shanghai.

The Hong Kong end broke down further into four parts. The first was the intelligence post of the Eighth Route Army's Hong Kong Office, run by Li Monong (that is, Li Shaoshi, original name Guojun, also called Zhen, born 1906, from Xinhui in Guangdong, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1926, and in 1930 married Liao Mengxing, daughter of Liao Zhongkai and He Xiangning; he set up a secret courier station in Hong Kong), with Lian Guan, Ke Lin, Xu Mingcheng, and others as its main members. The second was the intelligence post of the Northeast Anti-Japanese United Army's Hong Kong Office, run by Dong Lin'ge (that is, Dong Haiping, born 1900, from Jilin, secretary to General Li Du of the Northeast Anti-Japanese United Army and head of the Army's Hong Kong Office), with radio sets in Tianjin, Chongqing, and Hong Kong. The third was the Soviet intelligence post in Hong Kong, run by Zhu Bosheng (a liaison officer of the Eighth Route Army's Office in the Soviet Union), with Jin Zhonghua (born 1907, from Tongxiang in Zhejiang, at the time an executive member of the "China Defence League") and Shao Zonghan (born 1907, from Wujin in Jiangsu, at the time chief editor of the *Sing Tao Daily*), among others, as its main members. The fourth was Kong Xiangxi's political and economic adviser Hu Egong.

The Shanghai end fell into two stages. The first was the stage of the Eighth Route Army's Shanghai Office, that is, from August 1937 to June 1939, under Liu Shaowen, Xu Qiang, and Wu Chengfang. Wu Chengfang is the "Wu Jiguang" of the "Record of the Interrogation of Li Desheng of the CCP Espionage Ring." Li Desheng confessed to Section 1 of the Tokkō of the Japanese Metropolitan Police Board: "Wu Jiguang, also called Wu Yuanrong, ordinarily known as Old Wu, was head of the Shanghai intelligence section (during which time, in the winter of 1937, he went briefly to work for the CCP Jiangsu Provincial Committee)." The second stage came when the "Eighth Route Office" pulled out: Pan Hannian had Gong Yinbing take over the people Liu Shaowen handed off, Wu Chengfang, Tan Chong'an, and the rest, and at the same time set up the Li Bai station, the Yang Jiansheng station, and the Zheng Zhizhong station, reporting directly to Yan'an on a regular schedule. He had Shi Yong (real name Sha Wenwei, courtesy name Chongshu, born 1910, from Ningbo in Zhejiang, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1934, and worked in intelligence in Shanghai, Nanjing, Hankou, and Chongqing throughout), whom the Jiangsu Provincial Committee had transferred in, and others burrow into the enemy and puppet ranks. And he had Liu Renshou, Dong Hui (original name Dong Fengran, born 1918, ancestral home in Guangdong, raised in Hong Kong, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1938, and married Pan Hannian in 1947), and more than ten other back-office cadres of the Central Social Affairs Department out of Yan'an, along with Qiao Liqing (alias Cao Yachen, from Shanxi, at the time an editor of the fortnightly *Shisheng*) and Meng Shuxian (formerly head of the Northeast Anti-Japanese United Army's Tianjin Office and deputy head of the liaison department of the "Northeast General Command"), set up several intelligence posts and put a station on the third floor of No. 46, Lane 710, Rue Amiral Bayle (today Huangpi South Road) in the French Concession, to "report to Hong Kong and Yan'an and monitor the news of Xinhua." (Chen Bangben, 2004)

By this time, Zhang Zhizhong, Ye Zhongying, Tang Qi (Ye's mother), and Zhu Jiefu (Ye's stepfather), graduates of the Yan'an Communications School, had come to Shanghai. Ye was the family of a martyr; Zhu was an old comrade tested in the enemy's prisons. The organization arranged for a radio station at No. 46, Lane 710, Huangpi South Road, to report to Yan'an. But it kept calling and getting no answer, so the organization asked me to help, and I got it through. Besides reporting to Yan'an, this station also copied down Xinhua news dispatches. It stopped working only when it was raided in the summer of 1942. (Liu Renshou, 1999)

In the same period, the organization once again "arranged" for Liu Zhao, who worked under Wu Chengfang, to pack up the whole Central Archive afresh, disguised as "personal clothing," and move it to a freestanding little house at Shengshengli on Connaught Road (today Lane 1119, Kangding Road) in the International Settlement, where it was stored for the time being in the tingzijian and kept by Miao Guren.

### 5. To Silence Herself Forever

Miao Guren, also called Qingshang, aliases Li Nianci and Chen Yiming, was born in 1915, from Jiangyin in Jiangsu (today Wuxi). He joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1927, was arrested in 1932, released in 1935, came to Shanghai in 1937, went back to Chengxi in 1938 to do local armed work, then returned to Shanghai to work as a senior clerk of the city chamber of commerce, secretly running medical supplies to the New Fourth Army in northern Jiangsu, very much like "the Little Boss" in *No. 51 Depot*.

Once he formally took over from Liu Zhao as the Central Archive's new keeper, he too, like Ling Bing and Chen Weiren before him, lived apart from everyone: no more Party meetings, no more public activity, no more easy friendships, no more seeing whomever he pleased. Even with Wu Chengfang, whom he knew well and counted a close comrade-in-arms, he broke off direct contact, and kept in touch entirely through the courier, young Zheng.

The courier between Wu and Miao, young Zheng, was Zheng Wendao, also known as Cheng Hesheng, born 1914, from Zhongshan in Guangdong. He joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1938, and that September joined the CCP's covert front, serving as the liaison between Wu Chengfang and Nakanishi.

When he first started working with Nakanishi, Cheng Hesheng found the idea of a Japanese helping China resist Japan somehow past believing. In time he came to see that Nakanishi was pouring out his heart and strength for the resistance exactly as a Chinese would, and there grew in him a respect for the man he could hardly put into words. Nakanishi and he were both CCP members. Both knew clearly that a Communist should be ready to give his life for the cause of the revolution, and that even knowing the danger of it, he should work and hold on for the good of the revolution. So Nakanishi said: "This is how we are now. Some morning, or some night, the Japanese gendarmes may come and take me; well, I will still keep to my oath, I will not give up any secret of the organization." Cheng Hesheng's eyes went damp and red, and he answered him quietly: "Sir, I can tell you this: once I am taken, I will not let slip one word about you." (Fang Zhida, Liang Yan, and Chen Sanbai, 1995)

Zheng Wendao kept his word, and redeemed his promise with his own young life.

It was October 4, 1941. Sorge sent Moscow his last, and perhaps his most important, cable: "The Soviet Far East may be considered secure; the threat from the Japanese side has been removed. Japan cannot launch a war against the Soviet Union. On the contrary, Japan will go to war with the United States within the coming weeks." Moscow soon cabled back, well satisfied with his work, and declared that the mission of Sorge and his "Ramsay" group was accomplished.

On October 10, Miyagi Yotoku, one of the "Ramsay" group's core members, was arrested because of the arrest of a woman under him who also worked as a seamstress. The gendarmes tortured Miyagi savagely; Miyagi threw himself from a building to kill himself, but branches broke his fall, leaving him with broken bones and grave injuries. The gendarmes still would not let up and went on with the torture. Miyagi could not hold out, and gave up several other core members, namely Ozaki Hotsumi, Clausen, and Clausen's wife Anna.

On October 15, Ozaki Hotsumi was arrested. Unable to bear the torture, he gave up Sorge. But he did not know that Sorge's true identity was that of an agent of the Intelligence Directorate of the Soviet Red Army General Staff; he knew only that Sorge worked for the Comintern.

On October 16, the evidence being conclusive, the Japanese procuracy submitted to Justice Minister Iwamura a request for the arrest of those in the case. Iwamura approved the warrants for the important prisoners other than Sorge, including Saionji Kinkazu, Inukai Takeru (son of a former prime minister), and Clausen. But because the Foreign Ministry objected, Sorge's own warrant was approved instead by Tōjō Hideki. There is also an account that it was Konoe Fumimaro who approved the warrant for Sorge. But since the entire Konoe cabinet resigned en masse that morning, it is unlikely that Konoe Fumimaro ordered Sorge's arrest.

On October 18, Clausen and Sorge were arrested. Anna gave up the truth. Anna, married to Clausen, had once been vetted by the Intelligence Directorate of the Soviet Red Army General Staff; when Sorge and Clausen went to Japan, its women agents had even harassed and threatened her.

Nakanishi and the others had no organizational tie to Ozaki Hotsumi in their intelligence work, only an intelligence-exchange relationship, especially after September 1938. After Ozaki Hotsumi's arrest, Nakanishi and the rest went on working fearlessly, and the Japanese police found them out through the interrogations in the "Comintern espionage ring" affair, beginning the arrests in June 1942. At that point Nakanishi was still in Hangzhou, on survey and intelligence work connected with the Zhejiang-Jiangxi Campaign, and two "Tokkō" men from Tokyo had already come to Shanghai to wait for them. (Xie Xueshi, 2004)

The arrests of Nakanishi and of Nishizato Tatsuo (of whom the "Record of the Interrogation of Li Desheng of the CCP Espionage Ring" says he was "a member of the Shanghai intelligence section, openly the chief correspondent of the Nanjing branch of Japan's Dōmei News Agency, and a member of the Nanjing cell when it was formed in 1940") were carried out, under the direction of a prosecutor of the Tokyo Criminal Court, by the police of the Japanese Consulate-General in Shanghai, and three Chinese were arrested along with them. One of the three was Zheng Wendao.

Zheng Wendao's arrest threw Wu Chengfang into keen alarm. Wu Chengfang rushed to Miao Guren's home at once.

By then Miao Guren and his wife had quietly moved the Central Archive out of the freestanding little house at Shengshengli on Connaught Road and into their own home, in the third-floor loft at Jiayunfang, Jinjiaxiang (today Lane 175, Jiaozhou Road, and Lane 1851, Xinzha Road).

When he heard that Zheng Wendao had been arrested, Miao Guren, on his sickbed, fell at once into a fit of coughing he could not stop, spitting up mouthfuls of blood.

Miao Guren by this time was like Chen Weiren: not only did he have severe tuberculosis, but his condition was worsening by the day, and Wu Chengfang had meant to find another reliable comrade to take over.

Wu Chengfang signaled to Miao's wife to help Miao Guren lie down and rest a while. But Miao Guren struggled out of bed, set on climbing to the third-floor loft to shift things about, his gaunt face flushed red, whether from urgency or from coughing there was no telling. Unable to move him, Wu Chengfang could only go first to arrange transport and a new home for the archive.

As for Zheng Wendao, from the moment of his arrest his whole mind held only one thing, the words he had spoken to Nakanishi's face: "Sir, I can tell you this: once I am taken, I will not let slip one word about you." And the surest way to make good on that "not let slip" was to shut his own mouth forever, to fall utterly silent.

As the car carrying Zheng Wendao passed the corner of Kiangse Road (today Jiangxi Middle Road) and Sanma Road, he gave a great shout, sprang up all at once, tore free of his escorts, hurled himself from the moving car, and struck his head hard against the racing wheels.

Seven days later he came out of his coma to find himself lying in the Japanese gendarmerie headquarters, and, forcing himself past the pain that racked his whole body, he made a second desperate attempt on his own life. This time he flung himself not from a jolting car but from a fourth-floor window. In that last instant, his blood spattering, he fixed forever an unconquerable, faithful soul!

### 6. The Archive Comes Home to Us

After the Sorge affair broke, the Japanese of the CCP's Shanghai intelligence section, the CCP member Nakanishi (openly a staff member of the Shanghai office of the South Manchuria Railway's Research Department) and the CCP member Nishizato Tatsuo (openly the chief correspondent of the Nanjing branch of Japan's Dōmei News Agency), were arrested, and the head of the Shanghai intelligence section's Nanjing cell, Li Desheng, together with Wang Jinyuan, Chen Yifeng (real name Ni Zhaoyu), and Zheng Wendao (alias Cheng Hesheng), were promptly arrested as well. The "Record of the Interrogation of Li Desheng of the CCP Espionage Ring" is the record of sixteen court examinations of Li Desheng conducted by Section 1 of the Tokkō of the Japanese Metropolitan Police Board from September 1942 (the seventeenth year of Shōwa) to January of the next year. In the course of them Li Desheng gave up the organization, personnel, daily activity, methods of contact, priorities, means of obtaining and passing intelligence, and the intelligence itself, of the Shanghai intelligence section. Li Desheng's answers were exhaustive, down to the last detail, and they give us today a rare firsthand source for understanding the Shanghai intelligence section's work in full. (Cheng Zhaoqi, 2013)

In that confession, "exhaustive, down to the last detail," not one word touched on Miao Guren; which shows that however sincerely Li Desheng "repented" and however hard he "cooperated," he could get nowhere near the Central Archive; which is itself proof enough that the Central Archive was indeed the secret of secrets, the innermost of the inner, and which throws into ever sharper relief that Zheng Wendao was "great in life, glorious in death." He kept faith with his comrades and did his duty to the work.

In June 1942, as Miao Guren's condition worsened, he had no choice but to hand the Central Archive to Chen Laisheng (originally surnamed Zhen, born 1919, a Shanghai man, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1938). Chen Laisheng moved it the way ants move house: he set the whole family to carrying, each of them smuggling a few items at a time, back and forth without end, using bamboo baskets, rice-washing baskets, and flour sacks, until, after a full month and more, he had brought all twenty thousand-odd precious documents safely to Gengqingli, on the same Xinzha Road (that is, Lane 944, Xinzha Road, now demolished), and later moved them again to the west wing of No. 3, Lane 972, Chengdu North Road, and to the floor above the Xinglong flatbread shop at No. 488, Xinzha Road.

In September 1944, Miao Guren died suddenly, a second Chen Weiren.

In late May 1949, Shanghai was liberated. In early September, Chen Laisheng delivered all the documents he had guarded with his life to the organization department of the CCP Shanghai Municipal Committee. The department at once issued a certificate: the documents and materials, 104 bundles in all, sixteen trunks, had "suffered not the slightest damage from mildew, worms, or rats."

On September 18, the general office of the CCP Central East China Bureau received a cable from the Center: "The great mass of the Party's historical documents is most precious. Please have your office at once designate several reliable comrades to take charge of sorting, registering, and boxing them, and detail special personnel to escort them, and send them all to the Central Secretariat in Beiping; and please reward first, at your end, those who did meritorious service in preserving the documents."

In late February 1950, all the documents kept by the Central Archive reached Beijing, and in October 1959 they went into the Central Archives. The moment fell on the grand tenth-anniversary celebration of National Day, and so came true what Qu Qiubai had said in his lifetime: "to hand on to the future party-history committee (when the country is ours)."
