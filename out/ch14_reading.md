## Chapter 14. The Tongyi Company

In the 1930s, Shanghai's financial world had thirteen trust companies in all, of which the foremost was the Shanghai Tongyi Trust Company. Among its founders was Mr. Guo Yushang, who had returned from studying in England, and who was a brother-in-law—their wives being sisters—of Chen Zhigao's eldest brother, Chen Gengyu. The company's chairman, Huang Suchu, was a pioneer who opened up trust-company business combined with banking. When the company was founded, it had a capital of 1,360,000 silver dollars, quite substantial. The company had a three-story storage vault, holding more than sixteen hundred safe-deposit boxes imported from the United States. In the winter of 1935, this Chairman Huang lost money in speculative dealings on the exchange, and also suffered heavy losses in supporting the enterprise of the Democratic Socialist Party's paper, the *China Times*; this affected the company, whose capital could not circulate, so that it had no choice but to declare bankruptcy. Huang threw down the whole mess and washed his hands of it, fleeing his debts to Japan.

Previously, Guo Yushang had had wide connections with people in educational circles, and the Tongyi Company had absorbed, at high interest, a great deal of deposits from professional women in education—even Chen Zhigao's eldest sister-in-law had deposits in the Tongyi Company. According to the provisions of the "Bankruptcy Law," when a company itself went bankrupt, its directors and supervisors were also jointly liable, and had to repay the creditors out of their personal property; so many shareholders and depositors became victims of the company's bankruptcy. In this way the immediate interests of a great many people were at once involved, and the affair blew up into a great storm. Guo Yushang and Xu Jiqing, the managing directors of the Tongyi Company, though they too counted as prominent figures in banking circles, in fact had no great financial strength of their own and could not save the Tongyi Company from the crisis of bankruptcy; the only way to save it was, under the relevant provisions of the "Bankruptcy Law," to seek reorganization and resumption of business through mediation. Under the financial laws of the time, the court had a special bankruptcy committee responsible for handling such matters. So, on the Tongyi Company's side, Guo Yushang took the lead in asking Chen Zhigao to come forward, as the representative of the board of directors and supervisors, to serve as the lawyer seeking mediation and resumption of business. Zhigao further invited the senior Aurora University alumnus, the noted accountant Pan Zhaobang (Luyan), to jointly take on this case of mediation and resumption.

Liu Bochui was a well-known expert on politics and law within the Party, and when he learned of this situation, he said to me: "You might as well take this chance to break formally into the financial world, show your skill, and open up a new front for upper-level united-front work." From studying Engels's autobiography I had drawn the insight that one could detect the changes of the political situation and the outcome of wars from news of the fluctuations of the financial markets. I had also made some attempts—for instance, I once tried a "short sale" (which required no margin, being pure paper speculation—buying and selling what one never held) and made a profit of more than two thousand dollars, which I used to prepare my trousseau for my marriage to Zhigao—so I was not entirely ignorant of the state of Shanghai's financial world. Moreover, to mediate a resumption of business, to settle disputes and resolve difficulties, was of benefit to both the company and the creditors, so I took on this new mission with considerable confidence.

Under the provisions of the "Bankruptcy Law," a resolution to resume business could be passed only if three-quarters of the creditors attended and those holding more than three-quarters of the total debt gave their consent. After Pan and Zhigao took on the case, although they tried many times to convene creditors' meetings and win the consent of a majority for the mediation plan, the small creditors made up the great majority; and these small creditors, apart from those in Shanghai, were scattered in Suzhou, Hangzhou, Nanjing, Beiping and elsewhere (Tongyi had branch companies in all these places). They all feared that if not enough attended to make a quorum the meeting could not be held, and they would have wasted the round-trip travel money for nothing, so very few were willing to come to Shanghai to attend; and for more than a year it proved impossible to gather three-quarters of the creditors to a meeting.

So I suggested sending capable people separately to the various places to do the work through personal visits, and to win the consent and support of the majority of the creditors. Pan Zhaobang was very shrewd and quickly drew up a mediation plan. In the spring of 1937, I went ahead first with two woman secretaries, going to Beiping to do this work. That year happened to be the seventieth birthday of my teacher, the Hanlin academician Yu Biyun (who had by then settled in Beiping for many years); after arriving in Beiping, I enjoyed the loving care of my teacher and his wife and stayed at the Yu home, while the two secretaries stayed at a hotel. According to the plan agreed in advance, carrying a letter of introduction written by Chen Zhigao, I first called on the three great creditors of the Beiping branch: the barrister Zhang Shizhao, the lawyer Luo Jiaheng, and Professor Zhang Dongsun of Peking University (an old friend of Guo Yushang's, and a left-winger of the Democratic Socialist Party). I explained the main content of the mediation and resumption plan: the small creditors, of a hundred dollars or so, would all be paid off at once in cash; the larger creditors would be issued interest-free bonds of equal value together with company shares, turning the creditors into shareholders of the company, so that the company could resume business. At that time the company's property, apart from funds that could not be circulated for a while, also included a good deal of real estate: in Shanghai there was a five-story building, and the branch companies in various places all had some real estate locally, of which the Beiping branch had the most—almost the whole of Legation Street belonged to it. If it formally went bankrupt and this real estate were sold off at a discount at auction to pay the debts, they would naturally suffer a great loss; so, from the standpoint of their own interests, these large creditors too were unwilling for the company to really go bankrupt. Because of this coincidence of interests, the representatives of all three great creditors expressed their support, signing the preprinted "ballot of assent" in the mediation plan we had brought (printed with both a ballot of assent and a ballot of dissent), and signing letters of authorization asking me to be their plenipotentiary representative. I then had the two secretaries call, one household at a time, on all the small creditors. The small creditors of Beiping, seeing that two great barristers and a famous professor had signed their assent to the mediation plan, and that small creditors of one or two hundred dollars could get cash, all cast their ballots of assent one after another, all signing their consent to the mediation and resumption plan; and they all signed authorizations entrusting me—Huang Dinghui—to act as their plenipotentiary representative. Many of them even mistook me for Huang Suchu's daughter!

During my two months in Beiping, life was very pleasant. The work on the small-creditor households was done by the two secretaries separately, and the work of winning over the great creditors was done by me. In my free time I went along with Teacher Yu and Miss Xueying to tour the various famous sights of the ancient capital, and everywhere we went, the learned Teacher Yu would explain to us in full detail the history of these famous places and historic sites, greatly enriching my knowledge of the history and culture of this ancient city of Beiping. Teacher Yu's seventieth birthday was celebrated at his residence at No. 79 Laojuntang; the teacher's son Yu Pingbo and his wife were both lovers of Kunqu opera, and a stage for the birthday celebration was set up in the courtyard, with an opera troupe invited to give a private theatrical to add to the fun. There I met Teacher Yu's wife's nephew, Xu Bao; the Yu and Xu families were both eminent clans of the Hangzhou-Jiaxing-Huzhou region, old family friends of Chen Zhigao's, and quite a few of the younger generation had joined revolutionary work. Teacher Yu also wrote out four scrolls to give to Chen Zhigao, a younger member of that old family friendship, as a memento.

After I had successfully completed my task in Beiping and returned to Shanghai, Zhigao and the others followed my method, sending people separately to the branch companies in Suzhou, Nanjing and elsewhere to visit the creditors and their lawyers, and inviting Yu Qiaqing, Du Yuesheng, Wang Xiaolai and others to serve as guarantors for the company's resumption of business. The original "Bankruptcy Law" had provided that three-quarters of the creditors must attend and that those holding more than three-quarters of the debt must consent before business could resume; now, thanks to Lin Gengbai's help in the Legislative Yuan, the provision of the law was changed so that business could resume with the consent of merely more than half of the creditors. This "more than half" differed greatly from "three-quarters," and it reduced our difficulties and troubles a great deal.

In the course of taking part in handling this case—especially the trip to Beiping—that I, an outsider with no foundation whatever in the financial world, should try my hand and, remarkably, win at the first stroke and break through the impasse, was not because I personally had any special talent, but because I bore in mind the Party's teaching, analyzed the problem scientifically, and adopted our Party's mass-line working method; our mediation plan and manner of working won the trust of many creditors, secured enough votes of assent, and brought both the number attending the creditors' meeting and the number voting in favor up to the legal figures, so that the mediation and resumption plan was at last smoothly passed. As for the four hundred thousand dollars in cash to pay off the small creditors, it was obtained through the connections of the managing director Xu Jiqing, by mortgaging the Tongyi Company's building to the Zhejiang Industrial Bank. Had the company really gone bankrupt, implicating this managing director Xu Jiqing, even mortgaging the whole of his personal property would not have been enough to repay the debts. By mortgaging the company's real estate to repay the debts, he personally also escaped the calamity of bankruptcy, so it was a matter of benefit to both sides. The resumption of the Tongyi Company was an unprecedented piece of news in Shanghai's financial world; never before had any financial company been able to resume business after going bankrupt—why, even when the old Bank of Communications went bankrupt in the past, there had been no way to resume! For my part, "the drinker's heart was not in the cup": it was simply that, through taking part in handling this case, I widened my own influence in Shanghai's financial world, raised my public profile, and got to know many upper-level prominent figures of the financial world, thereby creating favorable conditions for later using this front of the financial world to carry on the work of the anti-Japanese national united front and to support the resistance and national salvation movement.

After this mediation and settlement, with the consent of the majority of the creditors, the court finally revoked the bankruptcy administration of the Tongyi Company, and the company then obtained the resumption licenses issued by the Ministry of Finance and the Ministry of Economic Affairs. The new board of directors and supervisors after the resumption was apportioned in the following proportions: the old directors and supervisors held one-third, the creditors' representatives one-third, and the persons involved in the mediation and resumption one-third, for a total of twelve directors and three supervisors. Guo Yushang and Xu Jiqing had been managing directors and representatives of the old board, so they were still elected managing directors of the new one. Wang Xiaolai was asked to be chairman, Pan Luyan general manager, Guo Yushang concurrently deputy general manager, and Chen Zhigao secretary-general of the board. Xu Jiqing was the managing director in charge of the company's external liaison, coming to the company for an hour each morning to inspect and follow the company's daily business. Yu Qiaqing, Du Yuesheng and the others had all been invited by Xu to help with the mediation and to stand guarantee, so they all became nominal directors as well. I was elected managing director and concurrently deputy general manager of the Tongyi Company.

The Tongyi Trust Company was located at No. 384 Beijing Road East in Shanghai—probably a hardware company now. That I, an intellectual woman with no financial capital or backing of my own, could from then on set foot in Shanghai's financial world was in those days truly as rare as a phoenix feather or a unicorn's horn. After I returned from Beiping, Professor Zhang Dongsun of Peking University wrote a letter to Guo Yushang (they were both Democratic Socialist Party men) praising me highly, saying that such an intellectual woman with both an economic head and a political head was one he had rarely seen. Xu Jiqing too greatly admired me and specially gave a banquet to welcome me back. Ever since the winter of 1935, when Chen Zhigao took on the case of mediating the Shanghai Tongyi Company's resumption, Xu Jiqing, for the convenience of meeting with us, had introduced the four of us—I, Zhigao, Pan Luyan and Guo Yushang—to join the Bankers' Club as members. Women members were very few at the time. In 1936, together with Xu Jiqing and Wang Xiaolai, I also joined the Shanghai Banking and Money Trades Friendship Association; I was one of its founders and was elected deputy chairman.

My breaking into the financial world to show my skill was entirely in obedience to the needs of the Party's work. The Party's instructions were the source of my confidence and courage. The resumption of the Tongyi Company opened up for our Party an open liaison front for upper-level united-front work, and also opened up a secret contact point for intra-Party work, set up an archive to keep documents and manuscripts for the underground Party and comrades, and provided an important treasury to support the activities of the Party's underground courier work and to keep working funds circulating for the progressive causes the Party led. Its historical contribution must not be effaced.

In 1938, A Ying (Qian Xingcun) organized the "Wind and Rain Bookroom," responsible for publishing *Documents*, the resistance-and-salvation periodical led by the "Eighth Route Office" (short for the Eighth Route Army's Shanghai Liaison Office), and also published such things as the Long March Album—one might call them heavy artillery shells to hearten people in the Nationalist-controlled areas. After Tongyi resumed business, the "Wind and Rain Bookroom" was hidden on the second floor of the Tongyi Company, with a separate staircase for coming and going up and down. There A Ying edited all the documents, and important documents could be locked into the Tongyi Company's storage vault, with only one working comrade living in the editorial office. In those years, when comrades such as Feng Xuefeng, Liu Shaowen and Yu Ling went to the "Wind and Rain Bookroom," they could go up the back staircase without passing through the company's business hall, which ensured the safety of their activities; it also made it convenient for Comrade Feng Xuefeng to use the "Wind and Rain Bookroom" to meet and talk with people of cultural circles. Because the bank's business hall was very imposing, with a very high ceiling, I proposed that an office be built especially for me, the deputy general manager, above the hall, with glass windows all around from which the whole hall could be seen; there was also a private staircase leading to the "Wind and Rain Bookroom," convenient for my contact with Comrade A Ying.

As for keeping documents and manuscripts, comrades such as Gong Yinbing, A Ying, Xia Yan and Liu Shaowen all had personal documents and manuscripts stored in the company's safe-deposit boxes. In those days the terms for renting a safe-deposit box at ordinary banks and money houses were very harsh, requiring a solid and reliable shop-surety and an advance deposit. In my capacity as deputy general manager of the Tongyi Company, I signed and paid the deposit on their behalf, dispensing with all other formalities. In this way, unconditionally, I supplied the underground Party and comrades a place to store secret documents and manuscripts. Besides all the manuscripts and page proofs of *Documents*, Comrade Qu Qiubai's letters, Comrade Fang Zhimin's diary, and many other important records of the time were all kept in the Tongyi vault, until after the founding of New China, when Comrade A Ying took them out and handed them over to the Central Propaganda Department.

In economic terms, relying on the Tongyi Company as an important base for turning over funds, I was able to facilitate the carrying out of all kinds of front-support work, and to provide financial guarantee or aid for the various progressive causes connected with the resistance-and-salvation movement and the patriotic democratic movement led by the Party; in a certain sense, it partly played the important role of the Communist Party's "underground treasury." For example, from 1937 to 1939, together with well-known members of society, I supported the Daily Translation Herald; I provided unlimited overdraft funding for the publication of the *Complete Works of Lu Xun*; and I provided an overdraft account for the Shanghai Drama Art Society headed by Yu Ling. Later, the main pretext the Nationalists used to seal up Tongyi was to say that those overdraft accounts were unclear—but that is a story for later.

According to the viewpoint of materialist dialectics, everything in the world has its dual nature, and the Tongyi Company could naturally be no exception. Because the people it involved were both numerous and complex, it caused some to think I was a capitalist or a capitalist's wife, and later I met with a great many troubles, difficulties and pressures, both economic and political. But I have always held, without resentment or regret, that to occupy this front of the Tongyi Company was entirely for the needs of the Party's cause. I hold that for all that I did through the Tongyi Company for the Party, the credit should go to the Party's leadership, and to Comrade Enlai's discernment in employing people. That in the end I could receive the appraisal made by Comrade Chen Yun on behalf of the Party organization—"no bureaucratic capital, a contribution to front support"—has been comfort enough to me.

My memoirs are bound to be of some reference value for the study of the Party's history of struggle in the white areas and its history of united-front work; and I, having both been valued by Comrade Enlai and been deeply influenced by the martyrs Wan and He—their parting admonitions ever in my heart—have always held to the principle that in doing anything I first consider whether it is beneficial to the Party, whether it accords with policy, and whether it accords with the Central Committee's instructions. Afterward, although I left Shanghai because of a change in my working post, I always maintained a single-line contact that was never broken—something that can fully stand up to investigation.
