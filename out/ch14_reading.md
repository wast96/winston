## The Huang Jinrong I Knew

{d} Narrated by Huang Zhenshi; set down by He Guotao

### I. Family

Shanghai's greatest Green Gang chief, Huang Jinrong (styled Jinyong), was born on the first day of the eleventh month of the seventh year of the Qing Tongzhi reign (1868). He grew up from childhood at Caohejing in Shanghai, and after making his fortune he bought up the land around his parents' graves and built the Huang family garden, with a Huang ancestral shrine within it, so that some said he was a Shanghai man. But in fact his native place was not Shanghai. By the account of Huang Yuzhai, manager of the Huizhong Bank, Huang Jinrong was a Yuyao man; while Chen Hanqin, owner of the Laodafang food shop, said he was a Chongming man; but Huang himself, whenever he spoke of it to me, always said his ancestral home was Suzhou. He had only two sisters: the elder lived at Caohejing, and but for weddings and funerals had little to do with him; the younger married a man surnamed Zou, whose husband died early, and she lodged in the Huang house ever after.

After his parents died, Huang Jinrong was taken as an in-marrying son-in-law by the Ye family. His first wife, Ye Guisheng, was later a notorious woman gangster too. She bore one son and took in a child bride, Li Zhiqing. This son was reared to the age of seventeen and then died, and Li Zhiqing remained a widow and never remarried. After Ye Guisheng married Huang, because Huang was carrying on outside with the Sheng family's seventh concubine and having a secret affair with the famous Peking-opera actress Lu Lanchun, the feeling between husband and wife gradually cracked. In 1930, when Huang built the Huang family garden around his parents' graves, Ye Guisheng, holding that Huang was the Ye family's in-married son-in-law, wanted it named the Ye family garden; Huang flatly refused, and from then on the two fell out and lived apart. The mastery of the Huang house then passed to his daughter-in-law Li Zhiqing. It was widely rumored abroad that Huang and Li Zhiqing had an improper affair, and the Huang servants said as much to me behind his back; but before his disciples Huang put on a show of unconcern about Li Zhiqing, as though there were nothing in it.

Huang Jinrong ran the Gongwutai theater and for a long time engaged Lu Lanchun as its billed star. It was just the heyday of modern-dress drama, and Lu excelled at modern-dress plays, so that the Gongwutai's business flourished greatly. Seeing that Lu was matchless in looks and art, Huang took her by force as a concubine. But the two were together only a short while, and Lu, following Ye Guisheng, also asked to be let go, and married An Shuyuan, a *laosheng*-role singer.

After this, Huang lived with the Sheng family's seventh concubine at No. 10 Rue Lucon, which became an open secret. After the victory in the War of Resistance, Huang hinted to Qiu Zijia that his disciples should propose raising the Sheng family's seventh concubine to the position of principal wife. It was brewed over for a long time, but in the end, because the reactionary government launched the counter-revolutionary civil war, the raising-up was put off undecided, and never came to pass.

Huang had an adopted son named Yuantao, milk-name Liandi, originally the son of a martial-role player at the Dawutai theater. When Yuantao grew up, he served as manager of the Gaofeng dance hall inside the Great World, and, leaning on Huang Jinrong's power, took in a hundred or two disciples of his own. After the victory in the War of Resistance, he colluded with the Kuomintang Juntong agent Qi Zaiyu and others, serving as commander of the volunteer-inspection brigade of the garrison command's inspection department, gathering a set of speculating merchants and gang men as his claws and teeth to help the inspection department as informers. Huang Jinrong's grandson Zou Zhengzhi served as an inspector in one of the volunteer-inspection brigades under Yuantao.

After her husband died, Li Zhiqing adopted two sons in turn, the elder called Chengfa, the younger Chengde. After liberation Chengfa lived with his foster-mother in Hong Kong, while Chengde stayed on the mainland and was the son-in-law of Zhang Shankun, owner of the Xinhua Film Company. She also took in a foster-daughter, whom she likewise brought to Hong Kong. Because Li Zhiqing had managed the household for many years, she had scraped together no little private hoard, and when she went to Hong Kong she swept off all the ready money and jewelry.

### II. From Apprentice and Constable to Detective Chief

In 1880, when Huang was twelve, an acquaintance introduced him to apprentice at a painting-mounting shop called the Lingyunge, beside the Deyi Lou by the Nine-Bend Bridge at the Shanghai City God Temple. By Huang's own telling to me, in such a small shop the apprentice, besides minding the shop, doubled as a kitchen-hand washing rice and cooking, and got no wage at all; the owner gave him only fifty cash a month, and after haircuts and baths there was next to nothing left. He reckoned there was no future in the trade and wanted to find a way to change his calling. In the Shanghai of eighty-odd years ago, before amusement halls had arisen, the City God Temple quarter was the liveliest, and at the Deyi Lou teahouse there were men every day "drinking reasoning-tea" and swapping tall tales, so that it became a haunt and gathering-place of hoodlums. In 1883, when Huang Jinrong finished his apprenticeship and had a wage of 9,600 cash a year, he took to going up to the Deyi Lou for tea now and then, and squeezed in among the hoodlum crowd to knock about, gradually coming to know the clerks of the Six Chambers and the constables and runners of the Shanghai county yamen. Around 1886, after many treats and gifts, he at last worked his way into a post as a hall-duty runner at the Shanghai county yamen, and before long served as a constable too.

At that time Shanghai county was under the jurisdiction of Songjiang prefecture, and every day he had to carry documents to Songjiang prefecture, going and coming more than 150 *li*; he would commonly wear straw sandals, an umbrella on his back and a lantern in his hand, setting out at three or four in the morning and not getting back to the yamen until eight or nine at night. As time went on, he felt that though the post let him throw his weight about and pick up some extra money, it meant rising early and coming home late, going out in wind and rain — too hard a life — and so he thought again of finding another way out.

In 1900 (the twenty-sixth year of Guangxu), the French imperialists expanded the Shanghai French Concession for the second time, and set up the central police station at the Great Chiming Clock on Rue du Consulat (present-day Laobeimen on East Jinling Road). As the district grew, the French police openly recruited Chinese constables by examination, and Huang Jinrong went to sign up and sit it too, but did not pass. Later, by getting someone to bribe the examiner with gifts, he was taken on as a third-class Chinese constable.

### III. His Ways of the World, and His Influence on the Gangs

As the Green-and-Hong Gang hoodlum groups developed down to the late Qing and early Republic, their power grew ever greater and they became a special stratum of the old society, producing such so-called "big shots" as Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng. Many roots lay behind this, to be sure, but the existence of the concessions was one main cause.

Huang Jinrong knew that, as a foreigners' lackey, to climb up he must win the trust of his French masters. To show that his detective talent stood head and shoulders above the rest, he bought up a set of habitual thieves as his informers, and used them to crack cases committed by another set of thieves, sometimes coming to an understanding with the informers, guaranteeing bail after arrest. The informers would gather a band to go into the concession and commit a robbery, whereupon Huang would secretly report it to his French masters, who would send out detectives to lie in wait in disguise and duly catch the thieves in a single net. Cracking several robbery cases thus in a row, his French masters reckoned him capable and able, and regarded him with special favor.

To build up his own power, he further set a set of small hoodlums to pick quarrels and make trouble with the local shops, residents, and well-to-do households, while he himself followed close behind and played the great benefactor. The French police had always had plainclothes constables patrol the streets with detectives to keep order, and each time Huang Jinrong went on patrol, whenever he met with hoodlums shaking down a shop, he would be sure to "stage the Huarong Road" at the extorting hoodlum, making a show of punishment, while the beaten and scolded hoodlum cowered before Huang Jinrong like a mouse before a cat, so that onlookers thought Huang Jinrong truly had the knack — when it was all a false play arranged beforehand. Sometimes he used another trick to raise his "prestige." At the Xingji salt-goods shop across the street from the French police station, for instance, Huang secretly had men go and make trouble, while at the same time setting his disciples to tell the shopkeeper: "For this you need only ask Boss Huang to step in and lean on them, and peace is guaranteed." The shopkeeper got someone to ask Huang to appear, and sure enough no one came to make trouble in his shop again. In a word, Huang Jinrong secretly manufactured disorder and lawlessness, throwing the concession into an uproar, and then came in person to put down the very disorder — this was his method of winning a name in his early days.

Later, once his position in the French police was secure, he went further, colluding with imperialism to foster the feudal gang forces. For example: "Hangzhou Ah Fa" was raised up single-handed by Huang Jinrong to become a noted grand hoodlum of the early Republic. On the Taikoo and China Merchants' Jinliyuan wharves, the whole right to take delivery of and ship opium belonged to the Eight-Share Party headed by Ah Fa, with Huang backing him and sitting to split the spoils. If anyone else went to take delivery, the French police would send out detectives to arrest him and bring him to book. Hangzhou Ah Fa also farmed the French Concession's brothel tax, so that every third-class brothel opened in the French Concession had to rely on Ah Fa's name to do business. Thus Hangzhou Ah Fa, in the Shiliupu quarter, leaning on Huang Jinrong's evil power, did wrong and oppressed the merchants and people to the utmost. Ah Fa's followers were forever "drinking reasoning-tea" and brawling at the Jubaolou and such places, and leading gangs to wound men with sharp axes.

The French police's hoodlum-squad foreman Xu Adong had always dealt with hoodlums by heavy-handed methods, and the hoodlums feared him like a tiger. Huang Jinrong took much pains to raise the standing of the "men about town" and to hold Xu Adong down. After the end of the First World War, Huang Jinrong, for his services in keeping order in the French Concession, was engaged by the French imperialists as an adviser to the French Municipal Council with the rank of major-general, and all the Chinese detective-chiefs and constables in the French police were promoted on Huang Jinrong's nomination. Xu Adong naturally wished to climb too, and so asked Huang Jinrong to nominate him; but Huang told him to go to the Jubaolou at Xinbeimen to find Du Yuesheng, saying that the detectives to be nominated for promotion this time were all for Du Yuesheng to make out the list. When Xu Adong went as bidden to the Jubaolou to see Du Yuesheng, Du had already had Huang Jinrong's hint, and had arranged for the men about town of the two concessions and of the Zhabei and Nanshi districts to be there awaiting Adong's arrival. After Xu Adong had explained his errand to Du, Du Yuesheng at once thumped his chest and guaranteed it, but said that henceforth, in official matters, he must ask Mr. Xu to treat the various gentlemen present that day with special regard. Xu had no choice but to agree on the spot. Du Yuesheng, before them all, proclaimed aloud: "Mr. Xu is one of our family now, and from now on he won't do as the rest have done" — that is, the beating and arresting that made things hard for the hoodlums. After this real lesson, Xu Adong dared no longer treat the hoodlums in his hard-handed way. From then on the detectives in the police of both the British and French concessions, far from daring to offend the men about town, actually sent in cards at the doors of the grand hoodlums to be taken as their disciples (as when the British police chief inspector Liu Shaokui and others did obeisance at Cai Hongsheng's door), and the hoodlum power grew more overbearing than ever.

Before Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng, the men about town had no social standing. Du Yuesheng's own master, Chen Shichang, nicknamed "Stick-Sleeve Fusheng," used to set out three rosewood sticks in the Tianhou Temple quarter for passers-by to toss bamboo rings at, cheating schoolchildren of their candy money; he also forced himself as contractor on the ceremonial processions at the neighbors' weddings and funerals, which was called eating "red-and-white rice," and so forth. Yet even Chen Shichang counted as a person of some face among the early men about town. As Huang Jinrong used gang men to make his way and slowly climbed to be Chinese-detective chief inspector of the French police, the hoodlum power rose greatly, with Huang as their reliable backer. Under Huang Jinrong's behind-the-scenes control, the two special trades of opium and gambling in the French Concession were farmed out, and so were made such grand hoodlums as Du Yuesheng, Jin Tingsun, and Ma Xiangsheng.

Du and Jin, again with money got from running gambling tables and monopolizing the opium trade, went into industry and commerce. Huang Jinrong himself ran the Great World, the Gongwutai, and other amusement halls. In this way the men about town's road to wealth widened from brothels, bathhouses, hotels, and restaurants to the ownership of factories, banks, and companies, and they pushed their way into the ranks of celebrities and notables.

### IV. Gathering His Wings, and Colluding with Warlords and Politicians

After Huang Jinrong had attached himself to French imperialism and gained standing among the men about town, he went further, drawing in every kind of evil force to serve him and colluding with bureaucrat-politicians as his backers. Thus Huang Jinrong became a half-breed of imperialism, feudalism, and the bureaucrat-politicians, and had an enormous influence in society.

How did Huang Jinrong collude with bureaucrat-politicians and draw in the various evil forces?

Lu Xiaojia, son of the Zhejiang military governor Lu Yongxiang, took a fancy to Lu Lanchun, the female lead at the Gongwutai, and for several days running went to the Gongwutai to watch the play and cheer her on. As already said, Lu Lanchun had long been seized by Huang Jinrong as his concubine, and when Huang saw what Lu Xiaojia was up to, jealousy stirred, and he sent his disciples to the Gongwutai to box Lu Xiaojia twice on the ear. Humiliated, Lu wept out his complaint to his father Lu Yongxiang. Lu Yongxiang, hearing it, flew into a rage and at once ordered the Songhu Defense Commissioner He Fenglin (formerly Lu's subordinate) to find a way to arrest Huang and escort him to Longhua, meaning to put him to death. He Fenglin's mother was a Buddhist, and hearing that Huang Jinrong often had dealings with the abbots of the monasteries, and that Huang Jinrong was moreover chief inspector of the French police with imperialism at his back, she held that this man was not to be offended and told He Fenglin to release him. Pressed by his mother's command, He had no choice but to let him go. This storm turned enemies into kin: grateful for the old lady's intercession, Huang did obeisance to her as his godmother, and so Huang Jinrong and He Fenglin became sworn brothers. From then on Huang Jinrong had not only foreign imperialism at his back but a Chinese warlord too. After Huang and He swore brotherhood, Huang went further and colluded with the then Songhu police commissioner Xu Guoliang in trafficking opium for profit; his ill-gotten money grew and grew and his evil power grew and grew, and everyone who plied an improper trade in the Shanghai concessions had to give him gifts and bribes.

Later, a set of troublemaking men about town on the Shanghai scene all "took their lead from Huang's horse's head." Such as: Blind Huang Qing (Shi Huangqing), Rosewood Fatty (Shen Wenyuan), Havoc-in-Heaven Fusheng (Xu Fusheng), Fish-monger Little Shun (Yang Xiaoshun), Songjiang Boss (Fu Fenglai), Wry-neck Li Derong, "Thrice-Hanged" Changsheng, Blind Jinfang (Zhu Jinfang), Apprentice Ah-Si (Pu Jinrong), Ji Yunqing, Sore-leg Binggen (Huang Binggen), Cai the Monk (Cai Hongsheng), and, of a later coming-out, Xie Baosheng, Fruiterer Changgeng (Shen Changgeng), Fruiterer Rongsheng, Tang Jiapeng (Ah Yu), Wide-mouth Yisheng (Zhang Yisheng), Du Yuesheng, Jin Tingsun (Ah San), Zhang Xiaolin, Ma Xiangsheng, Xu Baofu, Gu Zhuxuan, and the rest — all of them gathered under his wing, the very sum of the hoodlum power.

Huang Jinrong's colluding with warlords old and new had a snobbish eye that shifted with the wind. When Li Yuanhong came to Shanghai, for instance, Huang Jinrong sent Jin Tingsun and others to organize a band of hoodlums to guard him. When Xu Guoliang was Songhu police commissioner, Huang colluded with Xu Guoliang, and the commission's detective-squad chief He Shilin became a regular guest at Huang's door. Qiao Songsheng, Lu Ying, and others who later served as detective-squad chiefs of the Songhu police commission, Huang gathered in as disciples. In the early Republic, when Yuan Shikai was in power, Yuan's party got the chief of the French police to arrest Kuomintang men in the concessions, and once, when they were to go to Rue Wagner to seize Wang Jingwei and Chen Gongbo, Huang Jinrong used his two-faced method: before setting out at the head of a body of detectives, he secretly sent his disciple Cheng Ziqing to pass word ahead, so as to leave himself a way back. The most gratifying of all his collusions with officials in his life was his relationship with Chiang Kai-shek.

Chiang Kai-shek was at first under Chen Yingshi; after Chen died, Chiang lost his political backing, and took up speculating at the commodity exchange with Dai Jitao and Chen Guofu. His speculation failing, he was in sore straits, and decided to leave Shanghai for Guangzhou to join Mr. Sun Yat-sen. Pressed by debt and short of travel money, he was introduced by Yu Qiaqing to do obeisance to Huang Jinrong as his master. Huang, seeing this a good bargain, not only took no card-fee from Chiang Kai-shek but gave him two hundred silver dollars for travelling money and helped him on his way. In 1927, when the Northern Expeditionary army set out from Guangzhou and Chiang Kai-shek was its commander-in-chief, this naturally made Huang feel supremely honored. To please Chiang, Huang quietly returned the pupil's card Chiang had presented, and did not set himself up as "master," giving Chiang face, so that Chiang and Huang kept a close relationship throughout, each using the other.

To widen his social influence, Huang Jinrong further racked his brains to draw in evil forces of every sort, and even doled out small favors to the lower orders to poison and lull them. To the down-and-out drifters of the world, the men of the "three teachings and nine streams," he would spare a little relief. In his last years, retired and at leisure, he went every day to bathe at the Xiaoyao Baths on Zhejiang South Road, always taking twenty or thirty silver coins to hand out to the beggars of the pitch, two jiao to a yuan apiece, and never a day missed; the beggar-band took it for a rule, and when his car stopped at the Xiaoyao Baths' door they would send a deputy to receive the bounty. Toward the great thieves and habitual bandits who had committed crimes inland and fled to Shanghai, Huang Jinrong could also leave a way open, so as to keep a line to them for later use. Especially with the out-of-town gang men who came to Shanghai, he was thick as thieves: so long as someone introduced them and they had prepared a rich bribe, he turned none away and made wide ties. This was not merely because they were of a kind; more important, Huang Jinrong wished to widen his sphere of influence and spread his evil power to every place. Through a tangled web of connections, Huang Jinrong gradually made friends with the gang chiefs of other regions too — such as the Hong Gang chiefs of Wuhan, Yang Qingshan and Xiang Songpo; the noted Jiujiang opium dealer "Jiujiang Ah Sheng"; Liu Jinkang of Suzhou; Pan Zixin of Tianjin; Cao Youshan of Yangzhou; Gao Shikui of North China; and Yu Kangshu of Ningbo — and whenever their followers came to Shanghai to do their business, Huang gave the younger men every convenience and looked after them in all ways. For this reason, when Huang's disciples went to other regions to do their smuggling and drug-dealing and other lawless business, the local gang chiefs, loving the crow for the house it sat on, all gave them powerful help.

### V. Organizing the China Mutual Progress Society and Plunging into the Counter-Revolutionary Coup

On the eve of the Northern Expeditionary army's reaching Shanghai, the Shanghai workers, under the leadership of the Chinese Communist Party, were actively organizing an armed workers' rising. The concession authorities, on the pretext of protecting the concessions' safety, ordered Huang Jinrong, Du Yuesheng, and others to organize picket squads to help keep order within the concessions. The mere sixteen tanks the French police had cruised all day long up and down the busy thoroughfares of Rue du Consulat, Avenue Joffre, and the rest, to keep the French Concession workers from rising in answer to the Northern Expeditionary army; in truth they acted as the shock-troops of anti-Communism and counter-revolution.

Huang Jinrong's early disciples were mostly detective-chiefs and military-police of the two concessions' stations — such as Chen Sanlin, Jin Jiuling, and Cheng Ziqing of the French police; Lu Liankui, Shang Wu, and Feng Zhiming of the British-concession police; and Qiao Songsheng, Lu Ying, Zhang Rong, and Cao Mingde of the Nanshi public-security detective squad. His disciples in trade and industry were not many. Tang Jiapeng, manager of the Great World, was then a capable disciple under him; Tang's own disciples Wang Wenkui and Mei Liangbo each had a thousand or two disciples. Ma Xiangsheng, though a younger disciple of Chen Shichang, had in his early years been a cook at the Huang house in Junpeili, and was a grand hoodlum raised up single-handed by Huang; he too, like Du Yuesheng and Jin Tingsun, had made his start in opium and gambling and was fostered by Huang. The armed hoodlum forces Huang organized in his standing as chief inspector of the French police ran to two or three thousand and more.

Before Chiang Kai-shek plotted to launch the April 12 counter-revolutionary coup, on the one hand he colluded with British and French imperialism, and on the other he set the Green-and-Hong Gang groups on as his cudgels, to join the troops of the Jiang faction in the great counter-revolutionary slaughter. He sent Yang Hu and Chen Qun to Huang Jinrong, and Huang in turn called together Du Yuesheng, Zhang Xiaolin, and the rest to lay their plans; using the name of the China Mutual Progress Society, they published anti-Communist notices in the *Shen Bao*, the *Xinwen Bao*, and the other great papers, then gathered a great crowd of hoodlums, weapons in hand, to attack the workers' picket squads. By the account of Dong Mingde, who took part in this counter-revolutionary coup himself (Dong was a Zhenhai man, son-in-law of Hangzhou Ah Fa, and my sworn brother), he was then a detective sub-squad chief of the Kuomintang Shanghai public-security bureau, and every day escorted great batches of Communists and workers from the French Concession to the Longhua headquarters. Ma Xiangsheng and Jin Tingsun both put out their full strength in this bloody counter-revolutionary massacre. Jin Tingsun later spoke of it to me, and often boasted of it, saying that of the several things in his life he prided himself on, one was that when Li Yuanhong came to Shanghai, Huang Jinrong had ordered him to guard him, and another was that in the April 12 counter-revolutionary coup he had escorted Li Lisan out and expelled him from the French Concession.

The China Mutual Progress Society set up its office in the Anle Palace Hotel on Avenue Edward VII, with Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng in overall command. Beforehand they plotted with Liu Zhi's Second Division to seize the workers' pickets' arms, and Huang and the others sent out several hundred plainclothes hoodlums, armed, to surround and attack the pickets' headquarters at the Commercial Press and the Oriental Library on Baoshan Road. Just as they were destroying and attacking, Liu Zhi's troops suddenly drove the Mutual Progress men off and made a show of sympathizing with the besieged pickets; and on leaving, they invited the whole body of pickets to hold a "military-civilian friendship rally" on the square before the North Station. The pickets' leaders, not seeing the trap, went as invited; and as they laid down and stacked the arms they had brought and prepared to watch the Second Division's performance, they were surrounded by the pre-positioned counter-revolutionary troops and disarmed, and then the counter-revolutionary massacre began.

For serving Chiang Kai-shek this once as the shock-troops of anti-Communism and counter-revolution, Huang Jinrong, Du Yuesheng, and Zhang Xiaolin afterwards received no few medals and rewards from the Northern Expeditionary commander-in-chief's headquarters, and were given such titles as adviser and counsellor. In his old age, retired at home, whenever there was a wedding or a birthday or some such happy event at the Huang house, Huang Jinrong would be sure to pin these medals on his breast and preen himself with them; and among that dazzling array of medals were some conferred by the French Concession authorities and by the Beijing warlord government too.

### VI. The Huang-Du Discord and the Zhongxin Society

After the April 12 coup, Du Yuesheng rose to be a hoodlum-politician, and his strength and standing came to surpass those of Huang Jinrong, who set himself up as the "old master." The reasons, on examination, were of three kinds.

First, the three hoodlum chiefs Du Yuesheng, Zhang Xiaolin, and Jin Tingsun had made their start by farming, on the strength of Huang Jinrong's power, the two special trades of opium and gambling in the French Concession. Huang Jinrong, being Chinese-detective chief inspector of the French police, could only pull the strings from behind and could not openly come forward to run the opium dens and gambling houses, so the real power fell into the hands of Du Yuesheng and Jin Tingsun, and Huang only sat and split the spoils, taking a portion. Du and Jin drew rich profits from the drug trade, and in the old society, where money was power, even the French detective-chiefs of the French police looked on green with envy and were willing to take Du Yuesheng as their "old man." The chief of the outer French station, "Goose-brand," and the chief of the Xiaodongmen station, "Ma Song," for instance, both took Du Yuesheng as their old man, and could draw five hundred yuan a month from Du Yuesheng's gambling houses as salary. A set of men about town of standing equal to Du's — such as Gu Jiatang, Rui Qingrong, Sun Jiafu, Rong Binggen, and Yuan Shanbao — were glad to be used by Du and to fill out the Du party's power.

Second, after Du Yuesheng had thrown in his lot with the Kuomintang reactionaries, he formed ties with H. H. Kung and T. V. Soong and struck a life-and-death friendship with the spy chief Dai Li. He also gathered in, by every means, Kuomintang party and government men to do obeisance at his door, and hired "famous scholars" at heavy pay as his private counsellors, forming a brain trust to help him lay his plans and enlarge his following. This raised Du Yuesheng's standing greatly under the reactionary government. Huang Jinrong took in disciples only as a road to personal gain, and of his disciples only such as Tang Jiapeng and Ding Yongchang bestirred themselves for him, so that his strength gradually fell short of Du's.

Third, Du Yuesheng threw himself into industry and commerce, opening banks, shops, factories, and the like, and his influence grew ever greater; whereas Huang Jinrong stood still in his old ruts and would never put up money to run an enterprise jointly with another, so that, apart from the Gongwutai, the Dawutai, the Great World, and the like that he had run in his earlier days as chief inspector of the French police, he had only some real estate. His returns from industry and commerce fell short even of Jin Tingsun's.

Du had political ambition, was willing to spend money, was open-handed and ruthless in his methods, and could win others over and use them. Huang was content with things as they were, would spend only a little money, and would not take the least risk, so that all the "enterprises" he ran were businesses that could not lose. With what he got by fraud and extortion Huang bought up property and sat collecting rents, reckoning it safer and surer than investing in industry or commerce, and more secure even than keeping cash in the bank. When people urged him to buy shares and open a shop with them, he was afraid of being dragged into a loss and refused one and all, and to the end of his life never ran a single enterprise in partnership with anyone.

In his early years Du Yuesheng had smuggled opium with Hangzhou Ah Fa in the Shiliupu quarter, and his generation-rank in the gang was very low; it was only after Huang Jinrong backed him that he came to the fore, and so came at last to his brilliant standing. In the year of Huang Jinrong's sixtieth birthday, he had sworn brotherhood with Du Yuesheng and Jin Tingsun, exchanging cards; Du and Jin honored Huang as elder brother, and the two sides colluded closely. But when Du Yuesheng's power reached its very height, cracks appeared between Huang and Du, and they kept up only a surface cooperation.

It began thus. Huang's disciple Chen Peide was chairman of the British-American Tobacco union, this being before the September 18 affair. Du's disciple Lu Jingshi, who was chief of the military-law department of the Songhu Garrison Command, in order to strike at Huang's people and enlarge his own power, charged Chen with being a suspected Communist and had him arrested and held, refusing to release him. Later, when Huang Jinrong went out in person to plead with the commander Yang Hu directly, Lu at last let Chen out. After his release Chen Peide bore Lu a grudge, and wept out his slanders before Huang Jinrong, saying that in Lu's eyes there was only Du Yuesheng and no "old master," so that the discord and misunderstanding deepened by the day. After I entered Huang's gate in 1928 and joined in the founding of the fish market, I was squeezed and elbowed by the Du party, and, harboring my own discontent, I too fanned the fire before Huang. It was just then the height of the flourishing of Du Yuesheng's Heng Society. In the summer of 1936, Huang gave his tacit leave for his disciples secretly to organize the Zhongxin ("Loyalty and Faith") Society, expressly to deal with the Du party, in the hope of stirring up discord between Du and his disciples and of wrecking the Heng Society.

The name of the Zhongxin Society was taken from the "learning, conduct, loyalty, faith" stone tablet, in Chiang Kai-shek's hand, on the hexagonal pavilion before the corridor of the Huang family garden at Caohejing; Huang took the two words "loyalty and faith" for its name and formed the society. Even among Huang's disciples the Zhongxin Society's membership was not open; its chief members were Qiu Zijia, Ding Yongchang, Chen Peide, Zhang Shankun, Xu Lishan, Yao Songru, Chen Fukang, Lu Jinchen, Pan Ruisheng, Gong Tianjian, Hu Hanzhu, Hang Shijun, Wu Yusu, and Huang Zhenshi, who counted as its officers. Members were engaged by the officers' word of mouth, with no formal procedure, nor was any founding meeting ever held. Huang Jinrong was president in name, but he only gave us his tacit leave to act on his instructions, and never came to any of the gatherings. Before forming the society, Huang secretly summoned a few of us trusted disciples and said: "You can gather some solid material on Du Yuesheng, and I'll lay it before the Generalissimo myself." Having got the gist of it, we set about organizing the Zhongxin Society, meeting at first at the Huang garden, and every Sunday, under the name of a meal, secretly discussing our schemes for wrecking the Heng Society. Later, feeling the Du party too strong, we allied with Yang Hu's Xingzhong Society, forming a league. About the summer or autumn of 1936, the chief members of the two societies would meet every Sunday, by appointment, to dine at the Bansongyuan, each side filling a table or so — this became the custom. We chose the Bansongyuan to dine at because Huang and Yang still kept up a false show of feeling toward Du and did not wish outsiders to spy out the inside of the matter, and the Bansongyuan, lying in the outskirts of Nanshi, was out of sight and hearing. The plan first fixed on was: as a first step, to draw the active elements away from the Du party and stir up discord between Du and his disciples (among those marked to be won over were Liang Zanzhi, a standing director of the fish market, and Li Yanying, distribution manager of the *China Press* evening paper); as a second step, to gather up Du Yuesheng's political misdeeds as material for a denunciation, for Huang Jinrong to lay a complaint against him with Chiang Kai-shek. Six or seven such secret meetings were held, but the plot to wreck the Heng Society still could not be carried out, and the search for Du Yuesheng's misdeeds turned up no solid proof. Then the August 13 Songhu resistance war broke out, and the secret organization of the Zhongxin Society, amid the roar of the guns day and night, fell into paralysis, and at once faded away and dissolved of itself.

### VII. His Life of Ease After Retirement

After his sixtieth birthday, Huang Jinrong resigned the post of Chinese-detective chief inspector of the French police and retired into leisure. Shen Defu succeeded him, and after Shen came Ren Shuiyang; but both these men, because they did not flatter and fawn on Huang Jinrong, were squeezed out by Huang Jinrong's power in the French police and left their posts. Those who came after them were Jin Jiuling, and the head of the French police's political department, Cheng Zikai, both of Huang's gate. Qiaobianshi, who had been chief of the French police for many years, and the French consul in Shanghai, Dulai, would come after Huang's retirement to the Huang house to discuss matters of order in the French Concession. Huang controlled the French police power from behind the scenes, so as to keep his hegemony within his gang group. His retirement, then, was a retreat that was really an advance, a way of handing the mantle to his disciples while setting himself up as a retired emperor.

By this time he had about a thousand disciples, who every year had to bring him gifts in token of filial respect; and besides, from what he had got in bribes and graft during his term as chief inspector of the French police, he ran the Gongwutai, the Dawutai, and the Great World, a sure-profit business whose income was enough to keep him in luxury all his life. So in his old age he troubled himself with nothing outside, but took his ease at home and lived an idle life.

The Rongji Gongwutai was the theater he had run the longest. In those days Peking opera kept men and women apart, and a company of women players performing together was called a "girl show"; he was the first to bring men and women on together at the Gongwutai, and its business flourished greatly.

After the January 28 incident he rented the Gongwutai to Zhang Shankun. Of the rest, the Dawutai was first rented to Shen Changgeng, and later leased and run for a long time by Fan Hengde; the Huangjin Grand Theater was rented to Jin Tingsun; and the Great World and the Rongjin Grand Theater he had his disciples manage and run.

The Great World had first been opened by Huang Chujiu; Huang Jinrong put up five hundred thousand to take it over and renamed it the Rongji Great World. Huang had his own reckoning: as things stood then, five hundred thousand in cash placed in a bank would yield only some three thousand a year in interest, whereas the Great World could sell five or six thousand tickets a day, taking in five or six hundred yuan; after deducting the several halls' expenses, the actual daily income came to two or three hundred yuan, and on Sundays and holidays and at New Year and the Spring Festival there was extra income besides, so that a month's income was upwards of seven or eight thousand yuan. He reckoned the business could not lose, and that it could moreover settle a great crowd of his followers there to scratch a living, so he was glad to take it on and run it.

The domestic affairs within the Huang house at Junpeili were managed by the widowed daughter-in-law Li Zhiqing. In all the house there were more than thirty men and women servants. The accountant (Yu Yonggang), the secretary (Luo Zhenzhong), and the runners, pipe-fillers, gatekeepers, bodyguards, drivers, and the rest each had his own duty; besides these there were three cooks and five or six maidservants for the odd work of the rooms. Huang, withdrawn at home, was like a retired official; the guests coming and going to call on him were an unbroken stream, the carriages and horses at his gate like flowing water, the comings and goings wearing down the very threshold. Among these guests were military and civil officials from other provinces, rich local merchants and gentry, Green-and-Hong Gang chiefs, cashiered warlords, disappointed politicians, and high Kuomintang dignitaries — men of every sort. If there were women guests, Li Zhiqing would as a rule come out to receive them. The way these guests were entertained was either opium or gambling. In Huang's mansion there were at least four or five tables of mahjong every day, and the money taken from the rake-off Li Zhiqing shared out to the servants within and without, as a bounty.

Huang treated people with a snobbish eye, and even the cigarettes he offered his guests were graded: ordinary guests were given Da Qianmen, middling guests Ruby Queen, top guests Garrick, and those higher still were served the finest opium. It was the same with his disciples: a disciple of low worth got a mere nod of the head and was fobbed off, while only toward the disciples of high standing and great power did he show any warmth.

He was himself a heavy opium smoker. If a guest had some favor to seek, the matter would be settled at the opium couch. He commonly used four or five prized pipes, changing them about by turns. The opium he smoked was all aged raw opium, long stored and blended with fine sinking-incense pearl-powder and the like, so that, opium-fiend though he was, there was not the least opium-gauntness in his face.

Of the Kuomintang politicians who had dealings with Huang Jinrong, there were T. V. Soong, H. H. Kung, Wang Bailing, Yang Hu, Wu Tiecheng, Dai Jitao, Wu Zhihui, Yu Youren, and Jiang Bocheng, and the arch-traitors Chu Minyi and Chen Qun; while of the Shanghai business world there were Yu Qiaqing, Wang Xiaolai, Yu Zuoting, Fang Jiaobo, Huang Yanfang, Yuan Lüdeng, Lin Kanghou, Qin Runqing, Wen Lanting, Sheng the Third, Sheng the Seventh, Li Sihao, and Li Zucai. Besides these, such Nanshi grandees as Li Pingshu and Yao Ziruo; Yao Molian of the Nanshi Water and Electric Company; Mao Zijian of the fire brigade; and Wang Liangchen and Gu Zhiyi of Zhabei — all had their dealings with Huang and often came to call at his door. The gang men who paid him respect, of the three teachings and nine streams, were complete in every kind. Of those I met myself, there were the "Da"-generation Gao Shikui, Cao Youshan, and Zhang Jinhu, and there was the celebrated Beijing flying-thief "Swallow Li the Third."

After withdrawing into retirement, Huang would now and then go to enjoy himself at the Huang ancestral hall at Caohejing. His disciples, flattering and fawning on him, proposed enlarging the hall into a garden, which Huang tacitly allowed; and so Tang Jiapeng and Feng Zhiming were put in charge of the business, and levied subscriptions broadly among Huang's disciples and other gang men. Each gave according to the size of his purse, the least thirty or forty yuan, the most a thousand and more; I subscribed five hundred at the time, Tang Jiapeng put up a thousand, and Du Yuesheng and Jin Tingsun subscribed something over four thousand. This was a disguised form of extortion. Of the subscriptions taken in, after all the building costs of the Huang family garden were paid, there were still some tens of thousands over; and because the two men in charge kept muddy accounts, the two sides fell to accusing each other, and there was played out a sorry farce of dog eating dog. Huang, giving one-sided credit to Tang Jiapeng, was greatly displeased with Feng Zhiming and went so far as to drive him out, so that Feng did not come to the Huang house for a year and more.

The newly built Huang family garden covered about sixty *mu* and had a very large reception hall, named the "Four Teachings Hall." On its four walls hung the congratulatory scrolls of Li Yuanhong, Xu Shichang, Cao Kun, and the like, old things from the Huang ancestral hall; on the right of the hall was a great stone tablet reading "learning, conduct, loyalty, faith," the gift of Chiang Kai-shek. The building of the Four Teachings Hall was modeled on the great treasure-hall of a monastery. Before the hall was set out a set of carved-stone Eight Immortals; in the center were enshrined the Three Star-gods of Fortune, Rank, and Longevity; and on either side stood twelve great rosewood chairs — the feudal flavor of it was very thick. Behind the Four Teachings Hall was a row of two-story reinforced-concrete villas, a dozen rooms in all, serving as Huang's summer retreat.

In the garden were rockeries, pavilions, and pools, and winding paths that led to the hills, with many rare and precious flowers and trees — magnolia, black pine, great tree-peonies, and osmanthus most of all.

After the Huang family garden was finished, every Sunday the general run of Huang's disciples would gather there uninvited, coming to the garden at Caohejing to meet, dining there at noon, and playing about until three or four in the afternoon before dispersing at their pleasure. Huang himself, every year after the Start-of-Summer festival, would move into the garden to pass the hot season, staying until the autumn cool of the eighth month before returning to his own house at Junpeili. A pack of his idle, good-for-nothing disciples would keep him company all day long, smoking opium or gambling, whiling away the time in a life of pampered ease. Huang was fond of two forms of gambling, "flower-cards" and "brass-and-porcelain banners," and four disciples — Gong Tianjian, Yehu Ah-Si, Chen Fukang, and Lu Jinchen — attended him at them day after day. Now and then he would also go to a small teahouse near Caohejing to set up a fortune-telling stall (to brew a pot of tea and chat idly), reckoning it one of the pleasures of his retirement.

Huang Jinrong was steeped in feudal superstition. At the City God Temple's yearly incense-society in Shanghai, of which he was president, he would be sure to lead his trusted disciples to offer sacrifice and make their kowtows, and would even have hoodlums dress up as judges of the underworld, imps, and infernal yamen-runners, paired two by two, face to face, to perform in the streets. Within the Huang family garden there was a Guandi temple, and on the earthen hill behind it a Guanyin hall named "the Purple Bamboo Grove." On the thirteenth of the first, fifth, and ninth lunar months, when the Guandi festivals were held, each of Huang's disciples put up ten yuan to dine at the Guandi temple in the garden, the dishes supplied by the Huang kitchen, and the money left over went to the Huang family garden's own expenses — another trick of raising money under a borrowed name. He was also fond of consorting with monks and priests, and the abbots of Mount Wutai, Mount Jiuhua, Mount Putuo, and the Longhua Temple in Shanghai often came to Junpeili to beg alms. Huang was not open-handed, giving a few hundred yuan at most; yet the abbots of every temple counted it an honor to have his inscription, and reckoned little of the amount. Toward the down-and-out drifters of the three teachings and nine streams, if they had any request, he would habitually use small favors to widen his influence and draw in ties. To the various works of public charity he would not lend his name as a sponsor; when it was hard to refuse for face's sake, he would fob them off and have done. He did run a Jinrong Primary School at Baxianqiao in the French Concession, with his disciple, the lawyer Jin Liren, as principal — his one money-losing venture.

From his sixtieth birthday on, he held a birthday every year, and under the name of celebrating his long life openly took a squeeze from his disciples and his usual acquaintances. Disciples of any standing gave at least a hundred yuan in birthday money, and the more, often, five hundred or a thousand; Du Yuesheng, Jin Tingsun, and their like each gave two or three thousand every year, and took it for a rule. Because he was fond of money, those who were close to him had taken the measure of his temper, and so at weddings and funerals and the like at the Huang house everyone gave cash rather than gifts. The birthday banquets were, as a rule, catered by the Taohua Palace (later renamed the Dali Chun), run by Huang's disciple Wan Rong, or sometimes booked at the Hongyun Lou. From this yearly birthday, after all the costs of the banquets and the rest were deducted, he could clear at least ten or twenty thousand.

Besides this, Huang's disciples, more than a thousand of them, had to send festival gifts and respects at the three festivals every year — Dragon Boat, Mid-Autumn, and the Spring Festival. Someone like me would send the gatehouse twenty yuan, a tea-fee of a hundred, and a festival gift of a hundred, at each festival — over six hundred yuan a year in respects to the Huang mansion at Junpeili. With Huang Jinrong's many disciples, it added up to a considerable income. Why was I willing, heart and soul, to send Huang Jinrong so much money at the set times? In the old society there was a saying in vogue: "with power comes wealth." Before I took Huang Jinrong as my master I was only a budding "trade man about town," with not many ways of exploiting the laboring people; but once I had entered Huang's gate my standing in society shot up, I took on many posts as director of this or that public body, and my ways of exploiting grew the more. In the last analysis, it was by leaning on Huang Jinrong's evil power that I could come to such a rise, and so I was glad to draw some money from my own exploiting income to supply my "old man." The master-and-disciple tie of the feudal gangs was just such a thing of mutual use, of wolves in cahoots.

Huang often went to eat at small eating-houses; the Dexing Guan at Xiaodongmen was one he had frequented in his day as a detective-chief of the French police. This eating-house was later made over to Wu Quangui, a disciple of Tang Jiapeng's, who ran the Taixin Hotel and made "red pills," and was a grand-disciple of Huang's. During the War of Resistance, because the Dexing Guan lay on the edge of the Chinese quarter, and the French Concession authorities had set iron gates at the border and would, in an emergency, declare traffic cut off, its business was very thin, its customers to be counted on the fingers. Wu Quangui specially invited Huang Jinrong to feast at the house, using him as a living advertisement to draw in custom. Huang had always loved rich, oily food, and had a fond memory of the Dexing Guan's braised mandarin-fish and its casserole of lion's-head meatballs; so one noon two cars drove out from Junpeili to the Dexing Guan for a little meal, at once drawing a crowd of curious townsfolk to gather and gape, to see what a three-headed, six-armed creature this hoodlum chief might be — a fine bit of publicity for the Dexing Guan. After the victory in the War of Resistance, the owner of the Shijia restaurant at Mudu in Suzhou did the like, inviting Huang Jinrong there for a small meal and using him as a living signboard to draw in customers. The owner of the Shijia restaurant was a disciple of Huang's, and each used the other and got what he wanted.

### VIII. The Course of the Feud Among Huang's Disciples

In the first ten years of Huang Jinrong's retirement in his old age, he kept on taking in disciples, seeing it as a road to wealth, and sometimes made himself ridiculous by it.

The Anqing Gang had a generation-transmission passed from master to master, its generations fixed at first by twenty characters: "Qing, jing, dao, de; wen, cheng, fo, fa; neng, ren, zhi, hui; ben, lai, zi, xing; yuan, ming, xing, li" — twenty characters. By the end of the Qing the twenty had been used up, and four more were added: "da, tong, wu, jue." In the early Republic the highest gang generation was the "Da." Huang Jinrong had never formally taken an old man, and in the Green Gang was called a "hollow one." By gang rule, one who had not passed through an incense hall could not open a hall and take in disciples, so all whom Huang took in were "pupils," and pupils had no generation-rank. Only because his position was high and his power great, he had no need to go and take a master and worship the ancestors: a whole set of hoodlum chiefs of themselves honored him as "old master." Though not truly a gang man, his standing within the gang organization was in fact better than that of several of the "Da" generation. So when people asked him, he would say in jest: "I'm of the 'Tian' generation — one stroke more than the 'Da.'" Later his old friends of the "Da" generation, Gao Shikui, Cao Youshan, Wang Delin, and the rest, all urged him to take as his old man Zhang Jinhu, who had once been Tonghai garrison commander; and he neither refused nor accepted, but in fact never held any ceremony.

Even so, among Huang's pupils, in order to raise up a set of their own underlings, not a few were gang men who had formally passed through an incense hall. Thus I, together with Lu Ying, Dong Mingde, He Guoliang, and some thirty other Huang disciples, before August 13 jointly took the "Da"-generation Cao Youshan of the Anqing Gang as our old man, and opened a hall and took in disciples, carrying on this family business of the feudal gangs and setting up many disciples and grand-disciples of our own. In his old age Huang Jinrong took in disciples without limit, and no few of these disciples were my own disciples and grand-disciples, who, through someone's introduction, did obeisance to Huang Jinrong as master too, and so became fellow-disciples of their own old man. This, in the gangs, is called "loosening the incense-head," a matter both maddening and forbidden; so a few of us knockabout disciples once urged Huang to stop taking disciples, and to our faces he allowed it should be so, but before long he was opening his door to disciples again. This was inseparable from his money-grubbing, insatiable, exploiting nature.

Of the disciples Huang took in at the French police, apart from a few such as Jin Jiuling, Chen Sanlin, and Cheng Ziqing, the disciples who were closer to him later were mostly taken in after he had become a grand hoodlum chief — men of every color, from the military, the police, business, arts and letters, science and technology, and fortune-telling and medicine. The largest proportion, of course, were the idle men about town. Because they were so many, Huang himself could not even call some of his disciples by name. The only ones I can call to mind are the following.

First, those holding posts in the police of the British and French concessions, the public-security bureau, and the like: Chen Sanlin (detective-chief, French police); Jin Jiuling (chief inspector, French police); Cheng Ziqing (head of the political department, French police); Ding Yongchang (detective-chief, French police); Lu Jinchen (detective-chief, French police); Zeng Jiuru (nicknamed "Huakui," detective-chief of the French Concession robbery squad); Lu Liankui (chief inspector, British police); Shang Wu (detective-chief, British police); Feng Zhiming (detective-chief, British police); Tang Jian (detective-chief, British police); Wei Zhongxiu (detective-squad chief, Shanghai police); Ma Derong (detective-chief, British police, and in the war a detective-chief of the puppet special-high section); Qiao Songsheng (detective-squad chief, Shanghai police); Lu Ying (detective-squad chief, Shanghai police, and in the war a turncoat serving as puppet Shanghai police chief); Zhang Rong (deputy detective-squad chief, Shanghai public security); Dong Mingde (detective sub-squad chief); Zhou Xiangcheng (secretary of the detective squad, Shanghai public security); Song Yuntao (fingerprint section of the detective squad); Cao Anchang (detective-squad chief, Suzhou police); Gu Baoyu (Shanghai tax-bureau chief under the Beijing warlord government); Xu Baoming (commander of the Pudong defense corps); Zhang Zhiqing (puppet public-security sub-bureau chief); Yi Zongren (Nanshi Yimiao sub-bureau); Chen Yafu (in the Japanese-puppet period a standing committee member of the Chinese People's Anti-British-American Association); and Hu Shouqi (chief secretary of the Wang-puppet Shanghai social-affairs bureau).

Second, the feudal hoodlums and labor-bosses, the professional men about town: Gu Zhuxuan (grand hoodlum, owner of the Tianchan Stage); Ma Hongkui (the "Night-soil King" of the French Concession); Xie Binheng (a big labor-contractor); Zhu Shunlin (owner of the gambling houses at the Liuguo Hotel, the Hollywood, and the like); Liu Yizhang (Hankou grand hoodlum, opium dealer); Min Caichen (chief steward of the Great World); Xu Fubao and Jin Liren (hoodlum lawyers); Gong Zhaoxiong (nicknamed "Mute-throat Ah-Liu," owner of the Tiantian Restaurant); Gu Yushu (grand hoodlum of Xujiahui); Yao Songru (owner of the Cangzhou Hotel); Yan Boai (owner of the Zapaya Theater and the Dalu Hotel); Tang Jiapeng (manager of the Great World); Fruiterer Rongsheng (manager of the Great World); Shu Changtai (owner of the Rixin Baths); Wang Wuquan (Hangzhou grand hoodlum); Meng Lujiu; Lou Bingzhang; Yu Zegao (a fortune-teller labor-boss); and He Guoshen (contractor of the China Merchants' Dongjiadu wharf).

Third, the so-called "trade men about town" of industry and commerce: Zheng Renye (comprador of the Beijing shipping line, owner of the Zhengtai Rubber Factory); Zhang Zilian (owner of the Sanxing Cotton-and-Iron Works); Xie Keming (owner of the Renfeng Dyeing and Weaving Mill); Chen Jusheng (owner of the Hua-Ou Sugar Refinery); Le Baocheng and Le Rucheng (owners of the Taikang Biscuit Factory); Wang Yunpu (chief master of the Zhongxing Steamship Company); Chen Dianting (head of the Zhoushan Native-place Association); Wu Xintai (owner of the Shunda fish-brokerage); Li Yunsheng (comprador of the China Merchants' wharf); Huang Zhenshi (manager of a fishery company); Zheng Xiaozhou (head of the Guoxin Bank); Wu Xiaoshi (owner of the Wukai Photo Studio); Zhu Wenwei and Zheng Muzhou (both proprietors of customs-broker firms); Zhou Mengyue (owner of a fish-brokerage); Zhang Shankun (manager of the Xinhua Film Company); and Qiu Zijia (works manager of the Renfeng Dyeing and Weaving Mill).

Fourth, men of the press, the theater, and medicine and the healing arts: Wu Yusu (*tanci* balladeer); Wang Zhenchuan (Western internal medicine and surgery); Fang Shenhui (acupuncturist); Fang Gongbo (masseur-doctor); Ping Jinya (old-style novelist, keeping the Central Bookstore); Shen Juyin (editor of the *Xinwen Bao*); Ren Hejiao, Hu Hanzhu, and Hang Shijun (these three all newspaper editors); Ke Tianying (formerly named Bao Qinxuan, a magician); and Liu Liangfu (a dentist).

Of the list above, those who were often in and out of the Huang mansion and very close to Huang Jinrong were Tang Jiapeng, Yue Zuoxuan, Lu Liankui, Lu Ying, Jin Jiuling, Zhang Shankun, Ding Yongchang, Chen Peide, Zhang Zilian, Qiu Zijia, Dong Mingde, and myself — some twenty or thirty men. Some, though they had done obeisance to Huang Jinrong as master, seldom came except to pay their calls at the festivals. In the Kuomintang years, any honest merchant who wished to advance his business in the society of the day could not but bow a little to a hoodlum chief of such evil power as Huang Jinrong.

Zhou Xinfang had first performed at the Danguidi-yitai. At that time there was a *wenwu-laosheng* named Chang Chunheng who, at the Tianchan Stage that Gu Zhuxuan ran, performed the whole of *Han Guangwu Restores the State and Goes to Nanyang* to a very full house. Gu Zhuxuan had always refused to pay the players he engaged a big salary. Chang Chunheng, on the strength of the Tianchan's good business, asked for a raise in his contract price, and Gu would not give much; so Chang put forward a demand to stop performing. Gu secretly had his disciples beat Chang to death, and re-engaged Zhou Xinfang to perform at the Tianchan. Later, when Zhou too, unwilling to bear Gu Zhuxuan's exploitation, would not renew at the end of his term, Gu gave out to people that he would have Zhou put to death, and everyone feared for Zhou's life. Knowing Gu to be past all reason, Zhou had no choice but to fight poison with poison, and got someone to introduce him to do obeisance to Huang Jinrong as master. Huang granted Zhou's request, specially summoned Gu to the Huang mansion, and said to him: "Xinfang is now your own brother; from now on you must look after him." From then on there was peace between them. Zhou, in great fear that Gu might seize a chance to do him a secret hurt, hastily left Shanghai and went north to "make the rounds of the docks," and only after three years came back to Shanghai, first performing three days at the Huangjin Grand Theater, so that Gu dared not touch him.

Gu Zhuxuan was famous for killing among Huang's disciples. He was a Yancheng man from Subei, milk-name Gu Si, nicknamed "Tumor-mouth," and in the gang group all called him "the Fourth Boss." He had been a rickshaw puller at first, and later got in as a constable at the British-concession police; with the money he squeezed and extorted he opened a rickshaw firm, renting the rickshaws out to his poor Subei fellow-townsmen. As a constable, he had no fear of the pullers falling behind on their rent; and at the same time he passed the word to the constables on the beats at every station, so that if his rented rickshaws now and then broke a traffic rule, they were let off. In this way his rickshaw business did very well. Seeing there was profit in it, he simply resigned the constable's post, enlarged the rickshaw business, and opened the Tianchan Stage besides. Later, when the Wing On Company colluded with the British Municipal Council and forced Gu Zhuxuan to move the Tianchan Stage elsewhere, to be rebuilt as the south wing of the Wing On Company, he was unwilling to submit, and hired two foreign lawyers, Mu Ansu and Gan Weilu, to fight a great lawsuit against the Municipal Council, appealing all the way to the courts in England. In the end the English court ruled that the Municipal Council should compensate the Tianchan Stage 100,000 yuan for its losses. From then on Gu became a star in the gang, and his Subei townsmen called him "the Fourth Grand-master Gu."

Gu and Tang Jiapeng were the two great pillars of Huang's gate, each with a portion of evil power. Later, over the contracting of the Great World's booths and the profits of them, the two came to hate each other, and it turned to strife within the same house. First Chen Rongsheng, nicknamed Fruiterer Rongsheng, having been manager of the Great World only a short while, was killed by someone. After Tang Jiapeng succeeded him as manager of the Great World, the contracting of all the booths lay in Tang's hand alone. Now there was one Xu Fubao, a disciple of Ji Yunqing's, who, because he could not get a booth at the Great World, bore a grudge and set his mind on killing Tang Jiapeng to be avenged.

Xu shared with Gu Zhuxuan a long-let room, No. 301, at the Dongfang Hotel. That day Xu said to Gu: "This fellow Tang Jiapeng is no good — what if I beat him to death?" Gu smiled and did not answer. Xu then took the chance to drive to the house of Wang Xinggao, a Subei disciple of Gu's, and said to Wang: "Fourth Master Gu wants you to beat Tang Jiapeng to death." Wang took it for the truth, and at once got a pistol and waited at the Great World gate. As Tang, accompanying Wu Yusu, who sang *The Miao Golden Phoenix*, came out of the Great World and was about to get into his own rickshaw, he was shot dead by Wang Xinggao. After the case broke, Huang Jinrong at once telephoned the chief inspector of the French police, Jin Jiuling, and firmly insisted the culprit be run down. When the French police caught the murderer Wang Xinggao, he named Gu Zhuxuan as the instigator behind him, and Gu was thereupon detained, sentenced to five years, and, after two years' imprisonment, released on bail. Huang went on treating Gu as a confidant as before, and did not on this account break off the master-and-disciple tie. When this case broke, it put Jin Jiuling in a hard position, for Gu and Jin were connected by their children's marriage, and it was hard for him to lay a hand on Gu.

In Huang's gate I had originally belonged to Tang Jiapeng's faction. After Tang was killed, Chen Fukang, Qiu Zijia, Ding Yongchang, and I each raised our own banner. Of these, Qiu Zijia set himself up as of the elder-statesman party and colluded with me; Ding Yongchang kept up a surface cooperation only. Chen Fukang had always managed the Huang house's real estate and the Great World's accounts, and Gong Tianjian, Yehu Ah-Si, Lu Jinchen, and the like formed a clique with Chen, surrounding the old man Huang Jinrong and living a parasitic life on the split spoils. Tang Jiapeng's disciple Wang Wenkui joined the set too. Whenever there was a New Year or festival or a happy event at the Huang mansion, they would all come to flatter and fawn — a pack of petty men within the Huang house, with whom I would not cooperate. Once, over the matter of the wife of Huang's adopted son Yuanlin smoking heroin at a certain opium den in Nanshi, I raised it before Huang Jinrong, holding that if it were not stopped it touched not only Huang's face but the credit of us disciples too. Huang, hearing it, flew into a rage and actually drove Yuanlin's wife out of the Huang house. For this reason, though the petty men of the inner house found me uncongenial, they feared my plain speaking before the "old master," and mostly kept their distance and treated me with a wary respect; but behind my back they were forever setting themselves against me, spreading slanders, and sowing discord. After the January 28 affair, when Du Yuesheng was chairman of the Shanghai fresh-fish trade guild and I was its general secretary, I often had dealings with Du; and Chen Fukang then told Huang Jinrong that I was turning my back on Huang to league with Du and meant to do obeisance at Du's gate. Huang Jinrong half believed it, until, after my explanation and defense, he did not credit it.

Again, in the early autumn of 1937, I got hold of a great shark weighing more than six thousand *jin*, and wished to exhibit it at the Great World, the ticket takings to be split four to six, having already come to terms with Huang Jinrong. But because I had not let the Chen clique wet their beaks, they blocked the exhibition on the pretext that it endangered health and that the Municipal Council's sanitary department would not permit it. Seeing the thing would not do at the Great World, I turned instead to You Agen at the New World and came to terms to put the great shark on show at the New World. At that time the New World's business was poor, its visitors to be counted on the fingers, and its gate all but deserted; but after it was puffed up and cried in the papers, the crowds suddenly swelled. You Agen was greatly pleased, and I too cleared three thousand yuan by it.

### IX. His Life of Uneasy Peace During the War of Resistance

During the War of Resistance, when the Shanghai garrison had drawn back, the two concessions lay hemmed in on all sides by the Japanese and became a "solitary island." The enemy used traitors to set up a Peace Maintenance Association, and the desperadoes of the gangs seized the chance to bestir themselves on a grand scale; for a time the demons danced in confusion, more rampant than ever. By now Du Yuesheng and Jin Tingsun had fled to Hong Kong, and the Japanese secret service, having for a while set their eye on Huang Jinrong, wanted him to take the post of president of the Peace Maintenance Association. One day an enemy officer (whose name I have forgotten) came to press him; but Huang, having beforehand feigned paralysis, was helped out by two of his household to receive him, and, declaring that he was ill in body, could not read, knew nothing of the great affairs of state, and was unfit to be president of a Peace Maintenance Association, slipped through that pass.

Afterwards, when the gang men Xu Tieshan and Jin Yixun surrendered, the Japanese used them to organize a "Yellow Way Society," and Xu and Jin urged Huang to come forward and head it; he again used the same method to decline with soft words. But toward the visiting enemies and the collaborating traitors he was always courteous, and never dared to offend them. Through the eight years of the war, men of the Japanese secret service often came to call at Junpeili, and Huang received them with every courtesy; among them the puppet chief of the Huangpu sub-bureau, the Japanese Kobayashi, came and went at the Huang house most assiduously, and the tie grew closer still.

Among Huang's disciples there was no lack of men who curried favor with the enemy and clung to the puppets, of whom the most notoriously evil was Lu Ying. On the eve of the war Lu was detective-squad chief of the Shanghai police. In the winter of 1938, when the enemy army set up the puppet Dadao city government and looked to Lu Ying to take the post of puppet police chief, Lu, wavering and undecided, talked it over with Huang Jinrong. At that time the Japanese power could not yet seep into the concessions, and Huang told Lu Ying not to go over. As the Japanese-puppet power grew day by day more rampant, and the concession authorities yielded to the Japanese at every turn, Lu Ying's standing in the Wang Jingwei puppet regime rose from Nanshi police chief to a soaring leap as staff-general of the puppet military affairs council, and he became a leading figure among the "new nobles." When he came again to the Huang mansion to call on him, Huang treated Lu Ying as a prized disciple to show off Huang's gate, and doted on him greatly. That Huang Jinrong did not go into the water and turn traitor himself was not from any love of country — as may be proved from his attitude toward his traitor-disciples, and from his friendly relations with the so-called "three elders of Shanghai," Wen Lanting, Lin Kanghou, and Yuan Lüdeng, after they had gone over. He once said to someone that being president of a Peace Maintenance Association he was afraid of being assassinated — that it was to keep his own life safe.

In this period the activity of the societies was rather listless. Du Yuesheng's Heng Society and Yang Hu's Xingzhong Society had both moved inland, and the Zhongxin Society, having lost the target of its attacks, faded of itself into a standstill. My Zhen Society amateur-opera club, which I had organized back in 1935, seized the chance to bestir itself greatly, and Huang Jinrong would often come to sit idly at the Zhen Society; seeing the great number of members coming and going within it, he praised me to my face and reckoned it a gratifying thing for Huang's gate.

Toward all manner of social activity, he begged off on the plea of age and infirmity and would not go. Every afternoon he went to bathe at the Xiaoyao Baths, and took it for a fixed daily lesson. In the summer of 1941, when the gang man Gao Lansheng laid on a banquet at the Lido Garden for his fiftieth birthday, Huang Jinrong went to offer his congratulations, and among the fellow guests were the Settlement police detective-chiefs Shang Wu, Liu Shaokui, Tang Jian, and others. Suddenly there walked in from outside Wu Shibao, nicknamed "Killer Taibao," from the den of horrors at No. 76 in western Shanghai. (Wu Shibao was chief of the guard brigade at No. 76; his wife, Yu Aizhen, had in May 1941 driven to the International Settlement for a perm, and when a Western constable demanded to see her pass and her bodyguard refused, the constable had shot the bodyguard and the driver dead.) Before Huang Jinrong's very face, pointing at Shang Wu, Liu Shaokui, and the rest of the police detective-chiefs, Wu abused them by indirection, cursing the mulberry while meaning the locust: "This pack of pockmarked-threes — now that your day is done, if you cross this master again, this lordling will beat you to death one and all!" — a very extremity of threat and menace. Huang Jinrong, at his side, kept still as a cicada in the cold and did not utter a sound: truly he had well grasped the way of bending when one must bend and stretching when one may stretch.

### X. Some Activities After the Founding of the Rong Society

In September 1945 Du Yuesheng came back to Shanghai by train from the rear; and because he came from the rear, his worth rose tenfold at a stroke. The Heng Society he presided over grew lively again. By this time Yang Hu was director of the executive committee of the China Seamen's Special Party Headquarters, and his Xingzhong Society had been changed into a "Xingzhong Study Society," taking in more than ten thousand seamen as members. The Hongmen man Zheng Ziliang organized a Xiayi Society, which also had more than ten thousand members. Huang Jinrong, seeing all this, was green with envy, and wished to revive the Zhongxin Society, halted these eight years. Afterwards, on the proposal of Qiu Zijia and me, it was renamed the Rong Society, run in open form, to vie with the Heng Society for glory. Huang agreed to our proposal, and at once looked out a site and drew up an organization. The society's premises were set in the house of Zhao Zhensheng, former comprador of the French Municipal Council, next door to Zhenshengli on Neishan Road. In November 1945 the Rong Society held its founding meeting in due form; Du Yuesheng, Yang Hu, Wang Xiaolai, and others attended the ceremony as guests. The Rong Society's officers were chosen there and then, as follows.

President: Huang Jinrong.

Standing directors: Qiu Zijia, Huang Zhenshi, Chen Peide, Hang Shijun, Ding Yongchang.

Directors: Yao Songru, Wu Yusu, Gong Tianjian, Chen Fukang, Lu Jinchen, and the rest, fifteen in all.

Supervisors: Mo Shishan, Yang Chunhua, Zhang Zhiqing, Chen Jusheng, Zhang Jinshou.

Huang Jinrong was awkward of speech. That day, when the president gave his address, he spoke in the character of a family patriarch: "Today the Rong Society is founded, and we are all gathered under one roof, in joy and gladness. I am very glad. From now on we must all hold together the more…" — and said a few words of no clear sense.

After the Rong Society was founded, apart from holding a good many banquets at no fixed times, it was, generally speaking, not so lively as the Heng Society. Because I had my own Zhen Society organization, I was not very keen on the Rong Society's doings, and Qiu Zijia was no capable hand at running a society either. Add to this that certain persons who called themselves members but had no member's standing — such as Chen Xiong and Tang Xiong, disciples of Huang Yuanlin's, and Zou Zhengzhi — gave themselves out abroad as Rong Society members and practiced fraud and extortion, sticking at nothing, so that we core members too were greatly disheartened and unwilling to put our whole strength into developing the so-called society affairs.

Once Huang Jinrong put it to me that, since the Rong Society's house was very large, my Zhen Society and the Xing Society of my disciple Zhou Yixing might both be moved into the Rong Society and merged, so that with more men there would be more power and things would be livelier still. But as for me — the Zhen Society was a base from which I lorded it in my own right, and once it was merged in, with Huang Jinrong above and the pack of petty men of the Huang faction thrusting their hands in between, I naturally would not lightly give up the little body I had labored to build; so I put him off by saying that the Zhen Society's members were of a mixed sort and it would do more harm than good, and there the matter ended.

On the first day of the eleventh lunar month of 1947, Huang Jinrong turned eighty. In former years he would have laid on great banquets and made a lively show of it; but this time he suddenly urged thrift and no display, and would set out only a few vegetarian tables at the Jade Buddha Temple. The Rong Society's men, in organizing the birthday preparations for him beforehand, engaged Du Yuesheng as chief manager, Yang Hu as chief receiver, and me as general steward. That day the mayor of Shanghai, Wu Guozhen, and the various bureau chiefs all came to offer their respects, and H. H. Kung came too; and when Du Yuesheng and Yang Hu saw that Kung had arrived, they said to me: "We'll go keep President Kung company — you take over here for us." They did nothing but make much of H. H. Kung, and left the birthday-star out in the cold.

In August 1948, Chiang Ching-kuo, in the standing of deputy economic supervisor-commissioner for the Shanghai district, carried out the business of economic control in Shanghai, saying at every turn that he would beat a few big tigers, and now and then going out incognito in plain clothes. I was then engaged as an assessment commissioner, and got wind that Du Yuesheng was secretly setting his followers on to buy up and hoard goods in order to wreck Chiang Ching-kuo. I judged this a fine chance to strike at the Du party, and so laid a plan before Huang Jinrong: to invite Chiang Ching-kuo to a banquet at the Rong Society, to urge him to be more careful in his doings henceforth, lest someone should do him a mischief, and by the way to tell him the news that Du Yuesheng was inciting the hoarding of goods, so that he might make ready beforehand. Huang Jinrong thought the move quite necessary, and there and then had an invitation prepared and told me to invite Chiang Ching-kuo in person. When I went to see Chiang, he said modestly, "Please don't trouble yourself, old master!" and only after my repeated urging did he agree to come. But who could have known that the next day, when the banquet was already laid, Chiang would send two representatives, Wu Shaoshu and Jiang Hengxiang, in his place. This being so, it was of course not convenient to bring up the matter of Du Yuesheng with Wu Shaoshu, and our whole plan came to nothing. Not long after, Du Yuesheng's second son Du Weiping was detained by Chiang Ching-kuo, and Du, having got wind of the affair of the Rong Society's banqueting Chiang Ching-kuo, took it that I had secretly done him a mischief. In truth there was a cause but no substance to it: though we had had our design of toppling Du, it never became a fact.

### XI. Confessing to the People

After the liberation of Shanghai, Huang Jinrong's widowed daughter-in-law Li Zhiqing swept up all the gold, banknotes, valuables, diamonds, and jewels of the Huang house and, taking her adopted son Chengfa and her adopted daughter (whose name I forget), fled to Hong Kong, so that of the people's fat and marrow that Huang Jinrong had spent his life scraping together, nine parts in ten were already gone. By this time Du Yuesheng and Jin Tingsun had both gone to Hong Kong too. When people urged Huang to go there as well and settle in safety, Huang said: "I'm past eighty now. To die in Hong Kong wouldn't matter much; but I'm afraid of falling suddenly ill on the road — wouldn't that be to die halfway?" And so he firmly held to not going. He had always been stubborn in his thinking; in his last years he put his trust in Pan Qifen (styled Zixin) and me, and would often discuss the general situation with me, saying to me: "In anything, talk it over well with Old Seventh Pan." In December 1949, being deputed by the Ningbo Native-place Association to raise relief funds for Ningbo, I went, and before setting out went to see Huang, who told me, when I saw Du Yuesheng, to try to borrow fifty thousand US dollars. By then the Great World, the Gongwutai, the Dawutai, and his other enterprises were all under the management of their workers, and he could no longer exploit the people, while the cash in his hand had been stolen clean away by his daughter-in-law; so, finding it harder and harder to meet the great outlay of a Huang mansion, he told me to borrow from Du — most likely out of helplessness. After I reached Hong Kong I raised the matter with Du Yuesheng, and Du, to my face, expressed his deep regret, saying he would speak of it with "sister Huang" (that is, Li Zhiqing) before deciding; and afterwards there was no more said of it.

Before I went to Hong Kong, I had Yang Hu's charge to persuade Du Yuesheng, Jin Tingsun, Wang Xiaolai, and the rest to return to the mainland, the government letting bygones be bygones, in the hope that they would turn from their evil ways and, before the people, atone for their crimes by good service. Wang Xiaolai, after hearing my persuasion, at once declared himself willing to return to Shanghai; but Du, Jin, and the other exiled gang men, far from agreeing, flatly refused, and instead urged me to tell Huang Jinrong to go quickly to Hong Kong, that he might not fall into the net of the people's law. After I got back to Shanghai and told Huang what Du and Jin had charged me to pass on, Huang pondered a while, then asked me: "Zhenshi, what do you think is to be done?" I said: "Better not to go." Huang was silent, with nothing to say.

In some month of 1953, the vice-mayor of Shanghai, Sheng Pihua, urged Huang to make a frank confession to the Military Control Commission. Huang had Gong Tianjian draw up a written confession, and, accompanied by Chen Dianting, head of the Zhoushan Native-place Association, went to the Shanghai Military Control Commission in the Central Bank building on the Bund. They were received by the leader Su Yu and the vice-mayor Sheng Pihua. By Chen Dianting's later account to me, after Huang had submitted his confession and given an account of his historical crimes, he walked out of the leader's office together with Chen; and when the two had just reached the door downstairs, Chen suddenly found that he had left a thing of his in the leader's office upstairs, and told Huang to wait a moment at the door while he hurried up to fetch it. When Chen came down again, Huang was no longer there, and though he searched everywhere he could not find him. Afterward it was supposed that some upset had come over Huang, and that he had, in his impatience, gone back to the Huang house alone. Not long after this, Huang chanced to fall ill of a fever, and within a few days, past cure, he died.
