## New Year's Eve

It was not yet seven in the evening, and the mouth of Tongfu Li was already thick with the smoke of firecrackers set off there.

Chen Qianyuan and Dong Huiwen turned into the lane carrying a box of five-kernel New Year cake. Every household was making ready its New Year's Eve dinner, and in the clinic too a round table had been set out. Qin Chuan'an had ordered the dishes from the little Dexing restaurant slantwise across the road, striking a few of the grander dishes from the banquet menu; but, remembering that the comrades had just suffered in the lockup, he had added back the oil-seared shrimp and the zaobodou.

The round table was set in the downstairs parlor. Qin Chuan'an shut the front door he ordinarily used and left the back door, which faced the lane, on the latch, ajar. By now the guests had arrived one after another. Yi Junnian and Ling Wen came by rickshaw and brought a crock of Shaoxing wine besides. At seven the firecrackers outside were like rolling thunder, and every household in the lane had begun to eat; Qin Chuan'an, seeing that only Cui Wentai had not come and guessing that he was most likely tied up driving a fare and there was no knowing when he would arrive, called everyone to the table.

Yi Junnian raised his cup, and with some feeling said in a lowered voice: “Some of us, a month ago, did not know one another, and now we've become fellow-sufferers — no, you're right — comrades-in-arms. To lose touch with the organization, and then all at once to make contact again — this stretch of time has truly been enough to stop the heart. Come, comrades, let us drink a cup together, and hope the organization completes its internal inquiry as soon as may be, so that we can return to work the sooner.”

All raised their cups but for Lin Shi, who carried the gunshot wound.

Liang Shichao said, stubborn as ever: “All I want now is to go back to the Soviet area, back to the ranks to fight. Real blades and real guns — clean and hearty.”

Tian Fei asked Yi Junnian, “What exactly did the higher-ups say to you?”

“Wait patiently.”

“What's there to wait for? We ran the mole down long ago.” Tian Fei glared at Lin Shi.

“It's the height of the New Year — don't you go making trouble,” Yi Junnian glared back at him. He had once been Tian Fei's superior; later Tian Fei had transferred to another system, and as a rule, even if they met in the street, they would feign not to know each other.

Ling Wen laid a piece of fried fish in the bowls of both Lin Shi and Tian Fei, and said to Tian Fei: “As to this matter, everyone had their say here the other day.”

Tian Fei saw that Lin Shi said nothing at all, even smiled at him — a smile that seemed to say, you're so childish, I won't stoop to argue with you — and at once he flung the piece of fish he had picked up back into his bowl. “To my eye, of all these people it's Lin Shi who's the most like an agent. Never says a word as a rule, makes secret telephone calls behind the comrades' backs, and then there's this business of a bank safe-deposit box. A revolutionary comrade is open and aboveboard; you, I'd say, don't look like an honest man.”

“Hold your tongue!” Yi Junnian too was growing heated; he set his emptied cup down on the table. “Do you imagine that suspecting shadows and guessing wildly like this is any way to run down a mole? I can see nothing amiss in the things you've named; some people like to talk, and some do not.”

“Just so,” said Wei Dafu, nodding as he ate.

“And besides — the people at the meeting in the market, did you get a clear look at every one of them?”

“There is one more who was neither arrested nor got in touch with us afterward,” Ling Wen put in.

“The trouble may lie elsewhere too; everyone must submit to the organization's scrutiny — Old Fang included —”

“Old Fang has been missing so many days now.” Qin Chuan'an signed to everyone to quiet down. “Nothing can have happened to him, surely?”

“Comrade Old Fang has given his life,” Yi Junnian said in a low voice.

The air in the room froze. Several of them had been in single-line contact with Old Fang for years, long used to his bringing them their superiors' instructions, used to his mild and steadfast manner. All these days, though they had heard no word from above, it was only now, hearing that Old Fang had given his life, that a loneliness of having lost the organization seemed truly to rise from the bottoms of their hearts.

“Where did you get the news?” Wei Dafu asked urgently.

Yi Junnian was silent a moment. “The organization has an inside line.”

“Is the news reliable?” Wei Dafu had just ladled himself a bowl of yanduxian, but now he could not swallow even the salt pork he loved best; he did not want to believe the news that Old Fang had given his life. “What's happened lately truly beggars belief. And that special commissioner — there's something secretive about how he goes about things too, as though he'd sprung out of a stone, appearing before you all of a sudden.”

“This comrade, it's true —” Yi Junnian thought a moment. “Since I joined the revolution I've never felt the situation so grave as of late. The secret meeting-place was discovered by the enemy; after the release Old Fang was nowhere to be found; then the special commissioner suddenly appeared, seeming to know every means of contact and every recognition sign, meeting us one by one, yet giving no word of any instructions from above. We can't go suspecting a comrade lightly — but neither can we let down our guard.”

A gust of cold air, and Cui Wentai pushed the door open and came in. He stopped Qin Chuan'an, who was about to rise: “I've shut the back door. When you eat the New Year's Eve dinner the door should be closed.”

He seemed not to notice the heaviness in the air; the moment he sat down he caught sight of the bowl of zaobodou on the table, reached over and drew it to him, saying “Pig's offal is my favorite,” and ladled a big spoonful into the small bowl before him and stuffed a great mouthful in. “I'm famished — I ran a fare out to Dongjiadu and waited an age, cold and hungry, and a big bowl of noodle soup wasn't enough to see me through. What's the matter —”

He asked it, seeing that no one made a sound.

“Old Fang has given his life,” said Tian Fei, his eyes reddening.

Cui Wentai's face stiffened; his spoon fell with a clink into the bowl, though the chopsticks stayed gripped in his hand. He tried to squeeze onto his face some show of grief.

“What happened?” His voice was a little dry.

No one answered him.

“What happened?” he asked again, his voice grown hoarse.

Tian Fei, having held it back so long, let the tears fall at last, and said to Cui Wentai, choking on the words: “You were his courier — you were closest of all to Old Fang.”

“That's so —” Cui Wentai too wanted to get out a sob or two, but, mumbling, he could not quite bring himself to weep.

Yi Junnian watched him closely. “Have you seen Old Fang lately?”

Cui Wentai did not answer, but turned his head to look at Tian Fei. “What in the world happened?”

Tian Fei shook his head and looked at Yi Junnian.

It would be hard to say there was not a trace of remorse in Cui Wentai's heart, above all now that he had had to put on this performance. Old Fang was not only a superior; he had been like an elder brother. He had never thought Old Fang would be shot dead. He had even supposed that once the whole thing was over he might talk him round, get him too to jump clear of the “mire.” All those days he had been turning over what he might say to Old Fang to change his mind. Tell him the revolution had no future? He guessed that would likely be of no use. Old Fang had once said to him that underground work was like a ray of light in the darkness, and that to run toward that light he would dare to leap into the abyss.

“Old Fang's son was taken too, and badly hurt,” Yi Junnian said heavily.

Lin Shi quietly poured himself a little wine and drank it off at a draught.

Yi Junnian raised his cup too, and said: “To — Old Fang!”

The whole table raised their cups and drank the wine down.

“When did you begin following Old Fang?” Tian Fei asked Cui Wentai.

Cui Wentai took a big mouthful of the offal, gone cold now. “The year the Northern Expedition forces came, I suppose. I joined the workers' pickets, and after that I was with Old Fang the whole time. First the labor movement, then underground, as Old Fang's courier.”

The year the Northern Expedition forces drew near Shanghai, Cui Wentai was saddled with a load of gambling debts — a thing Old Fang never knew. The creditors chased him from his home to the bus company where he worked, for he was a driver there then. Just as he was at the end of his rope, the Northern Expedition all but handed him a way out. All at once the whole of Shanghai began to seethe.

In February the bus workers' union declared a strike, and without a second thought he joined the workers' pickets. With a hundred or two hundred picketmen standing at his back, the creditors could not come near.

Not daring to go home, he stood watch at the depot every day; with that mountain of gambling debt on him he feared neither heaven nor earth — foreign taipan, constable, gang boss, let who would come, not one bus would he let out of the depot. Before long the men of the pickets took him for a leader, and day after day he came and went with a few brothers at his side, and no longer feared the debt-collectors' coming to his door.

By March a tenants' rent-reduction movement had sprung up as well, and he lost no time in joining the tenants' union, which settled another great difficulty he faced. With all this he got the taste of revolution, and threw himself the more eagerly into the high tide of the Great Revolution on the eve of the Northern Expedition army's entry into Shanghai. It was then that Old Fang took notice of him.

“I wasn't with Old Fang long,” Tian Fei said to Cui Wentai, taking several fierce gulps of wine, his face flushed and his ears hot. “You tell us about Old Fang.”

“Old Fang saved me. In April of the sixteenth year of the Republic, the Twenty-Sixth Army surrounded the bus company and ordered the workers' pickets to hand over their weapons and disband on the spot. First they fired two shots into the air, then the machine guns, till the walls were all pocked with bullet-holes, and then they charged in. We fought a while, but couldn't hold — we had only a few Mauser pistols and a few dozen old-style rifles. Then they gave their word they'd only take our arms, not our men, and so we laid down our guns. But it was a trick: once we'd laid down our guns, they seized several of us pickets' leaders and shut us in a shed by the gatehouse, saying we were to be shot then and there. Old Fang led his men, and while the soldiers were rushing inside to search and ransack, cut his way in and got us out.”

As he talked, Cui Wentai shed tears. When the troops and police were combing the whole city by their lists for the leaders of the workers' pickets, Old Fang hid Cui Wentai in his own home. “He taught me a great deal about how the capitalists and the reactionary warlords oppress the people; he said that in dark times we must hold together all the more. From then on I truly set out on the revolutionary road.”

Was it so? Cui Wentai asked himself secretly. He had never been a man given to searching his own conscience, but now, for the first time in his life, he felt with astonishment that there were two different little men inside him, forever jeering and sneering at each other.

“— I went underground as Old Fang's courier. He told me that when the chance came he could send me away to study, but that for now I must train myself, and learn as fast as I could to be a seasoned underground worker. He would go with me into the streets and point things out — what lie of the ground to use to see whether an agent was tailing you behind, how to shake off a tail, how to change your look in a moment with whatever came to hand. He got hold of a Municipal Police training manual and taught me hand-to-hand fighting.

“The two of us drove out to the Fourth Company of the Fifteenth Bao in Fengxian, where there's a great stretch of reed marsh by the sea, and there I learned to shoot. Old Fang was a fine shot: a sparrow in a tree dozens of paces off, he'd raise his hand, and one shot brought it down.

“In the twentieth year of the Republic came the great floods, and cholera was spoken of everywhere; my wife and child both caught it, and within a few days both were dead. In that time Old Fang even set aside the discipline of underground work and had me stay in his home, sharing a room with his son — his son was then apprenticed at a barbershop, learning the trade.”

The more Cui Wentai talked the more worked up he grew, as though by it he could cover something over.

In truth he had long since wanted out. When the Twenty-Sixth Army's machine guns had raked them, he had taken fright. Without Old Fang he could never have held on all these years. He was on the troops' and police's lists; he had joined the strike; he had been a small leader of the workers' pickets — he could never have found work. Old Fang had brought him underground, and the organization, through its connections, had placed him as a driver at a car-hire firm. The wages of that post were nothing to sneeze at. And so, again, he could not say he wanted out. Even his surety bond had been arranged for him by the organization — could he simply up and run? Yet underground work grew more and more dangerous, and he felt that Old Fang was holding him fast by the bond of feeling — which, he thought, went to show that he was, after all, a man of feeling and loyalty himself.

When all was said and done, it was Xiaowuzi's fault — though, to be sure, it was he himself who had crept under the quilt. What could he do? His wife was dead. That woman was something else. Had it been another woman, he might have found some way to leave Old Fang, to slip quietly away after a time. But this woman had turned his head completely.

That day he had carried a letter to a secret Communist underground station, and coming out found someone shadowing him. Somehow, in a flash, he made up his mind. He kept going back to that moment — was it because of the night before? That night, muddled and giddy, he had turned off the light and in the dark lifted the quilt and gazed a long while at Xiaowuzi, as she lay there like a soft bar of glutinous-rice cake.

Perhaps it was because he was by nature a man who loved to have the wheel in his hands: in the driver's seat, a whole carful of people was his to command, and he could drive where he pleased and as fast as he pleased. Looking at the gaudy advertising hoardings along the street, thinking that danger might befall him at any moment, that in half a lifetime he had never known one truly good day, he resolved to see it through once he had begun — and all at once he switched off the engine, opened the door, planted himself before the little agent who was tailing him, and said: “I have important intelligence. I'm willing to come over to you — but I must see your highest officer.”

He was passed from one place to another, and at last was brought before Ye Qinian. Ye Qinian said to him: “We mean to send you back at once; time is short — you've been with us near two days now, and if we delay longer you'll not be able to go back. Henceforth your code name is Xi Shi.”

Yi Junnian asked him suddenly: “Before Old Fang gave his life, had you seen him?”

Cui Wentai was blank a long moment, thrown off by the sudden interruption, unable quite to gather himself — and it was as though his remembrance of Old Fang were just entering some moment brimming with feeling, so that he could not understand why the others had not been moved by his words.

“Only once. I fetched a letter from the secret letter-box and gave it to him.”

“What letter?” Tian Fei asked, unable to wait.

Dong Huiwen glanced at Ling Wen, and made as if to speak, then held back.

“I don't know,” said Cui Wentai, his head bowed. “Old Fang only had me carry the letters.”

He had sold intelligence, and sold comrades, and each time had a sum of money for it — this Ye Qinian had promised him beforehand. He was not uneasy over those betrayals; rather he felt a little pleased with himself. Now he drove his own car again, and need not take orders in everything from others, not even from Old Fang. Until he sold Old Fang — yes, he admitted honestly to himself.

Cui Wentai knew where Old Fang's son had learned his trade; he had only to go there and ask the master, and he would learn where the apprentice's shop had been set up. You knew all along where Old Fang was hiding: you took the letter from the box and handed it to him, and so you knew where he would go next. Director Ye had handed you over to Captain You, and Captain You told you to hand Old Fang over to him; but that time you could not bring yourself to do it, and let Old Fang slip away, not telling Captain You in time. You never thought that Captain You knew you had gone to fetch the letter, knew you had met with Old Fang; he flew into a rage, said you were riding two boats, two-faced and treacherous, and that if you did not give up Old Fang within three hours he would have you taken to Longhua at once and dealt with as a Communist.

The street blazed with light, and in the lane every household too had turned on all its lamps. Only the stretch beneath the archway at the mouth of Tongfu Li was a patch of pitch-black. Two men lurked in the darkness; while everyone else was in the bright warmth, setting out offerings to their ancestors and eating the New Year's Eve dinner, these two huddled in the dark, the cold wind boring ceaselessly in through their clothes. Of the two, one leaned in the corner of the wall smoking, and the other fished a handful of melon seeds from his pocket and cracked them, cracking a whole ground's worth of shells, and feeling the hungrier for it.
