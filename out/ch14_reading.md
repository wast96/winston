### Opening a New Chapter (Part 1)

### Correcting Pure Terror Tactics and Turning to Political Struggle

From the founding of the Central Special Section, Gu Shunzhang had been in charge of running its daily work, and oversaw the courier line between Shanghai and the soviet areas as well. For a stretch of the time he headed the Section, his appetite for terror actions, his disregard for the political direction of clandestine work, and his forgetting of Party policy in the course of operations turned serious political struggle into bare terror and did grave harm to the work again and again. In the Section's early days, for all the severity of the White Terror, Shanghai was a city with a revolutionary tradition, the place where the nation's working class was most concentrated and most powerful. The successive storms of struggle in the age of the Great Revolution had dealt very heavy blows to the reactionary forces of imperialism and the feudal warlords. As late as 1929 the influence of the Great Revolution still held; the workers and the revolutionary forces were still strong, the reactionaries dared make no rash move, and the Party's clandestine struggle went forward fairly smoothly. In those days Gu Shunzhang could muster many guns at short notice, machine guns among them; he could go openly to a foreign firm to buy weapons, or have a foreign firm ship arms in; he could move grain from a grain shop with a machine gun set on the cart, able to drive anywhere at will and open fire. That whole apparatus of terror could, under the right conditions, still bring victory, because the enemy was genuinely afraid, and a traitor could not stay in Shanghai for long.

Gu Shunzhang had come up as a Green Gang hoodlum and had wormed his way into the revolutionary ranks as an opportunist, and his gangster nature came out little by little. In his early days leading the Red Squad he had drawn the "little brothers" of his old Green Gang hoodlum outfit into the "Dog-Beating Squad," so that to a degree its ranks were impure and its thinking impure, a hindrance to the struggle. Gu Shunzhang even used the "Dog-Beating Squad" to fight the private feuds of his cronies in the Green Gang. Once, when the hoodlum boss Gu Zhuxuan—a man from northern Jiangsu, a "Tong"-generation Green Gang thug and proprietor of the Xin Wutai theater in Shanghai—fell out with Chang Chunheng, who owned another theater, Gu Shunzhang led Red Squad men to help Gu Zhuxuan beat Chang Chunheng to death. Such conduct ran flatly against the Party's principles and its organizational discipline, and left unchecked it would have brought harm to the Party's work. On top of this, in March 1928 some Red Squad men beat to death, at the Dingxin Hotel in Shanghai, Li Zhongdan, who had come to Shanghai to buy weapons and raise a landlord militia; it went badly wrong, and most of the Red Squad men involved were arrested, some turning traitor and some dying of illness, so that the Red Squad's fighting strength was gravely weakened. Zhou Enlai therefore set to overhauling the Third Section and the Red Squad with his own hand, moving out those with the hoodlum cast of mind and the unfit, and bringing in cadres of sound Party character and upright bearing, so that the Red Squad's whole face was much changed.

The Central Special Section wielded great power, yet Zhou Enlai kept it under the leadership of the CCP Center from first to last, and his demands on its work and its discipline were of the strictest. There was a time when certain Party members, unable to stand the test of the White Terror, gave themselves up and turned traitor, and the Party took to eliminating the individual traitors who did the most damage, gradually curbing that foul trend. But some comrades in the grassroots organizations, and Gu Shunzhang of the Special Section, made a game of killing traitors, striking blindly without regard for how much harm a man had done or none, manufacturing a "Red Terror," cutting themselves off from the masses and leaving themselves isolated, and doing real political damage. More than once Zhou Enlai criticized Gu Shunzhang and the grassroots organizations, and put a stop to the blind killing.

As the tide of victory rose, Gu Shunzhang's mistaken thinking came out more and more gravely. Momentary success went to his head; carried away with himself, he grew intoxicated with terror actions. He had killing in his eyes, and people came to see him as a "murder fiend."

Such a course plainly could not sit with the Party's long-term interests, and ran counter to the guiding policy of its secret work. Left to run on, it was bound to bring grave consequences: to lose the sympathy of people in society and put the Party in danger of isolation. And since the execution of the traitor Bai Xin, the enemy had gathered strength and changed his tactics, and the sudden shift in the objective situation forced us to change course. Zhou Enlai worked hard to correct Gu Shunzhang's terror actions and to turn the Party's clandestine struggle onto the track of political struggle. For a time the central office of the concession detectives held a weekly meeting of twenty or thirty detective chiefs at the Yipin Xiang Hotel. Gu Shunzhang meant to blow the whole lot of them up by terror, and had readied several large suitcases of explosives and time bombs, got through his secret-service connections from a Japanese barracks. Had he acted on his plan, the hotel building would have been destroyed, the Yipin Xiang reduced to rubble, and the residents round about grievously harmed. Zhou Enlai stepped in at that point and stopped these terror actions.

In leading the work of the Central Special Section, Zhou Enlai held throughout to the guiding idea on which the Section had been founded, keeping a firm grip on the political direction and steering the Section clear of the blind alley of bare terror. He stressed it again and again: no reckless killing of traitors, and only those who did great harm; no striking at the openly declared secret agents; no kidnapping for ransom. In this period he checked, and sharply criticized, more than once, Gu Shunzhang's leaning—in his handling of the Central Special Section's concrete work—away from the Party's long-term interests and the principles of secret work, his one-track pursuit of surface show and his zeal for bare terror; and he kept the Central Special Section holding a clear political direction in its clandestine struggle. He also went to work himself on Yang Du and others, setting an example.

Gu Shunzhang's vicious streak of personal character was fast developing at this time too, laying bare an overweening personal ambition. In his dealings with the Party he grew arrogant and overbearing, swaggering and unbridled, to a point past all bearing. He sank into corruption and dissipation, his life debauched; he smoked opium, and even sought out a "physiognomer" to read his face and tell his fortune, who flattered him that he had "the look of high fortune" and would one day be emperor. Gu Shunzhang kept a "mansion" in a shikumen house at No. 805 Weihai Road in the British Settlement, its furnishings quite lavish. On the pretext of "work needs" he pursued luxury in his living. When the motorcar was still an uncommon sight he already had one to get about in. Only Chen Geng and Li Qiang could go to his house. Chen Geng went a few times and said to Ke Lin on coming back, "If the two of us don't get killed, we'll surely live to see Gu Shunzhang turn traitor!" Zhou Enlai criticized him sharply more than once, pointing out to him that corruption and dissipation in one's private life ran flatly against the moral standard of communism. Because he feigned compliance and would not mend his ways for all the counsel, in the summer of 1930 the Center, on Zhou Enlai's proposal, took decisive measures against any mishap: it sent Gu Shunzhang to work in Wuhan, and put Chen Geng in charge of the Special Section's work. Chen Geng carried out with resolve the guiding policy Zhou Enlai had laid down. He worked hard to set the political direction straight and to break free of terror actions, conducting inquiries into every social class, applying himself to building ties with political figures, and developing relations among the detectives and the concession police—turning the Party's secret work onto the track of political struggle. And so the work opened up fast and wide, one victory following another, and Shanghai's Party offices could thereby be protected under a White Terror of the utmost menace.

### Yang Du, a Genius for the Ages

As the clandestine struggle shifted its weight toward the political field, coming to know one representative political figure after another, the work opened up a further stage. Zhou Enlai's winning of the extraordinary talent Yang Du to work for the Party, and Yang Du's joining the Chinese Communist Party, is one telling instance of success.

Yang Du, also known by his courtesy name Yang Xizi, was a legendary figure in modern Chinese history. As a patriotic intellectual, he travelled an uncommonly winding and rugged road in his search for the truth that would save the country. He went to study in Japan in 1920, and under the influence of Western bourgeois revolutionary doctrine and the revolutionary current among Chinese students there, he called for overthrowing the Qing by revolutionary means to save the nation from ruin; then, swayed by the constitutional-monarchist arguments of Kang Youwei, Liang Qichao, and their kind, he came to advocate "saving the nation through constitutional monarchy" and to oppose the democratic revolution that Sun Yat-sen led. Though he was always anti-Manchu, he did not believe China could work a republic, and held that China must have an emperor to rule it. So when the Revolution of 1911 overthrew the Qing emperor, he became a leading figure in supporting Yuan Shikai's bid for the throne. In 1915 he organized the Peace Planning Society, and with Sun Yujun, Hu Ying, Li Xiehe, Liu Shipei, and Yan Fu was called one of the "Six Gentlemen of the Peace Planning Society," manufacturing public opinion for Yuan Shikai's enthronement and "urging him on," a stalwart of Yuan Shikai's throughout. In those days he published his sprawling essay of ten thousand words, "Saving the Nation Through Constitutional Monarchy," crying up the Hongxian monarchy, a famed constitutional monarchist. After Yuan Shikai died in 1916, Yang Du was put on the wanted list. Thereafter, as the times moved on, Yang Du came gradually under the influence of the revolutionary movement, and around the time of the May Fourth Movement his thinking underwent a great change: he turned and struck back at the old forces, moving from reaction to revolution, from a famed royalist into a sympathizer of the Communists. In the winter of 1926 he poured out his high ambition in a poem in the seven-character regulated form:

{p} Teapot and medicine-mortar keep a lone man company; / the age turns vast and dim, and the white hairs come anew. / In all the market town, who knows the man of state? / The rivers and lakes will let you pass for a poet. / Within my breast are armies, reaching to the Dipper's war; / before my eyes lie spear and shield, out to the border dust. / Still, at a single shrine, I mean to take up the brush: / a scholar's heart and reach were boundless from the start.

Because Yang Du's political turns had been so abrupt, the changes in his thinking were hard to grasp, and he became something of an enigma. Historians of the modern age, speaking of him, mostly called him arrogant and vain, a man who "knew his errors and would not mend them." After Yuan Shikai's death, in particular, he had gone so far as to tell the press he would "sooner stand trial than admit a fault." But in the end he gave in before the lessons of hard fact, and did make an honest reckoning of himself. The short-lived farce of Zhang Xun's restoration in 1917 made him feel that the case for saving the nation through constitutional monarchy could no longer be held. After that restoration failed, he sent an open telegram to Zhang Xun and Kang Youwei declaring: to change from a republic back to monarchy is by its nature to swim against the current. And when Sun Yat-sen reorganized the Kuomintang and worked with the Chinese Communist Party, Yang Du went to Shanghai to see Sun Yat-sen, admitted to his face his past refusal to cooperate, granted that his old "constitutional-monarchist" case had not suited the demands of China's situation, and expressed a wish to join the reorganized Kuomintang. Introduced by Sun Yat-sen, he came to know Li Dazhao, who was calling at the same time. In his turn to revolution he was deeply influenced by Li Dazhao.

When Li Dazhao was arrested by the Fengtian warlord Zhang Zuolin in 1927, Yang Du did all he could to save him. The day before Li Dazhao and the others were taken, Yang Du happened at a private banquet to learn from Wang Daxie of Zhang Zuolin's plan for the secret arrests, and at once warned Hu Egong, one of the heads of the Kuomintang's Beijing municipal party bureau; but Li Dazhao could not get away in time. After the arrests, Yang Du and Hu Egong and others worked every angle to save them, all in vain, and the twenty—Li Dazhao among them—were sentenced to hang on April 28. Ruining his own household to meet the crisis, Yang Du sold his "Yuelu" residence in Beijing for 4,500 silver yuan and put the whole sum toward the cost of the rescue and the relief of the victims' families. That autumn, when Cai Hesen and others came to set a northern uprising in motion, Yang Du gave them money too, and his savings were spent to the last. In 1928 he moved to Shanghai, using the study of Buddhism and painting as a cover, and went on working for the revolution. After reaching Shanghai he wrote an essay, "On the Sage-Comrades," borrowing the great-harmony idea of the Confucian master and disciples declaring their aspirations to expound communist theory. His grasp of communist society was as yet not deep, but the essay showed he had accepted a communist worldview. In Shanghai he actively sought out the Party, feeling his way through the connections of the Central Special Section. The comrades concerned at first gave his request less than its due weight; when Zhou Enlai heard of it he went to work on Yang Du himself. After a period of vetting, in 1929, on an introduction and with Zhou Enlai's approval, Yang Du became a full member of the Chinese Communist Party, in single-line contact with a comrade the organization designated.

Accounts differ of how Yang Du came into touch with the Central Special Section in Shanghai and joined the Chinese Communist Party. One book records: How was Yang Du won over? This was in the time when Chen Geng ran the intelligence work. Chen Geng then had a fellow townsman, also a friend from his boyhood, nicknamed Wang Laojiu, who was on close terms with Yang Du. Wang Laojiu would sometimes catch a piece of news from Yang Du and report it to Chen Geng, and by degrees Yang Du became a source of intelligence. In the fierce upheavals of history, Yang Du, from a feudal relic and a disappointed politician, came step by step to see that the Chinese Communist Party was the most promising rising political force in the China of that day. Knowing that Wang Laojiu had ties to the Communists, he meant to draw near the Party through Wang Laojiu's connection. He told Wang that in his life he had passed through feudalism and through capitalism and had seen both fail, and that it seemed the Communists' socialism was bound to flourish and could change China's fate; and he expressed his willingness to lend his strength to the Communist revolutionary cause. Wang Laojiu reported his mind to the Party. The Party Center held that Yang Du's ability to see the trend of the world revolutionary tide and the prospect of China's revolution was rare and precious. Yang Du was now the man behind the scenes for Du Yuesheng (a claim, it should be said, that does not square with the facts of the time), which was of much help to our intelligence work. The Center directed the intelligence system to send someone for a long talk with him and to assign him the task of gathering intelligence directly. About 1929, on an introduction from an intelligence-system officer and with Zhou Enlai's own approval, Yang Du was taken in as a secret member of the Communist Party.

After Yang Du joined the Communist Party, he left his household and moved to Shanghai alone, keeping strictly to Party discipline and never letting his identity show to his relatives. He threw himself heart and soul into the work the Party gave him, not only supplying much important intelligence but helping the Central Special Section build ties on every side. Yang Du knew the politicians of the Political Study Clique well, understood a great deal of their affairs, and gave the Party organization much of the clique's inside situation. In those days Chiang Kai-shek held a number of the clique's leading men in his hand, and through Yang Du we could learn much of the clique's political activity and of its relation to Chiang Kai-shek. Through Yang Du the Central Special Section found not only the connection to the Political Study Clique but, through him, a link to the Kuomintang Reorganizationists, so that we could not only keep abreast of Wang Jingwei and Hu Hanmin but, by this new channel, follow at any time the moves of the whole Nanjing ruling group. Du Yuesheng, the great hoodlum boss of Shanghai, was a trusted claw of Chiang Kai-shek's and a sworn enemy of the Communists, and we had had no dealings with him. But Du Yuesheng affected the airs of a man of culture, set his heart on befriending this genius of the age, and through his legal adviser of the time, Zhang Shizhao, sent Yang Du his regards. This old friend of Yang Du's, charged by Du Yuesheng, came to Yang Du's lodging at the Zhaofeng Villa on Route Frelupt and explained that Du Yuesheng meant to draw on him for something. This set Yang Du a hard problem.

Who was Du Yuesheng? Yang Du of course knew. He was the most notorious of the great hoodlum bosses of Shanghai, and with Huang Jinrong and Zhang Xiaolin one of the three Green Gang magnates who lorded it over the city. Du Yuesheng was born in 1888 at Gaoqiao town in Pudong, Shanghai. His parents both dead in his childhood, he was taken in by an uncle's family; a drinker from boyhood, he pawned and sold off his parents' estate to nothing. In his teens he ran off to Shanghai to live as a rough, knocking about with petty hoodlums and making his living purely by theft and swindling. He first took a brothel madam for a godmother, then a Green Gang hoodlum he had met at the brothel for a "godfather." After that he joined some Green Gang hoodlums in extortion, blackmail, and robbery on the Huangpu River steamer wharves. In 1911 he joined the "Eight-Legged Gang," which ran contraband for drug dealers, and was also taken on as a detective's runner by the French Concession police. At the time the notorious Shanghai hoodlum chief Huang Jinrong was chief inspector of the French police, and Du Yuesheng fawned on Huang Jinrong and flattered him for all he was worth to win his trust. Backed by Huang Jinrong's power, he colluded with opium merchants, took control of the opium traffic, went on to armed robbery, and became an overlord of the Huangpu wharves. Afterward, for his part in getting Huang Jinrong out of prison alongside another great hoodlum, Zhang Xiaolin, the three swore brotherhood. In 1925, at the planning of the French consul-general in Shanghai, the three joined with Chaozhou-gang opium merchants to open the "Sanxin Company" to traffic opium, which brought the French concession authorities enormous profits; Du Yuesheng was rewarded by his masters, made chairman of the general association of the French concession chamber of commerce and inspector of the taxpaying-Chinese association, and his power grew greatly. In April 1927 the three great hoodlum bosses took Chiang Kai-shek's secret order, gathered the Shanghai Green Gang chiefs to organize the "China Mutual Advancement Society," drew funds from Chiang Kai-shek, and formed the hoodlum party members into squads in readiness for a counter-revolutionary coup. On the night of April 11, Du Yuesheng lured out and murdered Wang Shouhua, chairman of the Shanghai General Labor Union, and at the same time set the hoodlum party members bursting out of the Concessions to attack and slaughter the Shanghai workers' pickets, who had rendered great service in the Northern Expedition, colluding with Chiang Kai-shek's reactionary troops to seize the pickets' arms. Du Yuesheng then led his party members with Yang Hu to Ningbo to "purge the Party," slaughtering great numbers of Communists and revolutionary people. After the counter-revolutionary coup of April 12, Chiang Kai-shek appointed Du Yuesheng an adviser to the general headquarters of the army, navy, and air force, a major-general councillor of the Military Commission, and concurrently a temporary Chinese-director adviser to the French concession's municipal council. Wielding his power, he suppressed strikes, opened gambling houses, sold on Chiang Kai-shek's plundered opium in Shanghai, and, at Song Meiling's bidding, joined Wu Tiecheng and others in secretly setting up a morphine factory. He further used the great store of loose funds and dirty money in his hands to open, with others, the Zhonghui Bank in 1929, dealing in opium-secured loans and every other kind of profiteering business... In a moment he had become a reactionary of overweening power, of wealth beyond compare, who stopped at no evil.

Toward such a great hoodlum boss, steeped in the blood of revolutionaries and of the people, Yang Du, full of a sense of justice and of love for his country, felt of course the deepest contempt and loathing. Yet from the standpoint of gathering intelligence, to befriend this man could be of much use. After Zhang Shizhao had raised it several times, Yang Du came to ask the Party organization what to do; the Party encouraged him to make contact with Du Yuesheng. So, through his old friend Zhang Shizhao's urging, Yang Du became Du Yuesheng's nominal "secretary." Du Yuesheng gave Yang Du a small two-story Western house near Rue Chapsal in the French Concession to live in, and gave him 500 yuan a month besides. Some said Yang Du was Du Yuesheng's "disciple," but Yang Du said: I never handed in a pledge card, never lit the incense candles; I call him Mr. Du and he calls me Brother Xizi. To be plain, I am no Green Gang man, but a house guest. In truth, in dealing with such a man, Yang Du, as a revolutionary, was only using Du Yuesheng for a windbreak, nothing more.

In this way, from "no dealings" to "dealings," the Party came into indirect touch with Du Yuesheng too, and could learn more of the doings of Shanghai's hoodlum gangs and pick up some intelligence about Kuomintang notables. In 1930, during the anti-Chiang war launched by Yan Xishan and Feng Yuxiang, it was from Du Yuesheng that we learned Feng Yuxiang had sent a guard of more than three hundred men slipping into the Concessions through Du Yuesheng's connection, to serve as a fifth column once the war reached Shanghai. It was from Du Yuesheng too that we learned Song Ziwen, of the Four Great Families, was trafficking opium into Shanghai in bulk and manufacturing drugs on ships outside the Wusong bar. In 1930 Yang Du got, through these connections, an urgent piece of intelligence that spared the Party a great loss. Yang Du had come over from the enemy camp; the better part of his life had been spent among warlords, bureaucrats, and politicians, so that he knew intimately the inner workings of the old society's officialdom and many of the factional feuds within the Northern warlords and the Kuomintang. In his last years, with his own singular experience, he went into the tiger's den in a China smothered under White Terror and gave the Party the enemy intelligence he gathered—a singular service no one else could have rendered.

The intelligence Yang Du gave the Central Special Section was much of it, and important. Sometimes he put what he had gathered into a box of cigars, sometimes into a large wax-sealed envelope, or passed it to the Party organization by other means. He wrote a fine hand, and had studied painting under Qi Baishi in Beijing. The masthead of Red Flag, the paper the CCP Center published, came from Yang Du's brush. Du Yuesheng had long set his heart on getting Yang Du's calligraphy to hang at home, to screen his own vulgarity and coarseness with it. Because the Western house Yang Du lived in was Du Yuesheng's, the French concession police dared not come to such a place to arrest anyone. In an emergency Yang Du would keep a comrade the enemy was hunting here to "take refuge."

To further his work at the time, Yang Du, after joining the Party, went on in December 1929 to join the China Mutual Aid Society, and donated a sum of money as well. In February 1930, when Lu Xun, Yu Dafu, and fifty-one others launched the China Freedom League, he signed his name to it too. In this same period he took part, in his standing as a public figure, in the activities of the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights—led by Song Qingling, Cai Yuanpei, Yang Quan, Lu Xun, Zou Taofen, and others—and of the League of Social Scientists.

Yang Du's joining the Party, secret though it was, gradually became known within the revolutionary camp. When someone at the time called him arrogant and an opportunist, he said: I joined the Party when the White Terror was at its worst; if you call me an opportunist, the opportunity I seized was one of beheading and the extermination of my line. In the days before his death, gravely ill, he composed his own valediction couplet to speak his mind: "The way of emperors, the truth of Buddha—all of it is now a thing past; healing the people and saving the nation—those who take it up will surely rise after us."

Yang Du, worn out by overwork, died at his lodging in Shanghai on September 17, 1931. Zhou Enlai never forgot the important service he had done the revolution after joining the Party. Because Yang Du joined the Party late and died early, and was a secret member kept in single-line contact, few knew of his Party membership. Not until 1975, a few months before Zhou Enlai's death, did he send a secretary to the State Cultural Relics Bureau to tell its director, Wang Yeqiu: In the days when Yuan Shikai proclaimed himself emperor, the first of the "Six Gentlemen of the Peace Planning Society," Yang Du, joined the Communist Party in the end, introduced and directly led by Premier Zhou. The Premier said: Please tell the Cihai editorial office in Shanghai that if Cihai carries an entry for Yang Du, the fact of his joining the Communist Party in the end must be written in.

On Zhou Enlai's instruction, the Cihai published in 1979 wrote of Yang Du in its entry:

Yang Du (1874–1931), a modern man of Xiangtan, Hunan. Courtesy name Xizi. A student of Wang Kaiyun, he studied in Japan. In 1902 (the twenty-eighth year of Guangxu), with Yang Dusheng and others, he founded the journal Youxue Yibian; he later drafted the report for the five ministers the Qing government sent abroad to study constitutional government, and served as director of the Bureau for the Compilation of Constitutional Government. In 1907 he edited the monthly China New Journal, advocating constitutional monarchy and demanding that the Qing government convene a parliament. After the Revolution of 1911 broke out, at Yuan Shikai's instigation, he and Wang Jingwei organized the National Affairs Mutual Aid Society. After Yuan Shikai dissolved parliament in 1914 he served as a councillor of the Political Council; the next year he joined with Sun Yujun, Yan Fu, Liu Shipei, Hu Ying, Li Xiehe, and others to form the Peace Planning Society and plotted the restoration of the monarchy. After Yuan Shikai's death he was put on the wanted list. Later he inclined to revolution; around the time Li Dazhao was arrested by the warlord Zhang Zuolin in 1927, he tried in many ways to save him. In his last years he moved to Shanghai and joined the China Mutual Aid Society and other progressive bodies. In the autumn of 1929 he joined the Chinese Communist Party, holding to the Party's work under the White Terror.

In his essay "An Unforgettable Memory," Wang Yeqiu set down the words Zhou Enlai's secretary had come to tell him at the time: "After I heard it, I told the Cihai editorial office in Shanghai on the one hand, and asked around among many people on the other, and most found it very strange, having never heard that Yang Du had joined the Communist Party. But everyone agreed that the Premier, gravely ill, still remembered to charge the comrades concerned with this matter, which showed how he cared for his comrades, never forgetting anyone who had done any service for the revolution—truly a model of an open heart and of embodying Party policy at every turn."

### Two Members of Parliament: Mei Baoji and Hu Egong

The Party had built ties with well-known figures like Yang Du before. Back in the Wuhan period, a member of parliament from the Northern warlord era, Mr. Mei Baoji, had supplied intelligence to the special-service section of the Central Military Commission. He was the father of the Communist Mei Zhonglin and the uncle of the Communist Mei Gongbin. Mei Zhonglin was killed by the Kuomintang not long after the Nanjing Uprising. Mei Baoji's life had gone thoroughly Japanese; his wife was Japanese, and in Wuhan he lived in the Japanese concession, sitting each day on the tatami smoking opium. He was among the earliest of the upper-class men to work with us in the building of our Party's intelligence work. Because he had served long in the old society and knew a good many warlords and bureaucrat-politicians, his information was well connected and the intelligence he supplied was of real value. Living in the Japanese concession, he could regularly pass us intelligence got from the Japanese, and every day or two intelligence would come, most of it accurate. At the time it was Li Qiang, chief of the special-service branch of the Central Military Commission's special-service section stationed in Wuhan, who kept in touch with Mei Baoji himself, bringing him 200 yuan a month in living support and paying his rent for him besides.

In Shanghai, once the Central Special Section shifted the weight of its struggle to the political field, the upper-class political figures it kept in touch with grew by the day. Another member of parliament from the Northern era, and a close friend of Yang Du's, Hu Egong, was one such.

Hu Egong (1884–1951), courtesy name Xinsan, alias Nanhu, was a native of Jiangling, Hubei. A member of the Tongmenghui, he had in his early years followed Sun Yat-sen in the Revolution of 1911. After the Wuchang Uprising, Hu Egong served as commander-in-chief of the Hubei army's land and naval forces, leading the Wuhan land and naval forces in fierce battle with the Qing army. Later assigned as plenipotentiary of the Hubei military government, he went to the Beijing-Tianjin region to take charge of liaison in the north. Reaching Beijing, he joined the Beijing-Tianjin Tongmenghui and served on its military department; incensed at the domineering of its chairman Wang Zhaoming, that is Wang Jingwei, he joined with Xiong Deshan, Qian Rutie, and others, in the name of the Hubei army's representatives, to set up a general headquarters of the northern revolutionary army at Tianjin, with Hu Egong as its concurrent commander-in-chief, to attack the Qing army; it was disbanded after the Luanzhou uprising failed. He then joined with the various northern revolutionary bodies, the "Tongmenghui" and the rest, to form at Tianjin the "Northern Revolutionary Association," taking the chairmanship himself and pressing the revolutionary work forward. In April 1913 he was elected a first-term member of parliament for Hubei. Around the time Yuan Shikai proclaimed himself emperor, he took part in the anti-Yuan movement. At the turn of 1920 and 1921 he and Xiong Deshan and others linked up Hubei youth to organize a "Marxist Study Society" and put out the journal Today, with Xiong Deshan as its editor. Its members were afterward mostly taken into the northern branch of the Chinese Communist Party.

In August 1922, when the first parliament reconvened, Hu Egong returned again to Beijing as a member of parliament, until November 1924. In this time he served as vice-minister of education. In July 1924 he joined with 150 members of both houses and mass bodies like the Beijing Students' Union to launch the "Anti-Imperialist League," issuing a manifesto that demanded the imperialist powers, as the Soviet Union had renounced the aggressive policies of tsarist Russia, abolish all the unequal treaties forced on China since the Opium War. This "League" also sent letters to the peoples of Korea and Annam, now Vietnam, proposing that the small and weak nations of the Far East form an anti-imperialist united front and struggle together, a move that won Sun Yat-sen's warm praise. This was the period of Kuomintang-Communist cooperation; Hu Egong was one of the heads of the Kuomintang's Beijing municipal party bureau and was on very close personal terms with Yang Du. In this period the two parties had already built good cooperative ties in the north, and Li Dazhao too was one of the chief heads of the Kuomintang's northern organization. Yang Du formed a close friendship with Hu Egong, and through his ties with them took part in the activities of the Anti-Imperialist League. Later, the day before Li Dazhao was arrested, Yang Du happened at a private banquet to learn of Zhang Zuolin's plan for the secret arrests and at once warned Hu Egong, but Li Dazhao could not get away in time. After Li Dazhao was arrested, Hu Egong, Yang Du, and Zhang Shizhao tried in many ways to save him.

Hu Egong had served long in the old officialdom and had dealings with a great many upper-class men. He knew the Political Study Clique, the Kuomintang Reorganizationists, and the leading men of the several Northern-warlord factions, and the factional strife within the Kuomintang, well enough; and drawing on his many social ties, he regularly supplied important intelligence once he had made contact with the Party. Early in 1931 the Central Special Section was preparing to open political-intelligence work in the north. Hu Egong had many acquaintances among the northern upper class, and the Central Special Section decided to send him north to open political-intelligence work by his social standing and his official connections. Hu Egong asked that Yang Xianzhen be sent as his assistant.

Yang Xianzhen and Hu Egong had not in fact known each other. That January Yang Xianzhen had just returned to Shanghai from Kaifeng. The CCP Center let him rest a little while it waited to reassign him. In Shanghai Yang Xianzhen went by the alias Yang Zhongren and lived at the home of his schoolmate Wang Kaihua. A near neighbor of the Wang Kaihua household, Wu Nanhu—Hu Egong's alias of the time—was an intelligence officer of the Central Special Section. The back gate of the Wang Kaihua house faced Hu Egong's front gate, the two households were often in and out of each other's doors, and Yang Xianzhen, often coming and going by the Wangs' back gate, met Hu Egong at the gate more than once; so the two became acquainted and had a number of talks. When the Central Special Section now decided to send Hu Egong to Tianjin to open political-intelligence work, Hu Egong asked to pick out a capable assistant within the Party to help him carry it through. Short though his acquaintance with Yang Xianzhen had been, after their many talks, and through what he learned of Yang Xianzhen from the Wang Kaihua couple, Hu Egong judged that Yang Xianzhen was not only learned and widely versed, with a fair store of social experience, but upright in character, plain in bearing, quick in thought, hardy and hard-working, unafraid of difficulty—just the ideal assistant he badly needed. So when the Central Special Section told Hu Egong of this task, he recommended Yang Xianzhen to it to go with him, and got the Center's approval straightaway.

In mid-April Hu Egong and Yang Xianzhen took ship from Shanghai to Tianjin. Before they left, the Center had already learned that the Shun-Zhi provincial committee had been gravely broken in Tianjin, and that An Ziwen, Zhou Zhongying, Chen Yuandao, Liu Yaxiong, and others had been arrested one after another. A large batch of leading cadres, once arrested, were held in the detention house of the Tianjin municipal public security bureau; the court had just taken up the cases but had not yet had time to settle them. Here was an opening, and the first task the Central Special Section gave Hu Egong and Yang Xianzhen was to find a way, through every connection, to do all they could to get the arrested Tianjin comrades out of prison. On reaching Tianjin, Yang Xianzhen lived at the Datong Apartments in the Japanese concession; Hu Egong lodged elsewhere, in secret, and only Yang Xianzhen knew where—anyone wishing to see Hu Egong had first to go to Yang, who would receive him and then bring Hu Egong to the Datong Apartments to talk. By this time a new Shun-Zhi provincial committee had been formed in Beiping. On learning that they had reached Tianjin, the provincial secretary, Yin Jian, at once dispatched Lai De, secretary of the provincial "Mutual Aid Society" Party group, to come and help them with the rescue work. Mr. Liu Shaobai, once a fourth-section chief secretary of the Hebei provincial department of construction and a deputy director of the Tianjin municipal commodity-inspection bureau, also hurried from Beiping to Tianjin, running about everywhere to save his daughter Liu Yaxiong, the provincial secretary-general, and his son-in-law Chen Yuandao, organization-department head of the provincial provisional committee, among others. Using his social ties and working many angles, he at last got the Tianjin court to let slip: for four or five thousand silver dollars, this batch of Chen Yuandao's people could be released. Liu Shaobai and Hu Egong had long been good friends, and Liu Shaobai told him how the rescue stood.

Hearing there was hope for the rescue, Hu Egong hurried back to Shanghai to report to the Central Special Section. Having heard him out, the Central Special Section decided to study the matter closely. It was just in this period that, with Gu Shunzhang arrested and turned traitor in Wuhan, Chen Geng and his wife Wang Genying, and Chen Yangshan, left Shanghai fast and moved to Tianjin. Hu Egong had gone to Shanghai and not yet come back, but Yang Xianzhen was living at the Datong Apartments, which Hu Egong had already reported to the Central Special Section. So the moment Chen Geng reached Tianjin he came to the Datong Apartments to find Yang Xianzhen, and it was arranged there and then that Chen Yangshan—who on coming to Tianjin took the alias "Old Wang"—should stay and live with Yang Xianzhen.

Around July 10, Yang Xianzhen bought several of the day's papers early one morning and was suddenly startled by a piece of news in Ta Kung Pao: last night a Communist office had been raided at the "Tingzhou Guild Hall"... The Kuomintang reactionaries of the day had only to break up a Communist organization and they would put it in the papers the next day to show their "merit." Reading this, Yang Xianzhen felt a jolt: was not Lai De, sent by the Shun-Zhi provincial secretary Yin Jian, a Fujian man? He lived at the "Tingzhou Guild Hall," and had been to the Datong Apartments, and had seen Hu Egong here. Could it be... Yang Xianzhen told "Old Wang" at once. "Old Wang," with years of work in the Central Special Section behind him, alert and seasoned, decided on the instant to move house without delay. After they had moved into new quarters, Yang Xianzhen said he wanted to tell Liu Shaobai the new address, so that Liu could come if he had business. Chen Yangshan said no—the new address must not be told to anyone—and had him go to the National Hotel to find Liu Shaobai.

One day in mid-July Chen Geng came to Yang Xianzhen's new quarters and arranged that at eight the next morning Yang Xianzhen should take him to the National Hotel to meet Liu Shaobai. The next day Yang waited for Chen Geng at his lodging. As ill luck would have it they missed the appointed time, and when they reached the National Hotel Liu Shaobai's room was already locked. The waiter said, "Mr. Liu checked out early and left." They had no choice but to leave the National Hotel at once.

As it turned out, Liu Shaobai, hearing word from Beiping, had already moved on. The working funds of the Shun-Zhi provincial committee had all along been passed by the Shanghai Party Center through Liu Shaobai to Chen Yuandao and Liu Yaxiong. After the Shun-Zhi provincial committee was broken in Tianjin and moved to Beiping, the funds were still handed on by Liu Shaobai to the new provincial secretary, Comrade Yin Jian. That funds passed through Liu Shaobai's house was a top-secret matter, known only to Yin Jian and the provincial secretary Guo Yaxian. This time the Shun-Zhi provincial committee was broken in Beiping. Guo Yaxian was arrested and turned traitor, and at once informed the enemy that Liu Shaobai's house was a secret liaison post; the enemy immediately sent gendarmes to Liu Shaobai's house to seize people and search it, carrying off more than 1,500 yuan in the Shanghai Party Center's remittance orders and 1,500 silver dollars of the household's own. The gendarmes even kept up a stakeout search at the Liu house for three whole months. Liu Shaobai was not in Beiping. On the night the trouble broke, his second daughter, Liu Jingxiong, slipped past the gendarmes' watch and wrote a note reading "Trouble at home, on no account come back," which she had a household cook pass to the neighboring Wang family; the Wangs at once sent word to Liu Shaobai in Tianjin. Getting the news, Liu Shaobai left the National Hotel at once. He had meant to tell Yang Xianzhen of all this straightaway, but not knowing his new address he had no choice but to go without a word, taking himself off alone to distant Dalian.

Hu Egong now came back from Shanghai to Tianjin and found Yang Xianzhen, saying the Center had agreed to ransom the arrested Chen Yuandao and the others with money, and had brought the money along, and telling him to go find Liu Shaobai at once. Learning of it, Chen Geng arranged through Yang Xianzhen to meet Hu Egong on the topmost roof of the Zhongyuan Company in Tianjin—now the department store—because there were an open-air dance floor and amusement stands with films and cross-talk up there, and it would draw no notice. So the two of them talked several times running about the rescue of the arrested comrades. Liu Shaobai still had no news then, and did not know what had actually happened. For fear of losing the moment to save the arrested comrades, Hu Egong told Yang Xianzhen to make a quick trip to Beiping and get in touch soon about rescuing the leading comrades the enemy was holding in the prison on Caolanzi Lane.

On July 22 Yang Xianzhen took the train to Beiping. The contact point, as usual, was the Liu Shaobai mansion, Liu Shaobai's home in Beiping, at No. 60 Hufang Bridge outside Peace Gate—the present site of the Jinyang Hotel. This place had been, in the Qianlong reign of the Qing, the former residence of Ji Xiaolan. It had changed hands several times since; by the 1920s, after Liu Shaobai had become the well-paid deputy director of the Tianjin commodity-inspection bureau, he rented it from a great Liu-surnamed landlord of southern Shanxi then come to Beiping—a man who owned twenty-seven residences in the capital—and moved his whole family in in 1928, so that it came to be called the "Liu mansion." At the time Liu Shaobai was riding high in officialdom, the mansion was grandly appointed, its callers eminent, and the soldiers and police never dared suspect anything amiss here. Liu Shaobai had always sympathized with the revolutionary movement the Chinese Communist Party led; many of his household had taken part in revolutionary work, and he too used his social position to work secretly for the Party (he later joined the Communist Party). In the early 1930s the Liu Shaobai mansion was already a convenience for the Party's secret work. After the Party's Shun-Zhi provincial committee moved from Tianjin to Beiping, it became all the more a special liaison point between the Shanghai Party Center and the northern Party. Because Chen Geng and Yang Xianzhen had not found Liu Shaobai at the National Hotel a short while before, they did not know that this residence of his in Beiping had already run into trouble. Reaching Beiping, Yang Xianzhen first put up at an apartment house, meaning to go about his business the next day.

Before Yang Xianzhen left Tianjin, Hu Egong handed him five slips he had written out beforehand, "An Outline for Opening Political-Intelligence Work in the North," and offhand gave him five contacts in Beiping to meet, instructing him to leave each contact one copy of the "outline." These five were Zhang Kexia, He Jifeng, and others... Hu Egong said further that after making contact he should go find Liu Shaobai and then stay in Beiping to open the work. The situation in Beiping was menacing then, the White Terror severe, as they had already heard. When Hu Egong first came back to Tianjin, Yang Xianzhen had told him his own thought: Liu Shaobai's leaving the National Hotel so abruptly must have a reason. Could something have gone wrong at his home? Hu Egong heard him out but said dismissively: No—what could go wrong at his home? Go find him, and that's that.

The next day Yang Xianzhen took the five secret "outlines" and went to meet the contacts one by one, by the addresses, connections, and signals worked out in advance. Around noon he had already seen the fourth contact, whose home was very close to Hufang Bridge and who also knew Liu Shaobai. Yang Xianzhen asked him how things stood at the Liu house; the contact said nothing had happened at the Lius', and added: I know his house, let me take you. When they neared the Liu house Yang Xianzhen sent him back and walked on toward the Liu Shaobai courtyard.

Coming to the gate, Yang Xianzhen stepped through the black-lacquered doors and saw an old woman below the spirit-wall inside. He asked, "Is this Mr. Liu Shaobai's house?" Blank-faced, the old woman answered, "The Lius are in the back court." Many years later Yang Xianzhen recalled the scene: "Before I went into the courtyard I had made up my mind to read the faces of the people first, to judge good luck or bad, because I still suspected Liu Shaobai's sudden departure from the National Hotel must have a cause. But the old woman's wooden face let me make out nothing, so I went straight on toward the back court. In fact, only two days before, Comrade Liu Xiwu had come to Liu Shaobai's house too, and a kind-hearted woman in the front court had signaled with her eyes, 'The Lius are in trouble, run...' Comrade Liu Xiwu, very alert, knew the look of it was bad and turned and left. Who would have thought that just then the gendarmes saw him and chased him out and caught him. Though Liu Xiwu would not admit the neighbor had 'tipped him off,' the gendarmes gave the woman a savage beating all the same, and warned the residents of the front court that no one was to give any 'caller' the least sign, on pain of being charged with 'communicating with the Communists.' I did not know of Comrade Liu Xiwu's arrest, and the old woman below the spirit-wall just now had shown no reaction on her face, so I walked straight to the back court and pushed open the door of Liu Shaobai's house and went in—only to see the young Liu Jingxiong throw herself in terror into her mother's arms. One gendarme sat in the room, another stood behind the door; I was blocked in and could not get out. But it struck me at once that I still had a piece of intelligence on me, the outline; if the enemy searched me it would be ruined, and I had to find a way to destroy it. On the one hand I spun a false statement as I went, talking nonsense to the enemy, keeping them off... and on the other I sized up the two gendarmes. I saw one gendarme leave the room for the front court, probably to telephone a report; the other stood with his back turned, hunting for a scrap of paper to write some 'record' or other. So I sat down on a stool, and seizing this rare good chance I quietly drew from my pocket the slip on which the outline was written, put it in my mouth, chewed it in secret, and at last actually swallowed it down. With no suspicious evidence left on me, I could breathe again, as if a stone had dropped from my chest. The gendarme who had telephoned came back; they trussed me up, took me to the gate, hired two rickshaws, and hauled me straight to gendarme headquarters. There I saw Comrades Liu Lantao and Kong Xiangzhen for the first time; they too had been arrested by the enemy and were held there." —And so Yang Xianzhen's fighting life entered on a great turning point. Not until September 1936, through the rescue of the CCP Northern Bureau and the CCP Central Committee, did he come out of the Kuomintang's "Beiping Military Reflection Sub-Court," that is, the Caolanzi prison.

Word of Yang Xianzhen's arrest reached Tianjin, and Chen Yangshan moved house in haste. Chen Yangshan found a place in the British concession where a general store was below and he lived above it. Hu Egong and Chen Geng could not reach Liu Shaobai, and had lost Yang Xianzhen too, so the rescue work came to a halt.

Chen Geng's trip to Tianjin this time, with Hu Egong's able help, brought major gains. In this period the amusement place on the roof of the Zhongyuan Company building in Tianjin became the chief spot for Chen Geng to meet people from every quarter. Through Hu Egong's connections he met there many political notables of the Northern-warlord era, the house-of-representatives speaker Wu Jinglian, the senate speaker Chen Bolian, and others. Chen Geng also met some Communists in this place. Wu Chengfang, then working on the military committee of the Shun-Zhi provincial committee, made contact with Chen Geng here more than once. Chen Geng told him to strengthen political-intelligence work, to open united-front work, and to carry on military work as well, to be watchful in striking at traitors and eliminating secret agents, and to safeguard the Party organization. This was just the period when Wang Ming's "left" opportunism ruled the whole Party, and Chen Geng's seek-truth-from-facts way of meeting things left a very deep impression on Wu Chengfang. Many years later, speaking of Chen Geng, Wu Chengfang still felt the dealings of those days fresh in memory. He said: What I went through afterward made me see ever more clearly that Comrade Chen Geng was not a man who carried out Wang Ming's "left" opportunist line; he had a seek-truth-from-facts way, setting out from reality in all things. On this point I much admired him. Acting on the spirit he had spoken of to me, from 1931 until the whole country's liberation, the underground organization I was in charge of running was never once broken by the enemy.

After Hu Egong came back from Tianjin to Shanghai, he went on working solely on upper-class political intelligence. He was often in touch with upper-class figures, and kept especially close ties with Feng Yuxiang. At the time Feng Yuxiang was living on Mount Tai in Shandong, much out of favor, and Hu Egong often went to Mount Tai to call on him. Feng Yuxiang's letter, written in rice-water, asking to join the Chinese Communist Party, was the one Hu Egong carried to the CCP provisional Center in Shanghai—a sign of how much Feng Yuxiang trusted him. Through his active efforts, some reliable political intelligence was obtained.

Hu Egong made a certain contribution to the Party in intelligence work. In 1934 Hu Egong was arrested; he afterward went over to the Kuomintang's Kong Xiangxi, and died in Taiwan in the winter of 1951 of heart disease.
