## Chapter Fourteen

The night of thunder and rain had passed, and after it came a soft dawn breeze, a few streaks of rosy cloud, and a blood-red sun newly risen.

In the workshops of the Yuhua Silk Filature the several hundred reeling-frames that had been spinning at full speed all stopped at once. The thunder of the oppressed had broken loose! The women workers came pouring out of the workshops like a tide, swept like a gale to the notice-board before the management office, scattered the few clerks who stood there craning to look, and tore to shreds the wage-cut notice that had just been pasted up.

"Beat the scabs! Beat the running dogs!"

"Bite Qian Baosheng to death! Bite Xue Baozhu to death!"

"Wages as before! Overtime pay for Sundays! The rice allowance!"

The furious crowd shouted like thunder. They spread out their whole battle-line, pressing nearer and nearer the management office. This was their chain! They meant to break this chain!

"Down with Tu the Chamber-pot!"

"Gui Changlin, clear out! Wang Jinzhen, clear out!"

The crowd shouted in disorder, a little less in unison than at the first slogan. Their main body had already surged up to the veranda of that row of rooms that was the management office; they had the office surrounded. In front of them stood pockmarked Li and his twenty men, gripping lengths of lead water-pipe, cursing and threatening. A Xiang was among them too, again and again questioning pockmarked Li with his eyes. But pockmarked Li had had no orders as to what to do; they only watched, and made ready.

Suddenly Tu Weiyue's gaunt figure appeared before the door of the management office! He drew himself up straight, smiling still that cold smile.

The crowd started in surprise. The tide halted. This "chamber-pot"! What audacity! Yet it was only for an instant; then the tide of the crowd, with redoubled courage, pressed forward again. They were on the point of coming to grips with pockmarked Li's band of twenty; the din was louder than thunder; in their fury they now meant, deliberately, to make a frontal assault on the enemy, a hand-to-hand fight! The first spark burst. One party of the crowd had already surged onto the veranda at the other end of the office. Crash! A glass window shattered! It had begun! The crowd flung its whole line into the attack, and a great melee was upon them!

Pockmarked Li could wait for orders no longer. He and his twenty men, wedged into the press of the crowd, laid about them wildly, and step by step they gave ground.

Tu Weiyue too stepped back. From behind him a man suddenly sprang out — it was Wu Weicheng — bawling sharply:

"Pockmarked Li! Hit them! Beat these cheap trash! Seize them!"

"Hit them! — Call the police! Open fire!"

Two more heads thrust out of the window, shouting fiercely — Ma Jingshan and Zeng Jiaju.

By now pockmarked Li and his men were falling back and parrying at once; five or six women workers, caught in the confusion within their line, were fighting bitterly to break out. The main body of the crowd had gained the veranda, and it was plain the office "could not be held." But just at that moment a commotion arose in the crowd's rear. A squad of more than ten policemen drove straight into the ranks of the crowd, opening a way with their bayonets. At once pockmarked Li's men went over to the attack too, and the five or six women workers trapped in their ring were seized outright. The main body of the crowd fell back a little, and the police were all standing on the veranda.

But the crowd did not withdraw; they stood their ground, roaring in fury, making ready for a second assault.

Wu Weicheng, Ma Jingshan and Zeng Jiaju, the three of them together, all sprang out, stamping their feet and shouting:

"Fire! Wipe out these bastards!"

The main body of the crowd answered at once. Their line stirred, moved forward; the uproar was deafening! The police mechanically raised their rifles. Suddenly Tu Weiyue stepped boldly forward, waving the police off with his hand and shouting with all his strength:

"Don't fire! — Set your minds at rest! We will not fire; hear a few words from me!"

"We don't want to hear your dog's fart! Out of the way!"

Part of the crowd roared on and still pressed steadily forward. But the greater part had halted.

Tu Weiyue smiled his cold smile, took another step forward, mounted the stone steps of the veranda, and cried aloud:

"Think it over — can bare hands beat men with knives and guns? You curse me, you want to bring me down, but I am no different from you: I too eat my rice off this mill. If you wreck this mill, aren't you smashing your own rice-bowls? Whatever terms you have, go back and choose delegates to negotiate with me! Go home! I am the one man here standing for peace! Make more trouble, and you will suffer for it here and now!"

Gui Changlin too suddenly slipped out from one side, pressing close up to the main body of the crowd, which had halted and grown quiet, and called out loudly:

"Every word Mr. Tu says is well meant! Go home, everyone! The union will handle the talks, and we'll see to it that none of you loses out!"

"We don't want your dog's union! We want a union of our own!"

A storm of curses rose from the women workers. But now even that small party had halted too. At the same time an indistinct hubbub swelled up out of the main body. Plainly this was no longer the roar of attack, but the crowd itself thrashing out, in a confused babble, its second course of action. Presently one of them stepped forward — it was Yao Jinfeng. First she shouted to the crowd:

"Sisters! They've seized five or six of us! If they don't give them back, we fight to the death!"

The crowd's answer was a roar that set the heart shaking. But the crowd's aim had shifted! At once Yao Jinfeng stepped forward, fixed her eyes on Tu Weiyue's face, and said:

"Give us back our people!"

"They can't be let go!"

Wu Weicheng and the others squeezed out too, bawling fiercely. Pockmarked Li watched Tu Weiyue's face. Tu Weiyue, still smiling his cold smile, gave pockmarked Li his order, firmly:

"Let them go!"

"The people are freed! The people are freed! Go home, everyone! If you have anything to say, send delegates and we'll talk again!"

Gui Changlin shouted from the side as if his throat would burst, running round and round the main body of the crowd. Cheers rose out of the throng; the human tide surged again — but it had changed direction, and was heading for the mill gate. He Xiumei, as she went, shouted at the top of her voice, "Down with Tu the Chamber-pot! Down with Gui Changlin!" — but only a hundred-odd voices took up the cry with her. "Down with Qian Baosheng!" — Yao Jinfeng shouted too, and that answering roar came from the whole body of the women. Chen Yue'e and Zhang Axin walked together, grinding their teeth without cease. Now Chen Yue'e remembered the argument last night between Ma Jin and Cai Zhen. She feared the planned "storming of the mills" could not be brought off either.

But as the tide of the crowd was nearing the mill gate, Zhang Axin shouted "Storm the mills!", and the crowd's answering roar shook the four quarters again.

"Storm the mills! Storm the mills! Storm the 'new mill' first!"

"A general strike! We want a union of our own!"

The women workers, like thunder, like a raging gale, swept across the road and drove straight for Wu Sunfu's "new mill"; and then the combined army of the two mills burst open one mill after another, and their ranks swelled to two thousand, to three thousand, to four or five thousand. In under an hour every silk filature in Zhabei, great and small, had come out on general strike! All Zhabei was strung tight, and double sentries were posted along the roads!

Within the workshops of the Yuhua Silk Filature there was now a deathly stillness. At the main gate stood two pairs of police. But the management office inside was in extraordinary tension. Wu Weicheng and the rest crowded round Tu Weiyue, clamouring, saying he had been too soft. Tu Weiyue said nothing, only smiled his cool smile.

The frantic blare of a motor-horn came crying in from the mill gate. When Tu Weiyue, very composed, ran out of the office to see, Wu Sunfu had already got down from the car, an iron-grey murderousness in his face, his eyes narrowed to a glare, not deigning so much as a single glance at any of them.

Mo Gancheng stood to one side, head hanging, his face deathly white.

Tu Weiyue squared his chest, walked up to Wu Sunfu, and smiled, very cool and very open.

Wu Sunfu shot Tu Weiyue a glance, said nothing, and made a gesture for Tu Weiyue and Mo Gancheng to follow him. First he went to look at the pair of smashed windows at the office, then inspected the empty reeling-room, then inspected every part of the mill; and little by little his face grew easier.

At last Wu Sunfu settled himself in his office and heard Tu Weiyue's report.

Golden sunlight peeped in at the window. A little golden electric fan swung its head to and fro behind Wu Sunfu. Beyond the window a few dark shadows passed: someone was loitering outside, eavesdropping on their talk. As he spoke, Tu Weiyue took it all in, and sneered inwardly.

Wu Sunfu knitted his brows, his lips pressed tight, his sharp gaze darting on every side. Abruptly, impatiently, he cut Tu Weiyue short:

"You think they'd dare lay a hand on the machines, dare set fires, dare rise in revolt?"

"They're like things gone mad — they might do it! But madness can't last long, and once the crowd scatters the heat of it passes off."

"So today, having lost only a few panes of glass, we're to count it wonderful good luck? Count it a victory of ours, is that it?"

There was a barb in Wu Sunfu's words, and he shot Tu Weiyue another cold glance. Tu Weiyue drew himself up and smiled.

"I hear we've detained a few people — a few with 'proof of rioting' on them; I take it you've already sent them over to the Public Security Bureau?"

Wu Sunfu asked it coldly again. But Tu Weiyue saw through at once that the question was put on purpose; he guessed that someone had already reported to Wu Sunfu that those women workers had been let go, and with a good deal of malicious embroidery besides. He answered with a straight face:

"They were let go long ago!"

"What! Just let them go, as easy as that? It seems you released those people simply to keep my mill in one piece? Ha!"

"No! Nothing of the kind! 'There's no end to the arresting of them' — the Third Master said as much to me with his own lips the day before yesterday. Besides, they were only five or six who had followed the herd blindly; to hold them here would have been the more pointless."

For the second time Tu Weiyue caught the heavy sarcasm in Wu Sunfu, and returned him a rubber nail for it. He thrust out his chest and put on the air of one who holds that "a gentleman may be killed but not shamed." He knew that by this device he could bring the headstrong, ruthless Wu Sunfu to heel.

For a moment neither side spoke. Beyond the window another dark shadow flitted by. This time even Wu Sunfu saw it. He knitted his brows. He knew what that shadow meant. He had never liked this sort of furtive, sneaking business. Suddenly, with a grim smile, he said loudly, on purpose:

"Well then, Weiyue, everything here I put in your hands, with full authority! But I mean to start the mill running tomorrow! Tomorrow!"

"I'll do my utmost, according to the Third Master's wishes!"

Tu Weiyue too answered loudly, on purpose, understanding that his "regime" was for the moment secure again. Wu Sunfu gave a short laugh and waved his hand; Tu Weiyue rose to go, but suddenly Wu Sunfu called him back:

"I hear someone here is at odds with you — is it really so?"

"I don't understand which quarter of people the Third Master means."

"On the management side — your colleagues."

"That I myself do not know. I imagine there can be no such thing. We are all working for the Third Master. Before the Third Master, I and they are just the same. If the Third Master hands me the reins, I am only carrying out the Third Master's orders!"

Tu Weiyue said it slowly, with extraordinary composure, though within, a knot was tying itself. He bowed easily from the waist and walked out.

Then Wu Sunfu summoned Mo Gancheng. When the old man came in his legs were trembling a little, and the sight of it displeased Wu Sunfu at once. He deliberately would not look at the pitiful old fellow and said nothing, only rolled his eyes to watch the flickering grey-white play of light on the glass of the window over there. He was turning it over in his mind: could that young fellow Tu Weiyue really not know that quite a few on the management side were dissatisfied with the way he had handled things today? No! He must know. Then why would he not say so? For fear of losing face? Pride! The young man was proud. Well, let us see how he manages it today! — Wu Sunfu grew suddenly fretful, gave his head a hard shake, and, turning his eyes on Mo Gancheng, said sternly:

"Gancheng! You're a man well on in years. When the young fellows fall out, you ought to step in and smooth it over!"

"Third Master —"

"Ah! Not so fast. You know well enough that I don't like people buzzing this and that in my ear. I have my own mind; I've no need of anyone's gossip! Whoever has ability, I see it with my own eyes; coming and boasting to my face is wasted breath! Understand? Go and tell them so!"

"Yes, yes!"

"And I hear that Zeng the Second and Tu Weiyue were jealous over some woman worker and made a bit of a scandal at the mill last night — is there such a thing?"

"That — that! — I'm not too clear about it either."

Mo Gancheng answered in a flurry, the look on his face exceedingly comical. In truth he understood the matter very well, but having just had Wu Sunfu's grand, high-sounding speech clapped down over his head, he dared not say much. And this did not escape Wu Sunfu's eye. He could not help a short laugh, and said:

"What! You're not too clear either! When I ask you a plain question, you won't say. I know that crowd of yours in the counting-house — 'no eye for good deeds, all heart for bad'! Let there be a bit of jealousy and rivalry in the mill, and your ears turn clairvoyant! I hear that in this business Tu Weiyue was in the wrong — that he began by not playing straight himself. Is that so, or not?"

Mo Gancheng's eyes widened in a blank stare. For a moment he could not decide whether it were better to fall in with Wu Sunfu's tone or to tell the truth. In the end he resolved to tell the truth, for he knew that Tu Weiyue still stood high in Wu Sunfu's trust.

"Third Master! The plain fact is that the young second master of the Zeng family carried it rather too far. —"

Wu Sunfu nodded and smiled. Mo Gancheng, growing a little bolder, went on:

"Overseer number two, Wang Jinzhen, saw the whole thing with her own eyes. Mr. Tu never let slip a single word of it; it was all Wang Jinzhen who told me. Last night Mr. Tu sent Wang Jinzhen to fetch a woman worker named Zhu, to ask her which of the women had dealings with the Communists — it was in this very room he questioned her, and Wang Jinzhen was there too. Afterwards, when that Zhu woman went out, by the cocoon-room the young second master of the Zeng family stopped her and forced his attentions on her. There was thunder and rain just then, and none of us heard. But Mr. Tu and Wang Jinzhen came upon them. That is all it was." Wu Sunfu, frowning, said nothing, but in his heart he saw it crystal-clear. He knew Wu Weicheng's report had been wholly one-sided. It struck him all at once that to have planted the two kinsmen Zeng Jiaju and Ma Jingshan, and Wu Weicheng of his own clan, in the mill was not very wise; there would be no end of vexation from it in the days to come!

"Ah! Gancheng, go and warn them. This affair is not to be mentioned again!"

So saying, Wu Sunfu waved his hand and dismissed Mo Gancheng. He tilted his head, thought a moment, then took up his pen, meaning to write out an order transferring the three of them, Wu Weicheng and the other two, out of the mill, parcelling them among those eight mills of the Yizhong company. "Cram a mill full of kinsmen and old cronies, and the mill can never be run properly!" — so Wu Sunfu thought as he set pen to paper. But at that very moment a man came in unbidden — and it was precisely Wu Weicheng.

"Who told you to come in? Was it Mo Gancheng?"

Wu Sunfu flung his pen down on the desk and rebuked him harshly, his gaze fixed straight on Wu Weicheng's face, which wore a certain shrewd, capable look. Wu Weicheng halted a good way off from the writing-desk, reached back to shut the door, and, still composed enough, said straight out:

"I have a few words to say to you, Third Uncle."

Wu Sunfu knitted his brows at once, but still held his patience.

"Just now Qian Baosheng from the union told me the workers held a meeting last night, in the home of one of the women. The woman is called Yao Jinfeng. When the workers rioted today and were about to smash up the counting-house, this Yao Jinfeng was among them. And it was this same Yao Jinfeng who told the workers that if we did not release those six people they would fight to the death! A month ago, when the disturbance broke out at the mill, the one secretly at the head of it was, again, this Yao Jinfeng. They say Tu Weiyue afterwards bought her over — but last night the workers' meeting was held in her very house! She is a fierce one, and she is still leading them from the shadows!"

Wu Sunfu looked sharply at Wu Weicheng's face, gave only a faint smile, and said nothing. That the workers had met last night, and that Yao Jinfeng was there — this trifle Tu Weiyue had already reported; Wu Sunfu could get nothing new out of Wu Weicheng's words. But the name Yao Jinfeng lingered a moment in his thoughts. He remembered her: a lean, lanky figure, a small round face, a few fine pale pox-marks, past thirty; after Tu Weiyue had bought her over there had been a small hitch — an overseer named Xue, overseer number nine, had leaked the secret, but it had been patched up again afterwards.

"Third Uncle, as I see it, this whole disturbance was let loose by Tu Weiyue's conniving; yesterday he had ample time to head it off, and he did nothing! And today he plays the good man to the hilt! He is in league with a fellow in the union named Gui Changlin, meaning to buy the people's hearts!"

Wu Sunfu's face suddenly changed. He had heard something "new" at last! But on second thought he abruptly darkened his face, deliberately slapped the table, and barked:

"A Cheng, what talk is this of yours! I have now given Tu Weiyue full authority to handle matters; while you are in the mill, hold your tongue! — What you have just said may be said before me and nowhere else; not half a word of it outside! Understand? Off with you!"

Having waved Wu Weicheng away, Wu Sunfu picked up the note he had just written, glanced at it, and slowly crumpled it, his whole face full of irresolution. Presently he sprang up, spread the crumpled note open again for a look, shook his head, and with a rip tore it to shreds and dropped them in the spittoon. In the end he had cancelled, of his own accord, his decision that "no kinsmen and old cronies were to be kept in the mill." He snatched up the pen and wrote another note:

This mill's present reduction of wages is a thing that must be done; the moment silk prices rally in the least, wages shall of course again be paid at the originally fixed rates. It is hoped that all workers will this very day set their minds at ease and return to work, and by no means give ear to treacherous talk and so bring trouble upon themselves. Be it known that the manager of this mill has long borne with the factional wrangling within the union; should the mutual intriguing persist and the labour agitation be further fomented, the manager will have no recourse but to take resolute measures!

So proclaimed.

Handing the note to Mo Gancheng to post up, Wu Sunfu made ready to leave. As he was getting into the car at the last, he charged Tu Weiyue sternly once more:

"No matter how you do it, tomorrow I want the mill working! Tomorrow!"

It was one o'clock in the afternoon. Tu Weiyue paced to and fro in his room, now sneering, now knitting his brows. His restless disquiet was precisely because he stood at the crossing-point where he might win or lose. When the agitation broke out that morning, though he had heard many a cry of "Down with Tu the Chamber-pot," he had judged it rightly and been sure of victory. Since Wu Sunfu had come in person, that certainty had turned to doubt. For all that Wu Sunfu had said, over and over, "full authority is given to Mr. Tu," Tu Weiyue's wary eye could read the true meaning of the words: "full authority is given to you — until tomorrow!"

If the strike were not settled by tomorrow, only one road lay open to Tu Weiyue! Out!

And that Wu Sunfu had, this time, been of two minds from the very start — that too Tu Weiyue had long since marked. A man as headstrong and ruthless as Wu Sunfu, once he could not make up his mind, was very hard to serve; and this, too, Tu Weiyue saw with perfect clearness!

Suddenly a figure flitted past the window. Tu Weiyue stopped short, put his head out to look, and hurried from the room. The man outside was Gui Changlin; the two of them exchanged a glance and, without a word, walked together to Mo Gancheng's room, where three or four people were already sitting in a neat row, Mo Gancheng among them.

Tu Weiyue, smiling his cold smile, glanced round at them all and was the first to speak:

"The Third Master's orders are that tomorrow, without fail, they go back to work; and now only half a day and a night are left — it's terribly cramped! Earlier in the day we tried to find the workers' delegates for a talk, and could not. They refuse to recognize the old union; they have now got up a strike committee. Just now I sent Changlin to treat with their strike committee, and they turned round and said they must take their orders from the General Federated Strike Committee of the silk filatures. This is too obstructive! We care nothing for their 'General' this and 'General' that — our own mill we settle by ourselves! The first thing now is that the mill absolutely must open tomorrow! Even if we run at half strength, we shall have something to show the Third Master! Changlin, do you think the mill can open tomorrow? What, in the end, are their demands?"

Gui Changlin did not answer at once. He looked at Tu Weiyue, then at Mo Gancheng, and, shaking his head, sighed:

"I've lost heart! From last night till this morning these two cheap legs of mine have never once stopped, all for a bit of peace and quiet, so we'd all keep our faces bright — and who'd have thought there'd be someone tearing down my wall behind my back at the boss's ear! Now Mr. Tu calls me in to talk it over: if I offer no plan, folk will curse me for taking pay and shirking; if I do offer a plan, folk will say I'm nursing some private grudge, out to get somebody. Mr. Mo, don't you see what a hole I'm in?"

The room fell silent. Tu Weiyue frowned and bit his lip. Mo Gancheng's whole face was flustered. But A Zhen, seated in the corner, hid her mouth and laughed to herself. She gave Wang Jinzhen beside her a nudge, and slid a sidelong glance at Tu Weiyue. They all knew why Gui Changlin was grumbling. Pockmarked Li, though, could contain himself no longer:

"Mr. Tu, just give the order and we'll carry it out — and there's an end of it, isn't there?"

"Quite right! Give the order, Mr. Tu! Still, Changlin, no harm in speaking your mind if you have one — let's talk it over together."

Wang Jinzhen put in her word too, her eyes on Mo Gancheng. The old man was beginning to catch something of it as well. Tu Weiyue nodded slowly, glanced at pockmarked Li, and turned his face back to Gui Changlin.

"Well then, I'll speak a few words from the heart. The boss is losing money, and the workers know it. The notice the boss put up says it plain enough: wages cut to eight-tenths, because silk prices are too low, and the rest to be talked over by and by. The workers strike half for the money and half over a few persons; Xue Baozhu is a harsh, overbearing tyrant, and the workers hate her to death, and there's Qian Qiaolin and Second Sister Zhou too, thorns in everybody's eye! It won't be hard to open tomorrow — but these three had best keep out of sight a few days!"

Gui Changlin spoke slowly, his eyes never leaving Mo Gancheng's astonished face; and Tu Weiyue too let his gaze slide again and again over Mo Gancheng's face. Every eye was fixed on Mo Gancheng. Mo Gancheng was flustered, but he understood too; he was the man in the middle, with no call to pay the neighbour's bill, and he hastened to chime in:

"Good, good! So long as the mill can open tomorrow — can open tomorrow!"

Tu Weiyue smiled his cold smile, knowing this bit of "overture" had been quite enough, and that to draw it out longer would be so much waste; he was ready now to give his orders according to the plan already laid. Abruptly he darkened his face, raised his left hand and pressed it lightly on the air to call for attention, and said sternly:

"We can't trouble ourselves over all the gossip! We have a way to get the workers back tomorrow, and we'll do it strictly by the book! A Zhen, have you met with Yao Jinfeng? On that strike committee of theirs, besides Yao Jinfeng, who else is there? Which of them are close to Yao Jinfeng?"

"Who cares how many more of them there are! It's just He Xiumei, Zhang Axin and that lot! Close to Jinfeng there are two: Auntie Xu and Lu Xiaobao."

A Zhen said it with a pout of her lips and a sidelong look, never forgetting to show off her coquetry. Tu Weiyue suddenly grew angry.

"You do your work far too carelessly, A Zhen! Just who is on the strike committee — that you must find out for certain! I'll send Wang Jinzhen to help you. First have Yao Jinfeng hold on to the Xu woman and the Lu woman. Tell them to be careful! He Xiumei and that pack of bad eggs are Communists, and the Public Security Bureau means to arrest them! If they don't go back tomorrow, the boss Wu will not be polite about it; whatever's to be said can be said once they're back at work. Get all the overseers together, and let everyone split up and go through the shanties door by door telling the women not to be taken in!"

"That won't do at all! To go into the shanties now hauling people out is honestly to go asking for a beating!"

Wang Jinzhen and A Zhen cried out with one voice.

"What's there to fear! If it comes to blows, then blows it is! Do you two want bodyguards as well? Very well — Old Li, see that your men mind their guarding!"

Tu Weiyue said it very impatiently, harsh now in voice and look; A Zhen flushed red and would have argued on, but Wang Jinzhen, beside her, tugged at the hem of her gown and bade her hold her peace. Tu Weiyue paid the two of them no more heed, but turned to ask Gui Changlin:

"Who, in the end, are the people stirring behind this General Federated Strike of theirs?"

"Who else but the Communists making trouble as the chance offers! In Hongkou and Zhabei together, more than a hundred mills, great and small, are now all shut down. They have a central headquarters — set up in some hotel, they say — and by tonight we can find out where."

"Tonight is too late! We must have it clear this very afternoon! But, Changlin, there's another urgent errand for you first. The workers take courage from their numbers; if the mills near us don't open, our workers here won't be willing to listen meekly to our fair words. Changlin, you're to go at once and come to terms with those few mills, so that tomorrow everyone opens without fail. Force them back to work! Ask the Public Security Bureau to send more police; and if anyone dares to 'block' the mill gate, seize them!"

"Right, right! Let's do the same here! Mr. Tu, I've long wanted to deal with them good and hard!"

Hearing that force was to be used, pockmarked Li broke in quickly, slapping his two big palms down on his thighs. Pockmarked Li was a rough man; ever since that morning he had been unable to fathom why Tu Weiyue would not use force, and but for the "loyalty" he still bore Tu Weiyue he would have been running him down behind his back. Now he could hold it in no longer, and spoke his mind — yet still watched Tu Weiyue's face submissively for its temper.

Tu Weiyue looked at pockmarked Li's face and gave a faint smile, half soothing, half approving. And at the same time, half in explanation and half in command, he said:

"Don't be in such a hurry, Old Li. Your fists shall have their opening stroke of luck, never fear! The man who really knows how to fight won't strike the first blow — isn't that so? — And besides, our mill is not like the others; there are too many tangles here, and to lay on before we see clearly might only stiffen the whole business into a knot! Boss Wu has always been a lenient man, and we must go along with his wishes. Changlin, you understand? Let the other households kill the chicken, and it'll scare the monkey here at home!"

"Leave it all to me — I'll manage it smooth and steady on every side!"

"Good, then! — Mr. Mo, please put out the board at once: dismiss Qian Qiaolin, Second Sister Zhou, and Xue Baozhu!"

Tu Weiyue turned suddenly to Mo Gancheng, his manner exceedingly stern.

Pockmarked Li and Wang Jinzhen and the rest gave a slight start too. Who would have thought that what had been "keep out of sight a few days" had turned, just now, into a plain, outright "dismissal"? But seeing the resolve in Tu Weiyue's eyes, they understood the thing was past recall; Qian Baosheng's faction was in for a bad time of it this round!

Mo Gancheng too was taken aback; looking at Tu Weiyue's face with its chill striking through him, he could not find his voice. After a while, fingering his cheekbone hesitantly, he said:

"Give Xue Baozhu a little face — might we ask the Third Master to transfer her to the 'new' mill?"

"That is the Third Master's grace to bestow, and no concern of ours! Here we must still post the board and dismiss her!"

Tu Weiyue answered coldly, turned his face to glance at the four of them — Gui Changlin and the rest — and went on sharply:

"You all know that yesterday afternoon it was Xue Baozhu and those three of hers who first stirred up the workers in the reeling-room against the wage-cut to eight-tenths! They can get nowhere in Boss Wu's mill, so they set out to use the workers to pay off private scores and to strike at Gui Changlin! But they have behaved too badly all along, and for all their currying of favour the workers still hate the three of them to death! Now, in dismissing them, we've not a grain of private motive: first, because the three of them are troublemakers; and second, because we must punch open a few vents to let off steam, so that the workers will consent to come back tomorrow! The Third Master won't allow me to resign, but insists I carry on, so I've no choice but to play the villain! If, with everyone's help, we can get it settled tonight and open tomorrow in peace, I shall still beg the Third Master to grant my resignation!"

Mo Gancheng and the others looked at one another blankly and said nothing.

"The hour's getting late. Everyone set to it for all you're worth, and bring me word again by five o'clock! — Old Li, there's another matter I've an errand for you on!"

With an imposing, commanding air Tu Weiyue gave his last order, made a gesture to pockmarked Li, and went out first. Pockmarked Li pulled a face at A Zhen and the others, gave a laugh, and hurried out after him too.

At the end of the veranda of that range of management-office rooms Tu Weiyue halted. Pockmarked Li quickly stepped ahead and stood facing him, mouth split in a grin that bared a row of big teeth. One half of Tu Weiyue's face was in the sun, glistening and oily-bright; the other half showed faintly pale. He tilted his head, thought a moment, then fixed his sharp gaze on pockmarked Li's face and asked in a low voice:

"You've been tailing them half the day — still no thread to follow?"

"None. Everyone who comes and goes with those two is from the mill; we tail them too, but they only move about that stretch of shanties!"

"Can they have learned that they're being tailed?"

"That can't be! Those men of mine are all old hands; they'd never give it away!"

"Seen any strange faces?"

"None. Everyone who has dealings with He Xiumei and Zhang Axin is a mill hand!"

Tu Weiyue gave pockmarked Li another sharp look, then tilted his head and closed one eye. He reckoned to himself that pockmarked Li's men must be too stupid and had shown their hand. He himself had long ago made sure that the two, He Xiumei and Zhang Axin, had "something up their sleeve." His eyes rolled, and he asked again:

"Last night, when those two came out of Yao Jinfeng's house, who did they go along the road with?"

"Oh! Last night? He Xiumei went back together with Lu Xiaobao, the two of them quarrelling the whole way. Zhang Axin went off with two others; a few steps on, they parted, going three separate ways."

"Those two — are they mill hands? What are they called?"

"Mill hands. They came out of Yao Jinfeng's house together too. I didn't see them myself. My mate tells me one has a round face, neither tall nor short, a pair of moist, liquid eyes, the skin a shade dark. What the other looked like he couldn't rightly recall; a bit taller, that one."

Tu Weiyue suddenly smiled his cold smile. A small round face, a pair of moist, liquid eyes, dark skin, middling height: he knew who this was.

"Didn't they talk on the road?"

"I told you, they'd only gone a few steps before they parted. When they came out, the three of them were arm in arm, for all the world as if they were the best of friends."

Pockmarked Li too seemed a little impatient now; he wiped the back of his hand across his lips and stared wide-eyed at Tu Weiyue.

A figure flickered by the corner of the wall over there. Tu Weiyue, quick-eyed, ran forward a few steps to look — it was A Xiang. This was a man newly taken into service, to whom Tu Weiyue had not yet given any weighty task. Seeing Tu Weiyue, he stopped. Tu Weiyue knitted his brows and ordered:

"A Xiang! The whole gang of overseers is to go over to the shanties and tell the workers to come back tomorrow; the boss has put out a notice, and whatever's to be said can be said once they're at work. Go and see whether the whole gang has really gone; if any shirk, come back and report to me!"

"And if there's any trouble, don't stand on ceremony; just give a shout, and that's enough! Round the shanties we have our men!"

Pockmarked Li called out from the side too, laughing with his mouth wide open. Tu Weiyue smiled as well, then, his whole face turning grave, said to pockmarked Li:

"We too are going into the shanties to find someone. Have five or six men come along with us!"

Tu Weiyue was now sure that the dark, comely Zhu Guiying had "something up her sleeve" as well, and he resolved to go and reconnoitre in person.

All along the way they saw the police in double pickets, the defence corps on its rounds, and silk-filature women in twos and threes squabbling by the roadside. The sunlight seemed to have baked all the oil of their bodies up into their faces, yet they were not afraid; in high excitement they ran everywhere and clamoured everywhere. Nearer the shanties the air grew more strained still. The women were like mosquitoes at dusk, swarming and stirring in clusters. They were all talking of the three the mill had dismissed. "So the wages cut to eight-tenths — that's not to be talked of any more? A swindle!" — cries like this leapt out of the hubbub.

Tu Weiyue, still smiling his cold smile, walked with pockmarked Li and the others into the shanty quarter. But his face had gone paler. He felt hundreds upon hundreds of venomous eyes stabbing at him from every side. "Chamber-pot!" "Down with the chamber-pot!" — at first not very loud, and not many; but little by little they grew more, and grew louder. Tu Weiyue stole a glance at pockmarked Li, whose face was iron-grey, his teeth clenched.

The "loafers," in long black gowns or in short jackets and trousers of black glossed silk, moved in twos and threes through the knots of women in the shanty quarter too, like so many black-shelled beetles. They were all pockmarked Li's men; on purpose they barged into the clamouring clusters of women, on purpose they grabbed a feel at the women's sweat-soaked, tight-strained breasts. Here and there they came to blows with the women. A shout to fight went up! — but in a moment it subsided again. The women bore it with all their patience, keeping clear of a brawl with these men; and the men, for their part, had had no orders to strike in earnest.

Head down, Tu Weiyue walked fast, and had pockmarked Li lead him to the front of the shanty where Zhu Guiying lived.

"Tu the Chamber-pot's come to seize people!"

Suddenly this cry rang out beside a bamboo door of the shanty. Then a small body leapt aside — none other than Jin Xiaomei, the basin-girl who lived next door to Zhu Guiying. Pockmarked Li grunted, thrust out a coarse dark hand, stepped forward and would have caught the girl. But Jin Xiaomei nimbly ducked her body and dodged him, then ran off as if she flew. Tu Weiyue glanced at pockmarked Li and would not let him give chase; the two of them pushed straight into Zhu Guiying's home. The five or six men they had brought kept watch about the ground outside the bamboo door.

When Tu Weiyue's eyes had grown used to the dim light in the shanty, he saw Zhu Guiying standing before him, two flashing eyes riveted straight on him. Her comely dark round face showed the red flush of anger, but her small lips had turned white. There was no one else in the shanty, only the three of them: Zhu Guiying, pockmarked Li, Tu Weiyue. It was a taut silence. Outside the shanty, though, a roar of voices rolled up like a tide, coming nearer and louder.

Tu Weiyue forced a smile and said:

"Guiying! Someone has reported that you're a Communist! Now two roads lie before you, and you may choose for yourself: one is to tell me who else is in your party, and then we'll raise you to overseer; the other is that you refuse to speak, and off you go to prison!"

"I am not! And I know nothing!"

"But I do know! The other two are He Xiumei and Zhang Axin —"

Zhu Guiying could not keep her heart from a lurch, and her colour changed a little. Tu Weiyue saw it plainly, and went on with a smile:

"Who else there is besides — that you'll have to tell me!"

"I truly do not know. And at the police station it'll be these same words from me!"

Zhu Guiying's face grew a shade calmer, her lips whiter still, her moist liquid eyes full of a red glow. Tu Weiyue gave a low, cold laugh, then suddenly changed countenance, looked at pockmarked Li, and barked sharply:

"Old Li — search the place!"

By this time the tumult outside the shanty had swelled too. A din of cursing rose all at once, and all at once was gone, and all at once turned into the shock of flesh against bamboo and wood — thwack! thwack! — the hiss of clenched-teeth shrieks, cries to split the heart and liver, footfalls roaring up like fire. Then, like a thunderclap out of a clear sky, came a victorious roar, and a troop of people crowded into the shanty and flung themselves straight at Tu Weiyue and pockmarked Li. A wordless scuffle in the dark! The plank table and the broken bamboo couch were overturned!

Tu Weiyue, with a bench to open his way, sprang out through the gaps in the press of bodies. But a second troop charged in from outside the shanty and swept him back into the thick of the ring. Outside, the shouting shook heaven and earth. Tu Weiyue grappled with two of them in a tangle. In his panic he made out one of them clearly — it was Zhang Axin. Suddenly pockmarked Li came dragging a woman, and using her as a weapon opened a path and struggled to Tu Weiyue's side. At that the women hemming Tu Weiyue in all turned at once to fight for their captured comrade. Seizing the moment, Tu Weiyue fled out through the shanty's bamboo door, only to run smack into a band of a dozen or so. But this band was not the raging women — it was pockmarked Li's men. The tide of women rolled up hard behind this band. The great melee began again in the narrow lane before the shanties! But now a police whistle too shrilled sharp above the human clamour. Amid the women's dishevelled hair flashed the white bands on the police caps.

Bang! Bang! Warning shots to overawe them!

Pockmarked Li too broke out of the ring, one hand still gripping that woman worker. He gave Tu Weiyue a grim smile.

More than ten minutes later the ground about Zhu Guiying's shanty was quiet. On the mud lay many snapped-off bamboo laths, and among them a night-stool brush. The bamboo door too was broken, hanging there askew like a wounded wing. But on a rubbish-heaped open lot at the east end of the shanty quarter there was again a clamouring, bawling crowd. The women were holding a mass meeting. The police, being few, stood watching from a distance. Pockmarked Li's men, some eight or nine of them, stood scattered near the police squad.

This was a meeting that came on as sudden as a storm! This was a meeting that ended as fast as a flash of lightning! This was a meeting that caught the workers' fighting spirit at its very peak! The affair just now of "Tu Weiyue seizing people" had swiftly told upon the struggle within the ranks of the women.

"Tu the Chamber-pot is the worst of all! He dismissed Xue Baozhu and her lot to trick us into going back to work! Xue Baozhu and her lot are Tu the Chamber-pot's enemies! He kills with a borrowed knife! He brought pockmarked Li to seize us! Down with Tu the Chamber-pot! No work tomorrow! Whoever goes back is a running dog!"

Zhang Axin, standing on a rubbish-heap, flung her arms about and shouted wildly. Out of the packed ranks burst an answering roar like thunder:

"Whoever goes back is a running dog!"

"Whoever coaxes us back is a running dog!"

"Beat the running dog Yao Jinfeng!"

"If wages aren't as before, we'll die before we go back to work! We want Tu the Chamber-pot gone! We want Gui Changlin gone! We want Wang Jinzhen, pockmarked Li, A Zhen and Yao Jinfeng dismissed, and we want He Xiumei given back to us! We want —"

Zhang Axin's voice cracked, the shout dying in her throat; suddenly her body buckled, and clutching her belly she sank into a crouch. At once another sprang out beside her — it was Chen Yue'e; on her face were two streaks of blood, scratched there in the grappling with Tu Weiyue, and in a louder voice still she took up the cry:

"We must reorganize the strike committee! Drive out Yao Jinfeng, Auntie Xu, Lu Xiaobao! Anyone who means to go back tomorrow, drive them all out!"

"Drive them all out!"

The crowd answered with a roar that shook heaven and earth. Zhang Axin sprang up again, her face like pig's liver, and shouted once more as if her very lungs would burst:

"Without an order from the General Federated Strike Committee of the silk filatures, we do not go back! Sisters! A delegate of the General Strike Committee has a word to say to you!"

All at once the black-packed ranks fell dumb. A delegate of the "General Strike Committee"? Who? Who? The women's sweating, flushed, excited faces turned this way and that in confusion, questioning one another with their eyes, and a buzz of talk arose. But just then a young woman dressed as a worker, a woman whose pair of eyes seemed to speak, leapt up onto the rubbish-heap and stood between Zhang Axin and Chen Yue'e — and this woman was Ma Jin.

"Sisters! The 102 silk filatures of Shanghai have all struck together! You are the bravest vanguard of all! The scabs and running dogs in your own mill may be fighting among themselves, but in oppressing you they are of one mind! In cheating you they are of one mind! Only by your own strength can you win the victory! Down with the scabs! Down with the running dogs! Organize a union of your own! Without an order from the General Strike Committee, no going back to work!"

"Without an order, no going back to work!"

"— No going back to work!"

The dense black ranks sent back an echo. Almost a literal "echo." For all that Ma Jin strove to "purge out" the "formulas" and the "jargon," her words were still those of an "intellectual," and could not bore straight into the women's hearts.

"Sisters! Let us all be of one heart! No going back! No going back! — The meeting is closed!"

Chen Yue'e shouted it out again, and with Zhang Axin, Ma Jin and the rest ran down off the rubbish-heap. Clamouring as they went, the women scattered in all directions. Just at that moment the Public Security Bureau's armed bicycle squad arrived too, and a large body of police besides. But the women had already dispersed, leaving only the bare lot. The police then held the lot, to keep them from gathering to meet again. For a month past martial law had been proclaimed in the Chinese-administered districts, and assemblies were absolutely forbidden.

Yao Jinfeng and A Zhen had long since fled back into the mill and reported it all to Tu Weiyue, down to the last detail.

The two of them pressed close about Tu Weiyue, before and behind, begging him to "take their part."

Tu Weiyue frowned coldly and said nothing. The "roots" he had been at such pains to plant among the workers had now wholly lost their use — a thing he had not foreseen. He had supposed that only three parts of his strength would be needed to deal with the workers; only now did he see that it took the full ten!

"A pack of worthless sluts who don't know when to quit — it seems there's nothing for it but fists! I'll make you know Tu the Chamber-pot for what he is!"

Tu Weiyue muttered it to himself coldly through clenched teeth, then left the two of them, A Zhen and her companion, behind and went off to the management office in front. Coming the other way, in a great fluster, was Mo Gancheng, who caught hold of Tu Weiyue and said, stammering:

"My dear sir, my dear sir — I've been looking, looking for you! The Third Master's in a fury on the telephone, a fury! In the end — tomorrow, opening tomorrow — is it sure or not?"

"It's sure!"

Tu Weiyue was resolute still, full of confidence, the cold smile creeping back over his lips. Mo Gancheng gave him an odd look out of half an eye.

"The Third Master is coming directly."

"What's he coming for! —"

Tu Weiyue shrugged and said it low; but at once he darkened again and cried out spitefully:

"That pack of dog-heads, Wang Jinzhen and the rest, are truly hateful! Gone to earth till there's not a shadow of them to be seen! Mr. Mo, please send people to fetch them, and let them wait for me in the counting-house! Mr. Mo — the sooner the better!"

So saying, Tu Weiyue would let Mo Gancheng ramble on no further, and strode off. First he went to inspect the neighbourhood of the main gate. The iron gate was fast shut, with two pairs of police standing guard on it. Pockmarked Li was patrolling the area with his men. Among them a few sat like beaten cocks on the stone steps of the cocoon-room. Pockmarked Li ran up to Tu Weiyue and said in a low voice:

"In that wild scuffle just now there were some of Qian Baosheng's lot mixed in too — did you know?"

"How do you know?"

"A Xiang told me."

Tu Weiyue gave a cold laugh, glared up at the sky, and said to pockmarked Li:

"Now we can use fifty men! Old Li, go quickly and muster fifty men, and bring them all into the mill to wait on my orders."

Tu Weiyue left the main gate, went to inspect the back gate and the side gate too, and, his mind made up, came at last back to the management office. Wu Weicheng, Ma Jingshan and Zeng Jiaju, the three of them, were in close talk head to head on the veranda before the office. Tu Weiyue glanced at them as if it were nothing to him, then suddenly changed direction, slipped round behind the office building, and, reaching an empty room where scrap was heaped beside the boiler-house, pushed open the door and went in.

He Xiumei, her two hands pinioned behind her, was crouching there; seeing that it was Tu Weiyue who had come in, she turned her face away at once and gave her body a spiteful twist.

Tu Weiyue, smiling his cold smile, looked He Xiumei carefully over and kept quite silent. Suddenly He Xiumei stole her face back round, as if to see whether Tu Weiyue were still there. Her eyes met precisely Tu Weiyue's cold gaze. Tu Weiyue could not help a loud laugh, and said:

"He Xiumei! Have a little more patience. After six o'clock, once your delegates and we have agreed on terms, we'll let you out!"

Staring wide-eyed in a daze, He Xiumei did not answer, but no longer turned her face away either.

"The delegates are Lu Xiaobao and Yao Jinfeng; and — your good friend: Zhang Axin!"

He Xiumei started all over, her whole colour changing, and gazed at Tu Weiyue as if waiting for him to say a little more.

"Zhang Axin is a sensible one. I had a heart-to-heart talk with her, and she came round. She's a straightforward sort! She told me everything. She's truly a good friend to you. She thumped her chest and stood surety for you, saying you're a good person, that you were only muddled for a moment and were taken in by the Communists! Isn't that so?"

Suddenly He Xiumei cried out, her face gone white as a dead woman's, and stared in terror at Tu Weiyue's face.

"There are a few more in your band, all good friends, all 'comrades,' aren't there? Zhang Axin told me the whole of it! Set your mind at rest — I won't go and seize them! I've always been on good terms with you sisters! Only — traffic with the Communists, and if the police get wind of it they'll haul you off and shoot you. He Xiumei, think it over: whoever in there is a sensible one and can be talked round, I'll lend her a hand!"

"Hnh! Axin! Axin!"

He Xiumei's body shuddered; she cried it out, and then let her head droop as if in bitter grief. Tu Weiyue smiled with his lip between his teeth; he stepped forward, stooped, and in a voice that sounded utterly sincere said:

"Don't lay the blame wrongly on Axin! Don't blame her! If you'd only turn it over in your own mind, you'd see it clear. Ever so many of the strikes and disturbances in Shanghai have been mixed up with the Communists, but in the end it's still you workers who get hauled off to prison. The Communists live in fine foreign houses, snug and at their ease. Every time you strike, they put in for their expenses, draw tens of thousands in silver, and spend it to their hearts' content. Take that girl student who lured you and Axin in — none of you so much as knows where she really lives, do you? She lives in a great foreign house! She changes into ragged clothes to come and hold meetings with you. For coming out to one meeting she can draw ten or twenty dollars in cab-fare. And you? You wear out your two legs for nothing! She lives in a great foreign house. The amah in her home is far better off than you! Once Axin ran into her. She gave Axin five dollars and told her to say nothing of it. Axin never told you, did she? She's a bit less than honest still. But her friendship with you counts for something after all. Just now she's thumped her chest to stand surety for you!"

He Xiumei hung her head and said nothing. Suddenly she began to cry. The look of her weeping was just like a child's. All at once she checked her sobs, lifted her tear-stained face and looked at Tu Weiyue, looked and looked, the corners of her mouth twitching without cease, as if two things were fighting in her heart with neither yet the winner. Tu Weiyue read exactly what that twitching at the mouth's corners meant, and at once, his whole face full of a seeming compassion, pressed a step nearer:

"Xiumei! Don't be afraid! We'll let you out right away. We've already dismissed Xue Baozhu, so there's an overseer's place short; presently I'll speak to the Third Master and raise you to overseer. To live in peace with one another — how good that would be!"

He Xiumei blushed, and suddenly let fall two more rows of tears, though without a sound of weeping.

"But, Xiumei, think again: who in that band of yours can be talked round — let's go and talk her over!"

He Xiumei's gaze all at once went fixed and dull. She lowered her head, her fingers mechanically rolling and twisting the hem of her gown. Presently she sighed and said in a low voice:

"You'd better go and ask Axin again. She knows more of it than I do."

There were no more words. He Xiumei kept her head down, her body trembling a little. Tu Weiyue saw too that the talk was finished; he shrugged, despising in his heart this useless Communist; he shot He Xiumei a proud glance, then turned and hurried out. His whole heart glad, he ran over to the management office, and seeing A Xiang standing idle before the veranda, gave his order:

"A Xiang! Go to the shanties and lure Zhang Axin here! If coaxing won't move her, use brute force! Quick there, and quick back!"

At that moment a motor-car drove into the mill, and Old Guan the bodyguard jumped down and opened the door. Wu Sunfu clambered out unsteadily, and looking at Tu Weiyue who came forward to meet him, asked:

"Isn't it going from bad to worse? How can the frames run tomorrow after this?"

"Third Master, there's an hour before daybreak that is very dark indeed — no stars, no moon."

Tu Weiyue bowed and answered with great composure and great confidence. Wu Sunfu forced a smile, paced a few steps on the cinder path where the car stood, then turned and said to Tu Weiyue, who followed at his back:

"You're sure of it? Good! Out with it, and let me hear."

The tone was too mild, and hearing it Tu Weiyue grew uneasy instead, fearing that Wu Sunfu had suddenly shifted his attitude again. He thought a moment, then set out the main points of what had happened in a few words; and as he spoke he watched Wu Sunfu's face closely. The westering sunlight fell on one half of Wu Sunfu's face, glistening and oily-bright, and against the other half, out of the sun — one bright, one dark — he seemed like two men. Tu Weiyue let out a breath and looked up at the sky. In the eastern corner of the heavens hung a few great banks of fiery sunset cloud.

"Then that one you seized — He — He Xiumei — you mean to release her, is that it?"

"I mean to wait till dark, then let her out. I've set a man to tail her, and so we can net the whole catch at one haul."

Tu Weiyue answered, a thread of a smile passing over his lips.

"Let it be done so for the present, and we'll see how things go. But — Weiyue, put out another notice, giving them till tomorrow to return to work! Whoever does not come back tomorrow, dismiss them one and all!"

Wu Sunfu suddenly flared up again, and without waiting for Tu Weiyue's reply climbed into the car. Old Guan the bodyguard settled himself beside the driver, and the car drove slowly out of the mill. The two leaves of the square iron-barred gate were swung wide together, pockmarked Li tending to it at the side and bawling at his men. But the car had only just reached the middle of the gateway when suddenly a shout went up outside, and women workers beyond counting crowded forward and blocked the way. At once, all along the four sides of the gate, a new tumult began. Police, and pockmarked Li and his men, all came running at a dash; but the women too swelled on the instant to twice, three times, four times, five times their number — choking off all traffic on the road before the gate, and hemming in Wu Sunfu, car and all, so that it could not stir an inch. Inside, Wu Sunfu's heart went pit-a-pat.

"Free He Xiumei, and we'll free you!"

The women, clamouring, broke through the cordon of police and pockmarked Li's men and pressed right up to the car. They had no weapons, yet the fury of their onset was far, far fiercer than that of men fully armed!

Old Guan sprang onto the running-board, murder in his face, and drew his pistol. The women did not fall back. At the same time bits of broken stone and clods of earth came flying out from the rear of the women's ranks. But their aim was wild. The women had weapons now too, yet plainly they had no mind as yet for a pitched battle. Wu Sunfu, sitting in the car, his face iron-grey, barked out again and again:

"Drive on! Full throttle and charge through!"

The driver, at a loss, first squeezed the horn. The sound of it seemed to have some effect. The women nearest the front of the car drew back a step without thinking. The car moved; but the women gave no more ground. With one great yell, again a squall-burst of broken stones and clods came flying out from behind them and fell on the car. Old Guan gave a frantic roar and raised his pistol, taking aim at the massed women. Suddenly a man burst out of the throng, quick as lightning, and knocked Old Guan's forearm upward. Bang! — and the shot went off into the sky.

The man was Tu Weiyue. He left Old Guan and, wheeling round at once, cried loudly to the driver:

"You fool! Won't you back up? Back the car up!"

The car backed in through the gate. This time there was no squeezing of the horn first. Inside, Wu Sunfu leaned back against the cushion, baring his teeth in a grim smile. The driver quickly turned the car about, ran it across the cinder path of the mill, and went out by the back gate. By now a part of the women had charged in through the front gate, but the greater part had been held outside the iron gate. Within and without the gate all was a whirlwind of confusion. But they had lost their object now. The main body outside the gate was first driven off by the police, and the twenty or thirty inside were driven from the mill by force by pockmarked Li and his men.

The sky darkened by degrees, and a wind rose again. Within the mill and without it was quiet once more, but the air was strained still, and people's hearts were strained too. More guards were posted before the gate. The counting-house within was packed with people; Wang Jinzhen and A Zhen and the whole gang of overseers talked in a hubbub of the affair just past. The fifty-odd men pockmarked Li had summoned were drawn up in ranks along the veranda. The day was gone, only a night left, and everyone felt there was no certainty of opening tomorrow. But Tu Weiyue's forever-confident bearing and his firm, cold voice swept those waverings away at once. He said to the whole gang of overseers:

"No shirking! Tonight you're on the half-night shift! Go into the shanties and haul the people out! Tell them: whoever doesn't come back tomorrow is dismissed; and if no one comes back, Boss Wu will close the mill! Come making trouble at the gate again, and you'll all be hauled off to prison! Come back nicely to work tomorrow, and whatever's to be said can still be talked over! Off with you! No shirking! I'll send someone to check!"

In the overseers' gang no one dared open his mouth; they only stole glances at one another and put out their tongues.

Tu Weiyue then called pockmarked Li over and charged him:

"Old Li, are your men all mustered? They'll have a hard night of it! But only the one night! Have them go three and two to a squad, split up, and patrol before and behind the shanties. Charge them thus: wherever they see two or three women gathered in a knot, barge in and get fresh with them! When there's call to use the fist, use it, and don't stand on ceremony! If the women hold a meeting in a house, then break in — see one, seize one! Any women running to and fro must be tailed! — Have you got it all? Here's two hundred dollars; take it and share it out by heads!"

Tu Weiyue tossed a roll of banknotes down before pockmarked Li, then turned his face and cried sharply:

"Where's A Xiang? You've brought Zhang Axin, I take it?"

A Xiang pushed up from the back of the overseers' gang, his look utterly crestfallen. Tu Weiyue's face darkened at once.

"I looked high and low and couldn't find her. No knowing where she's hidden herself. The filthy slut! I'll go and look again later."

A Xiang said it with a flushed face, stealing a look at pockmarked Li as if begging him to put in a good word. Tu Weiyue gave a grunt, took no notice of A Xiang, and turning back said to them all:

"Have you all understood? One of the bad ones has slipped away already! — And you, A Xiang! You do your work far too carelessly, and let an important one get free! No need for you to go looking again! Wait a bit — there's another matter I've an errand for you on!"

So saying, Tu Weiyue stood up and waved his hand. The overseers and pockmarked Li all went out, leaving only A Xiang, who waited uneasily for further orders.

By now all was dusk beyond the window. Crows cawed on the roof of the workshop opposite. Tu Weiyue looked at A Xiang a while, as if to make sure whether the man could bear a heavy charge. In the end he decided, and fixing his sharp gaze on A Xiang's face he said:

"We're going to free He Xiumei — and you're to tail her! This time you must be especially careful!"

So everything was assigned, and Tu Weiyue telephoned in person to the nearest police station, asking them to send an extra detail of police to protect the mill.

Toward nine o'clock in the evening, some near kin and close friends gathered by chance at the Wu mansion, to condole with Wu Sunfu on the fright he had suffered at the mill. The lights were lit all through the house and all through the garden, and electric fans whirred everywhere. Here it was still a world of "light and gladness."

The Wu young mistress and her sister, and Mrs. Du and hers, had set up the card-table in the big dining-room. In the great drawing-room Wu Sunfu was entertaining his guests (among them Staff Officer Lei, just back in Shanghai), talking of the labour agitation of these two months in Shanghai. It was idle chat, with a few forced smiles. Wu Sunfu felt his one heart tugged by five or six threads, all of them pulling outward; and for all the strength he spent hauling them in, that heart of his still swayed and would not settle, so that his face went now iron-grey, now red, now white.

Suddenly they all fell silent together, and in the drawing-room there was only the monotonous whir of the electric fan, singing on like a lullaby. The click of the tiles came from the big dining-room, mingled with Ah Xuan's laughter. Then two men came out, arguing over something as they walked — the Du uncle and nephew, Xueshi and Xintuo.

"You say those words of mine are empty fancies that won't stand the test of experiment? And yours? When did you ever run a mill? All you can do is lie in bed and think!"

Du Xueshi spoke with heat, his cat-face turned to a rabbit's. Though he was in fact three or four years younger than his nephew, and no "Doctor of All Trades" back from France either, before his nephew he was forever putting on the airs of the elder uncle; he loved to lecture people. Du Xintuo, with the same air as ever of one whom nothing troubles and nothing pleases, leaned his back elegantly against the door-frame between the big dining-room and the drawing-room, and answered with a smile:

"There again your knowledge falls short. It was not something I thought up lying in bed. It was in England, or perhaps America — I don't quite recall, but at all events in one of those two countries — where someone tried it and it succeeded. An elementary book of economics tells of it too, saying that the joint-stock shoe factory prospered greatly and never had a strike. — Isn't that a thing that has been put to the test?"

"Then my own proposal too is being tried at this very moment, and with great results. Just look at Italy!"

Du Xueshi retorted on the instant, with a very self-satisfied laugh.

"But in China it won't work. Go and ask the men who run mills, and you'll see."

"Then does the method you speak of work in China? You go and ask the men who run mills too! Sunfu runs mills!"

Du Xueshi's face lengthened again; but through his anger there was still a touch of triumph. He had found a qualified judge. So without waiting for Xintuo to speak, and without asking whether Xintuo would even accept the judge, he stepped forward and called out loudly:

"Brother Sun! Could you get all the women workers in your mill to buy in as shareholders and, like yourself, become owners of a share in the Yuhua — could such a thing be done?"

The question was too abrupt; Wu Sunfu, half sunk in thought, turned his face and knitted his brows. Li Yuting, seated opposite Sunfu, looked in astonishment too at the grave-faced Du Xueshi. But Li Yuting was, after all, a professor of economics, and he had caught a phrase or two of the Du uncle and nephew's talk at the dining-room door, so he had a fair notion of it. Instinctively he reached up and rubbed his scalp — the indispensable preparation he made whenever he was about to deliver an opinion. But Du Xueshi had already got in ahead of him. His tone was hurried and heavy, and plainly he took the matter before them very seriously.

"We're discussing how to do away with labour agitation. Xintuo says that so long as the workers in a mill are all shareholders, there'll be no strikes. He cites an English shoe factory as his example. I, for my part, say his proposal can't be carried out! If they've money to be shareholders, they're no longer workers! All shareholders and no workers — what sort of a mill is that! —"

Du Xueshi, unable to get his breath back, stopped short. A burst of general laughter — Du Xintuo over there included. Only Wu Sunfu merely bared his teeth a little, without laughing aloud.

The laughter drew two more out of the big dining-room where they had been watching the cards — Wu Zhisheng and Fan Bowen. Seeming to know quite well why the rest were laughing, these two joined in the smiling too.

"Sixth Uncle has it wrong! What I said isn't so simple as that."

Amid the laughter Du Xintuo made his quiet declaration. Du Xueshi's face turned on the instant exceedingly ugly. He rounded on Xintuo and said with heat:

"Then be so good as to say it yourself!"

Du Xintuo shook his head with a smile, pursed his lips, and began to whistle a little French air. To Du Xueshi this was nothing less than an insult to him, the elder uncle. His whole face went crimson! Happily Fan Bowen stepped in to break the deadlock for them:

"I see what Old Tuo means. He wants a mill in which the shareholders are the workers and the workers are the shareholders. The capital is dispersed and held in the workers' hands, not in the hands of a few big shareholders. It may be a good scheme. Only, more's the pity, the women in Sunfu's mill are already so poor that all they have left is one mouth to beg with!"

Wu Sunfu could not help laughing out too. But still he said nothing. He had never approved of these young men's fondness for airy argument.

Staff Officer Lei, smoking his cigarette with one leg cocked over the other, was slowly shaking his head too. He had been two days in Shanghai now, yet the heart-stopping terror of the shellfire at the front, and the anxious, aggrieved bewilderment of the time he had blundered into the enemy's lines and been taken prisoner, had not yet quite faded from his mind; he was pessimistic about the course of the war, and pessimistic about his own prospects too. So it was over his own affairs that he shook his head.

"Just so! Xintuo's proposal simply won't do! Mine is the thing! I'm against mill-owners who, at the first little setback, think of cutting production or even shutting down. If China means to develop industry, it must first grit its teeth and take some losses. Everyone must do the nation credit: the workers not to be allowed to strike, and the mills not to be allowed to suspend or shut down!"

Feeling he had already beaten Xintuo, Du Xueshi put forward his own proposal once more, demanding that the whole drawing-room give ear. But, disappointingly enough, nobody would listen to him. Xintuo and Fan Bowen fell into talk and went off to the stone steps before the drawing-room veranda to speak of other things. Wu Sunfu, Lei Ming and Li Yuting, the three of them, though they took "let the workers buy in too" for their starting-point and began to talk again, drifted little by little back to the advances and retreats of the war. Du Xueshi set his cat-face in a scowl and, in a fit of pique, went back to the big dining-room to watch the women at their cards.

Here the three of them talked of the times. And Wu Sunfu's face flushed again with an excited glow. Though the recent military changes on the northern stretch of the Tianjin–Pukou line had cost the Yizhong company a fair loss on its bonds, still, thinking of the great hopes that the situation might unfold, Wu Sunfu could be cheerful. He looked at Staff Officer Lei and said:

"It looks as if the fighting will be over before long? The news of the withdrawal from Jinan was confirmed in banking circles today."

"Ah! It won't be over so soon as that. After Jinan there's still Xuzhou! War is a thing beyond all fathoming; sometimes a single line of defence, a single isolated town, can hold out half a year, six months. How is it to end so soon!"

Once Staff Officer Lei opened his mouth, though, he could not but play the "optimist." But Wu Sunfu smiled faintly. Though he did not know in detail just why Staff Officer Lei had gone from the front to Tianjin and then back to Shanghai, he could guess it to eight or nine parts in ten; and now, hearing Lei talk so, how could Sunfu keep from smiling? Besides, he most heartily did not want it to come to another deadlock in the neighbourhood of Xuzhou. That touched his own enterprises not a little! He turned his face to look at Li Yuting, when unexpectedly Li Yuting sprang up in a great fluster and cried out:

"Oh! Oh! Fight another six months? That would be past all bearing! Staff Officer Lei, that would never do! Only think — at this very moment He Long is at Shashi, moving in and out of Daye; Peng Dehuai is at Liuyang; Fang Zhimin is at Jingdezhen; and Zhu and Mao are watching for a chance to storm Ji'an! Fight another six months, and there's no telling how rampant these Communist bandits will grow! Won't that be the finish of us all!"

"Those roving bandits — what's there to fear! Once the main army arrives, they'll be wiped out at once. We don't count them as anything! It's only the Japanese newspapers that make so much of it, and that is done with a purpose. The Japanese spread rumours everywhere, to undermine the prestige of the Central Government."

Lei Ming's note of "optimism" grew thicker still, and his face too took on the air of one whose courage had swelled a hundredfold.

Li Yuting shook his head as if he could not believe it, and turning to Wu Sunfu gave him a grave warning:

"Sunfu! This labour trouble at your mill, breaking out at just this moment, neither early nor late, had best be settled quickly! Settle it by force! The general federated strike of the silk filatures is one item in the Communists' plan for a nationwide general rising in July — it's a signal-gun! And besides, the workers gathering in a mob to attack your car — that is already insurrection! If you don't strike first and put it down, it may well end in something like the burning of the mill! And then, even if you killed every one of them, the game would not be worth the candle!"

As Wu Sunfu listened, his face changed too. The terrifying scene of being hemmed in at the mill gate rose again at once before his eyes. The sound of the electric fan came to his ears exactly like the women's furious roar. And onto the terror of these memories a sharp point was now added: the servant Gaosheng suddenly ushered in two men — come, precisely, from the mill — none other than Wu Weicheng and Ma Jingshan, and with a pair of flustered faces!

Springing suddenly to his feet, Wu Sunfu asked, with a touch of alarm through his gravity:

"You've come from the mill? How are things there? There's been no trouble, I hope?"

"There was none when we left. But we've come to report some important news."

The two of them, Wu Weicheng and the other, answered with one voice, watching Wu Sunfu's face in an odd way.

At that Wu Sunfu's heart eased a moment, and without pressing to know just what urgent news was worth hurrying through the night to report, he paced slowly a step or two, forced a smile, and shot Wu Weicheng and the other a sharp glance, as if to say: "Come to run down Tu Weiyue again, no doubt — bah!" Wu Weicheng and his companion stood there stiffly and said nothing.

Seeing that Wu Sunfu had business, Staff Officer Lei took his leave first. Li Yuting too went off into the garden to chat with Du Xintuo and that set. In the great drawing-room only Wu Weicheng and Ma Jingshan were left, looking blankly at each other, unable to tell whether the errand they had come on would end in success or failure. The click of the tiles came from the big dining-room next door.

"What is this important matter? Something Tu Weiyue's done wrong again, I suppose?"

Coming back from seeing his guests off, Wu Sunfu said it with a stern face, and made a gesture for the two of them to sit down.

This time, however, Wu Weicheng and the other did not say much against Tu Weiyue. They had come to offer a method of settling the labour trouble; in truth it was Qian Baosheng's contriving behind the scenes, who had put the two of them forward to make the overture.

"Third Uncle! Qian Baosheng has great power in the union. He knows the workers' circumstances through and through; Tu Weiyue has been searching two days and still doesn't know which among the workers are Communists, whereas Qian Baosheng has long since had it all clear as day. His plan is, on the one hand, to arrest those Communists, and on the other to dismiss in a batch all the workers who do nothing but make trouble; hereafter the mill's hiring would all be on the union's introduction, with the union standing surety; and whenever the mill had any wage-cut, or any docking of the Sunday premium, it would first be squared with the union, and let the union deal with the workers; Qian Baosheng says that even if wages were cut to five-tenths or six-tenths, he could still guarantee there'd be no disturbance — Third Uncle, do it that way, and you'd be spared much daily worry, and there'd be none of this everlasting, itemized labour agitation. Isn't that far better? He'd long meant to lay these plans before you, Third Uncle, only you seemed not to trust him much, and so it was put off until today, when he's told me and Jingshan. That man — what he says, he can do!"

"As for opening tomorrow, I'm afraid that's more than Tu Weiyue can manage! The workers hate him to death! This afternoon he went into the shanties to seize people, and only stiffened the whole business the more! That was simply beating the grass and startling the snake! Now the workers all say: the boss is losing money, wages to be cut to eight-tenths, that can be talked over; but so long as this Tu doesn't go, they'll die before they go back to work! Now the whole mill's workers are set against him, that one man alone, and hate him to death! The whole gang of overseers and inspectors hate him to death too!"

Ma Jingshan added his piece to Wu Weicheng's speech, his two thievish, shifty eyes darting busily from Wu Sunfu's face to Wu Weicheng's, and back from Wu Weicheng's to Wu Sunfu's. Wu Weicheng, his whole face full of a seeming anxiety, sat there nodding respectfully, while with half an ear he listened to the click of the tiles next door and Lin Peishan's crystal, alluring laughter.

Wu Sunfu smiled faintly, putting on the air of one who "hears it for what it may be worth," yet a shade of hesitation and indecision plainly grew thicker and thicker in his eyes. Presently he raised a hand to finger his chin and lifted his eyebrows, as if about to speak; but the hand fingering his chin suddenly swept up, wiped down over his whole face, and dropped again onto the arm of the chair — still with no word. Besides the five or six threads that had long been tugging at his heart, one more was now added, and he felt he had no strength left to keep the whole heart in balance. The fire of a savage temper came flaming up from his heart. And at that moment Wu Weicheng said a few more words to add oil to the flame:

"Third Uncle! It isn't that I like to speak ill of others; it's truly that I can't hold it in, and can't but let you know. Tu Weiyue's magic treasure is big talk — all portentous airs, his mouth full of 'I have a way,' 'I'm sure of it'! His one talent is spending money to buy people over! He flings your money about, Third Uncle, without a pang! He tells the overseers and inspectors: go into the shanties and haul the people out! Haul one in, and there's a dollar's reward — is that any sort of way to do things?"

Wu Sunfu's face suddenly changed; his whole trust in Tu Weiyue was shaken, and pounding the arm of the chair he cried aloud:

"Is there such a thing? This is no lie of yours?"

"I'd not dare lie! Jingshan knows it too."

"Ah! How is it Mo Gancheng didn't come and report it to me? That old dog's-head never breathed half a word of it!"

"It seems Mr. Mo doesn't know either. Tu Weiyue is very high-handed, and keeps a great many things hidden from people."

Ma Jingshan hurriedly put in his word, stealthily throwing Wu Weicheng a wink. But Wu Sunfu, in his fury, noticed nothing of it. He sprang up and shouted sharply toward the outside of the drawing-room:

"Gaosheng! Go telephone and ask Mr. Mo to come — ah, no! Ring the mill, and get Mr. Tu to the phone!"

"But hold your hand a moment, Third Uncle, before you round on him! As yet there's only this word of ours, no hard proof, and Tu Weiyue will deny it!"

Wu Weicheng hastened to hold him back, and threw Ma Jingshan a look too. But Ma Jingshan was flustered, and, staring wide-eyed, could get no word out in his haste.

Wu Sunfu tilted his head, thought a moment, gave a snort through his nose, and went back to his seat; then he waved to Gaosheng, who stood outside the drawing-room door awaiting orders, and said irritably:

"Off with you! No need to ring after all!"

"Best of all, Third Uncle, send for Qian Baosheng tomorrow and question him. If Tu Weiyue can't get the mill open tomorrow, there'd be no harm in trying Qian Baosheng's methods for once."

Fearing the affair might be exposed, Wu Weicheng quickly cast about for a way to climb down, and at the same time passed Ma Jingshan a secret signal.

For a while the great drawing-room was silent. Out in the garden the wind set the leaves rustling, mingled with the loud laughter of Li Yuting and his set. In the big dining-room next door came a sound of shuffling tiles, and women's shrill, pert voices talking all at once of how the last hand had let the banker off far too cheap.

Wu Sunfu found all these sounds distasteful; and yet all these sounds beat, whether he would or no, hard upon his heart. His mind was a tangle in which he could form no resolve. One moment he felt that this Tu Weiyue had never been an easy man to master — stubborn, saturnine, over-bold; the next he felt that Wu Weicheng's words were not wholly to be trusted either, that he must use his own eyes and not his ears. At last, in deep vexation, he shook his head and turned his eyes on the two of them, Wu Weicheng and the other. On the faces of these two showed a faint look of unease.

"I understand! Off with you both, and no wild talk outside!"

Falling back, vaguely as ever, on the tone of the head of the house, Wu Sunfu stood up and left, his heart full of a savage temper shot through with a strange dejection he could not himself understand.

He shut himself in the study, and chewed over again, in fine detail, Tu Weiyue's manner and words of these two days, and Wu Weicheng's criticisms too. Yet the more he thought on these things, the fiercer grew the contradictory temper and dejection in his heart. His everyday firmness and decision had hidden themselves he knew not where, which struck even him as strange. And that energy of his that never knew fatigue seemed to have fled as well. As he mused muddily on, the sound of the wind stirring the leaves beyond the window called back the terror of being hemmed in before the mill gate; the sight of the patch of yellow light from the yellow-silk-shaded lamp on the writing-desk set him imagining, for no reason, the women setting fire to his mill! He was simply not his everyday self at all!

Yet the mischievous phantoms kept up their assault. From the mill they turned to the Yizhong company! The seventy or eighty thousand lost on the bonds, Zhao Botao's economic blockade, those eight mills athirst for great sums of money, Zhu Yinqiu's Qianhe Silk Filature turned into a "wet shirt"... all of it came crowding in! It spun in his brain like a wheel. Until he had wholly lost the power of clear thought, and only groaned beneath these pitiless phantoms.

Suddenly the latch on the study door rattled. Wu Sunfu started as if waking from a nightmare, and sprang bolt upright. Before his eyes was Wang Hefu's plump face, brows faintly knit in a wry smile. Wu Sunfu rubbed his eyes and looked again: the real, solid Wang Hefu had already sat down. Forgetting himself, Wu Sunfu suddenly asked:

"Ah, ah, Hefu! There's nothing wrong with those eight mills of ours, I hope?"

"A small matter, a trifling thing — why, Sunfu, do you know of it already?"

Wu Sunfu shook his head, in his heart still half thinking it a dream. He stared straight ahead, his gaze fixed on the two wisps of moustache above Wang Hefu's lip.

"For the moment it's only a small thing. Nothing but that everywhere has felt the effect of the war: trade is slack, and the goods we shipped out last week have all come back to us, to the last piece. But what's to be done hereafter? We sweat blood to raise the funds, sink them into those eight mills, the goods come off — and can't be sold, and on top of it there's warehouse and cotton-store rent to pay; that surely can't be a lasting arrangement."

Wang Hefu, having said it, sighed, and stared straight-eyed at Wu Sunfu too.

So that was all it was — not that the eight mills too had struck; and Wu Sunfu's heart was half eased. But once that unwonted instant of ease had passed, there came a fiercer temper and dejection still. Now the five or six threads that tugged outward at his heart all pulled at once, and his spirit could bear it not at all; he seemed to feel his heart already broken into pieces; he had no resolve left, only temper, only dejection.

Getting no answer, Wang Hefu knitted his brows and went on slowly:

"And there's more! They say that though the Central Army has given up Jinan this time, its strength is not impaired. For the present it still holds the line along the Jiaoji railway. And below Jinan, at every strategic military point they've thrown up very solid defence-works. This war, by the look of it, has yet some months to run! Some reckon it'll drag past New Year's Eve. A wretched business! So we must think of something practical, and quickly, for those eight mills of ours. Otherwise the pit the men ahead have fallen into, we too must fall into, to make up the pair!"

"Fight past New Year's Eve? It won't come to that! — Ah, but then, it's hard to say!"

Wu Sunfu spoke at last, but it came to the same as saying nothing, one utterance contradicting itself within itself. This was not his usual way, and Wang Hefu found it strange too. He guessed that Wu Sunfu had been too worn out these last days, and was a little distracted. Looking at Wu Sunfu's face, he thought its colour unhealthy too; and with a disappointed sigh he said:

"Sunfu, you're worn out and weary; I won't sit long. Let's talk again tomorrow."

"No, no! Not in the least! Let's go on talking!"

"Well then — Jiren and I have talked it over: we mean, from next month, in those eight mills, besides the layoffs and wage-cuts already fixed, to honestly run 'half time,' muddle through a month, and then see how things stand. —"

"Oh, oh, run a half-day shift? Won't that stir up trouble? The workers these days will smash up a mill, set fires, at the least provocation!"

Wu Sunfu sprang up with a start as he said it, his face red through the greyish pallor, dreadful to see, wholly unlike himself. Wang Hefu was taken aback a moment, but then answered with a smile:

"That won't happen — have you forgotten? Those eight mills of ours have three hundred workers or so at most, and at the least only about a hundred; they can't raise any commotion! Sunfu, you're truly worn out, overtaxed and heartsick; I urge you to rest a few days!"

"It's nothing! Never mind me! — Then run your half-day shift, by all means!"

"The silk-weaving mill must rush the new goods for the autumn sales, so it stays on full-day work."

Wang Hefu added this last, and seeing that Sunfu really was somewhat unlike himself, chatted on idly a few words more and then left.

By now the whole sky was black with cloud. Li Yuting and his set had gone too; there was no one in the garden, and the lamps among the thick leaves had been put out, so that the whole garden was dim and gloomy. Only from the big dining-room still streamed a dazzling light and the click of tiles, brisk as ever. In the great drawing-room the wireless set droned on with the last broadcast of the night, some ballad or other. Wu Sunfu went languidly back to the study, and only then, as if coming to himself, recalled little by little Wang Hefu's words just now, and his own fluster, the baring of his own weakness.

At this, the savage temper seized upon Wu Sunfu's whole soul once more! And not a simple temper only: he hated himself, and vented his anger on all that his eyes saw and his ears heard! He circled madly round the study, his eyes gone all red, his teeth clenched; he wanted only to find someone on whom to let off his rage! He wanted to smash something! All the vexations he had met with, on the side of the mill and on the side of the Yizhong company, turned now into a single savage impulse — to smash something!

Like a beast of prey poised to seize and rend, he sat in the swivel-chair before the writing-desk, his gaze darting on every side; he was seeking there the most gratifying object to destroy, the object best fitted to give his fury and malice their fullest vent!

Amah Wang came in bearing bird's-nest gruel, and Wu Sunfu did not so much as notice. But when Amah Wang set the bowl of bird's-nest gruel down before him, his burning gaze fell suddenly upon Amah Wang's hand. It was a hand white and plump, with little dimples at the knuckles. The furious, destroying flame that wrapped Wu Sunfu's whole body leapt of a sudden to white heat. His pair of eyes, that seemed about to drip blood, jerked up and riveted themselves on Amah Wang's face. The Amah Wang before him was Amah Wang no longer, but a thing! A thing that could be destroyed! A thing he could destroy with the keenest gratification!

He rose abruptly and lunged straight at the object of his destroying. Amah Wang seemed to start, but at once, as if understanding, smiled a coaxing smile and retreated lightly backward; and in her handsome eyes there showed at the same time a touch of doubt and fear and coyness; yet in a twinkling she had backed to the corner and set her back against the wall; then the plump white palm with the dimpled knuckles pressed the electric switch on the wall, and the big lamp in the room went out, leaving only the desk-lamp casting its ring of yellow glow, and then that lamp too went out, and the study was all in darkness, with only a distant light throwing the shadows of the trees upon the window-gauze.

By the time the lamp came on again, Wu Sunfu lay alone on the sofa, brows knit, staring blankly. The unnameable frenzy was gone, but a self-doubting, self-questioning — what had he done? — had taken its place in his heart. He felt he had dreamed some strange dreams. Little by little the conjuror's turning wheel — how would the mill open tomorrow? and what if the eight mills' goods could not be sold? and Tu Weiyue, and Qian Baosheng, what of them? — all of it circled back into his consciousness.

He gave a grim laugh, then shut his eyes and bit his lip.

At this hour the clock in the study pointed to the first hour of the morrow. In the big dining-room in front, the talk and laughter and the click of tiles were lively still.
