## Carnage

A few days later, one afternoon, a man in a deep woven hat slipped into the residence of Maeda Gen'i, the Kyoto magistrate—not through the front entrance but in at the back gate. The sun was still high. He had got himself up, of all things, in a loud large-figured kosode of mingled scarlet and yellow, light-green Iga hakama, black tabi, and a scabbard worked in gold lacquer: a costume calculated, deliberately, to make people stare. Taking his time, glancing about the grounds, he strolled at his leisure as far as the barrack-quarters, and there, sure enough, an off-duty foot-soldier who happened by pulled him up short.

"Who might you be? Where are you bound? To go about inside a lord's grounds still hooded—that's hardly proper."

"Fair enough."

Smiling wryly, the man untied the cords of his hat.

"And your name?"

"Tsuzura Jūzō, at your service."

"In whose household do you serve?"

"I have never yet served anyone."

"Your birthplace?"

"Iga."

Jūzō gave the answer in a pointedly loud voice.

"And whom have you come to see?"

"Well, now—I've forgotten the fellow's name, which is what has me in such difficulty. There are two of them. Once they were in the same trade as myself, but they've borrowed other names and gone to ground here in your master's house, so I hear."

"The same trade—meaning?"

"Thieves."

"Y-you jest, surely."

"Ha. Thief or samurai, I can't see there's so very thick a line between the two. Lord Hachisuka Hikoemon Masakatsu, whom the Taikō raised so high—track him back and he was a highwayman out of Owari. Further back, there was a man named Ise no Saburō Yoshimori, a retainer of Kurō Hōgan, and he was the chief of a band of mountain brigands. Still, the two I've come to see, in your master's keeping, are men whose skill at thieving puts all of those to shame; not to be spoken of in the same breath. I came in the hope of meeting them and talking over old times—but the one thing I need, the names, has slipped me. Clean gone."

"Wait—wait. Be so good as to hold here a moment. I'll send for someone of the household fit to deal with this."

The foot-soldier had gone pale; he all but sat straight down on the ground, and only by putting his strength into his knees did he keep his feet. To his mind it was inconceivable that this big man, who in a loud voice, in broad daylight, in the very grounds of the magistrate's residence, was calling himself and others thieves, could actually be the thief he claimed; he took him rather for one of those eccentric bravos so common in those days, and, wanting only to be out of the exchange as fast as he could, he had backed away in spite of himself.

Jūzō checked him gently.

"There, there. No need to go running for anyone. One of the two I've come to see has just now come along, as it happens."

Round the corner of the corridor Jūzō pointed to stood Kazama Gohei, passing by at that very moment, watching the pair of them with a fixed stare.

"Th-that gentleman—a thief?"

"Just so. In the same line as myself."

Jūzō, in high good humor, clapped the foot-soldier on the shoulder and made his unhurried way toward the corridor.

"Kazama Gohei. I've come today as a guest. Has your Yoshino wound healed yet?"

"It has not."

Gohei drew up one corner of his thin lips. At the same moment the pupils rolled upward and half hid themselves under the lids. When this man conceived a grudge, a shadow like an old fox's seemed to fall across the soft, almost feminine face.

"There's a part of me that will not heal."

"Now, that's a rarity. So even Kazama Gohei has a part in him that can be wounded?"

"Understand this."

Gohei thrust out his sharp chin.

"I am no longer your fellow, nor your fellow-pupil. I am your enemy. It is my office to take you, at the cost of my own life if need be. And our stations differ. I am a samurai, and no mean one. And you—what are you?"

"A rappa."

"If you know it, don't run on so easily."

"Am I not welcome, then, come calling?"

"I'll summon men and have you taken."

"Before that—do you see this?"

"I saw it long since."

Gohei answered sourly. To all appearances they stood grasping each other's sleeves and chatting like old friends—but in the crook of Jūzō's fingers a poisoned kozuka lay hidden.

"If you're seen standing about talking with a ronin of my sort, it will do you no good. Show me some empty room somewhere."

"Let go of my sleeve."

"Then walk ahead of me."

Jūzō pushed him on. Down the gallery to the east, out into a corridor running north and south, where the duty-rooms stood ranged in a row. The samurai on duty there stopped their brushes over the registers and stared, first at Jūzō, then at Gohei, and back again.

"My compliments, gentlemen."

Jūzō bowed lightly and passed on, at his ease. The men opposite hurriedly bowed in return. The difference in a man's own bearing, it seemed, was what did it.

As they turned one more corner in the corridor, a westering sun, blazing, struck the paper shoji on their left.

The blue leaves that roofed the little inner court caught the light, and the nape of Gohei's neck, walking ahead, was stained blue.

"So—summer again."

Jūzō murmured it to no one in particular. It was the fourth summer since he had come into the capital, and it was drawing on again already. Gohei made no answer, but turned his white, glinting eyes and said,

"Here."

And he slid open a paper shoji beside them.

"Go in."

Gohei said it.

As Jūzō turned his back to enter, he felt a killing intent that seemed to split him down the spine. He spun round on the instant.

"Gohei—that comes later."

And he forestalled the stroke in the nick of time. Gohei, letting the hand he had laid on the hilt of his short sword sink again, in vexation, said,

"If you imagine you'll leave the gate of this house alive, you're gravely mistaken."

"All right, all right. First put up that shoji, will you."

Gohei drew the shoji shut behind him, and Jūzō pushed his own sword far off across the floor. Neither would sit; they stood planted, legs apart, facing each other at a distance where, if either had the will, he could cut with a drawn short sword without so much as shifting his ground.

"State your business."

"To kill you."

"What—"

Gohei sprang back to the wall and drew his short sword.

"Don't be so hasty. There's something I must ask first. You killed Kuroami, the genin of the Tsuzura house—didn't you."

"What—Kuroami?"

"Ah. So it wasn't you."

A look of relief flowed across Jūzō's face. Jūzō had that weakness in him. To cut Gohei down as an enemy was one thing; but to strike this old comrade as an avenger of his own household, somehow, left him ill at ease.

"Let me ask once more. It was not you who killed Kuroami and all his band of Iga men."

"It was not. Only—someone robbed me of the deed I should have done myself."

"A Kōga man?"

"That may be."

"So here too Iga has been beaten by Kōga."

"—Jūzō. I beg you."

"What is it?"

"Take me with your rope. I'll spare your life afterward, without fail. And if that won't do, then at least give me one word—the name of the man behind you. With that, I can beat the Kōga."

"You've learned a strange little song. For a ninja, everything is settled by the arm. That Kōga man, though—Mari Dōgen, was it you named? Him alone I'll cut down for you. He should be here, in the barrack-quarters of this house. Where is he?"

"…………"

"If you won't say, I'll go and find him. He's not only Kuroami's killer—he's the murderer of my master."

"West of the barrack gate there's a black pine you could barely get your arms round."

"I saw it. A branch runs out to the south."

"His is the house under that branch."

"—So."

For a while Jūzō gauged the westering light on the shoji, and thought:

(Better to cut him after the sun is down.)

"Speaking of the master—"

Gohei said it, probing Jūzō's face.

"How is Kisaru?"

"Kohagi had her taken. But she was let go afterward and has gone back to Shimotsuge. Does it prey on you?"

"Heh."

Gohei's face turned suddenly base, and he laughed low in his throat.

"I can't call up her face—yet strangely her body alone won't leave my mind."

"Make her your wife, then. Why not."

Jūzō said it in a voice empty of interest.

"A rappa's daughter would only hinder a man's advancement."

"And you a rappa yourself, for generations back."

"No. Now I am a samurai, with a master to serve."

"It's in the eyes."

Jūzō laughed softly.

"Not the eyes of any ordinary samurai. The old rappa in you can't be hidden. Eyes cunning as a snake. Even to me, who keep to the trade still, they turn my stomach—the sight of my own kind."

"Enough idle talk. Your business is done; leave now."

"That I can't do."

Jūzō dropped his thick eyebrows into a rueful slant.

"There's still sun in the sky. Until that sun is halfway down, I have need to linger here a while yet. If I go out now, I'd only be walking straight into your snare. Mind you, once I've killed you, I could take my leave this very moment. For later's sake it might even be wiser to kill you now. So I think—and yet, strangely, when I look at your face I can't do it. Why, even I don't rightly know. Am I coming to the age where a man grows fond, little by little, of an old comrade?"

"…………"

"You talk like some rotten bonze. If you can kill me, why not try it and see? For my part, I'll show you no mercy."

"There's the fine thing about you, Gohei. Cold at heart as ice—just as a rappa should be."

"Are you mocking me?"

"No mockery. If this work of mine ends and I come out of it with my life, I've begun to feel I'd like to go into retreat in the hills of Iga. Were Kuroami alive it would be otherwise, but after a piece of work like this the small odd jobs weary me. To farm a little, to hunt, to enjoy months and days of my own free choosing—since that bad worm began to stir in me, you've somehow started to look great in my eyes. You loathed the rappa's rank and made yourself a kept retainer, but by your true nature you were born to be a rappa. It's I, who keep to the trade, who feel the more uncertain of it. You, now—you could cut down a man like me, talking to you so intimately, the moment a gap opened. And you would cut. Well? Can you cut?"

"If a gap opens, yes."

"Cut, then."

"Why?"

"If you strike, I too draw. Only then does the shape of a man's heart settle. After that my body moves of itself and cuts you down."

"Bold words. If it were merely a matter of killing you alone, I'd not raise a foolish dust here—there are other ways to hand."

"Shall I wait, then, for these ways of yours?"

Jūzō seemed to be growing bored; slowly he turned his head and looked toward the shoji. Seeing that even at that deliberate opening Gohei showed no sign of taking the bait, he smiled to himself and said,

"The garden's darkened nicely. About time I set off for Dōgen's quarters. Well—won't you come along? He's your master's murderer too."

"Absurd."

(So that's it.)

—was Gohei's instant thought.

Jūzō made as if to leave the room. His sword lay against the wall where he had pushed it a while before. To retrieve it he let his posture go slack, slow and clumsy. Seizing the gap—

(Now.)

The big toe of Gohei's right foot tensed before he knew it, and the mat gave, unluckily, a faint creak. With a start Gohei drew back the strength. And that, in turn, made the opening Gohei's own.

Into that opening Jūzō's careless voice slipped, deftly—

"What is it now?"

"Nothing. Nothing at all."

"So."

As he spoke, Jūzō, to open the shoji, unhurriedly laid bare before Gohei's very eyes the whole broad, foolish-looking expanse of his back. Gohei's hand went in spite of him to his short sword. A drawing cut, at this range, would sever a man even at the middle of a small blade. But something in Gohei hesitated.

(A lure. I'll not take it.)

He let the second chance go by as well. And at the sense of it, Jūzō's back seemed to laugh.

(Curse you.)

Gohei raged inwardly.

(The cunning is on your side. You say an old comrade can't be cut, and all the while you make a gap and try to draw me on.)

But Jūzō was doing nothing out of the way. Briskly he slid the shoji open. That artless sound announced the end of Gohei's wrestling-match with himself. He went limp with exhaustion.

Jūzō stepped out into the corridor.

Gohei crossed the threshold after him. It was the difference of a single stride, no more. Jūzō was gone. The corridor was full of the damp dark of a moonlit evening. A faint afterglow lingered in the western sky. Against that backlight, over toward Dōgen's quarters, the huge black pine stood pasted against the heavens. Fluttering, as if longing for the last of the fading light, circling the crown of the pine, things were tumbling like dead leaves. Straining his eyes, Gohei saw they were bats, in enormous numbers—yet to his eye it seemed for a moment that Jūzō had turned himself into one of them, and—

(I'm not myself.)

—he jeered at himself.

Jūzō had vanished into the garden and must have made his way, threading the plantings, toward Dōgen's quarters. Gohei glared into the dark where he had disappeared and hated him fiercely.

(Soon enough—before long—I'll make him understand.)

To gather men, Gohei ran sliding down the corridor and went off into the inner rooms.

But Jūzō was there.

He had only been clinging upside down in a pocket of dark behind the gable. Making sure Gohei had gone, he let go and began to drop, softly, and set his toe with a light tap on the polished boards of the corridor. The dark already wrapped him well enough. Jūzō began, quietly, to move.

The place Jūzō came into view was not Dōgen's quarters but a shrine of Benzaiten in the northeast corner of the grounds. A pond had been dug, and in the middle a small island floated. The shrine was built on that island.

Island though it was, it measured no more than three or four ken across. The roof of the shrine barely stood as high as a grown man. From the bank to the island ran an arched bridge lacquered vermilion, and by day, Jūzō thought, the whole would surely be as pretty as a scene set in a tray-garden.

Jūzō sank himself into the thicket on the island. Before long Dōgen came from the other side. Dōgen was kept in this house under the guise of warden of the Benten shrine. To appear at the island shrine at this hour every day was the man's natural duty, and nothing out of the ordinary. Jūzō had studied Dōgen's daily round beforehand, and, to avoid the danger of breaking into the barracks, had laid his ambush behind the shrine.

Gohei, meanwhile, knowing nothing of this, had planted men in ambush all round Dōgen's quarters and set still others in a close cordon outside the walls of the Maeda residence. This disposition was not to guard Dōgen's person from Jūzō. Of it Dōgen himself had not even been told. In Gohei's reckoning, Dōgen—who was his enemy too—must first be cut down by Jūzō's hand. Taking Jūzō was work for after that was done.

Gohei directed these men from an odd place: the privy of the house next to Dōgen's. To hide in the ceiling of Dōgen's own house would have been most convenient, but Dōgen was a ninja, and to be found out by those dog-keen five senses of his was scarcely to be avoided. From the privy next door he could catch, through a little window, a glimpse of the plantings in Dōgen's garden, and could also hear, more clearly than one would think, every sound and stir Dōgen made. The privy jutted out into the shrubbery a little apart from the house, and touched directly on the neighboring garden. So even coming and going was free enough to Gohei's thieving art—out through the shrubbery and over the low fence, and there he was.

The master of the neighboring house had of course not been asked leave. Naturally the household came in to use the place, none the wiser. The privy was divided into two rooms. If the tread crossing the wet veranda was a man's, Gohei hid in the inner room; if a woman's, he clung to the ceiling. In the first stretch of his watch the master came once, the wife once. Neither sensed him there.

It was when the young wife came in a second time. She set her hand-candle quietly on the board before her and did her business.

A faint night wind was blowing through the little window. Stirred by it, perhaps, a single loose hair that clung to the black cloth over Gohei's face came fluttering down onto the wick of the hand-candle. The hair burned with a small sizzling sound, like the wing-hum of an insect. A faint smell drifted up.

The wife started and looked up. A man in wholly black garb clung to the ceiling with all four limbs, like some monster. Before she could raise a cry, Gohei dropped soundlessly and clapped a hand over her mouth.

"Don't make a sound."

Even with her mouth stopped, the wife—a woman of no small nerve, it seemed—got to her feet and worked her hands desperately, trying to draw the disorder of her dress together. Looking at her face, he found her unexpectedly young; from the beautifully combed hair drifted the settled fragrance of a married woman.

"Cry out and it goes ill with you. I've borrowed this privy without leave, true enough, but I mean no harm—not to this house, not to you. I have my reasons. But they've nothing to do with you or your master. There, now—will you not be still?"

The woman writhed with all her might. Gohei put his mouth to her ear.

"Let me tell you. Hear me out and I'll release you at once. Until then, only, be still."

And in a whisper,

"Now. That I was here—breathe not a word of it, though your mouth be torn. Keep it locked in your own breast alone, and nothing at all will come of it. Speak of it, and I'll break into this house this very moment and raise the cry that I forced the mistress here in the privy. Then it's for you either to take your own life or be cast out—one or the other. Do you follow?"

"…………"

"Well?"

Even as he pressed the point, Gohei's hand went suddenly, parting the front of the woman's skirts. She caught her breath and struggled to slip free of his arm.

"I'll not force you. But it's as good as done. So you'll not speak of it, even once you're back among them. Go."

Gohei thrust the woman out. Released, she reeled to the wall. But she had gone quiet, as if made over into another person, and, head hung, tried to open the privy door. The hand-candle was left behind. Gohei picked it up.

"You've forgotten this," he said, and handed it to her. She took it in a white hand. And then, for the first time, behind his mask, Gohei let a leer come to his face, low enough that he noticed it himself. The woman fled from the privy and vanished into the dark of the wet veranda.

After that, a quarter-hour passed.

In the ear of Gohei, hidden in the privy, the sense of Dōgen's presence suddenly broke off.

(Surely he's not asleep yet. —Has he gone out?)

There had been no sound of any real preparation to leave, so Gohei judged he would soon be back.

Gohei waited patiently.

But by then Dōgen had appeared at the foot of the arched bridge before the Benten shrine, and Jūzō, hidden behind the shrine, had marked him.

Dōgen had begun, soundlessly, to cross the arched bridge. Bridge though it was, it did not span more than five ken. Over the sacred bridge, painted brilliant in vermilion and black lacquer, came Dōgen in the white robe of a shrine-warden. Peering through the leaves of the thicket, Jūzō saw him as a goblin dancing in the dark.

Jūzō brushed the moss aside and set his ear to the ground, counting Dōgen's steps.

Dōgen's foot left the bridge. When he had come within five paces, treading the grass, Jūzō showed his whole self from the shadow of the shrine, advancing his right foot at a slant, and—

"Dōgen."

"Jūzō?"

Dōgen answered at his ease. It was hardly possible he had foreseen that Jūzō would be lying in wait for him here tonight; but the instant his lightning intuition told him it was Tsuzura Jūzō, he turned the surprise about and, from the commanding position rather, called out Jūzō's name.

"The very man—Tsuzura Jūzō of Iga."

"A ninja giving his name—that's a rare thing. As for me, I am Ban Mari Dōgen, resident of Kōga, as you learned once at Kohagi's house. What is this ambush for, tonight?"

"After all these years you can feign such innocence? Ask your own breast."

"You mean Kuroami?"

"There's more."

"Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon, you mean. —Both met an unlovely end under a Kōga blade. And you say you'll avenge them? That, too, is a rare thing in the ninja's world."

Dōgen laughed a soundless, booming laugh.

"For a ninja, killing a man needs no justification."

"If you'd be killed without justification, so be it. You and I were fated, one way or another, to come face to face like this. Blinded by what gain, of late you've been meddling in the work of us Iga, in your petty way. Tonight I'll make you pay the price for it."

"Well said. My own case is exactly the same. Without my even setting the stage, how commendable that you came out to meet me of your own accord. Well—which do you choose? To be cut down, or to be taken?"

"Your tongue runs smoothly. But by the look of you, it seems you've nothing at your waist. I have another blade ready here. Want it?"

"Thorough courtesy. Also unbecoming a ninja. Still—since you're so kind."

"Take it."

Jūzō, four ken off, from within the thicket, sent the sword flying, hilt turned foremost, sheath and all. The sword passed over Dōgen's head and, with a flash, slipped free of its sheath. The sheath fell into the pond, and Dōgen already had the hilt in his grip in mid-air. The blade held, so, in the high guard. Or so it seemed—for no more than an instant.

Dōgen had leapt in, straight down the center. In point of time, Jūzō's throwing of the sword and Dōgen's leaping in were faster than turning a sheet of paper front to back. Unluckily, Jūzō's stance was not yet made. Perforce he was thrown onto the defense, and sprang back two ken. At the same time he drew his own sword. Half a step more back and there was already the pond.

The moment Jūzō gave ground, Dōgen, still in the high guard, doubled his leap once more. It was like a frog jumping. An ordinary swordsman would of course have shied from it, in fear of breaking his own balance—but only a ninja will, at times, use this uncanny swordplay.

Jūzō could give ground no further. Take the stroke, then? But even if he received it cleanly, the sheer force of Dōgen's downward cut, brought down with his whole body, would drive Jūzō's position back of itself. Behind him was the pond.

His left foot would go into the water and his balance break; and though he blocked the second stroke, the third onslaught would leave him nothing to do but wait to be cut straight down.

But Jūzō did not give ground. Nor did he take the stroke. Abruptly he brought his knees together and sank to the earth, and at the same moment turned the sword he had held low, edge upward, and—a hair's breadth ahead of Dōgen's falling blade—flung it up toward the sky. Had he been the least bit slow, Jūzō would have been cut like a fixed target. He had wagered on that paper-thin margin.

"Kya!"

Dōgen cried out. What flew up in a spout of blood was Dōgen's wrist. In the hand, the sword Jūzō had given him was still gripped. The two together fell scattering off into the middle of the pond. And the body of Dōgen, which had come hurtling on with his hips flung high, could not for its own momentum stop at the water's edge; it went over Jūzō's body and, as if chasing after the severed wrist, pitched down on all fours, and toppled face-first over the surface of the water.

The pond, unexpectedly, was shallow.

Dōgen sprang up quickly, and then he grinned. Not in embarrassment, nor in flattery. If anything, it was a haughty look. And with that expression he flung out, as a great man would command a menial:

"Jūzō. The bout is settled. Don't cut me. Put down that blade. Help me up."

"What?"

"I've lost. If it's your wish, I'll draw off just as I am and go home to my province."

"Grown fond of your life, have you?"

"Of course. When it comes to being cut, a man's life is dear. Now hear me. In my province a woman is waiting for me. Two of them. I can't throw my life away here for nothing. In exchange, from tonight on I'll leave off Lord Gen'i's work entirely."

"That won't do."

Something in Dōgen's frankness pleased Jūzō, and a friendly smile opened suddenly on his face.

"Kuroami was killed by you. Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon too."

"Both of them were men, were they not?"

"Men, you say?"

"Those who wait for me are women. Creatures who cannot live without me. A man great enough to avenge a genin should understand a feeling like that. Send me home to Kōga."

"An unlovely way to meet your end."

Jūzō set foot into the pond.

"Wait."

"You go on."

The moment Jūzō's sword flashed, Dōgen's already handless arm was severed at the root, and fell trailing blood into the water.

"Kōga man."

"Wh-what?"

Dōgen was barely on his feet, enduring the pain.

"Women shall nurse me the rest of my days, then."

"Aye, get yourself nursed. You'll have no thanks from me."

Dōgen's voice shook with pain, yet it kept still the haughty ring of Mari of Kōga.

Dōgen went on speaking.

"Now, Iga man. I played on the softness of your heart and begged for my life. Judged as a bout, that's a loss for you. With a temper like yours, you'll never be able to cut me down. A pity of a man, but you were born a ninja bungled and botched. —Or else."

"Or else what?"

"Can you cut me now, in cold blood?"

Jūzō gave a wry laugh in the dark.

"Whatever your arm, your tongue at least is a nimble thing."

The wind had begun to blow, the young nandina at their feet clashing ceaselessly, and the smell of blood had begun to hang in the air. Straining his eyes, Jūzō saw Dōgen standing planted in the pond like a Niō guardian, the blood streaming down and staining his upper body dark. Jūzō knit his brows faintly. At the same moment he grew aware, and was faintly amused, that he was troubling himself over it. To a ninja, it may be, he was by nature unsuited. Yet Jūzō had begun to feel that he wanted to clap this Kōga man on the shoulder—this man so wholly the ninja, so full of stubborn temper and the cunning of the heart. It was, in truth, the same warm fellow-feeling with which he had come to love his master Jirōzaemon, and Kuroami.

"Won't you let me dress it?"

He said it in the tone of a man speaking to one of his own.

"Spare me your kindness. A man who is Mari Dōgen doesn't faint at a scratch like this. Only I've one thing to tell you. Now that I'm brought to this pass, there's no road for me but to shut myself away in the hills of Kōga—but you too, wash your hands of it, and soon. Become an ordinary samurai; or if you've a field, a farmer. You've too much pity in you; you're not suited to be a ninja. In any case, tonight the victory was mine. Remember that. I look forward to the day when we may both live to pour each other a cup. Farewell."

When Dōgen had finished, in a broad, magnanimous manner altogether the victor's, he wheeled about and turned his back. Slowly, and with a faint sound of water, he began to cross the pond; but when he had come as far as the misted plantings on the far bank, the sense of Dōgen's presence suddenly broke off.

(The fool. —He's gone and died.)

Jūzō thought.

Even as he had stood there in the pond, the bleeding must have been prodigious. In his fading consciousness, wringing out the last of his strength, he had gone on saying you have lost, mine is the victory—all to keep, in the ninja's scale of rank, the face befitting Mari of Kōga: it was Dōgen's pitiful vanity in dying.

(Why did the man not vanish the moment he was cut and staunch his wound?)

Had he done so, Dōgen would of course have kept his life. But there lay the absurdity of Dōgen's death, a thing all his own. In the ninja's world there is no victory or defeat, no vanity, no human feeling, no face to keep. And by making that his boast, Dōgen kept up his own vanity, and to keep up that vanity he threw his life away.

(A strange man. I'm not the only one, it seems, unfit to be a ninja.)

Jūzō wiped his sword and gave a low chuckle.

The wind seemed to grow fiercer still. The trees within the grounds clashed, and presently rain came mixed in. From his breast Jūzō took a ninja's hood and drew the brim down low. His shoulders had begun to get wet. Running up to the wall, he came to its edge and sprang lightly. From the edge of the tile he gripped, rainwater came running down his right arm. Somewhere outside, there was a stir. Jūzō stopped moving.

(So, Gohei—you've laid your men in ambush.)

Hanging from the wall by one arm, Jūzō shut his eyes. To cut his way clear and slip out would be easy enough. But that was not what Jūzō was pondering. He was thinking whether, in this wind and rain, he might plunge of his own accord into Gohei's disposition, hunt Gohei out, and this very night make an end of Gohei's life too.

(Cut him?)

So he pressed the question on his own heart. How many times, by now, had he pressed it? Even come to this pass, there was in Jūzō a faint hesitation still. He shook his head, blotting out the softness of his own heart, and—

(But where is Gohei?)

Jūzō pictured to himself the placement of the ambush parties within and without the grounds, and tried to work out where Gohei, as their commander, was likely to be. The rain, running down the brim of the ninja's hood, had begun to wet Jūzō's face.

Gohei, for his part—he would not stay forever in the privy of the barrack-quarters. Having marked that the sense of Dōgen's presence had broken off, he had already slipped out of the privy, and, like a dog nosing out a man's footsteps, was prowling within and without the grounds after the presences of Dōgen and Jūzō.

When Gohei at last made for the neighborhood of the Benten pond, the matter between Jūzō and Dōgen was already over. Gohei, of course, did not know it. Parting the grass of a narrow path soundlessly, coming as far as the point where the vermilion bridge showed, he caught the smell of blood. In spite of himself Gohei pressed himself down onto the wet path.

Only his eyes he raised.

A wall showed.

On the wall's whiteness, dim even to night-eyes, a stain like the shadow of a tree hung and swayed.

(Not Dōgen. Jūzō. —So he's done the cutting.)

This smell of blood must be Dōgen's, Gohei thought, and grinned. As if the grin had carried across, the shadow on the wall let go with its hands and came down. Gohei gave a start.

(He isn't going over the wall. —Why did he come down?)

The moment Gohei wondered at it was the very moment Jūzō made up his mind to seek Gohei's figure within the grounds.

Jūzō was coming this way.

Gohei pressed himself lower. Presently he stopped his breath, and then let out a thread of a breath, and killed his presence.

Jūzō was coming near.

Gohei wavered. Should he finish him, or take him? To take him was best, but for that he must call here every one of the men lying in wait. A finger-whistle would do it. But by doing so, there was the danger of letting Jūzō get away. Better, after all—even if he could not finish him here—to give him a wound and rob him of freedom of movement.

Gohei twisted his body a little and drew a kozuka.

Between the two men the spray of rain hung thick.

Perhaps it had caught the sense of Gohei drawing the kozuka. The feet in black garb, which had been moving like a shadow-picture in the spray, went suddenly still.

(He's realized.)

Heedless of it, Gohei braced the kozuka's blade with three fingers. It was already coated thickly with poison. He held it to his nose to test its potency, and wet it with rainwater to melt the drug.

Gohei worked his wrist carefully, flexing it. Presently, from the crook of his fingers, the kozuka flew, turning slowly. On the instant, the shadow in the spray flung itself to the earth.

Gohei sprang. He rushed on with a force that seemed to split the rain. Gohei's intent was to bring a single stroke straight down before Jūzō could recover his stance. Hit or miss, he would go rushing past at the same speed. It is a swordplay peculiar to the Iga, handed down from their forefathers.

Jūzō, meanwhile, rolling on the ground, had, the moment he threw himself flat, plucked the kozuka out of his left thigh, and, at the strange smell of it, drawn his short sword and swiftly gouged out the flesh round the wound. Blood came spurting up in a gush.

The end of this instant's work, and Dōgen's—no, Gohei's sword coming down out of the pitch-black heavens, were very nearly of the same moment. Jūzō had no leisure to take the stroke. Barely rolling clear of the blade, he made sure his opponent had run past into the dark beyond, then, gauging the interval before the next onslaught, tore his hakama. It was to bind the wound.

Jūzō came reeling to his feet, the bared blade held point-up. Whether the poison had spread a little through his body, a light dizziness took him.

Gohei, having run past, watched Jūzō's movements again from the thicket. Seeing the stumble of the risen Jūzō's feet, he nodded to himself.

(That should do it.)

There was nothing left but to call the men. Gohei took thumb and ring-finger into his mouth and made a sharp whistling sound. At the sound, a grim shadow came over Jūzō's cheek.

"So you've done it."

His left foot was half without use. Limping, he closed on the direction the whistle had come from.

"Gohei, no need to hide. Come out. Where are you?"

Gohei gave no answer.

The wind and rain were at last beginning to abate. From far across the grounds came voices calling one to another, drawing gradually nearer.

"Come out."

Jūzō swept a shrub across level with a horizontal cut. The lopped leaves and twigs scattered on the wind. Gohei, as before, kept his cunning silence.

"Won't you come out?"

As he spoke, Jūzō pitched over with a crash into the thicket of shrubs. Hearing the sound, Gohei for the first time unknit his brows. The quarry was down. All that was left was to wait for the men he had placed to arrive.

But by then Jūzō was already gone from the spot where he had made the noise. Even Gohei, trusting too much in the effect of the kozuka he had thrown, did not notice that Jūzō's sound had used no more than the simplest beginner's trick of escape.

Jūzō ran under cover of the leaves, gained the wall, trod on his sword-guard to gain the roof, and dropped lightly onto the road. As he landed, a fierce pain shot up from his left foot, and in spite of himself he staggered two or three steps.

At his reeling flank a man posted along the road struck from behind, without a word. Jūzō turned the strength of the left foot he had barely checked straight into his right hand, and swept out with a heavy stroke. Blood came spurting over his shoulder. He did not so much as look behind.

Already, at Gohei's whistle, the cordon along the road had broken and the greater part of the men were gathering toward Gohei's position within the grounds. Along Jūzō's way now spread the dark that guaranteed his freedom.

Jūzō stripped off the black ninja garb as if tearing it from himself. From beneath appeared the clothes he had worn before. Soaked with rain, they were heavy as hide. From weariness and the wound, no doubt, a nausea rose suddenly to his throat; he remembered, barely, swallowing it back—but after that, in a state near to fainting, he only ran, only walked.

He had a memory of crossing the Kamo River at Sanjō. And at the gate of the Shōren-in at Awataguchi he remembered swallowing down a restorative. Only, what Jūzō found strange, in an action all his own: he had come as far as Awataguchi—so why had he not crossed the Higashiyama hills?

Cross them, and one came out at Yamashina. To go into the hills of Ōmi before the night was out, rest away his fatigue, and then by the byways of Kōga village return to Iga: there at last, for Jūzō as he now was, would have been the one place of ease. Yet in Jūzō, even in his state near unconsciousness, the thought of taking that road did not well up so much as once. —Almost like a man in a dream, he turned south at the foot of the hills of Awataguchi.

Several times, it seemed, he tumbled down into the ravines. Crawling up, he walked south with an empty mind. Go south along this road, and there was a house of one who—more baffling in his true nature even than Gohei or Dōgen—might be called the more dangerous for it. To that house, which he would surely never have gone near had his mind been sound, Jūzō walked, as a wounded beast finds by instinct the place of its own ease.

He had no memory of climbing the earthen wall. Cutting across the wide garden, he came and stood before a small building fitted out in the manner of a court pavilion, set among a pine grove, and presently, pressing the rain-shutters with both hands, Jūzō quietly gave way.

"Kohagi—"

He called it, faintly.

When Kohagi came out, Jūzō grinned and, without a word, only shook his head. Kohagi hastened to raise him. To her ear he whispered,

"Only a little while. I want to lie down. Won't you lend me this house a space?"

The voice was low, but he spoke it wholly composed. In spite of herself Kohagi said,

"Yes."

—and having answered, she reddened. That her face clouded at once afterward was, no doubt, from bewilderment at how to deal with this turn of things.

Without even calling a maid, Kohagi lowered her own shoulder, laid Jūzō's right arm across it, and barely got him inside the shoji. Blood and rain seeped, before her eyes, into her gay kosode.

"Wait a little, if you please. I'll make ready a change of clothes."

"No need for niceties."

Jūzō waved a curt hand. Kohagi, not pressing against him, said,

"Very well. But not here—it's too much. I'll spread you a bed."

"No need for that either. Ninja that I am, this bare floor will do. I mean to sleep."

Jūzō pushed Kohagi off and lay down where he was, with a roll. But after he had shut his eyes, opening them again a little as if something had struck him, he said,

"The drug has left my whole body numb. Once I fall into sleep, my body won't be mine to command. After I sleep, do with it as you like—take me, kill me, either. Once I wake and you repent of it, you'll no longer have me in hand."

Jūzō slept like the dead.

Kohagi drew the candle-stand near and sat beside Jūzō, and looked him over from head to foot. On the back of the sleeveless haori there was one faint mark where a blade had grazed it; on the hakama, two places seemingly torn on a tree-stump; and from the right shoulder down across the back the blood had soaked and stained through—all of it telling of the savagery of the fight this man had cut his way out of tonight.

Kohagi took up the sword Jūzō had flung down beside him and quietly drew it clear of the sheath. From the base of the blade to the point there remained a trace of grease, drawn like clouds.

(For an Iga man he was said to be one who rarely cut anyone—)

Whose was this grease? Kohagi narrowed her eyes with the air of one guessing at it. But then a thought struck her, and she laid a hand on the right hem of Jūzō's hakama. She drew it up, so, to about where the loincloth showed. The dusky, gleaming skin was fouled with blood and mud, and clothed swollen flesh that looked ready to burst; but when she had tucked the hakama as far as the join of the thigh, Kohagi caught her breath. Over a stretch the size of a copper coin the flesh was gouged and split, and blood still welled and washed the skin. To that wound Kohagi brought her face softly near. Smelling it, she found a strange odor like the sweet scent of osmanthus flowers. It was the numbing drug the ninja of Iga and Kōga use, the one called kasuri.

(So that's it—)

By this she knew the man was a ninja. And she could all but guess whom this man had fought tonight. Dōgen, or else the man they called Gohei, of Iga: the clouding of the blade was one or the other.

Kohagi raised her face, took up the hand-bell from the little Nigatsu-dō altar-stand beside her, and for the first time called for someone.

After a while the old woman Kusu, as ever, came into the room. Glancing at the strange state of the room, she showed no great surprise. This one had come with Kohagi from the Mochizuki house of Kōga, her former foster-home. Kusu, tilting her head quietly, said,

"Something?"

"Have we shōchū? And medicine for a sword-wound as well."

"Yes. And a change of clothes?"

"That too, if you would."

The old woman nodded without a word and withdrew.

Before long she came back bearing the things she had been bidden bring.

"Thank you. You may go."

"But in this state, surely I should help you."

"No. I have some thinking to do, and I'll manage alone. Keep to your room until I call."

"Yes."

The old woman set the things down where they would not tip and drew back on her knees, expressionless.

Waiting until the fusuma had closed, Kohagi turned again to Jūzō's body and drew up the hakama once more. Pressing the flesh round the wound with the fingers of her left hand, she took up the shōchū jar and poured it, gurgling, into the wound.

Then she took out a kozuka, held the blade in the flame of the candle-stand, wiped off the soot with paper, fixed a thin shred of cotton to the point, and, wetting it again and again with shōchū, worked it into the wound as if boring with an awl. It was to draw out the sand, the mud, the foreign matter from the wound; but with each turn of the kozuka Kohagi's face went pale, and even Jūzō, senseless as he was, writhed ceaselessly, trying to flee the fierce pain.

To stop his stirring, Kohagi suddenly brought her own two knees together and lay across the top of his hips sideways, and, bending down, pressed his right thigh hard with her elbow. Bowed over the wound in an attitude almost of an embrace, she went on with the work of the kozuka. There—a glint at Kohagi's fingertips as the kozuka caught the light. Each time, Jūzō groaned in pain. And hearing it, on Kohagi's blanched cheek would rise a cruel smile—strange, and, by another way of seeing it, one that might be taken for rapture.

Kohagi felt a dark desire in the work of the kozuka's point set into the flesh of Jūzō. That there was in her, in the woman of her, some inclination not of the common run, Kohagi had long been aware. It came, she supposed, of the Kōga training that had violently disciplined her heart and body from her infancy, and there were times when she thought of herself with sorrow.

Presently she cast the kozuka aside and opened the lid of a clamshell the old woman had brought. Within was packed the black wound-salve handed down in Kōga. She warmed it over the lamp, and when it had melted to the thickness of mud, poured it into the mouth of Jūzō's wound. After that, she bound it hard with bleached cloth.

(Truly, this time you were caught off your guard—)

The dressing done, Kohagi drew a breath of relief and, pinching in her lower lip, gave a soft laugh. She was amused that Jūzō—whom ordinarily she felt to be huge as a mountain—should, off his guard, have taken such a wound. Laughing herself out, Kohagi grew abruptly grave and laid a hand on the knot of Jūzō's hakama.

The dressing of a ninja is contrived so cunningly that sash and cords come undone at the touch of a single point.

Kohagi loosed the hakama and undid the sash. In no time the kosode, the undergarments and the rest were stripped away, and the naked form of Tsuzura Jūzō, like a great pine felled, lay sprawled spread-eagle on the boards of clear-grained cypress.

When Kohagi laid a hand at last on the loincloth, a shade of hesitation did, after all, come between her brows. But she softly lowered her lashes and, eyes shut, touched it with her fingers alone, and began slowly to unfasten it.

Presently, with new-bleached cloth from which a clean fragrance rose, she began to wipe Jūzō's whole body over, slowly, as if caressing it.

When she had finished, she passed a fresh loincloth about Jūzō's hips. Next she began to dress him in the clothes made ready. Cradling his shoulders as she dressed him, she called the old woman. To the old woman who came in,

"Make ready a bed."

—she said, turning her back.

"In which room shall I lay it?"

"That's—"

Kohagi had not in fact given it a thought. Seeing Kohagi pause and stop her hands to consider, the old woman put the back of a hand to her mouth and laughed, as if it were the most amusing thing.

"For that gentleman, it would be either the guest hall—or the cell."

"What?"

"Which will you have? You need only do as it pleases you, my lady."

"And you—what would you do?"

"The cell."

"For what reason?"

"Do not hide it from me. That gentleman is Tsuzura Jūzō of Iga, is he not."

"Just so—Sōkyū's messenger."

"I know it well, this one."

The old woman's way of naming him had changed—from that gentleman, to this one.

"Did my lady not fail to take him at Yoshino? If tonight we make the most of this sorry state of his, cage him, and hand him over as proof of Sōkyū's plot, then the work the Jibu-no-shō, Lord Ishida, entrusted to us will come to its happy end. Were it mine to decide, I'd put him in the cell. —But."

Once the old woman's tongue was loosed, there seemed no stopping it.

"But what?"

"My lady has given herself to this man, has she not."

At that, Kohagi clapped a hand over her mouth. Her eyes had flushed red. That, of all things, she surely had never told even this old woman of Kōga, who had reared her from childhood. The old woman said it again.

"You have, have you not."

"Such a thing—it's not for you to press."

Kohagi, for all that, glared at the old woman with real venom.

The old woman had set herself, from Kohagi's childhood, in something like the place of a mother to her. It was for that, half-ignoring Kohagi's sulk, that she said,

"I must press it. To cage this man, and tomorrow morning send word to the Jibu-no-shō and present him—that, given your standing, is self-evident. There's no cause now, at this late hour, to waver over it. And yet—"

The old woman laughed a little mischievously.

"My lady has given herself to this man. It's a waste of breath, surely, to ask my counsel about disposing of him. Do as your heart bids."

But even as she spoke as if the thing were settled, the old woman kept, in the depths of her smile, a coldness that chilled the blood. This one, too, was of Kōga. From the time she was reared she had, no doubt, been trained to a pitilessness toward men. What thought she hid at the bottom of her smile, even Kohagi had times she could not read. Now, for instance, was such a time.

"And if I have given myself—what of it, do you say?"

For a woman like Kohagi, the one being in all the world before whom she could safely sulk was this old woman. —The old woman looked on Kohagi's sulking as if satisfied by it, and, letting a smile all the softer curl her lips, said,

"A man you've once given yourself to is as good as a husband. Love, they say, is a woman's whole life. I too, though it's long ago now, am not wholly a stranger to it. Will my lady, now, die for such a woman's whole life—or else?"

"Or else?"

"Live for the mission of a Kōga ninja? Ho, ho—this may seem unkind of me, but it's a diverting spectacle. Will you make it the guest hall, or the cell? I press you, I know—but truly, either will do very well."

Having said it, as if the matter were done, the old woman shut her mouth and let the smile go. She was a small woman. Sitting with her thin knees together, as if she had struck root there, she began to fix Kohagi with a steady stare. Meeting that stare, Kohagi shuddered in spite of herself. She had known well from childhood the dread of those eyes. Ever since she was a little girl beginning her disciplines in the Mochizuki house of Kōga, this woman had always sat beside her while the ninja of the household drilled her, and each time she idled or blundered—

"My lady—"

—she would speak just one word. Not a scolding, not a chastisement. Only, with her eyes, she would fix her quietly. And from those not-large eyes the pupils would vanish, and two whitely gleaming holes would freeze Kohagi through the breast to the very skin of her back.

"Forgive me, old nurse."

She would blurt it out. And then the pupils would return to the woman's eyes, and her face would come back to the warm, smiling look that Kohagi loved, the old look. From the woman's young days Kohagi had called her nurse. She had been raised on the warm eyes of the woman, from her swaddling-days; yet why that same human eye should sometimes turn to that freezing white, Kohagi even now did not well understand. But it was, one may say, wholly that freezing white eye of the woman's that had reared the woman called Kohagi into a Kōga ninja.

The old woman, holding her off, was leaving Kohagi to choose the answer herself. Under that fixed white gaze, the reflexes of her girlhood came flooding back all at once; her body-warmth froze, and the flesh beneath her skin trembled in small shivers. This night, though, Kohagi did not, as before, ride along with the old woman's white intimidation. Barely, Kohagi too fixed her own stare on the two holes in the old woman's face, and by that held herself up.

"What will you do?"

The old woman said.

"Nothing at all."

Kohagi answered sullenly, and, drawing back her white chin with a jerk,

"I'll not forever take your orders as I did in childhood. Kohagi is twenty-one. My prime as a woman is passing. To do as I please—what harm in it? Make ready the bed, and quickly."

"You seem a little distraught. It was only to ask in which room, that I put the question—was it not."

"That's decided. This man is Kohagi's dear one. And so—"

"The guest hall, then."

"No. —Carry him to my own room."

"What—"

The old woman made a show of surprise, but her face was blank with the sheer effrontery of it.

"Quickly."

"You must not. What do you take this man for?"

"I know well."

"Clever of you. You'd bed down with your enemy, then?"

"Kohagi wishes to behave as an ordinary woman. Why must I be reared a Kōga, and used as a tool in others' plots? This night above all, Kohagi hates her own fate. I am your mistress. Cross my orders and it will not go lightly with you."

"Such a fine, high temper. Well—repent of it your fill, later."

The old woman rose, grudging every inch.

They laid a bed in Kohagi's room, and the two women between them lifted Jūzō's body and moved it to the bedding.

"Withdraw."

"No need to tell me."

The old woman began to slide the fusuma shut, but, thinking perhaps she had said too much, stopped her hand halfway and said,

"May you be happy together."

—and opened a warm smile, like one made over into another person. There is, after all, something of the shape-shifter about this old woman.

What remained were the senseless Jūzō and Kohagi. That night she sat up nursing him into the small hours; the next day too Jūzō lay all but comatose; and it was only on the day after that the two of them lay together.

At the dead of the night of that second day, Jūzō woke suddenly, aware that his fingers were being toyed with in the bed. At the same moment he found Kohagi lying beside him.

Kohagi had laid Jūzō's right elbow on her own breast and was playing with his fingers as with a doll—crooking one, straightening it out, at times taking it lightly between her teeth to bite. It was the manner of a little girl. That there should be so pretty a thing even in this woman, Jūzō found somehow painful, and he bore the toying. And as he bore it, a vivid tenderness stained this man's heart. The tenderness presently dripped down into his blood. It became a torrent, and drove him with a longing to flood it fiercely into her too.

"Kohagi."

"Yes?"

—and it was rather Kohagi who opened her eyes wide, clear, like one just waking.

"You've woken?"

"How long did I sleep?"

"Two whole days. —And your wound?"

"This is the world of the living, I take it."

"Why?"

"Neither taken nor killed."

"Did you wish to be taken?"

Jūzō did not answer that; he only gazed steadily at Kohagi, and said,

"I was dreaming."

"Of what?"

"A dream of stealing into Fushimi Castle. It was broad noon. Luckily I fooled the eyes of the day-watch and walked straight in at the great gate. The lay of the ground within the walls I have by heart. I waited in a miso-store for night to fall, stole into Hideyoshi's mansion on the east side of the main keep, and laid my hand on the fusuma of the room where, I believed, Hideyoshi surely slept. The chill of the metal fitting is still on this finger. And at that moment I was seized, all at once, by a feeling that the whole thing was a weariness. Not only the killing of the Taikō. To have stolen into that room, even to slide the fusuma open, grew irksome to me. What am I for, what am I doing, whose is my whole life, and what, after all, has it ever been—things I never in the ordinary way so much as thought of came flooding into my breast at that pass. And so I could not advance a step. Nor could I retreat. I stood, my hand on the metal fitting, and it seemed to me I would rot on my feet in that very posture. And then, for some reason, I remember your face rose in my mind and faded, rose and faded. When I think of the emptiness of my life, it came upon me, obstinately, that you alone were the one certain thing. A ninja's life is, after all, a hollow show. Would it not have been a man's happiness, I bitterly repented, to live idle and at ease? I stood with my finger stuck to the metal of the fusuma, unable to move. The finger that touched the metal grew, at last, hot. I woke, suddenly. And there you were, in the same bed as I. It's a strange feeling."

When he had finished, Jūzō drew Kohagi's body to him with a fierce strength. And to her ear he whispered.

"You'll be my woman?"

"—Yes."

Kohagi, as if her breath were failing, buried her face in Jūzō's breast.

It was the next morning that Jūzō became aware of the strangeness of his own heart. That he had dreamed such a dream, and, confiding it to Kohagi, let his heart be crushed by the sweetness of love, was surely because his strength had been much worn down. Jūzō's strength was recovered now. His vigor had filled out again to the point where it could support his everyday deceptions and the hollow show of his spirit. When strength ebbs, it will, at times, wring out a true cry.

Jūzō thought himself off his guard. With bitter regret he looked at the sleeping face of Kohagi, lying beside him. He could hear even her breathing. What he found irksome was not directed at Kohagi. The carcass of his own true self lay now breathing peacefully in sleep. Jūzō's displeasure was bound up in that. Now that the fresh, hollow spirit had come alive again within him, the sense of having been off his guard had begun to send up a black smoke of remorse.

Jūzō tried quietly to slip from the bed. Kohagi, keen-sensed, woke. In that eye there still lingered, irksomely, the true fire Jūzō had kindled the night before, and he could not let it pass unseen.

"Where are you going?"

"To the privy."

"That's a lie."

Kohagi tugged at Jūzō's sleeve.

"You did the same once before, at Otogi Pass. It's no good thing, to make sport of a woman's true heart."

"To the privy."

"That eye of yours."

Kohagi rose swiftly and knelt upright on the bedding.

"You are repenting."

"Something of that kind, yes."

"Is Kohagi, then, so hateful to you? Was your tenderness of last night no true thing?"

"Last night was last night, this morning is this morning. A ninja's heart is, like cloud, no settled thing. I can play at love, but it seems I cannot love. When I look at you, my own heart turns sour. And I cannot bear to go on showing that self of mine before your eyes for long. There is a lingering pull; but for all that—I go."

"I'll not stop you."

Kohagi swiftly altered her bearing. There is in a woman her own point of honor. She answered to Jūzō's heart. From her eyes, as if wiped away, the lingering feeling of the night before was gone.

"I, too, find it hateful to see Lord Jūzō so."

"That's as it should be."

In unbroken silence Jūzō changed into the new clothes Kohagi had made ready. Kohagi, too, silently helped him. Only, as he was leaving the room, he drew his short sword sheath and all with a sudden movement, and—

"An unsigned Awataguchi. It may serve as a keepsake, should it come to that."

—and set it into Kohagi's hand. Kohagi took it up in both her sleeves and gazed steadily into Jūzō's eyes.

Jūzō looked away. And he sprang from the wet veranda down into the garden and, without so much as a backward glance, walked off toward the brushwood gate. Presently Jūzō's broad back grew small, and vanished beyond the hedge where the sun had gathered down.

Kohagi thought, of a sudden, that Hideyoshi was these days in residence within Fushimi Castle. Him this man would kill. In the tread of the departing Jūzō there had been, surely, the uncanny breath of a living thing walking toward death—so now, too late, she could call it to mind. From that Kohagi's hand the short sword of Jūzō slipped. The sword fell to the boards with a violent sound, the koiguchi parting of itself, and came to rest at the edge of the wet veranda. Onto the little white sliver of steel that showed, the morning sun gathered quietly.
