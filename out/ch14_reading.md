## Chapter 4. Beset on Three Sides, Ever Forward

### 「Fortress Firm, Meeting Challenge from Every Side」

What this chapter sets down is the action itself.

When we flew across the passes and mountains and crossed far over the seas to carry out the Wang case, we did so, of course, under orders, purely in the public interest and with nothing of private motive in it; there was in it not the least particle of grudge or grievance. Yet it does no harm to make a supposition. Had we borne no "military standing," nor held any "public office," but were merely plain civilians, then, at Wang Jingwei's conduct in selling out our nation's interests and playing cat's-paw to the tiger, we too, stirred by righteous indignation, would have stepped forward to rid the country of a traitor. This is no idle boast spoken to flatter myself; what I mean is this: Wang's crimes were such that any man might rightfully put him to death; and our own image, too, ought to be that of hot-blooded youths and patriots of high purpose, and by no means to be twisted into that of murderous ruffians or professional killers.

Carrying out the Wang case fell into three phases. At first it was confined to keeping watch on his movements; after that, it entered upon the preparations before action; and only when the sanction order came down was the final decision made at last. The first phase ran as long as two and a half months, which shows that the authorities of the government still hoped he might repent while there was yet time. The second phase had about ten days of preparation, and may also be taken as an important pointer from Mr. Dai in his direction of the work. The last phase was but a single day; we were first pressed by circumstance and then carried away by our own agitation, and in that span we let the chance slip twice over, so that in truth we can hardly shirk the blame for having bungled it.

By rights, with a fairly long time for preparation, with ample support from the rear, and with a body of seventeen or eighteen men mobilized—and among these many able and gifted, four or five returned students trained in a special field, three or four field-grade officers who had come out of the military academies, three or four police officers schooled in special-police work, two or three men risen from the greenwood and possessed of some consummate skill, and besides these a good number more who lent voluntary help or worked their connections—with conditions so advantageous and a lineup so formidable, to bring the mission smoothly to its end ought to have been a thing to be had for the reaching, light and easy; and yet it failed!

Then where did the failure lie? Where lay the fault? Through the whole course of the operation there is a close and exact account, and an even-handed reckoning has been given.
