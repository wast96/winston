## 5

The vast reach of the Xiangfu marsh was like the sea, boundless and yet unruffled. Old Mo brought Yanzhi through two changes of small boat before someone led her up onto a reed-grown shoal. The reeds had all withered now and rustled lifeless in the wind. Zhu Qi wore a satin long-gown with a black woolen overcoat thrown over it and cradled a water-pipe in his hand. He stood beneath a reed shelter like a rich landlord on his own ground, watching Yanzhi be led all the way up to him. Zhu Qi said, why have you got yourself up like a man? At the moment she stepped off the boat Yanzhi had gone into a daze, not knowing where she was, and it was a good while before she came to as if startled awake, lifted the little packet of money in her hand, and said, I've come to ransom someone.

Zhu Qi nodded and raised a hand toward a boat-house not far off.

Pushing open the door of the boat-house, Yanzhi found it was the bandits' storehouse, though more like a general store, with everything one could want inside. All the way there she had pictured Baosheng trussed hand and foot like every ransom-captive, blindfolded, a rag stuffed in his mouth. But no. Baosheng sat before a bright gasoline lamp, stitching thread by thread on a piece of pale-green chiffon. The lamplight threw his huge profile on the wall.

That he still had the heart for needlework. Only when Yanzhi came close did she see it clearly: what he was sewing was a sleeveless cheongsam. Baosheng lifted his head, and on his face was a look on the edge of weeping, gone in an instant. He cast his eyes to a point behind her.

Zhu Qi had come, she did not know when, to stand behind Yanzhi. He asked, how far along?

“It's summer already,” Baosheng said.

Zhu Qi nodded. “No hurry,” he said. “Take your time.”

Yanzhi kept her eyes on Baosheng without a flicker. But Baosheng lowered his lids and, feigning calm, drew the needle through, though his hand would not obey him: the needle stabbed into the web of his thumb, and a drop of bright red blood opened like a plum-blossom on the pale-green chiffon. Yet it seemed to be Yanzhi the sting went through; she whipped her head round and looked Zhu Qi full in the face. Zhu Qi gave a laugh and said to Baosheng, tell her what you're doing. Baosheng bowed his head and did not stir. Zhu Qi let out a slow mouthful of smoke and said again, are you deaf?

Only then did Baosheng raise his head, look woodenly at Yanzhi, and murmur, this is your bridal gown.

That very night Yanzhi went to bed with Zhu Qi. Every woman who came here, willing or not, had to sleep with Zhu Qi, and then with his men, and then be taken back by two changes of small boat to the place she had come from, carrying with her the person or the goods she had ransomed. This was the water bandits' rule. In Zhu Qi's own words it was called plucking a feather from every passing goose. This time, though, was different. When Zhu Qi had rolled off her he showed a strange tenderness, a strange clinging. He held Yanzhi close, one arm pillowed beneath her, the five fingers of his other hand spread and pushed into her short hair, combing it stroke by stroke. Zhu Qi put his lips to Yanzhi's ear and said, I'm going to marry you. But Yanzhi seemed to be asleep. Zhu Qi shook her and said it again — did you hear me, I'm going to make you my wife. Only then did Yanzhi open her eyes and look at him, saying nothing. There seemed to be the shine of tears in them. Zhu Qi sighed, and the hand in her hair slid down to her neck and kneaded it softly. He closed his eyes and said, as if to himself, surely you're not meaning to wait till you're a widow before you'll marry me?

A month later, eighteen cheongsams for the four seasons of the year hung in a row in the storehouse. When Zhu Qi had looked them over like a general reviewing his troops, he turned to Baosheng and said, good, you can go. Baosheng did not move, but turned his head to look at Yanzhi standing in the doorway. Yanzhi was wrapped in a black woolen coat, and the sunlight, reflected in off the face of the lake, played wavering over her face. Your goods are all on the boat, Zhu Qi said. Still Baosheng did not move; he narrowed his eyes as though straining to find something in Yanzhi's face. Zhu Qi swept a hand round the room and went on, take whatever you can carry from here, help yourself.

He doesn't want to go — he wants to stay here the rest of his life. Yanzhi spoke up all at once, slow and unhurried, drawing the coat tight about her and walking toward the door.

Then set up a tailor shop by the lake and make grave-clothes for the drowned. Zhu Qi's laughter came from behind her.

Yanzhi leaned against the door-frame and watched Baosheng come out, bent-backed, walking both urgent and slow, as though the eighteen cheongsams had drained the strength of his whole life. Slowly Yanzhi put out a hand from the coat and held the packet of money to Baosheng. Go home and live well, Yanzhi said. Baosheng opened his mouth; he saw in Yanzhi's eyes a mist-gray, desolate color, and could not help a shudder, and took the money despite himself. Yanzhi smiled suddenly and said, it's nothing; being alive is better than anything.

Baosheng nodded, gave Yanzhi one last look, and set off toward the lakeside where the boat was moored.

Just then Zhu Qi came out with his hands behind his back, watched Baosheng's retreating figure, and said to Yanzhi, I've looked at the almanac; the day after next is a good day, fit for a wedding.

But three days later, what came sooner than the wedding was the Japanese soldiers. The moment Baosheng reached the town he carried the packet of money to Young Master Tang, and Young Master Tang led him into the Xiushui Elementary School where the Japanese were garrisoned. To get his wife back Baosheng cared for nothing else. It was dusk now, the dying sun along the way like blood, but Baosheng's face was corpse-pale. He bit hard on his lip, yet when he saw the sentry at the gate the corner of his mouth twitched all the same. Young Master Tang gave him a slap and said, what are you afraid of? Straighten your back!

Baosheng caught Young Master Tang by the sleeve and asked, ever so timidly, will the Japanese really act for me?

Taijun. Young Master Tang said, remember, you say taijun.

Taijun. Baosheng gave a hard nod and said, but what if the taijun won't help?

Young Master Tang was displeased. “Then go back,” he said. “There's still time to turn around.”

Baosheng thought a moment and said, I won't go back; I'll get her out if it kills me.

Young Master Tang laughed. “Then why dawdle?” he said. “In you go.”

In fact Baosheng never saw the Japanese captain at all; the moment he was through the gate of the Xiushui Elementary School he was taken into a room and locked up. The sky outside the window darkened bit by bit; Baosheng was frantic, but dared not cry out and dared not move, and suddenly he thought of the thirteen men buried under the playground, and it was as if a hand had closed on his heart and he could not draw breath. Baosheng slid down into the corner and huddled there with his eyes wide open.

Before it was light a Japanese soldier suddenly opened the door, and Young Master Tang came in behind him holding a flashlight, waved a hand, and said, let's go.

Baosheng's eyes stung terribly; he looked at him and said, how could you do me such harm.

Who's got the time to harm you? Young Master Tang waved a hand again and said, hurry up, the taijun is waiting for you to lead the way.

Baosheng followed Young Master Tang and trailed a squad of Japanese soldiers aboard a small steam-launch. The morning fog had not yet lifted when the boat, running along the Shili channel, entered the Xiangfu marsh.

On the boat Young Master Tang asked Baosheng of a sudden, do you know what I told the taijun?

Baosheng shook his head. Young Master Tang said with a smiling squint, I said the guerrillas hated me for being a traitor and so had seized my third concubine.

Baosheng leaped up. How can you talk such rot?

Young Master Tang said quickly, keep your voice down; if I hadn't said guerrillas, would the taijun turn out in such force?

Baosheng said, but you can't call her your third concubine — where would you come by a third concubine?

Wasn't I thinking ahead? If the Japanese soldiers took a fancy to your sister-in-law on the spot, then what? So saying, Young Master Tang turned his head and glanced into the cabin. Look, he said, all these men.

Baosheng shut his mouth and, looking at the many Japanese soldiers sitting cross-legged in the cabin, was at a loss all at once. Young Master Tang gave a little laugh and clapped his shoulder. Don't worry, he said; the taijun will grant me that much face at least.

All morning Baosheng kept his lips tight shut, his eyes fixed unblinking on the far place where water met sky. As noon drew near the sun blazed on the water, and the Japanese captain could hold his temper no longer. He gave a great shout of bakayarō, kicked Baosheng sprawling on the deck, and drew his saber to lay it against his neck. Young Master Tang hurried up, not daring to stop him, only waving his hands over and over: this won't do, taijun, if you kill him, where are we to find the guerrillas? So saying, he dropped to his knees beside Baosheng, seized him, and shook him hard. Do you remember the route or not? he said. You mustn't drag me down with you.

It was under that blazing blade that Baosheng saw the cooking-smoke in the distance. And at that hour, on Zhu Qi's shoal, two great cauldrons stood over the fire, the water already boiling, and a pig with its throat cut squealed free of its bindings and ran into the reeds, scattering blood the whole way. But no one had a thought to spare for the pig; the water bandits already had their weapons in hand, and every one of them took the launch coming from far off for a gift sent down by Heaven. Zhu Qi stood in the wind at the door of the house, cast a last glance at the black smoke drawn out in a thread, and said to the brothers, do it well; with this launch, come the new year we can go up to the county town and do big business. So saying, he took the great red flower from his chest, tucked the hem of his black satin gown up into his belt, took the fowling-piece Old Mo handed him, and turned to shout to Yanzhi inside the house, wait for me to come back and be wed!

Zhu Qi waved a hand at them all and said again, damn it, today's a fine day, damn it — joy come doubled. But before the time it takes to eat a meal was out, the fight before the shoal ended in a rout of the water bandits. They left seven or eight bodies behind and fled headlong into the reeds like a flock of startled wild ducks. But the Japanese soldiers did not give chase; they set fire to the reeds and the boats, raked the inside with machine-guns, and then fell to plundering. The Japanese carried off everything in the houses to the launch, then put all the houses to the torch as well. Baosheng and Young Master Tang shouted Yanzhi's name in the raging fire and searched everywhere. But all they saw was the pillar of flame that leaped up when a roof caved in.

But more dreadful still were those eyes. Baosheng had just darted out of a burning doorway when a hand suddenly seized his ankle, and he sat down hard on the ground and saw, in that mangled bloody face, a pair of eyes all but falling out of their lidless sockets. The man propped half his body up with his other hand, and when he opened his mouth the blood spurted like water from the seven orifices of his head and spattered Baosheng's trouser-leg. Baosheng, in an agony of terror, struggled wildly on the ground, but the man's hand was like a ghost's claw, gripping his ankle fast, as though to drag him down into hell, and struggle as he might Baosheng could not tear free of it. Mercifully the man breathed his last soon after, glaring at Baosheng to the very moment he died.

The Japanese captain, his battle won, was greatly pleased; he threw an arm round Baosheng's shoulder and, thumb up, said three times over: yoshi. But Baosheng stood dumb as a wooden chicken, shuddering without pause like a child just startled awake from a nightmare. Young Master Tang hurried up, took hold of him, and said, aren't you going to thank the taijun.

Baosheng looked at Young Master Tang, then at the Japanese captain, and all at once burst out wailing. Young Master Tang quickly kicked him to the ground and said to the captain with a grin, he's frightened, frightened; he's never seen so grand an affair.

The Japanese captain looked at Baosheng sprawled weeping on the ground, nodded, and said, yoshi.
