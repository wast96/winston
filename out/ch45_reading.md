## Chapter 45

It was near daybreak when I got home; we had agreed to talk again at Bai Ping's place at half past twelve the next night. This would be our last gathering before the masked ball: all that was undecided must be decided at it, all that ought to be thought of must be thought of at it, and all that had been weighed must be brought up for discussion at it.

On the twelfth of March I woke at twelve noon, took a bath, ate a little, and my heart was uneasy all the while; I could not take in a book, could do nothing at all. After supper I went alone to see a film, which of course could rouse no interest in me, but by it I did at last pass the time to the hour of the appointment.

When I reached Bai Ping's place it was probably only about eleven forty-five, and I thought Mei Yingzi could not yet have come, and Bai Ping perhaps not yet returned. But I had made up my mind to wait for them, and so had not planned to kill any time outside. When Ah Mei opened the door I did not even ask whether Bai Ping was in, but went straight through; and once inside, I saw the door of Bai Ping's bedroom standing open, Bai Ping in a gray cloth gown seated on the sofa, playing with the cat. The electric stove in the room was warm, the light very dim, only the yellow-silk, silver-flowered floor lamp behind her alight; she seemed to have come back early and to have been sitting a long while at her ease. Seeing me, she said, not quite naturally:

"Sit here."

I stepped into her bedroom, and only then did she set Jimi down slowly on the carpet and look up to watch me come in and sit down beside her. She said:

"You seem very ill at ease today."

"Has Mei Yingzi not come yet?" I asked.

"Rest a little first." She showed the smile of a lily just opening. "Are you cold?"

"Not too bad," I said.

"Will you have a cup of hot coffee first?"

"Yes, thank you."

So she rose and went out to give the order to Ah Mei. Meanwhile I lit a cigarette, and when she came back she said:

"You have not slept well, I see."

"I slept very well," I said flatly, not knowing why this atmosphere so wore on my patience; only afterward, thinking of it, I felt that what made me so fretful was not a question of good or bad, but that the atmosphere did not answer to what I had looked for, so that it could be called a disappointment.

"Nervous!" Bai Ping murmured, as if in mockery.

"Nonsense," I said stiffly. "You ought not to insult me."

"Your nerves seem to have been strung tight all along, and your temper is out of sorts too."

"Do not tell me whether I am well or ill," I said. "I am in no mood to joke with you. Tomorrow night is our work; ought we not to have a proper, serious conference today?"

"Only by being wholly at leisure in the tensest hour, and tasting the deepest humor in the gravest, can one meet every hard problem with ease to spare." Bai Ping fondled again the Jimi that had jumped onto her knee, her eyes on the back of her own hand as she said: "To be like you—that, meeting a thing, you cannot even eat, cannot sleep, feel no want of any pleasure or comfort—why, let several weighty matters press on you one after another, and your nerves would break down on the instant!"

"I am in no mood to talk it over with you," I said. "I think this is each person's own temper, and we need not discuss it; what we ought to discuss is…"

"Is tomorrow's work, I know," she said. "My friend, yesterday I asked you whether there was any problem, and you said you knew it all; today you want to talk again—so, talk; how would you talk of it?"

"This is strange indeed; was not today's meeting of your own appointing?" I said. "If there were nothing to discuss today, I should have gone off to enjoy myself instead."

"Can we not talk of something else?" Bai Ping said, showing the smile of a lily just opening. "For instance—your work tomorrow goes wrong, you are found out by the enemy, you are seized, you are put to torture, you die: have you then nothing to talk of?"

Bai Ping's tone was calm and light, but I felt she was simply threatening me, and I grew a little angry, and said:

"Unless you are a defeatist, Bai Ping, then you make light of me as unequal to tomorrow's work."

"But this is reality, my dear," said Bai Ping. "Who, before work so hard as this, can have an absolute certainty?"

"I can, I can…" I said hotly; but at the same moment I was conscious that I was indeed, below thought, shrinking from the fearful outcome she had named—I feared to hear it, feared to think of it—and I felt a shame and a dejection, and for a while had no word. Then Bai Ping looked at me and said:

"You who study philosophy—that you cannot, after all, see through this life."

But I avoided her gaze; I felt oppressed. I rose, went to the door, and switched on the ceiling light; the room was all at once bright. Mastering my restlessness, I said, more calmly:

"Do you really think I am afraid? You are wrong; I only feel oppressed—your manner, this atmosphere… Why has Mei Yingzi still not come?"

"Mei Yingzi? Tonight she has gone to Umetake's to make the arrangements; she is not coming," said Bai Ping, easily. "Have you something to say to her?"

"No," I said.

"Then it is as well she is not coming," said Bai Ping. "I can talk with you alone."

"I have nothing to talk of with you either; I only wished to see you both, that is all."

"But I have something to talk of with you," she said. "Are you meaning to go to Beiping with Helen?"

"Yes," I said. "But that is a thing there is no talking of yet."

Ah Mei brought in the coffee, with cake; Bai Ping then poured the coffee for me, and said:

"I have long hoped you would give your whole mind to your own study; how ill-suited to you is this work here, now."

"Yes," I said, in a grateful tone. "But I do not know whether the Beiping of today can let me settle to my study in peace?"

"That lies wholly with yourself," said Bai Ping serenely. "I think that if you leave this world, you will be able to find your own world."

I did not answer, but drank my coffee and ate a little of the cake. Then Bai Ping went on, in a gentle tone:

"Every person's life belongs to some one world; to leave that world is a spending without recompense, a waste. As tomorrow—if this one venture should lose you, then all your plans of sowing hereafter, and the flowers you should open and the fruit you should set, would be utterly gone."

"Of course," I said; "but is not tomorrow night's work, too, a flower I should open and a fruit I should set?"

"It is not a flower you should open, nor a fruit you should set," said Bai Ping quietly. "It is what I have sown; so, if you will not take it that I make light of you, could not tomorrow's work be carried out by me instead?"

I was startled a moment, and felt an indescribable awkwardness; but by some force I know not I held my temper down. I was clearly conscious that this was an insult, and clearly conscious too of the tenderness and goodness in Bai Ping's tone; I was silent a good while, and then said:

"Is this Mei Yingzi's meaning, or yours?"

"It is hers and mine both."

"Is it that you so distrust my being equal to this work?"

"I feel that, at the least, it is rather that I value too highly your talent on that other side, and my hopes of you."

"That is to say, on this side you make far too light of me."

"I feel that you are really not worth the running of this risk."

"And if you were the one to do it, would it not be a risk?"

"My life has grown up in just such risks; I look on it as a common thing, and shall not be tense, or afraid, or anxious and uneasy…"

"You mean that I am afraid?" My voice rose without my knowing it.

"What is wrong with being afraid? Everyone is afraid of what he is not used to. Being afraid does not mean one is a coward. I am afraid of the life in the trenches under gunfire, but the soldier in the trenches under gunfire is afraid of the plight I am in now; we are often afraid, too, when we go to meet a stranger; yet your familiar friend might make me afraid, and my familiar friend might make you afraid. Some are afraid on a mountain path, some are afraid sailing the open sea; some fear crowds, some fear solitude, some fear ghosts, some fear trouble; some think stabbing a man a risk, and some think that far less dreadful than being forced to drink down a bowl of cold, unboiled water. Some fear the sight of a long mathematical formula, some fear to hear classical music; some say they would sooner sit two days in jail than sit two hours at a concert of classical music. So when I say you are afraid, is that again to make light of you?" Bai Ping spoke gravely and evenly, forever keeping her eyes turned from mine; and at last she gazed into my eyes and said, low: "My friend—for the work's sake, for your own sake—give tomorrow night's work over to me to do; will you not?"

"No," I said. "This was settled by drawing lots; I think there is no need to talk of it today."

"It is because we are friends, and because this work is so important."

Bai Ping's manner was very composed, as if she were treating with me over some grave question, and yet as if uttering her views quite carelessly. I felt weary, I could truly bear no more of this pressure; I stood up, and, blowing smoke, walked away from my seat, and said in an attacking tone:

"So you are afraid that if I fail at the work I shall drag you down with me."

"More than that," said Bai Ping coolly: "the whole work, and the whole organization."

"Good, then I give it over to you," I said angrily.

"Truly?" Bai Ping stood up in excitement. "Thank you. Now we can leave this matter and talk of something else, of pleasant things."

"Then what of my work?"

"You," said Bai Ping, as if in jest, "you dance merrily with me."

"What kind of talk is this?" I said angrily. "So you have been making light of me like this all along?"

"If you count me your good friend," said Bai Ping, her tone turning very tender, "you ought not to think in this way."

"No," I said. "Bai Ping, we are good friends, true; but in this matter we are only fellow-workers. Your words may be taken as a friend's tender care, but they may also be taken as your striving for the credit; in friendship I may take them as the depth of your hopes for me on that other side, your prizing of my talent on that other side; but in the working together on this one task, I can only take them as your contempt for me, and I cannot give up my duty and my right." Bai Ping fell silent; she went quietly, with her back to me, to a farther sofa, and sat down, and I saw that her look had turned grave and deep. At last she said:

"If you truly mean to take on this work, since it fell to you by lot, then of course I have no reason to bid you yield it to me."

"Then good," I said. "I hope you will make no more unreasonable demands of me."

Bai Ping fell silent again, and for a good while said nothing; then suddenly she went to the coffee-table, sat down, and said, with her back to me:

"Then you must consider, a little coolly, the settling of things after your failure."

"You think I must fail?"

"This too may be called only the rule of the work."

"I do not understand the rules," I said. "Teach me everything, and I will do just as you say."

"Have you made a will?"

"No," I said. "I have no need to prepare a will."

"Your family?"

"I need only write a letter to my uncle."

"Then write it," she said. "Write it here, and be done."

So I wrote a letter at her writing-desk. It was a very simple letter, and in less than ten minutes I had it done, and said:

"If by any chance I die, please send someone with it," and as I spoke I put the letter into her drawer.

This letter, simple as it was, was yet like signing a consent form before an operation at the hospital: to my spirit it was a blow; but I mastered myself, went quietly over, dragged up the Jimi from the carpet, and sat down across from Bai Ping. Bai Ping now changed again from her easy manner, and said:

"If you are taken, are you prepared to kill yourself? Or prepared to endure the pain and wait a chance to get out?"

"Must this too be decided beforehand?"

"Of course," said Bai Ping, her eyes on the cat, gently. "If you do not kill yourself, then we must contrive to rescue you."

"Very well, then I will not kill myself."

"But you must keep to one condition: that however cruel the torture you suffer, you must not give up us, or any trace connected with us."

"That, of course."

"You think it very easy to do?"

"If I cannot, I will kill myself after all."

"That is utterly impossible," she said; "for by then you will no longer have the freedom to kill yourself."

"Then do you believe, or not, that I would absolutely never confess?" I asked.

"If you cannot absolutely trust your own self, how can you ask another to trust you?"

"Then how is one to kill oneself?"

"To kill yourself—that must be in the very instant you are seized."

"You think there will be such a chance?"

"Only if you are resolved," said Bai Ping.

"If you truly fear that I might not bear up under the torture and would drag you down," I said, "then I think the way of killing myself is the more convenient."

"Good." Bai Ping rose lightly as she spoke, went to the bedside, and took from the drawer of the lamp-stand a box that had been used for holding letters; she opened it, took out a bottle that had held quinine, and poured from it three pills, wrapping them in a paper. At last she put everything back, and only then brought the little packet of pills over to me, as lightly as if she were handing me a few painkillers of the ordinary sort, and said: "This can spare you all pain."

I took the pills she gave me, and, putting them into the pocket of my waistcoat, said:

"Thank you."

"Now, let us talk of something else." Bai Ping leaned back on the sofa, like one who had finished a piece of work.

But I could find nothing to say, and yet seemed to have something I wanted to say, so I still sat there and did not take my leave. After a few minutes Bai Ping said:

"I never thought you would still understand me so little."

"Just as you do not understand me," I said.

"But I respect your own work; you ought not to give up your work."

"I shall be forever grateful to you, but…"

"But what? My friend, with all my heart's sincerity I beg you—there is still time now for you to give this work over to me. To speak truly, in this work the danger I run is but four parts in ten, and yours is eight; in the chance of success I have six parts and you but two. If I am the right hand of your soul, and you the left, why should you give up what the right hand can do so smoothly, and let the left hand run the risk? You count me far too little your own." Bai Ping's tone was full of grief, and I was indeed wholly moved by her, whether in gratitude or in shame; my nose stung, and my eyes felt a little moist.

"…" I could say nothing.

"Listen to me, my friend," said Bai Ping, almost in a tone of entreaty. "Let me take your place; I shall surely win, and you shall come the morning after to congratulate me."

"No, Bai Ping," I said. "For all you have thought of for me, I thank you. But since I have resolved, after this affair, to go back to my own garden, I must carry this work through; or else I fear even I myself could not make out clearly whether it was for the love of philosophy, or merely out of cowardice and fear of death, that I had given up this task."

Bai Ping began to be silent; she bowed her head, and, as if in thought, drew in the grieved look of that moment. I too said nothing, and this stillness made me feel the emptiness of the universe and the desolation of the night. I rose, paced to the window, and lifted the thick silver plush curtain; the sky was faintly white. I opened the window a little, and a chill air swept in, and I felt easier, and drew a deep breath; I heard faintly the crowing of a distant cock, and thought it must be past four; but I did not look at my watch. I did not close the window, but sat down behind her, patted her shoulder, and said:

"Bai Ping, you may sleep now."

Bai Ping made no sound, and I said again:

"I think I shall go back; I shall probably sleep till two or three in the afternoon. Need I come to see you again?"

"Yes," said Bai Ping. "I shall be home from half past four till five in the afternoon; if you have come to your senses," she rose, and added, "then come to see me; if not, we shall meet over there tonight after all."

"Then I think I shall not come to see you."

"Do not be so hard…" said Bai Ping, stretching out her hand to me. I took her hand and said:

"I shall be grateful all my life for your kindness tonight; but I will never think to answer your kindness with danger to you."

"What kind of talk is this?" Bai Ping let go my hand, a flash of displeasure in her eyes.

I avoided her eyes and said:

"I mean—if I gave this work over to you and you came to harm by it, then do you think I could go on living at peace in this world?"

"And do you think that, when you come to harm, I shall have the face to go on living at peace?"

"This is fate; it was I who drew the lot to take on this work. You have already been good enough to me; for your kind heart tonight, I have no way left to repay you."

"But…"

"No, no more, Bai Ping—goodbye!" I forced down a smile and said: "Perhaps this is the last talk we shall ever have; and, last of all, I beg you to smile at me."

"…" Bai Ping looked at me and did not smile.

"Smile! Set your heart wholly at rest; if by any chance something happens tomorrow, you need not be alarmed, need not fret, nor be afraid, still less think of any rescue for me—for I have already, very gladly, swallowed the 'aspirin' you gave me."

"…" Bai Ping leaned back against the sofa, her head bowed, and made no sound.

"Look at me, Bai Ping!" It was as though it were truly a parting in death, and a grieved feeling colored my entreaty.

Bai Ping raised her head and looked at me gravely.

"Smile at me, Bai Ping!" I did not know whether this was a tone of command or of entreaty; and Bai Ping did indeed smile at me.

She smiled, but it was a bitter, painful smile, and at once she bowed her head again.

"No," I said. "I want your smile like a lily just opening, Bai Ping; forget everything, and, to wish me victory, smile."

"Good—I wish you victory." Bai Ping spoke, roused and resolute, and did indeed let break a bright smile, a smile like a lily just opening; and then she said, slowly: "I wish you victory."

And I saw crystal teardrops rise up amid her smile, like the dew of early morning glittering on a lily.

My nose stung, and I bowed my head under cover of a bow. I said:

"Thank you, Bai Ping."

Turning, I stepped quickly out of the door; I did not look back at her again, but I was conscious that she still sat there, dazed, where she was.
