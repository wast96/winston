### The Woman Physiognomist

The face-reader is one of the trades that ply the roads, and the whole secret of them is to fish for hints from a man's own words and to lay the flattery on thick; those who can truly read the complexion and pronounce on a man's fortunes and misfortunes are, I fear, not one in a hundred. And of all the face-readers, the woman physiognomist is the most laughable and the most contemptible.

They style themselves woman physiognomists, take out advertisements and hand round flyers; and if they cried themselves up with the four words "the art of physiognomy, marvelous," it might pass. But instead they set out the words "a celestial made flesh" — and pray, what has "a celestial made flesh" to do with reading faces? By her own account it is not a bit like reading a man's face; it is of a piece with selling one's charms. And to her customers she is all accommodation: be it in a brothel, a hotel, or a restaurant, one telephone call and she comes tripping at once, bearing her jade person along to instruct you, and, what is more, "the fee for a reading, two dollars, and no extra for the house call." So for the sacrifice of two silver dollars any lecher or idler may feast his fill on the fair looks of this "celestial made flesh," and hear out a stretch of her sweet, soft, cloying-passed-for-charming patter — a rare bargain indeed.

Ever since the murder of the woman physiognomist Wenying last year came to light, the inside workings and the true face of the woman physiognomist's trade have been laid wholly bare, exposed without remainder.

In my old box of papers I still keep a flyer of the woman physiognomist Lingqing, printed in red ink on peach-blossom paper, with a fetching photograph of Lingqing and Wenying together below, and above it these words: "Gentlemen who would inquire of their prospects may call and be received at any hour"; "and there attends besides the wise-hearted, orchid-natured Miss Wenying, skilled to read the palm, her method marvelous and profound, able to turn back the very workings of fate — one look and I warrant you'll come away pleased." Such wording may be called titillating past all bounds, and boastful to the last degree. The cream of it is those last four words, "warrant you'll come away pleased," so full of unspoken meaning; any friend who is out of luck and would come by his pleasure and his heart's content had best hurry along and take his lesson!
