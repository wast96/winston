### Patronage Tickets

The seat-agents of the theatres—they style themselves "the receiving trade"—are the sort of friends who, every year as the festival season nears, are bound to come hawking a certain "patronage ticket" to their familiar patrons; and the price marked on the ticket is always at least double the fixed rate. Suppose the house charges a dollar a seat in the flower-gallery and the flower-hall: the patronage ticket must add two dollars on top. The old patron finds it hard to refuse for courtesy's sake, and can only buy up a few. This fashion has come down to the present and lasted many years now. And what, in truth, is all this "patronage" or want of patronage? Stripped bare, it is nothing but a squeeze.

By the custom of the Shanghai theatres, people who go to the opera are mostly shown in by a seat-agent, and few buy their own tickets. A man of any reputation and lavish style need not pay the seat-fee on the spot; he need only write down his name and address and hand it to the agent, and a few days later they will come to collect of themselves. After a few such times they know you. Not paying on the spot is convenient enough, and saves face handsomely too—only, when the festival season comes round, a few of those squeeze-laden patronage tickets will be delivered to your door, and there is no fear of your refusing.
