## Written Before the Third Volume Went to Press

During the war of resistance, the working comrades of my “Juntong Bureau Shanghai District” who gave their lives in the anti-Japanese action work at Shanghai were most tragically many—in truth far more than the few set down in the third part of Nameless Heroes; and as for the cases carried out, first and last, they too were by no means only these few. That I have not been able to record them one and all in detail is because some of their deeds are past all tracing, most of them buried away together with the death of the men themselves; and there are cases, besides, that by memory alone I could not keep so many of in mind. That is to say, one part can no longer be found out, and another part is owing to the writer’s not having gathered them with all his heart and strength—for which, on this point, I ought to render the martyred forerunners who died my deep contrition.

The serial publication, part by part, of “Shanghai Behind-the-Lines Operations Against Japan” has, though it now comes to a pause, on my reading it over from beginning to end left me with no little sense that the words fall short of the fitting and the meaning short of the perfect; and there are, to be sure, places I dared not paint out to the full—such as the “sabotage” cases, the most telling of all our blows against the enemy’s forces, which I passed over lightly, or left unmentioned altogether.

It is not to be denied that, in the book as a whole, the writer has indeed concealed certain facts; and this was wholly out of good intent—chiefly, that I did not wish to mar an image already molded and formed.

Beyond this, where—by readers’ pointing out, by the hints of friends, by my own noticing, or by what I have seen in other writings—there have proved to be things tangled and unclear, or turned about one for another, I shall take this chance as well to make some small correction, adding and cutting a little.

For this cause, the places where I purpose to alter the original text when the book is published are the following:

1. Concerning the account of Geng Jiaji (see the second chapter, page sixty-five), some small correction is to be made. There is other material on record which says that this Geng was once marked out by the Wang puppets as a “Member of the Central Supervisory Committee of the Kuomintang.”

2. Concerning the story of the woman worker of the “Qingdao station” who was strangled to death (see the third chapter, page eighty-four), by the instruction of Mr. Liu Zhongkang there is to be a large revision and enlargement.

3. In the case of Yu Yefeng (see the fourth chapter, page one hundred and twenty-four), the old materials say that he once held the post of chairman of the puppet “Shanghai General Chamber of Commerce”; but there is as yet no stronger material to serve as corroboration.

And further, according to the mainland Peking-opera actor Li Hongchun, who tells in his “Anecdotes of the Pear Garden”: “Xin Yanqiu’s showcase piece on her first day in Shanghai was The Tale of Hongfu; Liang Huichao and I took the second-to-last turn with Xiaoshang River, Liang playing Yang Zaixing and I playing Yue Fei.” On this passage, Mr. Suibo had earlier made a correction as well.

4. Concerning the date of martyrdom of the three forerunners Xu Shouxin, Yu Yanzhi, and Zhou Xiliang (see the sixth chapter, page eighty-seven), as Mr. Xu Zhan has informed me by telephone, it remains the twenty-seventh of the twelfth month of the twenty-eighth year of the Republic.

5. Lin Huaibu, the principal figure in the case of Zhang Xiaolin (see the eighth chapter, page one hundred and twenty-nine), has now been traced. According to a man come from Hong Kong: “A few years ago, Lin Huaibu was undergoing reform through labour in the Third Brigade of the ‘Shanghai No. —— Farm’ in northern Jiangsu.” As the man had yet to go back, I can but leave it at that.

6. Chen Gongbo was president of the puppet Legislative Yuan, while the presidency of the puppet Executive Yuan was held by Wang himself concurrently (see the ninth chapter, page eighty-five). What was set down in the text—that “Chen Gongbo was at the time the puppet president of the Executive Yuan”—is in error.

7. Concerning the matter of “No. 76” shooting to death a great number of innocent bank clerks (see the ninth chapter, page eighty-nine), something is to be added.

8. Sections one, two, and three of the tenth chapter are from Brother Liu Yuanshen’s original manuscript; from the fourth section on, it is once again the writer’s own narration. The text having made no clear statement of this, it reads confusedly; a note of explanation ought to be added.
