## 1

When Jiang Yongnan was arrested he was working a splinter of shrapnel out of an opened abdomen, his hands slick to the wrist with warm blood.

Days of fighting had long since filled the little church past capacity. The hot, bloody air carried a reek of corpses; everywhere the wounded wailed and the dying groaned, and explosions near and far shook the ground. So when the military police from the garrison command burst into the operating room that had once been the priest's bedroom, Jiang Yongnan did not even lift his head. Out of habit he said a single word to the nurse beside him: "Sweat."

The nurse's hand, reaching for the towel, froze.

"Wipe," said Jiang Yongnan, and as the second word left him he saw the two soldiers with their loaded rifles.

By nightfall the gunfire had begun to slacken in a sudden downpour, but flares still rose far off, lighting the city and the smoke that would not clear, and lighting too this row of warehouses by the river. Jiang Yongnan crouched in the rain, on the muddy ground before the warehouses, among a crowd of men and women. Most were the city's peddlers and clerks, dance-hall girls, gang men. Hardly any of them knew what had happened. Only the ones with no sense stuck out their stubborn necks and asked, in the local speech, What's going on? What's all this?

The military police stood like a row of statues, the rain sheeting off the hems of their oilcloth capes.

It was near midnight when Jiang Yongnan's turn came. In a room that looked like a counting-house, two candles burned on the table. The interrogator's shirt hung open, soaked through with sweat. Gnawing at half a steamed bun, he asked, "Name?"

"Jiang Yongnan."

The interrogator turned his head to check the register on the table. "Why did you turn traitor?"

"I'm no traitor." Jiang Yongnan faltered a moment. "I'm Korean."

At that the interrogator raised his eyes. "Then you're a Jap devil's lackey."

"I'm no lackey," said Jiang Yongnan. "I'm a doctor—"

The interrogator had no patience left for anything more. He waved the half-eaten bun in his hand at the military police. "Next."

As the two military policemen dragged Jiang Yongnan out of the counting-house he was still pleading his case: "I'm a surgeon, I'm a member of the Hankou Red Cross, I've saved the lives of a great many of your Chinese—"

Early the next morning the Japanese warships pushing upriver opened fire again. In the roar of the naval guns many people were forced down onto the open ground before the warehouses and shot where they stood, while more were locked into a pitch-dark storeroom. As though the place were only for waiting to die, that makeshift cell was thick with a stench of excrement harder to bear than the fear itself.

A few days later Jiang Yongnan was moved to the garrison command's prison. Through the last dozen-odd days of the Battle of Wuhan he was packed into a cramped cell with the real spies. Soon even he had begun to believe he was a Japanese spy, and had been since the war came—that his days had been spent not saving lives at the Red Cross but out with a little mirror, guiding the bombers overhead . . . until the order to withdraw came down from the supreme command to the prison itself.

That day the prisoners were hauled from the cells in batches. To make the shooting go faster the prison had brought in two Czech machine guns.

From the cell window Jiang Yongnan watched the people go down like cut rice, though he could not hear the machine guns. Every report merged into the din of the fighting beyond the wall. All at once he found himself thinking of his brother—the only kin he had left in the world.

What saved Jiang Yongnan's life was a Nationalist plane. It came down screaming, trailing a long tail, and plowed head-first into the prison, shearing off half the cellblock; then came the blast, and the fire . . .

He crawled out of the rubble with his ears ringing and shapes blurred and doubled before his eyes. The one thing Jiang Yongnan was sure of was that he was still alive. He was covered in blood and shreds of viscera.

The Iwai Surgical Clinic stood on the liveliest stretch of Sizajie. Years before, buying that little two-courtyard building had cost Dr. Iwai nearly half a lifetime's savings. Then, a year after the Battle of Shanghai, the Nationalist government abruptly announced that it was taking back the Japanese concession in Hankou, and overnight he and every other Japanese resident were expelled and sent home.

On the day he left, Dr. Iwai's face was as untroubled as it was before any operation. He washed his hands carefully with soap, and only when they had dried did he take up his case. As he walked out he reminded Jiang Yongnan, "Remember, tomorrow is the day the electric bill is due."

"Rest easy." Jiang Yongnan lowered his head and answered in Japanese. "I'll be here waiting when you come back."

Dr. Iwai nodded. Out on the street he looked up at the clinic's signboard. "It wouldn't be a bad thing," he said, "to change it to Yongnan's Clinic . . ." Iwai went, and there would never again be an Iwai on that street.

But in the end Iwai's clinic did not escape the fires of war, nor did the whole quarter around it. Only when Jiang Yongnan had crossed most of the city and reached the corner did he see it clearly: the familiar place was a field of ruins, timbers buried in the rubble, still smoking heavily.

The little church, at least, still stood, an ornament forgotten at the gate of hell under the failing sun.

The priest was Jiang Yongnan's countryman, from the old land. He came in from outside carrying a bowl of hot soup and said there was no food left in the church. He set the bowl on the table, then turned and took from the cupboard a Japanese leather medical case. It was Jiang Yongnan's most cherished possession, a gift from the medical school of Kyoto Imperial University to each year's finest graduates. The priest set that on the table as well. "There's a boat again tonight," he said. "You leave tonight."

It was as if only now did Jiang Yongnan remember he was a doctor at all. In the priest's old shirt, hanging open, he came forward and slowly opened the case. Inside, along with the full set of instruments, were his diploma and his license to practice. The two sheets of paper had hung framed on the wall of Iwai's clinic. Jiang Yongnan looked up at the priest. "How do you come to have these? You knew I'd come back alive?"

The priest did not answer. He sat down against the edge of the table, let out a long sigh, and said, as if to himself, "By daybreak this may all belong to the Japanese."

"I'm not going anywhere." Jiang Yongnan snapped the case shut, took up the bowl, and drained the soup in a few swallows. "I can be useful to you here in the church."

"You'll go to Guangzhou." The priest turned his face aside, as though speaking to the small flame on the candlestick. "Yongzhu should have reached Guangzhou by now." The last Jiang Yongnan had heard of his brother's movements was months old. At the time every newspaper in Hankou carried the news of Kim Ku's shooting in Changsha. A faithful adherent of the Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea, his brother Jiang Yongzhu had sworn to follow his leader to the death.

All at once Jiang Yongnan understood. He looked down at the priest. "So you are not only a servant of God."

The priest's mouth twisted into a grin, and he crossed himself. "God has a country of His own, too. One day we all go back to our roots." The whole way from the little church the shelling had ceased, but rifle fire still rose and fell on every side. Nationalist soldiers who had lost their units were everywhere, stragglers with nowhere to run, darting about in the moonlight; some had already thrown away their rifles and pulled on clothes stripped from the bodies of civilians.

It was on the boat, after it had cast off, that Jiang Yongnan met the Tang women, mother and daughter. Mrs. Tang was frail and often ill, a regular at the Iwai clinic; now she was wedged into the crowded deck, one hand clamped over the opposite arm. At the sight of Jiang Yongnan she let out a small breath of relief and said to her daughter, "A familiar face at last."

Miss Tang kept her lips pressed shut the whole time. A student of Chinese letters at Wuchang University, before the war she had ridden the ferry home every weekend, passing the door of the Iwai clinic with her head down. She often wore a blue cotton cheongsam, a lace-trimmed ribbon tied into hair neither long nor short. But her face had long since lost the arrogance and the fearlessness of a coed. The way she looked at Jiang Yongnan was the way a frightened kitten looks at a world it cannot fathom.

Mrs. Tang was on her way to Changsha to join her husband. Boarding, she had been crushed by the surging crowd until her arm was pulled from its socket. It took Jiang Yongnan several hard efforts to set it, and the pain nearly made Mrs. Tang faint. In the end he unbuckled the belt at his waist and bound the arm against her chest, then turned and said to Miss Tang, by way of apology, "I trained as a surgeon."

Something of the coed's arrogance and reserve came back into Miss Tang's eyes. She gave Jiang Yongnan a nod and opened her mouth, but no sound came.

Toward dawn a Japanese gunboat intercepted the refugee boat on the Yangtze. Several panicked men jumped almost at once; the searchlight on the gunboat swung to the water and the shooting began. Through the screaming the Japanese sailors fired their rifles into the river without pause, until they had driven the ones they had not killed back aboard. Then they put a single pilot on the boat, who directed the helmsman back by hand signals, brought her in at a wharf on the city's outskirts, and handed her over to the army on shore.

To catch the Nationalist soldiers mixed in among the civilians, the Japanese searched everyone's baggage and questioned each person in turn through an interpreter. When they came to Jiang Yongnan, he answered in Japanese more fluent than the interpreter's: "I'm not a refugee. I'm a Korean living in China."

A middle-aged officer in spectacles came over at the sound of it and studied Jiang Yongnan. "Then why are you fleeing along with these Chinese?"

"I'm taking this boat to Changsha, and then on to Guangzhou," said Jiang Yongnan. "My clinic in Hankou was bombed to rubble. I'm going to my brother in Guangzhou."

The officer took the passport and the two certificates a soldier handed up, and his manner grew a good deal warmer; he even let a faint smile show at Jiang Yongnan. "No wonder you speak with a Kyoto accent." He looked Jiang Yongnan up and down once more. "A doctor the Empire has trained ought to serve the Expeditionary Army."

Jiang Yongnan's eyes went wide. "But I'm Korean."

"A Korean under the rule of the Empire." Behind the lenses the officer's gaze turned severe, fixed on Jiang Yongnan. "You too are a subject of the Emperor, and to serve the Imperial Army is your highest honor."

"But I'm only a doctor," said Jiang Yongnan. "Beyond treating the sick I can do nothing at all."

"The army needs doctors now more than ever." The officer brought his face close to Jiang Yongnan's ear. "You know well enough what happens to a Korean who refuses the Expeditionary Army's call."

As the officer's guard led Jiang Yongnan past Mrs. Tang, she suddenly broke from the line. Mrs. Tang caught the young doctor by the sleeve as if clutching a straw for her life, and begged him in a rush: "Doctor Jiang, if you have any standing with the Japanese, help the two of us."

Jiang Yongnan glanced at the guard, steadied Mrs. Tang, and walked her back to her place in the line, at a loss for anything that might comfort her.

Mrs. Tang was near tears, and heedless of everything she said again, "Doctor Jiang, we beg you, we'll repay you."

Again Jiang Yongnan saw Miss Tang's round eyes, like a block of ice already melting under the blazing sun. He hesitated, then stepped up and snatched the papers she was holding out for inspection, and opened them for a look.

"What are you doing?" Tang Ya spoke at last, and her voice sounded so timid.

So her name was Tang Ya. Jiang Yongnan pressed the papers back into the guard's hand and said in Japanese, "Go tell your officer—if I can't even protect my own fiancée, how am I to become a soldier of the Empire?" He waited until the guard had turned away before he looked at Tang Ya. "Remember: you are my fiancée. We were engaged this New Year's Day."
