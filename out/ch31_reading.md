## Chapter 31

The telephone was hung up. I began to wonder: with so important a task set before her tomorrow night, how was it that today she had the leisure and the whim to invite me out for a night's amusement? And this was a thing that had never happened before. Of course I thought too that perhaps she had something to charge me with about tomorrow's work, but ordinarily she would simply tell me to come and see her, or to go to some place or other, and would never have telephoned in so wholly soft and womanly a tone. —

In the midst of these misgivings the door opened, and Mei Yingzi came in dressed in a wholly unexpected way. She had thrown on a man's heavy herringbone tweed overcoat and wound a white woolen scarf about her; taking off the coat, she had on a hand-knitted evergreen jumper of coarse wool, and her gray woolen cheongsam seemed to be the very one she had worn on the trip to Hangzhou, without a trace of powder or rouge. Faintly giving off her own peculiar fragrance, she said to me in a lively, childlike tone:

"Tonight I want you to be host."

"That is my first honor," I said. "Then choose a place."

"A place I've never been to."

"Is there still a place under heaven the sunlight has not reached?"

"A cold, out-of-the-way little lane, a dim tavern, where not a soul will know me, and I shall know no one."

"Very well," I said.

At six o'clock I went out with her; before the door stood her black car, and she told me to drive, sitting quietly beside me herself. We stopped the car on Fourth Avenue, and I took her into a little lane to a tavern called the Yuanyutai; as we went in the door, I said:

"Your first time here, I suppose?"

"Yes."

"Then is it this place's honor, or yours?"

"All the honor I give to you." As she spoke she only laughed, a childish laugh with something rustic in it, something foolish, such as I had never seen on Mei Yingzi's face, and seemed to have seldom seen among those around me either. And Mei Yingzi laughed so genuinely and so like the thing itself — yet how ill it accorded with her talk!

On Fourth Avenue I naturally knew there were more splendid taverns to go to, so in bringing her to this damp, dirty place I meant to give this noble and gorgeous woman a deeper stimulation; at the same time it occurred to me that perhaps she had something to charge me with, and here was rather more suitable for that too.

There were not many people in the room, all of them slovenly in dress and loose in manner. At one table sat three or four people, two of whom had their hats hanging at the backs of their heads, talking loudly of coarse matters of the flesh; behind them an ailing old man sat alone in a corner, sighing faintly, and behind him there was an empty table, and I took Mei Yingzi in to it. Thinking Mei Yingzi would surely not be used to such an atmosphere, I said with a smile:

"Today you've been made sport of by me."

"What do you mean by that?"

"In all honesty, are you used to an atmosphere like this?"

"I find it fresh and interesting."

This remark was indeed no forced one. I ordered several bowls of wine, and she too drank quite freely, and then there was very witty talk and lively, sweet laughter; she spoke of many things she had never spoken of before, telling on and on of the many places her wanderings had taken her to — the customs and people there, the music and songs, the dress and the life — but never a word of our plan for tomorrow.

After six bowls of wine, I ordered two bowls of noodles and a dish of dumplings for our supper, and then she said:

"Will you take me to some out-of-the-way dance hall tonight?"

"If only you wish it."

"Tonight I need a fresh stimulation," she said. So I drove again to a dance hall behind the Great World, where the music was noisy and the crowd clamorous; but Mei Yingzi danced wildly with me in high excitement. I would rather have talked with her of tomorrow's work, but could never find a chance. The night slowly deepened, and still there were many people; several times I proposed going to a café to talk a while, but Mei Yingzi vetoed it each time, and seemed to take great pleasure in dancing wildly to the noisy music. She said:

"If tonight you don't follow my pleasure, it may make you regret it forever."

What was the dark shadow behind these words? Tomorrow's work? My heart quivered at its tip, and I felt how tender and delicate a life she was in my arms. I dared not ask, and had no way to ask. Rousing my already weary spirit, I turned with her round and round in the heavy, stifling air.

At two o'clock she had me drive her back to the Benner Inn, and told me to come up to her room and sit, and I naturally supposed that now, surely, we should talk of tomorrow night's work. But no; she sat serene and happy on the sofa and talked with me of all sorts of things seen at the tavern and the dance hall — such ordinary encounters, and yet, to my wonder, she had so many observations and questions about them. At last I really could not bear it, and asked:

"Then how will it be tomorrow?"

"It ought to be a very lively gathering." She had none of her childlike manner of a moment ago now, but wore a weary, sorrowful look.

"Am I to...?"

"Isn't Bai Ping going with Arita?"

"Yes."

"Then your work is only to bring Helen there."

"And after that?"

"There's no need to stay always at Helen's side." She laughed, very unnaturally, and went on:

"In fact this is a needless thing for me to say; even if you wished to stay by her, it might not be possible."

"Then..."

"Better to stay by Bai Ping." she said. "But that, of course, is even less possible."

"Why? If it is a thing I ought to do in the work," I said, "then of course I will do my best at it."

"But an impossible thing is labor spent in vain."

"Then I..."

"Go and find your pleasure, child; wild dancing and reckless gambling — I surely needn't teach you those." This remark took me altogether for one who understood nothing; though there was something of a jest in it, the mockery within it much displeased me. But the expression on her face accorded ill with her words: at the corners of her eyes gathered an inscrutable anxiety, and this made me think at once of the strangeness of her manner today. It seemed that this arrogant, self-important remark and the broken words about "regretting forever" of a moment ago ran along one vein, and it dawned on me that this was the terrible shadow of tomorrow's work, forming the pent-up anxiety in her heart. Mei Yingzi had ordinarily never shown a shadow of weakness; then this state of mind showed the danger of tomorrow's work, and I felt, superstitiously, that I hoped the words "regret forever" would not prove her own prophecy. I could not keep my heart from pounding; all of Mei Yingzi's cruelty and sharpness that I had once opposed I forgot utterly in an instant, and I felt for her an indescribable sympathy, a sympathy that made me notice her matchless beauty and loveliness, which I had long overlooked.

I could not imagine this life, if tomorrow it should meet with some mishap — should meet with a terrible, murderous hand and torment — I know not how, but on Mei Yingzi's beautiful face I saw the ghost of Stephen: the iron-gray face, the deep-purple lips, the gaunt frame, and the lightless eyes —

Mei Yingzi's eyes were half closed, as if she held herself to a serene manner; I remembered how I had stroked my hand over Stephen's eyes, and my fingers trembled faintly, and in an instant tears surged up from my throat. Whether it was grief for Stephen or worry for Mei Yingzi I did not know; I stood up, and to keep Mei Yingzi from noticing, walked to the table with my back to her.

"Mei Yingzi." I said clearly, in a slow, resolute tone: "In tomorrow's work, I hope I might change posts with you."

"What do you mean by that?" she asked serenely, and I believe there was a ripple of a light smile on her lips.

"I think I ought to take on the most dangerous and heaviest mission for you."

"Because my words just now have made you want to be a hero?"

"Not so."

"Do you know what mission I must complete tomorrow?"

"No," I said — I was strong now, so I turned back, and going up to Mei Yingzi, said: "But I know it is dangerous work."

"Then are you willing to run the risk?"

"Between the two of us," I said, "I ought to be the first to cross the dangerous threshold."

"For the work?" she asked.

"For the work it is the same," I said. "The work to come needs you far more than me."

"In the work, for the moment I am perhaps more important than you. But in the long run you are more important than I."

"But what I do it for is..."

"Is me?"

"You could say it is for you."

"I thank you," she said. "But this is impossible."

"Can you not even let me try?"

"For you it is ten in ten, for me three in ten — that is the ratio of the work's failure." She smiled darkly. "And as for danger, for you it is ten in ten, for me only six in ten."

"That is to say, my going is pure going to my death, and it is you who are the sacrifice of the work."

"Clever man." She smiled darkly. "Don't worry about me; your worrying is no act of a friend's love."

"But..." I did not go on; an indescribable feeling took hold of me and made me sink to my knees before her; I took her narcissus-like hand, and the fingers were so cold and dark, and I said: "Mei Yingzi, then may you go after that seven-in-ten victory, and let me bear the six-in-ten danger."

This remark seemed to touch the tender part of Mei Yingzi's feelings; she gazed at me with lightless, moist eyes and said:

"You are too good!" But at once she closed her eyes and rested her head against the back of the sofa.

"Allow me," I said, still holding her hand. "For one who works together with you to be unable to take your danger upon himself is, to me, a disgrace."

"My friend," she said, "with so lofty a soul as this, you will yet go on existing in the world; but my life was a matter of luck from the first — or rather, terrible things should long ago have befallen me, and moreover," she suddenly let out a sweet smile and said, "will you look closely at me?"

I gazed at her face, and she asked:

"Am I beautiful?"

"Of course," I said. "It is our Mei Yingzi."

"Then only by dying now," she said, "shall I leave the most beautiful impression in the world. Do you know that?"

But I thought again of Stephen and his iron-gray face, his deep-purple lips, his gaunt frame, his lightless eyes, and so I said:

"Only by my dying now will you leave the most beautiful impression in the depths of my soul."

Once this was said, I rather regretted it; but she seemed not to know that I was contradicting her meaning. She said:

"Your most beautiful life is bound up in your studies; that is lasting work, and the longer you live the more beautiful an impression you leave in the world; but I — I know that only my present impression is worth people's lasting remembrance."

I was too moved to speak. But I felt all at once that our whole conversation was as if we were admitting tomorrow's failure, and I felt that at such a time what she needed was brave encouragement, not broken sympathy, and so I said:

"Mei Yingzi, give the danger to me; I believe it would raise your victory to nine in ten."

"So you think I am afraid and cowardly, and that is why you step forward to protect me?" She straightened herself, opened her eyes wide, and grew angry in her excitement.

"No," I said. "I have no such chivalrous courage; what I answer for is the wholeness of my life and the standard of my ideals."

"An expression of individualism."

"Perhaps," I said. "When I work together with you, all the danger I ought to bear ahead of you, or else it is a maiming of my life."

"But I do not need it," she said, standing up. "When I stand before the work, I have the courage to bear the hardship and danger of the work that is my charge, or else it is a maiming of my work."

"But..." Just as I was about to speak again, Mei Yingzi cut me off, and said:

"If the danger of my work is to be borne by another, that is my own not believing in my own work, my own having no assurance in my own work."

"What I mean is..."

My words had barely begun when she cut me off again, and said:

"It's getting late; you ought to go back."

"You won't let me say one more word?"

"This question is not to be raised again. If you will not obey my command, you ought at least to keep your own promise; this is discipline."

As she spoke she walked to the table, took up the car keys from it and shook them — these I had tossed there as I came in — and now she came over and handed them to me, and said:

"I give this car into your keeping." Then she held out her hand to me and said gravely: "I am going to sleep; good night to you!"

I shook her hand in silence, feeling an extraordinary oppression in my breast.

Carrying this oppression downstairs, it struck me all at once that at the moment of this parting handshake, Mei Yingzi's fingers had been warm.

This warmth I carried to the door, carried to the car, carried over the long road, carried into my cold and cheerless room.
