## Chapter 44

But I did not go often to see Helen, for the masked ball of the thirteenth of March was now close at hand, and I had for the present to keep up some intercourse with the great merchants like Honsa Jiro. By our resolution I was to go to the ball together with Honsa and his set, so beforehand I was to draw closer to Honsa in the guise of a man newly back from the country. Honsa Jiro and I were partners in the same concern, and though we had now and then dined and made merry together, there was still a certain distance between us; but now, after some days of tipsy carousing and horseplay, all ceremony between the two of us was gone. The occasions of pleasure bear on human society in subtle ways: every rank, every distance, every pretence and stiff dignity—all are broken through at once. And after several outings together, outsiders too seemed at once to take our peculiar relation for settled, so that they naturally thought it the most natural thing that we should go to the masked ball together.

Bai Ping had already decided to go once more together with Colonel Arita; Mei Yingzi would perhaps go alone, though it was not yet certain; and as for Helen, ever since she had gone to Qingdao she seemed to have cut every tie with the Japanese side, and no one, it seemed, would think to invite her and her mother—in our strained and crowded life we naturally never thought of them either, so that their not going seemed already a settled thing. And on the rare occasions when I chanced to meet them, I felt there was no need to tell them of it.

But on the morning of the eleventh of March, no sooner had I come into Bai Ping's apartment than Ah Mei told me that Bai Ping and Helen were in the room I used to live in. I knocked and went in, and Bai Ping first told me that Helen had received an invitation. Helen said to me at once:

"Are you going too?"

"Yes."

"Won't you have me go with you?"

"You mean to go too?" I raised my voice and asked her, as though she had long since given me to understand she would not go.

"No." Her quick eyes went blank for a moment. "Only I never thought you would want to go this time."

"This time I have specially asked him to go," Bai Ping said to her, easy and lovely. "I think it does not matter your not going; they have probably invited you from the last list. And there is no help for my own not going."

Bai Ping's explanation was very good, very natural, very sincere, and her tone did indeed convey to the full that her going was a thing she could not help. In such a case this manner might seem a piece of dissembling, but I sensed that Bai Ping's inner feeling was truly so; and this, perhaps, was the most lovable thing about Bai Ping—perhaps the very reason she loved the color silver.

Helen at once put on a natural smile and talked of other things with Bai Ping, and there I saw Helen's friendship for Bai Ping. Ever since the day Helen was saved from the tiger's jaws and stayed the night at Bai Ping's, it seemed that all Helen's gratitude and trust toward Mei Yingzi had passed entirely over to Bai Ping. Helen was truly an innocent, willful child.

In the instant that Bai Ping left the room, Helen began to tell me that she had been to see Mrs. Stephen, who had urged her with all her might to go to Beiping to study music, and was willing to help Helen with money; she had given her a check on the spot, and Helen had not taken it, and the next day she had sent someone to bring it to Helen's home—a sum of ten thousand yuan, no small sum in those days. So that though Helen had accepted it, she was still not clear in her heart: grant that Mrs. Stephen prized Helen's gift, still she had never shown it so before, and the thing was, to Helen, rather abrupt.

I thought at once that Helen's coming to see Bai Ping was to talk of this very thing—to see what the money really meant. So I asked:

"And what did Bai Ping think?"

"Bai Ping said it was wholly Mrs. Stephen's hope for me, and told me not to doubt it."

"I think Bai Ping's view is right." So my mouth said, but in my heart too I felt it a little abrupt; and at last it came to me all at once that this must be Mei Yingzi's payment for that time she had made use of Helen. I was sure this was not Mrs. Stephen's meaning, nor Mei Yingzi's, but that there must be some such sum drawn down, which they had used this means to pay over to Helen—only there was no need to explain it to Helen; and Bai Ping too, I thought, must have believed it good for Helen so, or what was there she did not know, that she had not long since told her plainly?

When Bai Ping came in, Helen's talk had already turned elsewhere. A person's talk, in such a place, is a subtle thing: she was willing to speak with Bai Ping, and willing to speak with me, but not willing to speak with the two of us together; and though I knew she had already spoken with Bai Ping, I could not know whether Bai Ping knew she had spoken with me as well. In short, there are many things that pass not through our thought but through some atmosphere of the group that governs us, so that of themselves we give up our freedom. In short, this matter, from first to last, came only to this degree of plainness; and looking back on it now, I feel that Bai Ping's telling Mei Yingzi of Helen's coming to see her was a very likely thing.

Helen and I both took our midday meal at Bai Ping's, and after it came out together; on the way, Helen said suddenly:

"Bai Ping was very glad to hear you had a mind to go to Beiping with me."

"You told her?" I was rather startled.

"Of course; I can tell her anything," said Helen. "Do you think it wrong?"

"What did she say?" I asked quickly.

"She said that after you had been to the masked ball, she would encourage you to go with me to Beiping the sooner."

These words gave me much to ponder, and I fell silent; but I could not think it over calmly, for I thought at once of that one thing she had said when we parted the other day, and I marveled that I seemed at the time to have raised no objection to it, though, thinking of it now, it was a great mistake! How, in that moment, had I so forgotten the connection between Mei Yingzi and Mrs. Stephen? So I asked:

"Did you tell Mrs. Stephen anything of my wish to go to Beiping?"

"Of course." "Truly?"

"What of it?" she said.

"Nothing," I said. "I should think she would be much surprised."

"She asked me whether…" she seemed unable to go on, and her eyes turned away.

"Whether what?" I asked.

"She asked whether I meant to go together with you," she said, and then, as though it had come back to her, went on quickly with a foolish smile: "I said yes, and she said, that is very good; now you will have someone to look after you."

"She said nothing else?"

"Nothing else."

"She did not say whether I was fit to go?"

"No."

Helen said no, and of course there was no; Mrs. Stephen would never let her feeling show. So I had no way to know what was in her heart, still less to know when she would go and tell Mei Yingzi—and whether, indeed, the whole question turned only on Mei Yingzi's insisting that I be her helper. For my part, to come out of the world of philosophy and go back into the world of philosophy was the most natural of things. That world was my native place, just as music was Helen's. In the so-called work I was no more than a man Stephen had made use of; in nothing had I failed to give my conscience and my strength; I had paid with a grievous wound to win the linking of Bai Ping and Mei Yingzi, and had solved the problem both sides thought so hard; and now I was about to carry through one piece of work. Once it was done, I would go back to my own native place, and that, I thought, was a thing they could surely understand. But my talking it over with Mei Yingzi would be after the work was done, and never now; to tell her now would be bad for me, for her, and for the spirit of the work alike. And that day when I saw Helen home, when she brought it up, I had utterly forgotten the connection between Mrs. Stephen and Mei Yingzi, and so had not charged Helen to say nothing.

This forged a vexation that lay uneasy in my heart, and all the way as I saw Helen home, and alone on the road back, I was troubled by it still.

But in every vexing matter, if one thinks it through in the stillest hour, every heart can dig up some store of relief. As I lay down for my noon nap back at my lodging, I thought of what Bai Ping had said to Helen; so that if Mei Yingzi should learn of the matter first, she would surely go and speak of it to Bai Ping, and Bai Ping would surely take my part—for I knew she understood, from first to last, that I was not fit for such work and ought rather to go on well with my study.

And so, in a mind more at peace, I got a spell of rest.

Night, at Bai Ping's lodging. We three held a meeting. This meeting—or rather, it was less a meeting than a mapping-out of my work; looking back now, I believe the two of them had long since arranged everything between them, and only on that night gave me my exact instruction.

My work was to climb, from behind the little foreign-style house in the back garden of Umetake's official residence, up to the second floor, get in at the window, take the thing I sought, and climb down again the same way; and Mei Yingzi would be waiting below, and I would hand it to her and, feigning an idle stroll in the garden after the dancing, bring her back round to the front.

"The rest of it you need not trouble about," said Bai Ping.

"Then when may I begin?" I asked.

"You know that Korean girl?" asked Mei Yingzi.

"Who?"

"The singing-girl at the Standford," said Mei Yingzi.

"You mean Miko?"

"Yes."

"She is a Korean girl?"

"What of it?"

"I took her for a Japanese."

"You may dance often with Miko, and learn to flirt with her as if quite carried away, and she will bring you into the back garden. After that you must act as the moment serves; if it will not do, you have only to go back to the ballroom and come out again." Mei Yingzi said gravely: "But you must first dance with me—I will hand you the key."

There was a minute's silence; both Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping watched me with a look of great solemnity, and the air of the room grew all at once heavy, as though numberless pressures bore in upon my heart, and my very breath seemed on the instant to come hard. After a while Mei Yingzi said:

"Have you understood it all?"

"Yes."

"Think it over carefully—is there still any problem?" said Bai Ping, graver still.

Now I felt an indescribable awkwardness rising in me, for the four of their eyes were fixed sternly upon me, waiting for my answer as though questioning a prisoner.

The room was still as death, and but for the steam floating on the coffee-cups, the air was simply congealed into a solid! I took a cigarette from the table, lit it, drew a mouthful, and said:

"I think there is no other problem." Again the awkward stillness; then Mei Yingzi rose, went quietly to the window, and, turning back, said:

"You must consider: at that ball everyone will be masked, and among so many people, how are you to find the one you seek? To find me, for instance."

This was indeed a problem I had not thought of, and I was struck dumb for the moment; a shame at my own incapacity and oversight rose to my heart and to my face as well. Bai Ping seemed to notice it, and with a most unusual gesture reached for her coffee, as though on purpose letting the diamond ring I had given her, on her finger, remind me; and I said:

"From her ring, could I fail to know Bai Ping?"

"And me?" said Mei Yingzi.

"If that day you still wear the pearl-string collar you wore at the last ball."

"And Miko…?"

"Won't you tell me beforehand what sort of ring you will have her wear?"

At this Mei Yingzi came quietly over again, drew a ring from her own hand, set it before me, and said:

"I think you will know it easily." And she said again: "We will take this cross for our sign; while dancing, trace a cross with your finger in our palms, and we shall know it is you."

I bowed my head low, and gazed at the ring: it was a white-gold setting, an exquisitely worked band, with red diamonds forming a square frame about a cross made of white diamonds. It was a very beautiful arrangement, but one that would ever give me a strange feeling, calling up to me the cross at the head of Stephen's grave, and the wreath on wreath ringing that cross. I played with it a long while, and slipped it on my own ring finger—too small—so I set it on my little finger, looked at it, and silently drew it off and handed it back to Mei Yingzi. By now she had seated herself across from me, a faint, dim smile at the corner of her mouth, while Bai Ping looked wearily aslant; and for a moment I dared not look either of them full in the face.

Silence, silence; I felt the air all a heavy glue that would not let my mouth open, and the many things I had to say found no way out.
