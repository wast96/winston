## 4

The winter fog always gathered deep in the night and came drifting in along the face of the river. It was on such a night that the water bandits out of the Xiangfu marsh stole in without a sound. They came in two wooden boats and set at once to breaking open the town's few shops. One of Zhu Qi's men kicked in the door of the Taishun tailor shop. It was the first time Yanzhi had faced the water bandits: her hair was wild, her clothes in disorder, and she was in an agony of fright. Zhu Qi raised the oil lamp to Yanzhi's face and looked a good while through narrowed eyes. Zhu Qi's gaze was like a honed knife. He turned his head and said to Baosheng, you've married yourself a beauty. Baosheng dared not speak. Little by little he worked his body in front of Yanzhi. Zhu Qi gave a laugh and said back over his shoulder to his men, lovelier even than her mother.

His men let out a few laughs, none of them hearty. They all knew well enough: the chief had an eye for women, but the chief never let a woman spoil the work — what was to be looted was looted, what was to be smashed was smashed. As they left, Zhu Qi patted Baosheng's cheek and told him to remember to carry word to every shop in town: don't forget to pay your respects to the brothers out on the marsh; the Japanese have guns, and what Zhu Qi held in his own hand was no fire-poker either. That said, Zhu Qi never gave Yanzhi another glance, and with his men he was gone in a blink into the black night. But Yanzhi had seen clean through his mind at a glance. The water bandits came fast and went fast. As the boats passed the east stockade-gate of the town, Zhu Qi meant to show the people of Xietang a thing or two, and at a word from him the brothers opened fire together on the Japanese soldiers posted at the gate. The gunfire crackled through the thick fog like popping beans; Zhu Qi, seated at the bow, spat into the river and cursed: damn these Eastern-ocean turtles. Then he waved a hand and said, hoist the sail, we're off to drink!

In reprisal, the next day the Japanese soldiers turned out in full force and, like shepherds driving a flock, herded everyone in the streets onto the playground of the Xiushui Elementary School. The Japanese captain slung an ammunition box over his shoulder, well pleased at the sight of the terrified people. He nodded and waved a hand at Young Master Tang.

Young Master Tang pointed at a heap of shovels on the ground and shouted at the top of his voice that the Imperial Army had come to ask everyone's help. Dig the pits well, Young Master Tang said, and there'll be no trouble. A stir went through the crowd, but no one stepped forward; they only looked at one another. Growing impatient, Young Master Tang took up a shovel, went over to Benliang, thrust it into his hands, and said, you — take the lead; once it's dug there'll be no trouble.

Thirteen men set to digging pits on the playground, their faces blank; as they dug they kept turning to look at the Japanese soldiers standing all around with rifles leveled. Benliang thought of something suddenly. The Japanese mean to make latrines, he said. But at once he half doubted it and asked, can they really shit that much?

What's all this jabber for? Dig!

The noon sun was pale and weak; the Japanese soldiers opened their tins and ate together with the thirteen men. Yanzhi, pressed into the crowd, dared not move; she heard many a belly growling and made herself swallow her spit. She heard someone rueful too, saying that if he'd known there'd be Japanese tins to eat he'd have gone to help dig long ago.

Young Master Tang, eating the beef out of a tin, said to Benliang with an air of triumph, this is Japanese beef; you're getting a taste of the foreign today.

Benliang nodded and nodded. Young master, he said, honest truth, it's got more chew to it than pickled veg.

After the meal the Japanese captain, hands behind his back, looked the thirteen men over one by one, hauled Benliang up, grunted something with a smile, and shoved him into a pit.

Benliang scrambled up several times and could not get out; his face went red and he cursed, fuck your mother. The Japanese captain hauled him up with a smile, brushed the dirt from his head with one hand, then stripped off his own tunic, down to bare skin. In the cold wind the captain put out a hand, and a soldier passed him a military saber. All at once Benliang half understood, and would have fled, but he was already pinned. Benliang lay on the ground like a heap of mud, his eyes sweeping the crowd in despair, and at last he stared wide-eyed at Young Master Tang, his mouth opening but no sound coming out. It was Young Master Tang who spoke; his legs had gone so weak that the moment he opened his mouth he sat down hard on the ground. His voice was as if weeping. Taijun, taijun, he said, what are you doing?

Benliang the soy-works clerk was the first to be beheaded that noon. Under the blazing sun the Japanese captain went through four sabers to take off thirteen heads. Worn out at last, on the final neck he hacked four times running before the head came away.

After that the playground of the Xiushui Elementary School was never free of restless ghosts; come night, the headless men drifted one by one on the wind, colliding here and there with a moaning cry, searching the whole world over for their heads. And the living went in fear and trembling; many in Xietang fell ill of a strange, unaccountable sickness, and in their sickness dreamed the same dream, and would often start awake from their nightmares all at once together. After her grave illness Yanzhi was pale, and though she sat in the shop all day she was more a shadow pressed against the dark. It left Baosheng deeply uneasy; he walked to the wharf and came back again, set down his shoulder-bag, and said, never mind, I won't go after all. Yanzhi said nothing, only watched her husband without moving. It was a look heavy with meaning, the kind only a woman has, a look to make a man restless and ashamed to his soul. Baosheng shouldered the bag again and said, all right then; mind you take good care of yourself.

Yanzhi nodded.

On the fourth day after Baosheng left, a water bandit in boatman's clothes, Old Mo, burst panting into the tailor shop, laid the gray cloth shoulder-bag on the counter, and said straight off that something had happened to Boss Hu. Baosheng had run into Zhu Qi on his way back with the goods, his boat with nowhere to flee in the Xiangfu marsh. Old Mo brought the words of the bandit Zhu Qi. Zhu Qi said he would keep Boss Hu, would house him out on the marsh like an honored guest. Afraid Yanzhi had not understood, Old Mo went down the steps, then turned back and said, you'll have to go and ransom him yourself; send no go-between — Zhu Qi can't abide that.

Yanzhi said nothing, a hand on the door-frame; she thought again of Zhu Qi's knife-like gaze, yet she did not feel much afraid. By closing time the whole street knew what had happened at the tailor shop. Yanzhi sat in the counting-room of the pawnshop with her jewelry and the deed to the house. The head clerk took off his glasses, wiped them a long while on the hem of his gown, then shook his head and said, the property's no good; these days a house is worth less than a bomb — one blast and it's gone.

“I'm going to save a life,” Yanzhi said.

The head clerk shook his head still and sighed. “A human life's worth nothing,” he said.

“Then do a good deed,” Yanzhi said.

The head clerk said no more, put his glasses back on, and lifted his teacup. To lift the tea meant the guest was dismissed.

That night Yanzhi sat blankly at the worktable before the oil lamp, and just then Young Master Tang knocked at the tailor shop's door with a packet of silver dollars in his hand. When he had shut the door behind him, he said to Yanzhi, your trouble is my trouble. Yanzhi looked at him a good while and asked what that was supposed to mean. The little packet had been set down carelessly on the worktable, ringing with the clear chime of silver coin. Young Master Tang asked her back, don't you understand my meaning yet?

“With this money,” Yanzhi said, “you could take yourself another concubine.”

Young Master Tang gave a little laugh. “Two years ago I sent someone to ask for your hand,” he said. “Do you know what your father said?”

Yanzhi shook her head. “No,” she said.

Young Master Tang said, rich water doesn't run into another man's field; that's what he said — rich water doesn't run into another man's field.

“My father was a man of backbone,” Yanzhi said. “He'd never have let his daughter be some man's concubine.”

“You're cursing me,” Young Master Tang said. “I know it — you all curse me behind my back.”

“Why would I curse you?” Yanzhi asked.

If the Japanese had come two years sooner, I'd have taken you into my house for certain. Young Master Tang sighed and said, had I married you, you wouldn't be in this state today.

Yanzhi hesitated, then took up the packet of money from the worktable and weighed it in her hand. “You really don't grudge the money,” she said.

Young Master Tang laughed. “That depends where it's spent,” he said.

Yanzhi's eyes moved, still weighing the packet of silver. Was this money for one night? Or for a lifetime?

“Don't put it so ugly,” Young Master Tang said. “I've come to help you.”

Help me? So saying, Yanzhi stood, turned, and walked slowly into the inner room. After a long while her voice came from behind the door-curtain: then what are you waiting for?
