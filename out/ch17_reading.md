## Chapter 17. Moving to Hong Kong

After the War of Resistance broke out, overseas Chinese donated money and goods with the greatest patriotic fervor, but the Kuomintang, in the name of “unified planning and unified remittance,” demanded that all of it be sent to the Kuomintang government, which would then allocate its use. In practice, not a cent of these donations, not an ounce of these goods, ever reached the hands of the Eighth Route Army or the New Fourth Army. To break through the Kuomintang’s “unified planning and unified remittance,” the salvation groups in Shanghai tried every means they could — sending comfort missions and the like — to get medicine and other military supplies to the New Fourth Army. After Shanghai fell, Song Qingling and the other salvation leaders moved to Hong Kong. On June 14, 1938, the China Defence League, founded by Song Qingling, was proclaimed. Through the enormous influence she and He Xiangning had abroad, the League was able to make wide contact with progressive people in countries all over the world, among them many officials of the Hong Kong British government, bankers, and prominent figures in commerce, industry, and the cultural world. The League publicized to them the state of resistance and salvation work across China, described the heroic struggles of the army and people in the Communist-led resistance base areas, won their sympathy and support for the war, and carried its fundraising drives forward with tremendous energy. Patriotic overseas Chinese donated a great many goods, some of them earmarked specifically to relieve the Eighth Route Army and the New Fourth Army fighting so bravely at the front. After Guangzhou fell, all these goods piled up in warehouses in Hong Kong, with no way to ship them out. At that time the only link between Hong Kong and Chongqing was by air, and moving the overseas Chinese donations of resistance supplies by plane meant not only small loads but heavy freight charges. Through Liu Shaowen, He Xiangning sent a letter asking Chen Zhigao to go to Hong Kong and find a way to move these front-line resistance supplies out.

Why did they think of Chen Zhigao? Between 1937 and 1939, Zhigao worked in Shanghai as a lawyer, and drawing on his father’s eminent standing and influence in the French Concession, he not only gave warm support to every kind of resistance and salvation work but, through his friendly relations with the French, carried on people’s diplomacy, and together with friends in the international anti-fascist united front he brought off several things that ordinary patriotic resisters could never have managed. The chief of these were: (1) Under the name of the Association for the Support of Resistance to the Enemy, he chartered French steamers and carried many prominent people of both the Kuomintang and the Communist Party who had stayed on in Shanghai safely off to the great rear. (2) Through Father Rao, he dealt with the Japanese army command and repeatedly chartered ships flying the Red Cross flag to carry, batch by batch, able-bodied young refugees — who had applied to return home to farm but were in fact going to join the New Fourth Army’s resistance — safely to their destinations. (3) The Japanese command suddenly notified Mei, the French consul-general in Shanghai, demanding the suppression of the China Drama Society led by Comrade Yu Ling within the French Concession, and the expulsion of all its members from the Concession. After Liu Shaowen, A Ying, and Yu Ling talked it over, they had Zhigao go to consul-general Mei. The consul-general was a friend of Zhigao’s father; he told Zhigao that the Japanese said the drama society was full of “Communist troublemakers” and were therefore demanding that the Concession authorities take measures. Zhigao told the consul-general that the people in the drama society were patriotic young people and friends of his, and that there were no “Communist elements” among them. The consul-general then said he had decided to ignore the Japanese ultimatum, and he even had Zhigao bring the drama society into the Sino-French Friendship Association, of which the consul-general was president. So Yu Ling and the others were introduced into the Sino-French Friendship Association under Zhigao’s sponsorship. After that, besides the drama society, other resistance and salvation groups also used this connection to borrow the Association’s premises for their meetings and activities. (4) After Shanghai became a solitary island, the food supply for the several million residents of the Concession ran into difficulty; here too it was Zhigao and Father Rao who, dealing through Annam (Vietnam) and Siam (Thailand), then under French colonial rule, shipped the “Siam rice” grown there into the Shanghai Concession to relieve the grain shortage. So right up until the Japanese occupied the Concession in 1942, the residents of Shanghai were always able to eat fine imported Siam rice, unlike under Japanese rule, when they had to queue to buy moldy “ration rice” and suffer all kinds of humiliation. Because these several foreign affairs that passed through Zhigao’s hands were all carried off so successfully, Song Qingling and He Xiangning went out of their way to invite Zhigao to Hong Kong, to use the connections of the Vietnamese members within the Sino-French Friendship Association to open a sea route from Hong Kong to the port of Haiphong in Vietnam, and then link it to the Yunnan–Vietnam Railway running straight through to the great rear.

Zhigao had already agreed to open the southwestern transport line, and was in Shanghai making preparations — organizing a company, assigning personnel, and so on — when something happened that sent him hurrying off to Hong Kong in the spring or summer of 1939. At that time we were living on Fuxing Middle Road: the garden faced the street, and off to one side ran a small lane. For several days running, a wonton-vendor’s carrying-pole stall kept going back and forth, loitering between the lane and the street corner. Look closely and you could even see the collar of a white shirt showing beneath the man’s coarse cloth jacket — clothing an ordinary peddler could never possibly have. Recalling that many assassinations had already taken place, all of them aimed at progressive patriots, might this peculiar peddler be targeting our household? For safety’s sake, Zhigao decided to leave Shanghai for Hong Kong ahead of schedule. He went together with Pan Luyan, the manager of the Tongyi Company. After reaching Hong Kong, they used the name of the Zhengda Transport Company, a subsidiary of Tongyi, and, working with Liao Chengzhi and, on the Kuomintang side, Li Fang, Li Zhifu, Wu Tiecheng and others, joined together the Southwest Transport Company, the Taichang Company and others; going to Haiphong in Vietnam and drawing on the connections of old fellow-students there from Aurora University who had studied in France, they opened a sea-and-land through route from Hong Kong to Haiphong to Kunming to Chongqing, and signed a contract giving them the use of one railway car on the Yunnan–Vietnam Railway each week, thereby opening for our Party and for the progressive resistance forces a new transport line from the coast to the interior. This was no easy thing in those days. During his time in Haiphong, Zhigao had the honor of making the acquaintance of Chairman Ho Chi Minh. Chairman Ho gave him a seal he had carved himself — “Scholar in the Saddle” — to commemorate forever the friendship of the Chinese and Vietnamese peoples in their common fight against fascism. I was very surprised that he should have such a rare honor. He said: “You know I love a cup of wine, and when wine meets a bosom friend a thousand cups are too few. Chairman Ho asked after my wife, and I said that you had taken part in the Northern Expedition in 1926, that because you had long known the Mother of the Nation Madame Sun, and Madame Liao, and had won Madame Liao’s trust, she had entrusted you as her plenipotentiary representative in all matters, inside and out. When the nation is at stake, every common man bears responsibility; to be able to lend some strength to the soldiers at the front is my duty and my honor. He praised me as a bold and discerning ‘Scholar in the Saddle’ — truly, in a foreign land I had found a soul who knew me!”

Not long after this transport line was opened, Liu Shaowen conveyed to Zhigao a further suggestion from Comrade Zhou Enlai: that he accept the letter of appointment from the Chongqing Nationalist government and go to Qujiang in Guangdong (present-day Shaoguan City; after the fall of Guangzhou, the seat of the Kuomintang’s Guangdong provincial government) to serve as the special commissioner of the Central Relief Commission for the Ninth War Zone, with charge of relief work in the two provinces of Guangdong and Fujian. Through the Ninth War Zone, the transport lines of the southeast and the southwest could all be joined up: from Qujiang one could reach Guilin in Guangxi and go on by way of Guiyang to Chongqing, and one could also reach the southeastern regions of Fujian, Jiangsu, Zhejiang and Shanghai — so it was a pivotal region of the utmost importance. I too was ordered to draw a salary in Hong Kong under the title of “adviser” to the Relief Commission, tending to matters both inside and out. Zhigao had always prided himself, as a commoner, on holding high office in contempt, and had no wish to become a Kuomintang official. Liu Shaowen told him: it is not that we want you to become a Kuomintang official; it is that we want you to make full use of the official position the Nationalist government confers to support the work of resistance and salvation, to unite for resistance, to face the national crisis together. Zhigao gladly accepted the suggestion, at once went through the formalities of handing over the transport company, and without the least reluctance gave up this fine chance to “make a fortune” that others long for and cannot get. His three years of hard struggle afterward in Qujiang grew out of his reverence for Zhou Enlai and his wish to repay the kindness of being so well understood. His actual deeds proved all the more that he was a patriotic and progressive man who valued honor over wealth and who sincerely wished to unite with the Communist Party in the resistance.

On an evening in early May 1939, the “Wind and Rain Bookroom,” where A Ying was in charge of editing, printing and publishing the monthly Documents, was suddenly raided by Japanese-puppet agents. The room happened to be empty at the time, and they carried off every scrap of written material — they even took the ink bottles, perhaps meaning to have them analyzed! Ordinarily a clerk named Chen Shaoxian slept there on a floor-mat; he had come from the literacy training class run by the Tongyi Company, and under A Ying’s guidance his political consciousness had risen and he was very earnest in both work and study. Every day after work he would hide the important files and manuscripts in the special safe of Tongyi’s storage vault. It happened that a Tongyi workman had taken leave that day, and Chen Shaoxian had gone to sleep on that man’s mat — which was how he narrowly escaped. Early the next morning after the raid, Chen Shaoxian telephoned me and Comrade A Ying, telling us not to go in to the Tongyi Company: the “Wind and Rain Bookroom” had been destroyed. Later Chen Shaoxian went off with A Ying to the New Fourth Army; after the founding of New China he served in the Nanjing military and later transferred to civilian work in the localities. After this the monthly Documents was forced to suspend publication. On May 18, the Daily Translation Herald, along with the Wenhui Bao, the Shanghai Evening Post and the Sino-American Daily, was ordered to suspend publication for two weeks for having printed Chiang Kai-shek’s speech to the National Production Conference. Then the Japanese-puppet secret agents bought off Fees and Bonner, the publishers of the Daily Translation Herald, so that it could not resume after the suspension expired. In this perilous situation, Zhigao’s mother, fearing I was in danger, had me move for a while to Cao Bingsheng’s home to go into hiding. Because after Cao Bingsheng was assassinated by Japanese-puppet agents only his wife and daughter were left, and the agents were no longer paying much attention to their household, so it was rather safer to lodge at the Cao home. To the outside world it was given out that I was ill and in the hospital, and Mother Qian brought me my meals each day, keeping up the contact. During this time Zhigao was hurrying back and forth between Kunming and Vietnam; Mr. Chen Xunshe, head of the Shanghai branch of the Kuomintang’s Central Daily News (the younger brother of Chen Bulei), handed over the Central Daily News’s radio set and codebook to Zhigao, so that from Vietnam, Kunming and elsewhere he could keep in touch with the household in Shanghai by coded wireless. Chen Lifu’s cousin Chen Baohua was also in Shanghai at the time; during the Northern Expedition he had worked with Pan Hannian in the propaganda section of the General Political Department, and after returning from study in England he had become a Kuomintang official in Shanghai. Because of the second Kuomintang–Communist cooperation, he and Chen Xunshe both had contact with us in the solitary-island resistance work. I wired Zhigao, who was in Kunming, about the raid on and destruction of the “Wind and Rain Bookroom” and the forced suspension of Documents and the Daily Translation Herald. In the autumn of 1939, the Central Committee instructed me to leave Shanghai together with Liu Shaowen and move to Hong Kong. At that time I was a well-known figure in financial circles, with thousands upon thousands in funds passing through my hands to support resistance and salvation activities, yet I myself had no money. Even the fare to Hong Kong I raised by pledging a batch of Tongyi Company shares to Hu Yongqi’s insurance company (Hu and Luo Shuzhang were both among the founders of the “Tuesday Dining Club”), and I redeemed them only after the victory in the war.

Zhigao’s mother was an enlightened, warm-hearted old patriot. After Chen Gaoyong was arrested in 1932, she had helped support the living expenses of Chen’s first wife and their daughters; after the nationwide War of Resistance broke out, prominent people of every party, faction and walk of life in Shanghai often held meetings at our home to plan resistance and salvation together, and she would have Mother Qian shell lotus seeds and cook late-night suppers to entertain the guests warmly. In those days we often met deep into the night, and she would sit up with us all through it, then rise early the next morning to see to the household. She often exhorted her children that the common task of the whole nation now was to unite in resistance, defeat the Japanese invaders, and recover our land, so that everyone could go home and rebuild. When we celebrated her fiftieth birthday, Hu Egong (Nanhu) wrote out a birthday screen as a gift, and among those who signed it were Liu Yazi, Lin Gengbai, and A Ying; Liu Shaowen signed under the alias Liu Weimin. Worry over affairs of state and toil over affairs of the household caused her health to deteriorate rapidly. After I was ordered to leave Shanghai, she could not rest easy, and in the winter of 1939 she too came to Hong Kong, bringing Yunzhong. But after the old lady reached Hong Kong she could not adjust to the climate; her blood pressure was high and her heart weak, and having probably caught a chill on the way, she fell ill the moment she arrived.

With the nation in crisis and Zhigao already gone to work in Qujiang, I had no choice but to stay in Hong Kong, at once caring for the sick old lady and my own children and shouldering the work of the Hong Kong transfer station; at once maintaining the old southeastern transport line centered on Shanghai and joining it to the new sea-and-land route beginning at Hong Kong; and, moreover, under the title of “adviser” to the Nationalist government’s Central Relief Commission, receiving and arranging on Zhigao’s behalf, for the prominent public figures going from Shanghai to Hong Kong or on to the great rear. In those days there were many “high-class refugees” who had fled from the Shanghai solitary island to Hong Kong; most had left their property behind in Shanghai and carried little money with them, and so all had to accept subsidies from the Central Relief Commission. Beyond that, one had also to find ways, by the underground transport lines already opened, to move some of them safely on to the great rear. So during my time in Hong Kong I ran both special rescue and special relief, which came together into one continuous, interlinked special front.

As soon as I reached Hong Kong I went to see Du Yuesheng and asked him to come forward and rescue Qian Nashui, the editor-in-chief of the Daily Translation Herald, and Jin Xuecheng of its print shop, who had both refused the Wang puppet regime’s inducements to surrender and had behaved well. Du sent an urgent wire to his chief disciple Xu Caichen to come forward and handle it. Qian Nashui, after being bailed out by Chen Bulei’s brother Chen Xunshe, went on to become an editor-in-chief at the Central Daily News, and at the end of the 1940s left for Taiwan with the paper and his whole family. Jin Xuecheng, after his release, drew closer to the left-wing figures in the Local Association, and was a close friend of Wang Genzhong and Li Nanxiang. In the early days after Liberation, Jin Xuecheng served as deputy secretary-general of the East China Military and Political Commission.

In Hong Kong, Liu Shaowen was a member of the Chinese Communist Party’s Hong Kong–Macau Work Committee and concurrently head of the Hong Kong–Macau office of the Central Committee’s Transport Department, in charge of transport, liaison, confidential matters, the radio station, and funds. Besides keeping in touch with Shanghai, Shaoguan, Guilin, Hainan Island and other places, he also had to keep up the overseas transport line. Using Hong Kong as his base, he was constantly traveling between Shanghai and Chongqing, and each time he flew it was I who bought his plane ticket. Once, as he was taking a night flight from Kai Tak Airport to Chongqing and I was seeing him off, he even recited the lines from Su Dongpo’s “Shuidiao getou” — “May we live long, and share the moon though a thousand li apart” — to express our mutual comfort and care.

After Old Madame Pan reached Hong Kong, her illness grew worse by the day. In the summer of 1940, I went to Haiphong in Vietnam to wind up the affairs of the Zhengda Transport Company, and afterward had meant to fly on to Chongqing to report to and seek instructions from Comrade Zhou Enlai, when I suddenly received a telegram that the old lady was critically ill, and hurried back to Hong Kong. When I got home, though thanks to the venerable He Xiangning’s concern the Kuomintang Central Committee member and famous physician of Chinese medicine Mr. Peng Zemin and other Chinese and Western doctors had been called to consult together, the illness was too grave; the assembled physicians were at a loss, and there was no bringing her back. Seeing that things looked bad, I at once wired the ninth brother Chen Shijun in Guilin, the eighth brother Chen Yingu in Shanghai, and Chen Zhigao in Qujiang: “Mother critically ill, come to Hong Kong at once.” All three sons flew to Hong Kong, but after two weeks of anxious efforts to save her there was no reversing it, and the old lady at last departed this world on July 31, 1940 (the 27th day of the sixth month by the lunar calendar).

Old Madame Pan’s funeral was conducted with great solemnity. Representatives in Hong Kong of the Kuomintang central government and of Guangdong and Guangxi provinces, representatives of the local patriotic organizations in Hong Kong and prominent local figures, and the well-known patriots who had fled from Shanghai to Hong Kong, all took part in the memorial service. Everyone who came to the funeral signed the visitors’ book and left an address. After Hong Kong fell, it was by the names and addresses in this very visitors’ book that we sent people to rescue and escort many prominent figures to the great rear. The Communist Party Central Committee also specially deputed Liu Shaowen to come and pay his respects, but because he could not thereby expose my true identity or Zhigao’s special relationship with the Communist Party, he did not attend the public memorial held in Hong Kong; instead, before Chen Yingu and Chen Shijun took my daughter and escorted the coffin back to Shanghai for burial, at the time the Buddhist rites for the old lady were being held at Qingshan Temple in the Kowloon outskirts, he came to Qingshan Temple in the name of the Communist Party Central Committee’s Organization Department to offer his condolences, comfort Zhigao in his grief, urge him to put affairs of state first, and go back to Qujiang once the “full sevens” of mourning were done. Shaowen stayed a night at Qingshan Temple, spending almost the whole night “chatting” with Zhigao, assigning him new tasks: he was to use the official, legitimate standing of the Central Relief Commission to give secret support to the East River anti-Japanese guerrillas led by our Party, and he pointed out to Zhigao that this task was of the utmost importance for sustaining a long war of resistance. As soon as his mother’s funeral was seen to, Zhigao returned at once to Qujiang. By this time I had moved to Yuexian Lou on Kimberley Road, renting the two rooms and a hall downstairs — the rooms very large, the hall very small. We used one room and the hall; the other room was lived in by the famous poet Yang Yunshi, once secretary-general to Wu Peifu, and his wife, and it was there that Yang later died. I even put his manuscripts in order after his death, and Madame Yang went on living with me until Hong Kong fell.

In those days many prominent patriots of every circle were staying in Hong Kong: the Liu Yazi family lived near Du Yuesheng’s residence; Du Yuesheng lived on the second floor of the residence; the office of the Chinese Red Cross was set up downstairs in the Du residence, and the secretary-general of the Red Cross headquarters, Pan Xiao’e, and his wife — she a schoolmate of Song Meiling’s — were young, promising and very capable, and on close personal terms with Zhigao; the family of Zhang Shizhao, president of the national bar association and a Chongqing national political councilor, also remained in Hong Kong; and there were Zhang Zhongren, who had been education minister in the Northern Warlord era, Yang Yunshi who lived with us, the famous lawyer Qin Liankui, Wang Shao’ao who would be one of New China’s first vice-ministers of finance, and Ye Gongchuo who had been transport minister under the Northern government, and so on. Most of these prominent figures were already old people of sixty or seventy and above; though they were living idle in Hong Kong for the time, they still had much influence and drawing power. The Central Committee had me do some liaison work with these upper-stratum figures; those in financial difficulty at the time were sent a monthly living subsidy by Zhigao in the name of the Central Relief Commission. Men like Liu Yazi, Yang Yunshi and Zhang Zhongren were all masters of classical poetry, and I, a mere lover of poetry, dared not presume to trade verses with them, yet we got on very well in conversation. I even copied out poems for Zhang Zhongren.

At Du Yuesheng’s residence, a table was set every day for the guests who came. The prominent figures who had fled for the time from Shanghai and elsewhere to Hong Kong mostly lived in Kowloon, and they often came to the Du residence to sound out news of the resistance and the times; whether or not Du Yuesheng was at home, when the midday meal was served, a caller had only to sit down and eat. As they ate, people would talk in passing of the state of affairs. These veteran scholars and elders all had a keen political eye, and in their talk they all held that the ones who could truly strike at the Japanese invaders were the Communist-led Eighth Route Army and New Fourth Army. With such a political leaning, they were naturally the objects of our work of winning and uniting them in those years. Though they were regular guests at the Du residence, what they received were subsidies from the Relief Commission, not the personal favor of Du Yuesheng. Though I was often coming and going at the Du house, which aroused some people’s suspicion and drew a certain amount of gossip and slander, my conscience was clear. I was a Communist Party member, acting on the orders of the Party organization and Comrade Zhou Enlai to carry on upper-stratum united-front work in the guise of a non-party democratic figure. Zhigao was all the more a genuine non-party patriotic democrat, who had never joined any party or secret-society organization.

On Du Yuesheng’s fiftieth birthday, Wu Jufang, wife of Li Hanhun, chairman of the Guangdong provincial government, came to Hong Kong on behalf of the Guangdong provincial government to wish Du well. At that birthday banquet I met Wang Xinheng for the first time. I knew that Cai Shuhou, who worked so closely with us on our rescue operations, was doing the work of winning over Wang Xinheng in particular — the two were from the same district. Cai Shuhou was a Communist whose organizational affiliation lay with the Comintern branch; because he had studied electrical engineering while a student in Japan, he opened in Shanghai a “Shaodun Electric Company” as a cover, and at the same time supplied the underground Party with the electrical materials its clandestine radio stations needed. When I carried a letter to Shen Yanbing after the failure of the Great Revolution, I already knew of the Shaodun Company, but at that time Cai went by another name. It was in the joint activity to rescue the internationally known Noulens couple that I got to know Cai Shuhou, and we later became close friends.

Wang Xinheng and Chiang Ching-kuo had been good friends while studying in the Soviet Union; later Wang returned home to take part in the resistance and became an important figure in Chiang Ching-kuo’s “crown-prince faction” and in the Military Statistics Bureau. After getting to know Wang that time, I established contact with him too. I had long known that there were comrades of ours within Huang Yanpei’s Chinese Vocational Education Society, and that it was the Party that had arranged for them to close in politically on Du Yuesheng through the Society. In the 1980s, reading Comrade Xia Yan’s unofficial biography of Yang Du (Yang Du, one of the “six gentlemen of the Chouan Society” who had submitted a memorial urging Yuan Shikai to make himself emperor, was in his later years kept by Du Yuesheng, yet had secretly joined the Communist Party and done much work for it), I learned still more clearly that the Central Committee had long made arrangements, sending people to do the special work of winning over and splitting the secret-society forces. Even among Du Yuesheng’s disciples there were left, center and right. The right threw in with the Kuomintang’s Military Statistics agents; the left, like Zhu Xuefan, though he represented the yellow unions in the world labor organization, raised a bright progressive banner among Du’s disciples after the war broke out; the center were people doing business in Shanghai who needed a backer and so had no choice but to bow to Du as their “old man” — a rather numerous sort, and also objects of our work of winning and uniting. During the second anti-Communist upsurge, when General Ye Ting was arrested, the venerable He Xiangning raised funds to comfort Ye’s wife in her own name, and had me carry the subscription book to ask Du Yuesheng to sign and contribute, which also moved the fellow-provincials in Hong Kong to give; Du Yuesheng at once donated a thousand Hong Kong dollars. He took the subscription book and also signed to join the contribution on behalf of the Red Cross. When Elder Sister Deng came to Hong Kong as a Communist Party member to thank Song Qingling and the venerable He in person, Du Yuesheng laid on a banquet, and I attended as well. These facts are quite enough to shatter the groundless rumor that I was “a disciple of the Du gate and now an official’s wife who had forgotten her origins.” That I was jailed in Chongqing from 1942 to 1944 and happened to share captivity with General Ye Ting — this was Heaven having eyes, letting the gossip and slander collapse of themselves!

In Hong Kong, besides my title of adviser to the Relief Commission, I was also entrusted by the venerable He Xiangning to carry out, on her behalf, much open resistance and salvation work. Every week I went to the venerable He’s home, and she would always keep me for the midday meal. Hong Kong had a Chinese Women’s Temperance (Birth Control) Association, whose head was Liu-Wang Liming, wife of Dr. Liu Zhan’en, president of Shanghai’s University of Shanghai, who had been assassinated by Japanese-puppet agents; I had known her back in the Women’s Comfort Association in Shanghai. Though she was a Christian, in all objectivity and fairness she was the earliest pioneer of family planning in China, far earlier than Mr. Ma Yinchu. Her contribution in this field ought not to be belittled or effaced. Liu-Wang Liming made me chair of the South China branch of the Temperance Association. Zhu Libo of the Shanghai National Salvation Association came to Hong Kong at this time too, and I asked her to be the branch’s secretary-general. The branch’s premises were in the Kowloon outskirts, several dozen li from the city center, and Zhu Libo lived in the refugee shelter the South China branch had set up in Kowloon; her beloved daughter Shi Weici, who had come to Hong Kong with her, boarded at my home while attending middle school. The shelter had taken in sixty women refugees who had fled to Hong Kong after the fall of Guangzhou, and we organized them to knit socks, gloves, towels and the like, to relieve themselves through production and economize on the shelter’s expenses. All this practical work was done by comrades like Zhu Libo.

Li Fang, husband of my old comrade-in-arms from the Northern Expedition Wang Huazhen, was a diplomat who knew six languages; during the Northern Expedition he was superintendent of the Jianghan Customs, had dealings with Zhang Tailei, who was then interpreter to the Soviet advisers, and was on very deep terms with us. After the Kuomintang’s betrayal in Wuhan, it was this couple, together with Zhang Duhe and Elder Sister Zhao Wanhua, who devised the plan to disguise Comrade Dong Biwu as a pastor and get him safely out of Wuhan. Li Fang had served as China’s consul-general in Siberia in the Soviet Union and as a foreign-affairs negotiator for the Guangdong provincial government; after Guangzhou fell and the former Guangdong governor Wu Tiecheng became the Kuomintang Central Committee’s overseas department minister, Li was again transferred to Hong Kong to be the negotiator dealing with the British authorities. Because Li was head of the Hong Society, and was sworn brothers in that society with Ye Peng, the Wang puppet regime’s “defense minister,” Wu Tiecheng had Li Fang feign defection and burrow into the Japanese-puppet organization to obtain secret intelligence, becoming the Wang puppet government’s “ambassador” to Romania. This was only an empty title; in fact he never went abroad to take up the post. When Zhigao and I learned of it, we voiced our opposition, because in those days the common people hated traitors far more than they hated the Kuomintang; so I said to him that once you burrow in, there will be no explaining yourself when the time comes. He did not heed our advice, and I even urged that his wife, Wang Huazhen, divorce him! Over this, Zhigao and I made a special crossing from Kowloon to Hong Kong to see Wang Xinheng of the Military Statistics Bureau, to put it to Wang that we opposed sending Li Fang into the Japanese-puppet organization. Wang Xinheng said: “This is something the (Kuomintang) Central Committee has arranged — you two keep out of it!” We felt that the Li Fang couple had not only had a friendly, cooperative relationship with our Party back in the Northern Expedition at Wuhan, but that in opening up the overseas transport work he had taken part on behalf of the Taichang Company as well; and in the Shanghai solitary-island period, when the upper levels of both the Kuomintang and the Communist Party agreed to set up an intelligence-exchange group, Li Fang was the Kuomintang’s representative. For such a friendly-party figure who had cooperated with us in friendship twice over now to have to bear a traitor’s infamy by feigning defection — we felt very sorry for him, and could never quite feel it was right. But later events showed that it was truly a case of “how can you catch tiger cubs without entering the tiger’s lair”: not long after Li Fang’s feigned surrender to the Japanese puppets, our Communist intelligence group obtained from him the top-secret information that Japan was about to launch the Pacific War, and at once wired it to the Party Central Committee. The Kuomintang Central Committee received this major intelligence at the same time and told the U.S. government, but the Americans did not believe it was true. Song Meiling, however, would rather believe it than not, and flew at once to Hong Kong to bring her second elder sister Song Qingling to the great rear in Chongqing, sparing the “Mother of the Nation” in the nation’s eyes the hardship of exile and dust. This was both a contribution of our Kuomintang–Communist cooperative intelligence group and a sign that Song Meiling, though politically at odds with Song Qingling, could still cherish the bond of sisterhood and care for her second sister’s safety. Of course, making good preparation for the coming outbreak of the Pacific War was also very much to the advantage of our war against Japan and our recovery of lost land.

Unlike Song Meiling, Song Ailing, who was also in Hong Kong at the time, had a private plane parked at Kai Tak Airport, and had meant to leave only after receiving a batch of American aid supplies for China’s resistance; unexpectedly, on December 8, 1941, the Japanese army landed in the Kowloon outskirts, and it was only when the Hong Kong city area was about to fall on the 11th that she hurriedly flew off. But on her way to the airport she passed by He Xiangning’s residence, and though she knew full well that the venerable He was still in Hong Kong, she did not take her along in leaving for Chongqing. Though the two of them represented the left and right wings of the Kuomintang respectively and had a deep rift between them, in a national crisis of foreign invasion such conduct on her part could hardly escape people’s censure. The venerable He Xiangning was thereby stranded in fallen Hong Kong, until Zhigao sent people to bring her out.
