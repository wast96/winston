## The Yamanoo in Gion

Kyoto: forty great temples, five hundred small.

When, by the old calendar, the seventh month came and the season of the Bon festival, every great avenue and back-lane of the town filled all at once with the sound of the nenbutsu, the hand-gong, and the chanting of sutras.

"It stinks of Buddha,"

{j} said Toshizō, as if spitting the words out. The Bon of Bushū smelled of the earth, but it was never so gloomy and dispiriting as this.

"It's more than one can bear. Walk through the town and the very seams of your clothes seem to soak up the smell of incense,"

{j} said even Okita. In the Shinsengumi, of course, no memorial rites were held for the corpsmen when Bon came. On that head they were blithe and unconcerned. To save or be saved, their only god and Buddha was the sword at their own waist—that strain lay at the very bottom of the corpsmen's bellies.

On the morning of such a day, the corps had a new dead man. Word had come from the magistrate's office that one who seemed to be a corpsman had been found brutally cut down at Senbon-matsubara.

"Yamazaki, Shimada,"

{j} Toshizō called the men of the inspectorate.

"Go, if you will."

They set out.

Presently they came back and made their report to Toshizō in the vice-commander's room.

"The body was Akazawa Morito,"

{j} so they said. One stroke to the right shoulder from behind—that seemed the first wound. Then, from the front, a left diagonal cut, and two places on the neck. These last had been dealt, it seemed, after death, for they had not bled, and the white fat showed.

"I see.—"

Toshizō thought for a while. His eyes began, before long, to glitter, but he said nothing. The inspectors too seemed to grow uneasy, and,

{j} "in due course, when we have looked into it thoroughly—"

{j} they withdrew.

Toshizō went at once to Kondō's room next door.

"What is it,"

{j} Kondō said only that, and did not raise his face.

He was learning his letters. Teaching himself.

(Schooling at thirty.)

{j} Toshizō often teased him.

Kondō had, in his Kantō days, written a wretched hand; but since coming up to Kyoto—

{j} (this will not do for a commander of the Shinsengumi.)

{j} he had of a sudden taken it into his head to begin practicing. The one adornment of a man of the gentry was calligraphy, surely. If a man's hand was poor, that alone was often enough to have him ignored—so Kondō thought.

"Ho,"

{j} Toshizō peered in.

"You've grown a good deal better,"

"I had a good hand in me from the start,"

Kondō's practice was, from first to last, an imitation of Rai San'yō's style of hand. That Kondō should most have loved the calligraphy of this man of letters, the very fountainhead of the loyalist movement, is a curious thing.

"Toshi, take up the brush yourself,"

"I, is it,"

"You'll not stay a Bushū sweet-potato swordsman forever."

"I'm well enough as I am,"

"You are not. Calligraphy makes the man, I'm told,"

"That's a Confucian's lie,"

"You are too full of your own opinions; it won't do,"

"Bah—as if a man could be made by such empty daubing. I'll go my own way, in all things.—"

"Your own way is well and good. But—"

"My own way will do,"

"But it settles the spirit, mind,"

"Settle it, and where would I be? In an age of disorder like this, let your spirit settle carelessly and you'll take a bare blade in an instant. And you, Kondō—I'd not have you learn some strange mold like that and forget the mettle of a rough Kantō barbarian."

"Speaking of bare blades—"

{j} Kondō, avoiding a needless argument, changed the subject.

"the corpsman cut down at Senbon-matsubara this dawn was Akazawa Morito, I hear."

"Oh-ho,"

{j} said Toshizō, his face gone suddenly sleepy. But what ran swiftly through his mind was: who had put it, at a single leap, into the ear of the commander Kondō, rather than into his own, the vice-commander who held the inspectorate in hand? The order was wrong. To disorder the order was, for Toshizō, who counted the organization his own handiwork, the greatest evil in a breach of corps discipline.

"Who was it—this forward inspector who put it in your ear,"

"It was no inspector,"

"No inspector?"

Toshizō took the brush from Kondō's hand.

{j} "That is strange. I have just now heard the tale from the inspectors back from the scene. I came here meaning to pass it to you. And yet you, it may be, knew it sooner than the inspectors did. How does that come about?"

"I heard it a good while ago,"

"A good while ago—how long?"

"When I got up to go to the privy, so an hour ago, perhaps. That Noguchi—Kenji, the jokin—when I passed him in the corridor, Noguchi said it: Akazawa Morito has been done in by the Chōshū men, said he."

"By the Chōshū men? That Noguchi should know even the culprits is a strange thing,"

"How is this writing,"

{j} Kondō showed him a poem of San'yō's he had written out. It was a few lines from the long poem on the Honnō-ji.

"You can read it, I suppose,"

"I'll thank you not to make a fool of me,"

—Down west of Oisaka lies the road to Bitchū.

{j} Toshizō read it silently.

(It may be—)

Toshizō, running his eye slowly over the long poem in Kondō's clumsy hand,

{j} (the ones who cut down Akazawa—was it not the Serizawa faction, and not the Chōshū men? The enemy, it would seem, is at the Honnō-ji after all.)

It was intuition.

But Toshizō trusted his own intuition above any god or Buddha.

Noguchi Kenji was, with Niimi, Hirayama, and Hirama, one of Serizawa's right-hand men from the Mito days; a good arm, a good tongue, some learning, a certain low cleverness.

But he was shallow, hollow, a man like a fart. Toshizō could not like that kind at all.

***

Toshizō went back to his room, called a servant, and had tea made.

A tea-stalk stood upright.

"A lucky sign, sir,"

"They say so back home too; is it so in Kyoto as well,"

{j} Toshizō, peering into the bowl with a sour face,

{j} (what sort of luck, I wonder, is to come to a man like me?)

—Now, then: Akazawa Morito.

{j} Toshizō fell to thinking.

The dead Akazawa Morito, to tell the truth, Toshizō had not much liked either.

A rarity in the Shinsengumi, he was a Chōshū man who had quit his domain.

He was a man who, this sixth month, when the Shinsengumi's main body had gone out to Osaka, had come running to their temporary post at Tenma—the Kyōya, a boat-inn—and,

{j} —I have been insulted by men of my own domain.

{j} so he had stated his case. I have no wish to go back to a domain like that. Not the least intention of returning. Rather, let me join the Shinsengumi and spy out the movements of the Chōshū domain. I should like to be of such use, he said. Pressed, he proved to be a Kiheitai man risen from the townsfolk of Shimonoseki, no proper house-samurai; his loyalty to the domain had, from the first, been thin.

—Very well.

{j} said both Serizawa and Kondō.

So they set him under the inspectorate, made him to all appearances no part of the Shinsengumi, and had him go on frequenting the Chōshū mansion in Kyoto as before.

Akazawa brought back two or three pieces of intelligence, and these were so exact that he seemed, after all, not the spy planted by Chōshū they had first suspected. (For of late two or three men had joined as Chōshū spies, and there had been a hunt within the corps, with such doubtful men as Mikura Isetake and Arakida Samanosuke cut down.)

But Akazawa—

This man had, in his purse, ample money passed to him by the Shinsengumi.

And so he would often take Chōshū samurai and Tosa domain-leavers along to the pleasure quarters—Gion, Shimabara—and bring to Toshizō the intelligence that slipped from the Chōshū men's mouths in such places.

Now, Akazawa's intelligence had an unlooked-for by-product.

When one caroused in Gion or Shimabara, he said, one generally came face to face with men of the Shinsengumi. And these were Serizawa Kamo and his faction; the Kondō lot, having no money, scarcely showed themselves.

—Ho, now that is interesting.

{j} and Toshizō took an interest in that side of it.

"Akazawa—what manner of carousing is it,"

"Ah, well—"

{j} it is a shocking thing, said Akazawa. To bilk the reckoning for their pleasures seemed the least of it. Worse for the house-master was that Serizawa, a violent drunk, would fly into a rage when the drink took hold, smash the vessels, and set upon the guests in the next room.

For this reason a certain house in Gion, they said, had come to be shunned not only by townsfolk but by the liaison officers of the domains' Kyoto mansions, and had gone as dark as a snuffed lamp.

(So it is, after all.)

{j} Toshizō had heard as much, too, from a senior official of the Aizu domain, the Shinsengumi's overseers.

It was when Kondō and Hijikata had dined with the Aizu liaison officer Toshima Kihee and the rest at a restaurant in Sanbongi.

"Master Kondō,"

{j} said Toshima Kihee.

"In the capital, however high a man's office, let him be hated by even one of these three—Gion, the Hongan-ji, the Chion-in—and he will fall from his post. Did you know it?"

"No—I am quite in the dark,"

"And Master Hijikata?"

"Who knows,"

Toshizō set down his cup. Toshima went on.

"It is a maxim of worldly wisdom the Shoshidai and the local officials have sung down the generations: monks and beautiful courtesans may have the favor of who knows what great house, and their whispered slanders reach, unlooked-for, into high places. To tell the truth, my own lord—"

Kondō's face went taut. The Kyoto Protector, the Aizu Middle Captain Matsudaira Katamori?

"—knows the whole of Master Serizawa's conduct better than we do ourselves."

"Hm."

"Both of you, gentlemen,"

{j} said Toshima Kihee, with a subtle expression.

"I will say no more. Only take this one thing well to heart, I beg you."

"Understood,"

{j} said Kondō.

On the way back, Kondō said to Toshizō,

{j} "I gave so understanding an answer; but what Toshima said—what did it mean?"

"It means: cut down Serizawa Kamo."

"But, Toshi—Serizawa is, when all's said, a commander of the Shinsengumi; and, that aside, he passes for a fervent patriot whose expel-the-barbarian preaching has rung through the realm. May one cut him down in the dark, just so?"

"Where there is guilt, cut. Where there is cowardice, cut. He who disorders the corps' law, cut. He who defiles the corps' name, cut. There is no other way to set the Shinsengumi as heavy as Mount Fuji."

"Toshi, let me ask,"

{j} Kondō drew in his neck as if in jest.

"If I myself should touch on one of those four, would you cut me too?"

"I would,"

"You'd cut me, Toshi?"

"But in that hour my own life, the life of Hijikata Toshizō, would end as well. I would cut my belly and die beside your corpse. Sōji—Okita—would die too. The Tennen Rishin-ryū, the Shinsengumi—that would be their end. Kondō—"

"What is it,"

"You are the supreme commander. I'd not have you think of yourself as a man of flesh. Never proud, never unruly—I would have you be the mirror of the warriors of the realm."

"I know it,"

Such a thing had passed between them.

Afterward, through Akazawa, Toshizō got wind of all manner of the commander Serizawa's misdeeds.

Forced loans, killings for a slight—and the worst instance, this not from Akazawa's reports but a matter that set all Kyoto in an uproar: Serizawa, taking his faction along, went to extort from the house of Yamatoya Shōbei at Yoshiyachō on Ichijō, and, being refused,

{j} —then we'll burn them out.

{j} and, setting the corps' cannon on the Ichijō avenue, heated iron balls and fired them into the storehouse one after another, until they had wrecked the whole storehouse and withdrew.

Kondō, that whole day, kept his paper doors shut, showed his face to no corpsman, and did nothing but practice his writing. It must have been that he could scarcely stomach it.

—The inspector Yamazaki Susumu came back.

It was the matter of Akazawa Morito.

"I have it, near enough,"

{j} said this dutiful young man. Yamazaki was the son of a famous acupuncturist at Kōraibashi in Osaka; he could use the sword, and the staff. But above all, being city-bred, he was quick-witted—a man well suited to the inspectorate, one might say.

"For certain he was at the Sumiya in Shimabara the night before, carousing. He was with several Chōshū men."

"Hm?"

{j} Toshizō was disappointed.

"He was with Chōshū men for certain?"

"There's no mistake. The Chōshū samurai Kusaka Genzui and four others."

"A big fish,"

"Dead drunk, he left Shimabara at the hour of the Dragon. So much is clear. After that, likely, he was led out to Senbon-matsubara and there cut down."

"Wait. Did he leave together with Kusaka and the rest?"

"Yes,"

"You're sure?"

"If you wish, I'll go and make certain,"

"No, never mind,"

From dusk Toshizō made ready. A haori of ro silk, hakama of Sendai-hira, and, for swords, the long Izumi-no-kami Kanesada and a Horikawa Kunihiro short-sword.

He went to the Sumiya in Shimabara. Once, when he had gone up with Kondō, he had taken his pleasure with a tayū called Katsuragi. The woman seemed to have grown quite fond of Toshizō, and had since kept pressing him to come, sending old poems by the hand of the serving-maids.

This night he took his pleasure with this Katsuragi-tayū. Toshizō could not drink much.

He sat sullen and silent.

The tayū too seemed a little at a loss with him, and,

{j} "will you play at sugoroku, maybe,"

{j} and she brought out a gold-lacquered board fit for a great lord's furnishings; but Toshizō would not so much as look at it.

"Is it your stomach, maybe, that pains you,"

"I have a thing to ask of you,"

"What sort?"

"A boorish errand,"

{j} and he told her briefly the matter he wished to ask about.

"Now, that's a hard one,"

{j} The tayū laughed it off. This was a fairyland, where by unwritten law the business of the floating world was neither spoken of nor brought in.

"No good, then,"

"It cannot be done,"

And yet the tayū rose quietly and whispered in the ear of a serving-maid she was close with.

It was learned.

That night Akazawa had left together with Kusaka and the other Chōshū samurai, but Kusaka and his party were in palanquins. Akazawa Morito was on foot. If so, one might take it that by the time they left the great gate of Shimabara they had already parted. So Toshizō would have it.

But an unexpected thing came to light. Serizawa and his right-hand man Niimi Nishiki, too, had been carousing at the Sumiya that night, and had left, they said, almost directly after.

A light rain had begun to fall. Serizawa and Niimi borrowed umbrellas and a lantern, and at this Niimi,

{j} "did Akazawa go out with a lantern?"

"Yes, sir,"

{j} the manservant nodded.

"A Sumiya lantern, I take it,"

"Just so,"

{j} such was the exchange he had had with the manservant, they said.

(I see.)

{j} Toshizō thought it over. Beside Akazawa Morito's body at Senbon-matsubara there had lain a lantern bearing the Sumiya's crest.

A night some days after.

That evening Toshizō went up, without warning, into a Gion house of assignation, a restaurant called the "Yamanoo."

"Official business,"

{j} and he stilled the master and the serving-maids.

"Master Niimi Nishiki, commander of the Shinsengumi, should be taking his pleasure here. Which room is it?"

"Y-yes,"

{j} the master's legs had given way beneath him, they said.

"The-the detached room, sir,"

Toshizō swiftly made his dispositions, and set the men who had come with him—Okita Sōji, Saitō Hajime, Harada Sanosuke, Nagakura Shinpachi—in ambush in the inner garden that faced the detached room on the south.

"Master—any who make an outcry, any who raise their voice, I cut down."

Toshizō handed his long sword to the master and crossed the corridor alone, at his ease. In his hand he carried another long sword.

It was a keepsake of Akazawa Morito's.

On the paper screen, two shadows.

The sound of plucked strings could be heard. One of the shadows was a geisha.

The other shadow, by its great topknot, one could tell: Niimi Nishiki. Serizawa's follower from the Mito days, his sword the same Shintō Munen-ryū of the same school as Serizawa's, and he held the full transmission.

Of Niimi's skill, Toshizō had once had a taste, in a bout at the barracks dōjō. With the shinai one might call them evenly matched.

"Who's there,"

{j} Niimi thrust the geisha aside and came up onto one knee.

"It's me,"

{j} and Toshizō, with the chape of his long sword, slid the paper screen sharply open.
