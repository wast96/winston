## Chapter 17

From that time on Helen did indeed practise her singing every day. But coming out from her practice she always sought us out, and we lived our gay, noisy life as before. If some difference must be named, it was that Helen, for her singing's sake, now kept a slight restraint in food and drink and hours: she had once learned to take a little wine, and now drank none at all; she had once learned to smoke a cigarette now and then, and now would absolutely not; she had once often made merry with us till daybreak, and now always went home at about one o'clock. Mei Yingzi was like a guardian to her, forever reminding her of her many prohibitions and bidding her keep them. Sometimes, loath to leave the dance-hall, she did not want to go home; but at a word of reminder from Mei Yingzi she would very naturally obey.

Still I kept them company; but once home again I felt always a grief and remorse past telling. Sometimes I refused them over the telephone, but Mei Yingzi would drive round to fetch me, telling me Helen would be lonely without me. In truth that loneliness was only for want of one supporting player in the troupe, not that I counted for anything in her life. My reason, at first, for heeding Mei Yingzi and being with them every day had been wholly to lift Helen out of her wretchedness and turn her interest back to singing. Now that another power had taken Helen in hand and set her working at her singing, my sacrifice had become meaningless, and I strove with all my might to shake them off. At last I hit on a way of escape; I set everything in order, and one day at a gathering I said:

"In three days I must make a trip back to my native place."

"Back to your native place?" Helen was the first to ask.

"There are many things at home for me to see to," I said.

"Let us all go together," said Stephen eagerly. "We'll all go and make a holiday of it for a few months."

This startled me greatly, but in the end I held myself in and said with a smile:

"By all means—only my country is not Hangzhou; there's nothing there to make a holiday of."

"Can you not go a little later, after Helen's concert is over?" said Mei Yingzi. "Then we might all go and stay a few days together."

"No," I said; "the sooner I go the sooner I return—I'm sure I can be back by the time of Helen's concert."

"Will it take so many days?" said Helen.

"Yes," I said; "I've not been home in ten years, and have many things to see to."

Of the company only Bai Ping smiled and said not a word. Helen seemed to feel for me some wistful reluctance past telling; she made as if to speak, and then did not. Mei Yingzi said:

"Can you not put off going?"

"It can't be helped," I said. "Can you not lead your merry life all the same?"

"Can you not stay for Helen's sake?" said Stephen.

"For Helen's sake I mean to go early and come back early—come what may, I'll have my seat at her concert."

"That won't do," said Mei Yingzi. "You're to bear the greater part of the outside arrangements for the concert."

"With you here," I said with a laugh, "need I trouble myself over such things?"

"Wait till my concert is over," said Helen; "I'll go to the country with you."

"We'll all go," said Stephen; "we'll see you there and see you back."

"You don't know how things are at my home," I said. "Do you think I want to go myself? Searched by the Japanese coming and going—how inconvenient it is; but there's truly no help for it!"

My words brought them round at last, but they insisted I be back a week before the concert.

On all this Bai Ping never said a word, but smiled serenely.

That night we were dancing at the Paramount, and as Mei Yingzi was about to leave for home Bai Ping said to me:

"Will you stay on a while for me?"

"Don't you want to go home yet?" I said with a smile.

"…" Bai Ping smiled at me, and said to Stephen:

"Stephen, will you see Mei Yingzi and Helen home?"

"If you're all still in the mood for it," said Helen, "I'll stay with you too."

"No," said Bai Ping with a smile; "that won't do—you ought to go home early; tomorrow morning you're to go to Professor Paci's to practise."

"Well, how much longer will you play, then? Why so keen tonight?" asked Mei Yingzi.

"I mean to go on to the gambling-house and gamble the night through," said Bai Ping.

"And at dawn walk to Xujiahui for the seven o'clock Mass," said Stephen with a laugh.

"…" Helen fell silent.

"That is a programme for the two of you," said Mei Yingzi. "We'll go on ahead, then." And rising as she spoke, she settled that we should meet the next afternoon at the DD's Café.

Stephen went out with Mei Yingzi and Helen, and as she left Helen said in my ear:

"You needn't go back—don't go."

I smiled at her. Watching the shadows of the three of them vanish at the door, I said:

"Are we really to go from the gambling-house to the church again?"

"Have you no wish to play it over once more?"

"I rather thought you'd long since forgotten the taste for it."

"It is no taste for pleasure," she said. "It is self-rescue." And rising again she said: "Wait for me a moment; we'll go at once."

I paid the bill, came out with Bai Ping, and got into a car; she gave the driver an address, and I said:

"What—are you going home?"

"Yes," she said; "I must go home a moment."

"I've some money on me still—there's no need to go home."

"Tonight I mean to gamble big," she said with a laugh, and told the car to drive on; but when we reached her home she paid the fare, and I said:

"What—won't you have him wait?"

"I want to change my clothes," she said; "we can call another later."

So I went up with her, into her silver room; she bade me sit and gave me a cigarette, and then went into the bathroom. I sat on the silver sofa, savouring the silver tenderness all about me—when suddenly a startling scarlet on the silver coverlet broke in upon the peace of my sight. I remembered it for Mei Yingzi's dress; but how had it come here? I pondered it for the space of two cigarettes, and then Bai Ping came out, all her powder and rouge washed away, changed into a black cotton cheongsam and soft-soled cloth shoes. A little surprised, I said:

"Aren't you meaning to go out after all?"

"Do you still want to go to the gambling-house?"

"I want to go from the gambling-house to the church again."

"And then back from the church to the gambling-house."

So saying, she went out and poured two cups of tea, and brought a little cake, and said:

"Now let me have a quiet talk with you."

She smiled, and sat down; she seemed a little weary, and closed her eyes a moment—which made me think of how she had fallen asleep on the train coming back from Hangzhou, and of the likeness of her I had drawn then, a likeness I remembered tucking afterward into a book, though which book I could not call to mind. But now she roused herself at once, her face grew very grave, and her two great eyes shot a straight, upright light. She said:

"You are willing to say that Bai Ping is your best friend."

"Of course I am willing."

"Then say it."

"Bai Ping is my best friend."

"Then are you willing to say that you will be forever faithful to her, as she is faithful to you?"

"I am willing."

"Then say it."

"Forever faithful to her."

"Good." Then, in a low, plain, distinct voice, Bai Ping said: "When, then, do you go back to the country?"

"…" I hesitated, and said: "The day after tomorrow."

"Is it for affairs at home?"

"…" I was drinking my tea, my eyes on Bai Ping.

"I'll tell you," said Bai Ping. "By what I know, you are lying still."

I raised my head and looked at her; she was pressing me hard with a grave gaze, her eyes brimming with a moist sincerity, and she said:

"I do not want a friend of mine to treat me so."

"Then…"

"I won't expose you," she said, leaning back on the sofa; "say it yourself."

"Forgive me, Bai Ping," I said.

"Go on," she said, her eyes closed, leaning serenely on the sofa.

"I must leave the gambling-house for the church," I said; "I could not but lie."

"But why to me?" she said. "What is it, then, you mean to do?"

"I've rented a room in an apartment house on Route Prosper Paris. I mean to hide away." "When do you mean to move in?"

"The day after tomorrow."

"Then will you move in together with me?"

"You mean…"

"Let me ask you," she said, smiling like a lily just opening: "can you guess how I knew your going home was a lie?"

"By your cleverness."

"Do you think Mei Yingzi is stupider than I?"

"In some part, perhaps."

"No." She shook her head. "Did you not engage that room a week ago?"

"Yes." I was surprised.

"Is the rent not 340 dollars a month?"

"Yes," I said; "but how do you know?"

"Did you not pay two hundred dollars' deposit?"

"Yes." I was truly surprised now. "But how do you know it?"

"Is the place not laid out the same as this one?" she said. "Only with one more room than here."

"Yes," I said. "Have you been there, then?"

"Do you know who the landlord is?"

"A friend of yours, surely," I laughed. "But I did not meet the landlord that day—I dealt only with one of their people inside."

She rose slowly, went to the desk, pulled open a drawer, took out a name-card, flicking it with the fingers of her left hand, and came and handed it to me. It was the very card I had left with the owner of the other place, with the words "deposit paid, two hundred dollars" written on it before their eyes. Bai Ping went back to her former seat and said:

"I am your landlord."

"You?"

"Yes!"

"You mean that place too is rented by you?"

"Does it surprise you?"

"Of course," I said; "then it's your…"

"You mean my paramour?"

"Your household."

"To tell you the truth," she said, "I too am meaning to move."

"Move where?"

"Just so," she said. "I'm changing places with my friend; that place is a little larger."

"And she has agreed to it?"

"Of course," she said, and at once changed to a mischievous tone: "But she said she had already let one room a few days ago. I told her we need only return the deposit twice over. Then I looked at your name-card…"

"And so you meant to let that room to me after all."

"I wondered greatly at the time how you should come to be renting a room. I thought there must be something behind it—perhaps you were renting for a friend—and only today do I understand your purpose."

"I truly want to be free of this life of engagements and society."

"But what of Helen?"

"What of Helen?"

"Her gift."

"Her gift has become an ornament to her life, and her life an ornament to vanity."

"Do you not, then, wish her to become an ornament to your life?"

"And the ornament of my life is my work."

"Then come and move into my place—but on the condition that no one is allowed to come and see you."

"Very well—but what of you?"

"I not only let no one come to see me—I tell no one at all my address."

"And why is that?"

"Because too many people come to see me here."

"Too many?"

"Not so very many, really," she said, knitting her brows of a sudden; "but there are a few who've grown used to coming, and come often."

"Am I one of them?"

"Do you come often?"

"Some tiresome dancing-patron, is it?"

"Do you suppose I haven't even the art of putting off a man I don't wish to meet?"

"A woman, then?" I said. "And what does a woman matter?"

"Do you still not know I'm a star dance-hostess?" Her mischievous smile heaped itself very high.

"Why speak so, again?"

"Because I am a dance-hostess," she said, in a tone as though disputing; "all men are my patrons, and women are my enemies."

"What do you mean by that?"

"It's a joke," and she really laughed.

I had nothing to say, and we drank our tea in silence; her laughter melted into the silver air and turned to a plain, ordinary calm. My mind was still and clear through and through. Just then I felt a sudden wish to read Tao Yuanming's poems, as though I were in my own home, and looked about me as if for the book—when that glaring red spoiled the calm of my mind and disturbed the silver quiet, and I could not help asking: "Is that your dress?"

"Whose but the far-blazing Mei Yingzi's, of course."

"The sun shines forever over all mankind," I said. "Does she come often?"

"Often," she said; "sometimes she even stays here."

"And do you often go to her place too?"

"Often," she said; "and I've stayed at her place as well."

"I hadn't known you'd become such close friends."

"Perhaps," she laughed coldly; "perhaps the best of enemies."

"Because the two of you love the same man at once?"

"You think…"

"That way you would be the best of enemies—rivals in love."

"Not so," she laughed; "but I won't hide it from you—my moving is in fact to get away from her."

"How so?" I was surprised. "Then you'll have no more to do with her?"

"I'll not let her come to me."

I was eating my cake, but my mind ran all the while on this strange affair; yet I could put no further question, and only said:

"It's strange to me that in all these many meetings I never once heard the two of you speak of your dealings with each other."

"Perhaps neither of us, because the other did not bring it up, was willing to be the first to."

"I don't understand."

"It is well not to understand." She rose of a sudden and said: "Now you had better go home."

I looked at my watch: it was past three. I stood up. She said:

"Are you truly resolved to move?"

"Of course."

"Then be sure, whatever you do, not to tell anyone the address."

"Of course."

"Then the day after tomorrow bring over the books, manuscripts and things you need," she said. "I'm sure I can give you a setting fit for your work."

She went to the passage and took up the telephone to call me a car; I took my leave and went down, the riddle of her and Mei Yingzi floating still in my mind. There was not a soul on the road, and a desolate feeling stole over my heart, and weariness followed close upon my brain; I sprang into the car, and gazing at the empty streets, I felt I would rather not be troubled any longer by their riddles, and thought of the work I would do once I had moved to my new lodging.
