### Communications Chief "Zeng Peihong" — Li Qiang

### Li Qiang, Founder of the Party's Communications Work

The Central Special Section's Fourth Branch, the wireless communications branch, was set up somewhat later than the others, to meet the needs of a developing revolutionary situation. (It was first called the liaison branch, and soon after its founding was renamed the wireless communications branch.)

Around the time of the Chinese Communist Party's Sixth National Congress in 1928, the Party's work in the White areas was gradually reviving. Armed uprisings kept breaking out; the base areas and the Red Army kept expanding, and one Soviet regime after another was established. The Party Central in Shanghai badly needed to break through the enemy's blockade and to strengthen its leadership over the several Red regions and the local Party committees. Until then, all communication within the Party had depended on couriers traveling on foot, and moving documents was extremely difficult. A single round trip by a secret courier between Shanghai and Jiangxi took more than two months. It was not only slow but unsafe: if a courier ran into trouble on the road, there was the added danger of secrets leaking. To guard against such leaks, the Central Secretariat had set up a special reagents office to handle invisible-ink documents. Documents were distributed by printing the Central texts in invisible ink on the backs of paintings and calligraphy, on handkerchiefs, or on the reverse of the pages of thread-bound books, to be developed with a chemical once they arrived. Such backward means plainly could no longer meet the needs of the new struggle.

The Central Committee therefore urgently needed radio stations to keep in touch with the organizations across the country. By the time the Sixth Congress met in 1928, the task of building them had been set out. Preparation began that autumn. Building stations had not originally fallen to the Central Special Section, but when the Central Committee had special tasks that other departments were in no position to carry out, those tasks were commonly handed to the Section. And so the Section took on this most important and most difficult job, setting up for it the Fourth Branch, charged with building the radio stations.

The head of the Fourth Branch, Li Qiang (Zeng Peihong), was the founder of the Chinese Communist Party's radio-communications work and a founder of broadcasting in New China as well. In recognition of his achievements in the theory and practice of radio electronics, he was elected a member of the technical-sciences division of the Chinese Academy of Sciences in 1955. He was also a leading figure in the people's munitions work of the revolutionary war and an outstanding leader on the foreign-trade and foreign-economics front.

Li Qiang (1905–1996), originally named Zeng Peihong, courtesy name Youfan, went by the alias Zeng Peihong and was also known as Zeng Zongda. A native of Changshu county in Jiangsu, he was born into a scholarly family; his grandfather had been a Qing-dynasty provincial graduate and his father a licentiate. At six he entered a private school, finished primary school in his home district, and in 1920 went to study at Zongwen Middle School in Hangzhou. He read hard and did brilliantly, a model student — "developed in every respect, in moral character, learning, and physique," as the phrase now goes. But under the influence of the new thinking that came with the May Fourth Movement, he threw himself into shaking off the old conventions and the feudal code of ritual, and in his second year, in a classroom essay, he attacked the outworn customs of that code. The tutor teaching the class held this to be gross disrespect toward the old rites and toward the Sage Confucius, and he was expelled.

Just then he received encouragement and help from a primary-school classmate, Zhou Nai, who was in Shanghai searching out books. On the strength of his excellent middle-school marks in mathematics, physics, and chemistry, he was admitted as a third-year new student to the middle-school division of Nanyang Road Academy in Shanghai (later renamed Donghua University). At the time this was one of the few schools in Shanghai funded and run by Chinese. It had a modern atmosphere that made no cult of revering Confucius, and teaching methods that took more care to develop each student's individuality and talent — all of which was a comfort to Li Qiang. Many of the teachers had studied in Britain and America and taught mathematics, physics, chemistry, world history, and geography in English. His English teacher, Niu Huizhu, taught with such life and spirit that Li Qiang laid a solid foundation in the language here. On graduating from middle school he earned two diplomas, one in Chinese and one in English, and went on to become a student in the civil-engineering program of the school's university division.

In his university years Li Qiang made a great leap forward in his thinking. As he himself put it: "When the May Thirtieth Movement broke out in 1925, I was a young student of civil engineering at Donghua University in Shanghai. In those days the nation's peril and the disasters of our people pressed on our hearts like a mountain, so heavily we could scarcely breathe. And the flame of the anti-imperialist, anti-feudal struggle kindled by the May Fourth Movement still burned in the hearts of us young people. In January 1924, Sun Yat-sen presided in Guangzhou over the Kuomintang's First National Congress and set out the Three Great Policies of alliance with Russia, alliance with the Communists, and aid to the peasants and workers. The two parties, Kuomintang and Communist, achieved their first cooperation, and this brought people hope; not a few young people joined the Kuomintang as a result."

The man who brought Li Qiang into the Kuomintang, in May 1924, was Ye Chucang, a Chinese-language teacher at China University, a member of the Kuomintang Central Committee and head of its Shanghai Executive Department.

Li Qiang soon saw that Ye Chucang was a thoroughly reactionary figure of the Kuomintang right, and he took his stand firmly with the left. The two who influenced him most at this time were two Communists on the Shanghai Executive Department — Yun Daiying, secretary of its propaganda department, and Zhang Yuan, secretary of its organization department. Li Qiang often went to Shanghai University to hear political lectures, and the speeches of certain Communists inspired him deeply. He joined the Communist Youth League in June 1925 and in July, at the Party's Caojiadu branch in Shanghai, was transferred into full Party membership. In November he was appointed secretary of the Caojiadu Youth League, and in December was co-opted as an alternate member of the League's Shanghai district committee. After the split between the Kuomintang and the Communists, the rightist Ye Chucang went so far as to announce in the newspapers that Li Qiang was expelled from the Kuomintang. This was the second expulsion Li Qiang endured, and both expulsions marked leaps in his thinking. It was in the high tide of the May Thirtieth Movement that Li Qiang set out on the revolutionary road and, step by step, became a professional revolutionary.

In early 1926 Li Qiang became secretary of the Pudong Youth League in Shanghai. Here he organized the workers, and he set up a charity hospital and a charity school for them as well. On the one hand he helped the workers with their difficulties; on the other he gave them supplementary schooling to raise their cultural level and political awareness. His work drew praise from Ren Bishi, secretary and head of the organization department of the Youth League's Central Committee. That June, Li Qiang became secretary of the Youth League in Wusong district; in July, Luo Yinong, secretary of the Party's Shanghai district committee, transferred him back. By then the Northern Expeditionary Army had entered Jiangxi, and the Shanghai district committee was preparing an armed uprising. It wanted Li Qiang to make explosives and hand grenades for the emergency. He had never made weapons, but drawing on the chemistry he had studied, he bought carbolic acid, nitric acid, and other materials from a chemicals company, compounded them to the right proportions, and produced yellow explosive. Because conditions were lacking, the grenades did not come off; but by other means they still got hold of plenty of them.

From October 1926 to February 1927 the Shanghai workers' first two armed uprisings both failed for want of preparation and against a hopelessly unequal balance of forces. But the third armed uprising, led on March 21, 1927, by Zhou Enlai and others, at last succeeded. The explosives and detonating charges Li Qiang had tested before the uprising, and the grenades he had bought, were all put to use.

On the eve of the armed uprising, Luo Yinong sent Li Qiang back to Changshu to build up the Party organization. There he recruited a batch of Communists and set up the Party's Changshu special branch. In February 1927 Li Qiang became its secretary. In March the National Revolutionary Army's Sixty-second Regiment of the Twenty-first Division (its colonel was Chen Cheng) moved into Changshu and wiped out the collapsing forces of the warlord Sun Chuanfang. Zou Renyi, a Communist Li Qiang had recruited, served as chairman of the Kuomintang's Changshu county party headquarters, and most of the Party's work was done through him. A provisional administrative committee was also set up for Changshu county, chaired by a local enlightened gentry-man, Zhen Nanshan, with Li Qiang as an administrative commissioner. The Kuomintang county headquarters in Changshu mobilized the masses, arrested and dealt with three of the worst local bullies and evil gentry, and organized the masses into trade guilds and a general labor union, along with a workers' picket corps, a peasant association, a women's association, and more.

After the April Twelfth counterrevolutionary coup, the Party's Changshu special branch went underground and decided that Li Qiang should leave Changshu at once. On April 4, Li Qiang slipped back to Shanghai. Through a storm of blood he found the Central Committee's offices; the Central was then in the middle of moving to Wuhan. Acting on Luo Yinong's instructions, Li Qiang finished winding up the affairs of the Central offices and moved on to Wuhan.

As has been told, in Wuhan Li Qiang took part in the work of the special-services branch under the Central Military Commission that Zhou Enlai directed, serving as head of its special-services section. After the "August 7" conference, the Central Committee moved from Wuhan back to Shanghai. Li Qiang returned to Shanghai as well and joined the work of the Central Special Section, which Zhou Enlai had proposed and personally led; he was one of the Section's most active figures.

### Building the Party's First Transceiver

At the Chinese Communist Party's Sixth National Congress, held in Moscow from June to July 1928, it was decided to begin building wireless-communications facilities in Shanghai, the better to strengthen the Central Committee's leadership of and ties with the local Party committees. That October the Central Special Section's wireless communications branch took on the task. Radio was still a new thing in China at the time, and because the Kuomintang government controlled and obstructed it, the Party had to build transmitters itself and train its own operators — a formidable task in terms both of technical cadres and of material conditions. Not long after Zhou Enlai returned to Shanghai from the Sixth Congress in October, he decided to have Li Qiang develop a wireless transceiver and to send Zhang Shenchuan to learn the technique of sending and receiving. Zhou Enlai spoke with the two of them himself.

When Zhou Enlai proposed that Li Qiang undertake the work, Li Qiang felt daunted. At university he had studied civil engineering — building railways and bridges. He had never had anything to do with telecommunications and had never so much as seen a transceiver. The reactionaries kept radio equipment under very tight control, and there was not a single Chinese-language book on the subject to be found in the bookstores. Zhou Enlai said: if there were no books in Chinese, he could read English ones; his English was good, and he was perfectly capable of teaching himself. Zhou Enlai knew that to make the weapons needed for the Shanghai workers' armed uprising Li Qiang had once worked painstakingly at manufacturing yellow explosive and grenades, and he pressed the point further: explosives and grenades were not your field either, and you turned those out, didn't you? The Kuomintang controlled radio gear, transceivers above all, so strictly that no finished sets were sold on the market; that was exactly why the Party had to build its own. Chinese books on the subject were hard to find, but your English is good — you can buy English books and journals to learn from. Whatever difficulties arose, the organization would do all it could to help solve them.

Hearing Zhou Enlai out, Li Qiang said at once: since the Central had decided to take up radio and had given the task to him, he would learn as he worked and throw all his strength into it.

Having accepted the assignment, Li Qiang first made friends throughout the radio trade in the guise of an amateur enthusiast, studying alongside them; and he did business with shops such as the Yamei Radio Company, run by the Su brothers, and the Dahua Radio Company, so as to buy radio parts and tools there, and circuit diagrams for building transceivers. After buying a good many American-published English technical books on radio from the bookstores and studying them, he set about assembling a transceiver. The Dahua company specialized in large transceivers; Li Qiang managed, through his connections, to get one of their products out, dismantled it and studied it, and gradually worked out how it was done. Then came the building. On the market one could buy only ordinary parts; the important components had to be ordered from foreign trading firms, but even they could not supply things such as the coils wound from red-copper tubing, which he had to make himself. In the course of buying parts, Li Qiang came to know Zheng Guonian, an employee of the Dahua company. This man had been a radio operator on the steamer Nanjing, knew the trade fairly well, and gave Li Qiang a great deal of help, supplying many of the parts he needed. There was also an amateur radio enthusiast who taught Li Qiang how to wind coils.

The one who helped Li Qiang most was Cai Shuhou, a Communist who had studied electrical engineering in Japan and had founded the Shaodun Electrical Company in Shanghai. Li Qiang recalled: "I set about assembling a transceiver. I needed to buy and make some radio parts, and I had to have an expert to help me. The Party Central transferred Cai Shuhou to the Central Special Section to assist in my work. By then the Shaodun Electrical Company had moved to No. 403 Fuxi Road (now No. 369 Yan'an Central Road). Upstairs he cleared out an overhead room spanning the lane as an underground workshop for me. Inside it I set up a lathe, a drill, a milling machine, and a planer — four machine tools — and did machining with several comrades, making parts. All the radio gear and technical materials we bought were stored at the Shaodun company, two whole crates of them…" Li Qiang went on: "Cai Shuhou's shielding of our work sounds simple, but it was in fact very dangerous. Had the enemy discovered that we were secretly building a radio station there, Cai Shuhou and his whole family would have been in mortal danger. Yet he did not take it to heart in the least, and gave us his all."

And so, under conditions of extreme shortage of both equipment and technical materials, Li Qiang and Tu Zuochao worked together to build a transceiver. They lived above the neon-sign shop that Cai Shuhou ran. To keep their lodging safe, Chen Geng came to look the place over. Li Qiang and Tu Zuochao stayed hidden there, pouring themselves into it heart and soul, puzzling at it from every angle. Through days and nights of hard labor, through countless failures and trials, after a year's struggle, they at last built in 1929 the first wireless station of their own design and make, a set that both sent and received. Its power was only 50 watts and its sensitivity was not high; how well it would actually work, Li Qiang had no way of knowing. As it happened, Zhang Shenchuan had just finished his training in the sending-and-receiving technique at the Kuomintang's National Revolutionary Army General Headquarters Sixth Military Radio Station, and Li Qiang asked him to operate the newborn set and put it to the test. Using it, Zhang Shenchuan began by copying the international news and weather forecasts of Kuomintang stations, then made contact with amateur stations around the world. The results proved the homemade station fully up to standard and fit for use. Before long it began the arduous work of sending and receiving telegrams.

From then on, Li Qiang and Zhang Shenchuan together set up our Party's first underground radio station. In the winter of 1929 they rented a two-story shikumen house at No. 9 Fukangli on Rue de l'Ouest (Daxi Road) in the British Concession as the site of the secret station; after a short spell of installation and setup, the first station came into being there. The one in charge of operations there was Zhang Shenchuan; Pu Qiuchao, Huang Shangying, and others took their turns interning at it. The station's antenna was strung across the third-floor terrace and along the roof ridge, screened by various disguises. To practice, they would sometimes make contact with other Shanghai amateur stations at night under an amateur station's name for a few minutes, then take the antenna down when the exchange was done. The station's chief purpose was still to test the equipment and to practice transmitting. In March or April 1930, after the station had made contact with the one the Party's Southern Bureau had set up, it was moved for safety to a two-story house at No. 32 Fudefang, Hede Road, on Jing'ancun Road, to keep up its contact with Hong Kong. In September of that year, Comrade Wu Yunfu took over as operator; before long the station moved again, to Rue Moliere, with Comrade Zeng San as operator. That October Comrade Mao Qihua returned to Shanghai from the Soviet Union, lived for a time at the Rue Moliere station, and set about preparing an international station.

Because the operators' skills were still unpolished, the homemade set none too efficient, and the transmitting antenna hemmed in by its surroundings, working this transceiver often took twice the effort for half the result: a job that under normal conditions could be finished in an hour took two. But it had, after all, ended the era in which contact within the Party, up and down and side to side, had rested on couriers alone. So Li Qiang still counted it the mark of "an epoch-making communications revolution," and cried out with delight: the coming of wireless technology had greatly strengthened the ties within our Party and army. As Comrade Mao Zedong pointed out: because of wireless, even in our rural surroundings we are not politically isolated; our ties with the whole country and the whole world are very close. And though the revolution is cut apart village by village, through wireless a concentrated leadership can still be formed. ("On the Ten-Year Civil War.") It is no exaggeration to say that the founding of wireless communications work was, in our Party's history of communications, a revolution of epoch-making significance.

### Setting Up a Radio Station in Hong Kong

At the end of September 1929 the Central Committee sent Li Qiang to Hong Kong to set up a radio station. To complete the task he went twice.

The first time Li Qiang went alone, to find a location and choose a house — as far as possible somewhere both easy to hide in and convenient for work. In the end he settled on a house not far from the shore, on the second cross street off Nathan Road in Kowloon, an old-style four-story building. It was very cramped and oddly built: only the front and back rooms had windows, and the two rooms in between had none. Li Qiang chose the fourth floor and rented the whole floor, all four of its rooms. To build a secret station there, safety of the work demanded nothing less than the whole floor. Once the house was found, the Party organization located a Korean comrade in Hong Kong to serve as operator. With the house rented and a couple named Lu Kun, sent from Guangdong because they spoke the local dialect, installed as cover, Li Qiang returned to Shanghai.

At the end of that year Li Qiang went to Hong Kong a second time. Going with him this time was the operator Huang Shangying. Li Qiang recalled: "We carried the station and the ciphers by ship from Shanghai to Hong Kong. Before boarding, I bought two big steel-plate trunks. Such trunks were very fashionable then; they looked heavy and clumsy from outside but held a great deal, and stood upright like a large wardrobe — open it up, and clothes hung on one side, other things went on the other. I put the transceiver in one of the steel trunks and carried it aboard. To cover my identity, and as the work required, I dressed very grandly and rode first class, like a man of standing. When we came ashore at Hong Kong, a British policeman — a Chinese — was about to inspect us; but they never meant to inspect in earnest, and I knew the trick well. As he came up, I gave them four dollars, two per trunk. They took the money, chalked a mark on the trunks without so much as a glance, nodded, and walked off."

"After we reached Hong Kong, we handed the ciphers to Wang Menglan. Whether Wang Menglan decoded the messages herself or passed them to someone else to decode, we were not too clear. Once we had the station installed, following a prearranged wavelength, callsign, and time, we first listened for the voice of the Party Central in Shanghai. When each side had heard the other, we began exchanging traffic. The first formal exchange was around February 1930; on the Hong Kong end was Huang Shangying, on the Shanghai end, Zhang Shenchuan."

"The wireless communication between the Party Central in Shanghai and the Southern Bureau in Hong Kong opened a new chapter in our Party's communications work. Through long and arduous effort we broke through obstacle after obstacle and were the first within our Party to achieve wireless communication successfully — a truly remarkable thing. That is why I call it an epoch-making revolution in our Party's history of communications."

Zhang Shenchuan, the operator of our Party's first station, once spoke of the joy of the first successful exchange between Shanghai and Hong Kong, and of the hardships and hazards of working the set:

"In December 1929, Li Qiang and Huang Shangying went to Hong Kong. Under the leadership of the Southern Bureau's Guangdong provincial committee, they set up an underground station on Nathan Road, Tsim Sha Tsui, Kowloon. Huang served as operator, working together with a Korean comrade who had been a radio operator in the Japanese air force. In early January 1930, when Shanghai and Hong Kong first exchanged traffic, Pu Qiuchao and I leapt for joy, and Huang and I congratulated each other on the key at our success. I kept the first message slip as a memento; it was lost when I was arrested at the end of that year. We could exchange traffic, yes, but because our skills were unpolished, the accuracy and sensitivity of the homemade set were low, and the transmitting antenna was hemmed in by its surroundings, a job that under ordinary conditions could be finished in an hour took two. Luckily there was not much traffic then, and each day's task got done."

He went on: "The station's equipment was imperfect and the room was poorly soundproofed. The transmitting antenna could not be strung up to specification and could only be laid flat, just above the roof tiles, raised late at night for work and lowered when it was done; the red tiles and the antenna's red-copper wire were nearly the same color, hard to make out from a distance. At 50 watts we exchanged traffic with Kowloon, but the signal was very faint. We tried a 100-watt transmitter, with the transformer gear set in the back room of the second floor while I worked in the front room. Press the key, and every household's lights on the whole lane flickered. Hearing the neighbors cry out, 'Why are the lights jumping like this?', I stopped at once. I could not attend to both the front and back rooms alone, and as a result the two big bulbs on the transformer gear burned out. I learned my lesson and made a self-criticism. One deep night a thief came to the second-floor window to steal my clothes while I was not yet fast asleep. Hearing quick breathing, I saw beyond the mosquito net a tall fellow carrying the clothes out the door and up to the third-floor terrace — where we set our antenna poles — and away. I raised no alarm; I did not rouse the neighbors to come and look at the scene. The next morning the neighbors came to urge me to report it and recover the goods. I said, 'It can't be got back; don't report it.' That way, I spared the station from exposure."

Zhang Shenchuan kept this station with him the whole time, living in a three-story shikumen house at No. 9 Fukangli on Route Ghisi (Jisipeier Road) in western Shanghai. Because the station had been there a long time, for safety it was moved in May 1930 to No. 32, Jingnong, Fudefang, on Bubbling Well Road (just outside stood the International Cemetery), where it kept up its exchanges with Hong Kong.

On his second trip to Hong Kong, besides completing the formal exchanges between Hong Kong and Shanghai, Li Qiang saw to two other matters.

The first was to bring two radio sets from Hong Kong back to Shanghai. These two sets had been bought in Hong Kong by the Guangxi warlord Yu Zuobai and handed to his younger brother Yu Zuoyu for safekeeping; Yu Zuoyu dared not use them, and so gave them to Li Qiang to carry back to Shanghai. This Yu Zuoyu was Yu Zuobai's own brother and a cousin of Li Mingrui. He had studied at the Whampoa Military Academy of the constitutional-protection army in Guangzhou, and in the Great Revolution took part in the Northern Expedition as colonel of the Third Regiment under Li Mingrui's brigade — a brave and able commander, with battle honors at Tingsiqiao, Hesheng Bridge, and De'an in Jiangxi. After the Great Revolution failed, Yu Zuoyu joined the Chinese Communist Party in Hong Kong in October 1927, took part in the Guangzhou Uprising that December, then returned to Guangxi to engage in revolutionary activity, leading the peasant movement and organizing self-defense corps. In 1929 he was ordered to do secret work within the Guangxi army units of Yu Zuobai and Li Mingrui. When Yu Zuobai and Li Mingrui showed themselves willing to make common cause with the Chinese Communist Party, he put them in touch with the Party. At the end of that year, when Deng Xiaoping led the Bose and Longzhou uprisings, the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Eighth Red Army was founded at Longzhou on February 1, 1930, with Yu Zuoyu as its commander (its political commissar was Deng Bin, that is, Deng Xiaoping. The Seventh Red Army, founded in the Bose Uprising on December 11, 1929, had Zhang Yunyi as commander and Deng Bin as political commissar. By appointment of the Central Committee and the Central Military Commission, Li Mingrui served as commander-in-chief of the Seventh and Eighth Red Armies, and Deng Bin as their political commissar-in-chief and concurrently secretary of the front committee). The two sets then in Yu Zuoyu's hands were made by the British Marconi company. Hand-cranked and rated at 10 watts, they were quite advanced for the time.

Yu Zuobai had left them, after buying them, in the keeping of a British trading firm. To fetch these two sets was no small risk for Li Qiang, for they were military sets, and reselling them was forbidden. Li Qiang had a friend who worked as a clerk at the German firm of Siemens, and using the name of this German firm he collected the sets from the British firm — recording no entry and putting nothing into storage — and handed them straight to Li Qiang to carry back to Shanghai.

The second matter was this: at the end of 1929, Deng Xiaoping passed through Hong Kong on his way from Shanghai to Guangxi to lead the Bose Uprising. Guangxi needed at that time to exchange traffic with the Central Committee in Shanghai, and this had to be relayed through the Hong Kong station. Deng Xiaoping sought out Li Qiang, and once they had settled the callsign, wavelength, and times for radio contact, he went on to Guangxi by way of Haiphong. There was in Hong Kong then a paper called the Xiao Ribao, which served as the Party's secret contact point. Ye Jizhuang lived at this newspaper's office; his cover name was manager, and he was in charge of outside liaison. The one who did the actual liaison work was a little fellow with a small moustache called Zhou Tianluo, a Zhejiang man, whose cover was editor-in-chief of the paper. It was here that Li Qiang met Deng Xiaoping. Soon after Deng Xiaoping left, the Bose Uprising succeeded, and the news was quickly relayed through the Hong Kong station to the Party Central in Shanghai. After Li Qiang left Hong Kong, he heard that the contact point had been broken up: Ye Jizhuang made his way to the Seventh Red Army in Guangxi, while the "little moustache," after his arrest, turned traitor and went to work for Hu Zongnan, and after the liberation of the whole country was assassinated in Shanghai by his own accomplices.

Huang Shangying, who worked at the Hong Kong station, found the work hard and the living conditions poor, and later contracted a lung disease and often coughed blood. Consumption was then an incurable illness, with no effective cure yet, and he had to return to Shanghai for treatment, then went to Hangzhou to convalesce; in August he died, at only twenty. His passing grieved everyone deeply. Many years later Li Qiang still wrote in his memoirs: "For this comrade — diligent and devoted, working in obscurity, the first to give his life for our Party's radio cause — I have carried a deep and lasting remembrance." (My Revolutionary Course.)

On December 1, 1930, the Hong Kong station was broken up. Zeng San was then the operator of the Central station. After the authorities broke up the Hong Kong station, they gained full control of the station and its means of contact. On the first day the British still exchanged traffic with our Central station; on the second they sent a message in English. It read: "Your friends are quite well; they are now in prison." Zeng San did not know the English word "jail" and brought it to Li Qiang. One look and Li Qiang was aghast: this is bad — they're in prison! Pi De, who had gone to relieve Huang Shangying after his return to Shanghai, together with a man named Luo who was manning the station, had both been arrested after someone caught in Hong Kong gave them away. Li Qiang at once reported the situation to the Central Committee, and contact was suspended for the time being. The Hong Kong station had worked for less than a year, but it had done a great deal. Half a year later, having found no evidence to hold them, the enemy released the operators arrested in Hong Kong.

### Training the Party's First Generation of Radio Operators

For the Party's communications work to grow, it first had to train a large corps of people skilled in radio technique. In March 1930 the Central Special Section held its first radio training class, which on Zhou Enlai's instruction Li Qiang took charge of.

The first radio training class was run in secret to guard against the enemy — the students housed separately, contacted along single lines, and taught in home visits. Its students came from the Soviet areas, and there were comrades transferred in from elsewhere as well. Wang Zigang, for instance, had been a cipher clerk at the Yong Bao in Tianjin; in the second half of 1929, Gu Shunzhang learned in Tianjin that he could hold three or four thousand plain-code characters for Chinese in his head and could translate an ordinary message without consulting the codebook, and so had him too transferred to Shanghai to study the equipment and operating sides. The instructors of the class were Li Qiang and Zhang Shenchuan. Because the students differed in background and in schooling, they taught according to each one's aptitude, working from the practical. The students studied very hard, and in two or three months the first batch of radio technicians was trained. Those who studied at the time included Huang Shangying, Wang Zigang, Wu Yunfu, Zeng San, Zeng Hualun, Wang Youcai, Liu Guanghui (a woman), Zhao Yinxiang (a woman), and Pu Qiuchao (a woman).

Wu Yunfu, who took part in this first batch, recalled afterward how they had studied the operator's craft: "The training class really had only a few students, housed separately in several garret rooms. There was just one instructor, Comrade Zhang Shenchuan. Old Zhang had been assigned by the Party in 1928 to learn the craft at the 'Shanghai Radio School' run by the Kuomintang, and was now in charge of training his own comrades. Every morning and afternoon he came once to my garret to teach me sending and receiving. The teaching equipment then was just one Morse key, one buzzer, two pencils, and a few pads of paper. Sometimes I tapped the key and he corrected my posture from the side; sometimes he sent and I took down the dot-and-dash sounds coming from the buzzer. Old Zhang, got up like a civil servant, was in and out of my garret so often that the landlord never came to doubt our true identities."

But in the end all sorts of signs showed that the landlord did have some doubts. Zhang Shenchuan then had Zeng San study with him too, and Wu Yunfu and Zeng San moved into a larger garret well away from the place.

"After this move the study conditions were a bit steadier. I was a little ahead of Comrade Zeng San, so the instructor had me tutor him; and I, too, just needed to practice taking messages while he sent. So both of us improved our skills quickly. We made a simple oscillator; you had to put on headphones to hear the transmitting. That way, once the door was shut we no longer had to worry that anyone outside would hear the goings-on inside our room."

"It was time to practice on an actual transceiver. But how were we to manage that? Even had we been given a set, keeping it in a garret would not have suited our station in life. We could only rig up a crystal receiver and copy the news telegrams of ships and coastal stations for receiving practice. Shanghai summers are quite hot, and the garret, baked by the sun, grew hotter still, like a steamer basket; stripped to the waist to practice, we were soaked through. Comrade Zeng San dreaded the heat even more than I did, yet the moment he put on the headphones it was as if he forgot everything; the sweat often soaked the paper pads through, and still he took down, in rapt concentration, every code group that came into the headphones."

"The difficulties of living were no lighter than the difficulties of study. Because the Party was short of funds, our living expenses could not always be guaranteed, and often we ate one meal with no next in sight. Pawning clothes to buy rice was a common thing. For Communists this counted for nothing, but living in Shanghai's world of gilded dissipation, we could not help keeping up a front, for anyone living too shabbily would draw suspicion. I remember one festival day: mealtime was nearly on us, and there was still no rice in the pot. The landlady, whether without meaning to or in deliberate mockery, said to us, 'Gentlemen! It's a holiday today — what fine dishes are you cooking up?' We answered, 'No cooking today; a friend has invited us to a banquet!' We went out and wandered a circuit and another, killing the time of this hard-to-reach banquet. It was not until the afternoon, when Comrade Zhang Shenchuan brought a little money, that we took out a few coppers and each had a bowl of plain noodles at an open-air stall."

The first class training communications personnel yielded some experience. The Party Central asked that another batch of radio workers be trained for the provinces and base areas, setting the quota at twenty; in fact sixteen came. The task of training the second class was given to Gu Shunzhang and Li Qiang, with Li Qiang and Zhang Shenchuan seeing to the concrete organization.

Drawing on the experience of the first class, Li Qiang and Zhang Shenchuan proposed to keep to scattered teaching, or else to open two electrical-goods shops as cover. Zhang Shenchuan had once put forward a concrete plan: "The British Concession is a commercial district with much coming and going and a complicated scene, fairly easy to blend into. We could open two electrical-supply shops, register with the authorities, take out business licenses, and have the owner, clerks, and apprentices all in legal identities, selling light bulbs and other electrical goods and taking on the fitting and repair of lamps, water pipes, and radios. The students would live divided between the two shops, doing normal business by day and studying radio technique deep in the night. If one shop were broken up by the enemy, there would still be the other." But Gu Shunzhang would not consider his plan, insisting again and again that inside the French Concession police there was a reliable inside line, so that if any trouble arose at a Party organ set up in the French Concession he would be sure to know beforehand and could take measures to cope. And so Gu Shunzhang decided to rent a three-story shikumen house at No. 12 Sichengli on Rue Ratard (now Julu Road) in the French Concession and open there a radio training class. To the outside it posed as a private enterprise, with a signboard at the door reading "Shanghai Fuli Electrical Company Works."

The head of the second radio training class was Gu Shunzhang, but the man actually in charge was Li Qiang, who also handled the equipment side, with Tu Zuochao and Song Lian assisting; Zhang Shenchuan handled the operating side, with Wu Yunfu and Zeng Hualun assisting; and Wu Kejian handled organization and funds. The six who had returned from studying radio in the Soviet Union — Tu Zuochao, Fang Zhongru, and the rest — all took part in the class's teaching and leadership. Fang Zhongru taught electrical theory and also handled the students' political study and organizational life. Shen Kanfu taught the equipment side. Fang Zhongru lived within the class at the time, responsible for the Party's work and the teaching. To guard against the enemy, and as the requirements of underground work demanded, he and his comrades carefully drew up six measures: one, the class would appear openly under the name of the Shanghai Fuli Electrical Company Parts-Manufacturing Works, and everyone would address one another as apprentices; two, each person would fill out a false résumé, to be kept by Fang Zhongru, and each would have a cover name and a curriculum vitae (Fang Zhongru's own cover name was Li Huiting); three, no secret Party documents or progressive books, papers, or periodicals were to be kept within the class; four, students were to have no contact with outsiders without the organization's approval; five, the buying of supplies, the cooking, and the cleaning at the class were all to be done by the students themselves; six, if arrested, everyone was to answer according to the false résumé.

Despite these rules laid down against the enemy, the class had not been running long before trouble came. Zhang Shenchuan said: "Many of the students in the class were college men. Though they had been issued workers' clothes and dressed as workers, some of them, when they went out or into the street to buy grain or coal, wore their hair slicked and glossy and their shoes polished bright, and put on red or green woolen sweaters, looking neither like workers nor like students. Inside the works it was all young men in their prime, with no business ties to the outside and no dealings with society — a situation all too apt to rouse the enemy's suspicions. One day in November 1930, a man in old blue workers' clothes who called himself a worker from the waterworks pushed into the works, saying he had come to inspect the water pipes, and looked over all three floors. From the experience of living inside the Shanghai concessions, when a household's water pipes went bad, though you asked the company again and again to send someone to repair them, no one paid you any mind — yet here was a man come of his own accord to inspect the pipes: it warranted suspicion. On a morning in early December 1930, in a drizzling rain, four men in raincoats crowded into the works to deliver an image of the bodhisattva Guanyin, saying they were street idlers. The moment they were in the door they looked all around, and said, 'You've newly opened a works here; we've come specially to bring congratulations — give us some drink money.' They were full of gangster talk. Comrade Li Qiang dealt with them. They took him for one of their own, saying, 'Come now! We're all family here! So — you've opened a works here; have you paid your respects to the boss?' In the end Comrade Li Qiang gave them four silver dollars, and only then did they go. This was another suspicious sign. We reported these suspicious circumstances to our leader, Gu Shunzhang, at the time, but no contingency measures were taken, and grave consequences resulted."

"On the afternoon of December 17, 1930, it was cold, with a wind blowing and a fine rain falling. Comrades Li Qiang, Wu Kejian, Tu Zuochao, Song Lian, Mao Qihua, Wu Yunfu, and others had not come to the works. Only Zeng Hualun, I, and eighteen students were inside, studying sending and receiving in the front room of the second floor. Beside the front room of the second floor was a lectern, and on the wall a blackboard. The day before, when Comrade Fang Zhongru taught electrical theory, the circuit diagram he had drawn on the board had not yet been wiped off. I sat at the lectern working the key to send, with two code sheets I had made up at random for the lesson before me. In the room stood tables and chairs, with batteries and buzzers on them, and the students each wore a headset, practicing receiving. As we were studying, the door was suddenly pushed open, and in came six agents, one of them a foreigner, walking in front. Two agents came before me, and each drew a pistol across the lectern, leveled it at my chest, and shouted, 'Don't move! What's your name, where are you from? Write it on paper.' I wrote: 'Shen Zhesheng, Shimen, Hunan.' He took it and put it in his pocket. The students all wore blue workers' clothes. I lived outside the works, and to suit my surroundings I wore a Western suit, a woolen overcoat, and a felt hat, and I was in the middle of teaching, so the agents took me for the 'chief culprit,' and in the many later interrogations they always started with me. Three agents shut us up in the second-floor front room, while the others rummaged through everything on the first and third floors, searching in detail, ripping open every quilt to look. We had prepared a false confession beforehand: the works was privately run, the boss was named Wang Fucheng, and I was inside learning the electrician's trade. We had also agreed on a signal: coming to the works and turning into the lane, one was first to look carefully at the curtain in the upper right corner of the glass window of the second-floor front room — if it was already drawn open, then trouble had come, and one was to turn back at once. In the second-floor front room there were also two beds. After we were shut up, the student Xie Xiaokang lay sideways across a bed, his hands cradling his head, resting against the lower corner of the curtain, and pretending to turn over, he drew open the curtain's right corner."

On the day of the disaster, Li Qiang, Mao Qihua, Wu Kejian, and some others were luckily not on the scene and were not arrested. Many years later, in October 1981, Li Qiang said at a meeting: "Twenty of us were arrested in all, on December 17, 1930. I was eating at Mao Qihua's house and sleeping there, and so was not arrested. And there was one more (note: this was Song Lian), who saw that the signal atop the training class had changed and ran back to tell me." (Mao Qihua, recalling the affair, said: "That day Li Qiang came as usual with Zeng San and Song Lian to eat lunch at my house. After the meal, while Li Qiang and I chatted idly, Song Lian wanted to write to Hong Kong, and since he had no writing paper on hand, he went to Sichengli to fetch some. Before long Song Lian came rushing back and said: something's happened! The signal at the back door of our training class has changed. Just then the man who watched the lane was waiting outside, and told anyone of ours he saw: 'Don't go in — the twenty people from your works have all just been hauled off by the police.'") I hurried to the Zhenhua Hotel on Fourth Avenue to find Wu Kejian, who was there waiting for funds (living expenses); the living expenses then were only twelve dollars, food and use all included. Luckily the money got there in time, or I too would have been arrested.

Tu Zuochao also came within a hair of arrest this time, but by his composure and courage got wittily clear of it. He said afterward: "On the day it happened, it so chanced that Song Lian went to the 'company' on some business. Before he had even turned into the lane, an old worker who watched it waved him off, signaling that trouble had come and he should not go in. Once Song Lian had made out the situation, he told only Li Qiang and then ran to hide at his father-in-law's house, without coming to warn me. After lunch, carrying a can of oily grime, wearing a child's cap and a very dirty cotton gown, I went, at Li Qiang's bidding, to the 'company' to paint some steel plates. I knocked at the back door of the works, and for a long while there was no answer from within; a sudden sense of foreboding flashed across my mind. But I could not run, still less show the least flustered look, for if I were spotted by agents lying in wait all around, that would be disaster. So I steeled myself and went on knocking. After a good while a foreign policeman came out; he first jabbered at me in some foreign tongue, which I could not understand, and I dared not leave. Then this foreigner took out something like a work permit to show me, meaning that this was no longer the original 'works.' But I still pretended I wanted to go in, and said in half-broken English 'Dollars' — meaning I had come to collect a debt. Seeing me forever pestering there, the foreigner gave me a savage kick, shoved me out the door, slammed it in a fury, and went in. I seized the chance to leave at once — first walking slowly out of the lane, and still keeping up an air of unconcern, I watched a plaster-selling conjurer's tricks for a while in an open lot outside the lane. Sure that no one was tailing me, I changed trams twice in a row and came to the International Station on Seymour Road. Here I found Li Qiang lying on the bed, worried sick. I told him the news that the 'company' had been broken up. 'I know,' he said."

That afternoon, Wu Yunfu did not know that trouble had struck the training class; needing a piece of gear urgently, he had to go to the works to fetch it. Just as he reached the mouth of the lane, the old gatekeeper of the lane gave him a covert signal. He sensed something was wrong, but could not turn back, for the agents were watching everyone in and out of the lane all around. So he walked into the lane with perfect composure, and from a distance saw that the white curtain in the upper right corner of the roadside window on the second floor of the works had been drawn halfway — full confirmation that trouble had come. He wove through several turns and came out the back of the lane. Still an agent posted there had seen him, grown suspicious, and tailed him. Nimbly Wu Yunfu jumped a tram to "shake his tail" and was at last thrown off.

The day after their arrest, on December 18, the Shanghai Shen Bao carried news of the "case":

RATARD ROAD REACTIONARY ORGAN UNCOVERED / RADIO EQUIPMENT SEIZED / MORE THAN TWENTY MEN AND WOMEN TAKEN

Yesterday, around noon, Yuan Liang, chief of the municipal public security bureau, received sudden secret intelligence to the effect that a large number of reactionary elements were hidden at No. 10 Sichengli, Rue Ratard, in the French Concession, where they had privately set up radio equipment and were plotting sedition, and requesting that officers be swiftly dispatched to arrest them. On this intelligence, Chief Yuan at once ordered out a number of detectives, who took a warrant to the special organ of the French police to request assistance in the arrest. The chief of the police detachment further sent Chinese and Western detectives along with them. Sure enough, on the third floor of the house they turned up radio headsets and various kinds of wire, and on the spot took more than twenty men and women, all brought back to the station; after a brief examination by the detachment chief, they were handed to the visiting detectives to take away for prompt disposal.

Beforehand, Zhang Shenchuan, seeing that the training class was poorly concealed and that enemy agents had come twice to reconnoiter the works, while Gu Shunzhang's mind was dulled and he took no precautions at all, had grown worried. He had, on his own initiative, moved twenty-one secret Party books and periodicals and documents of the Party's Third Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee to his own lodging beforehand. On the night of the disaster, Zhang Shenchuan managed from jail to smuggle out a letter telling of this. The next day, Li Qiang and Gu Shunzhang rode a motorcycle to Zhang Shenchuan's lodging and fetched all the documents back. On their way to Zhang Shenchuan's house they came to a crossroads, met a red light, and ran it, breaking the traffic rules; a policeman shouted after them, "You've broken the law — I've got your plate number down." They fetched the documents, and passing this post again on the way back, since they had the documents in their coats and feared the man would make trouble and cause a problem, Gu Shunzhang took the initiative to tell the policeman: "I didn't see clearly just now — it wasn't on purpose!" — while covertly holding two silver dollars in his hand, he took the policeman's hand and pressed them into it (silver, Li Qiang said, was a must, because banknotes are not easy to tell the amount of at a glance). The policeman took the silver, drew out his register, and said cheerfully, "All right, I'll cross out the record." So they passed safely and returned to the organ. When they had gone to Zhang Shenchuan's house, fearing his lodging was already under enemy watch, Li Qiang and Gu Shunzhang had not dared enter openly. Li Qiang said: "Gu Shunzhang and I ran to Zhang Shenchuan's house to get the documents out; we got in like burglars and carried them out."

At the same time, Mao Qihua went to the lodgings of Fang Tingzhen and Li Yuanjie, moved out everything in their homes, and contrived to carry off the important equipment of the Sichengli training class. Mao Qihua said: "Before the action, the Special Section sent someone to reconnoiter and found that the police posted there had withdrawn and the door was sealed with a strip of paper. A few more days passed, and then we organized some people, rented a truck, and drove to the mouth of the lane; going in, we tore off the seal, loaded the electrical equipment, the small generator, and the other goods onto the truck and carried them off, then pasted the seal back exactly as it had been, and gave the old man who watched the lane five silver dollars to thank him for his help."

Some days after the disaster, the Central Special Section rented a suite of rooms at a hotel on Fujian Road and held a meeting to sum up the lessons of the affair. Under the conditions of the time, gathering so many people together for so long to run a training class had never been allowed by the principles of secret work. Yet Gu Shunzhang had not considered Li Qiang and Zhang Shenchuan's proposal to run the class in scattered form, and even after the works had been visited twice by the enemy, and after people went on warning Gu Shunzhang of the danger of the enemy breaking it up, he always said: I have intelligence work in the French Concession, and inside lines within the police; I'll know of any trouble very quickly. Should the enemy discover it, I'll tell you at once to move. The result was to invite this disaster. At the summing-up meeting, everyone pointed out that the leadership had underestimated the enemy and overrated itself, always supposing intelligence to be all-powerful. To concentrate so many people for training under enemy rule ran counter to the Party's principles of secret work.

Through this painful lesson, everyone raised their guard. To narrow the target and guard against enemy plots, Li Qiang came to the station rarely, and before long the Central transferred Chen Shouchang, who had worked in the Central Special Section's Second Branch, to lead the Fourth Branch. On the Central's instructions, in January 1931 Chen Shouchang went on running the radio training class, training operators and technicians to repair the sets. He restored the scattered-housing, home-tutoring method of teaching taken up in the first class. Mao Qihua, for instance, was put in charge of training five operators and equipment men, teaching them in two places, Hongkou and Tongfu Road. Once again it proved that training by this method was safer and more effective.

In June 1931, Xu Yixin and Yue Shaohua, who had just returned from the Soviet Union that March, were sent to run a class for confidential-work cadres. This one was led by Ke Qingshi, with Xu Yixin and Yue Shaohua handling affairs, Wu Yongkang handling funds, and Wang Zigang handling instruction. There were more than twenty students in this class, most of them Party members among the students at Shanghai's universities, again housed separately in several places; besides studying the relevant Central documents and current affairs, they chiefly studied radio technique. Among the students were Song Yaofu, Wang Yiqun, Zhu Bangshi, Yang Nanshi, and Gong Wei; after finishing, most were assigned to work in the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Soviet.

The comrades arrested at Sichengli held up remarkably well in prison. Though put to harsh interrogation and torture, not one of them gave away his identity. It was not until August 1937 that sixteen of them, through Zhou Enlai's many efforts, were at last released. Four other comrades — Su Jianping, Chen Baoli, Ying Qingfu, and Xie Xiaodong — died in prison, tortured by the enemy in every way. The sixteen comrades, once out, threw themselves straight back into the revolutionary struggle.

### An "Epoch-Making Communications Revolution"

The founding of the Central Special Section's first radio station was a very model of how the Chinese Communist Party's revolutionary work went from nothing to something, from birth to growth. In this period the first generation of the Party's radio workers, led by Li Qiang, cut through thorns and braved every hardship, and at last broke through obstacle upon obstacle to build, by their own research and their own manufacture, the first Red radio station. This was the earliest radio work created by the Party in the White areas, a communications revolution of epoch-making significance.

The Party's radio work in the Soviet areas, too, began from zero, from nothing to something, and grew step by step through bitter and arduous struggle. In the early days the Red Army had no radio stations, and communications relied entirely on infantry or cavalry couriers. The forces could not use radio to command in battle, still less to keep in touch with the Central Committee. In August 1930, on the road to attack Changsha, the First Front Army wiped out the Kuomintang Dai Douyuan brigade stationed at Wenjiashi; that unit's radio station still had its charging generator running, but because the Red Army soldiers lacked knowledge of such things, they smashed it when they took it. In the first "encirclement and suppression" campaign, from late 1930 to early 1931, in the fighting at Longgang the Red Army destroyed an enemy division and captured alive the Kuomintang front-line commander-in-chief Zhang Huizan, seizing a radio station. But the transmitter had been wrecked, and only a receiver was left; the very earliest thing the Red Army possessed was this half a radio station.

In March 1931, to open a line between Shanghai and the Central Soviet in Jiangxi, the Central Committee sent Zeng San, Wu Yunfu, and Tu Zuochao — three men — from Shanghai to the Central Soviet. The plan they had worked out for linking the two sides was to reach the Soviet by way of Hong Kong. From Shanghai they took a French mail steamer to Hong Kong, then a steam launch to Shantou in Guangdong, and passed through Sanheba and Yongding in Fujian, at last reaching Changshang in Xingguo, Jiangxi, where they were assigned to the Red Army General Headquarters. After they reached the Central Soviet, they were for a long time unable to make contact with the Central Committee in Shanghai, chiefly because the station's power was too small. Later, in the second "encirclement and suppression" campaign, the Red Army captured a 100-watt station from the Gong Bingfan unit of the Kuomintang's Twenty-eighth Division. With this station, on the Mid-Autumn Festival of 1931, Wang Zheng and Zeng San made contact with the Party Central in Shanghai. From then on the Central Soviet and the Central Committee were linked; the ties between the Party Central in Shanghai and the Red Army General Headquarters and the Soviet's Central Bureau grew far closer. What had once taken more than two months for a single exchange of letters now went through in a dozen minutes, or an hour or two. Comrades sent by the Central Committee from Shanghai to the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Soviet used a station captured from the Kuomintang's Liang Guanying Sixty-ninth Division in December 1931 to exchange traffic, very soon, with the Central Committee, with the Central Red Army in Jiangxi, and with the Second Red Army Group in western Hubei-Hunan. At the same time, operators sent in 1931 to the Second Red Army Group in western Hubei-Hunan opened radio links with the Party Central in Shanghai and with the First and Fourth Front Armies. The Central Special Section could now accurately grasp the enemy's military intelligence and forward it in good time to the several Soviets, which played a major part in the Red Army's operations in those areas. With radio, the Red Army could rapidly learn the enemy's situation, more fully, quickly, and accurately than by relying on scouts alone; and command through radio could be swifter and more timely. Once there was radio, it was just as Mao Zedong said — the Red Army had gained "the clairvoyant eyes and wind-borne ears of science," and like a fierce tiger given wings, it grew stronger in battle and swifter in its growth.

Even when the First Front Army had only its "half a station," it played a great part in the fighting. Wang Zheng, who had come over with that half a station captured from Zhang Huizan's Eighteenth Division to join the Red Army, said the Red Army "captured, in the fighting against the Tan Daoyuan force, a complete station and battery set from the enemy brigade headquarters, a set whose design had the bulb plugged in from above. With this station and a half we still could not exchange traffic, and mainly did two things: copying the news and reconnoitering the enemy. At that time the Kuomintang forces' cipher offices and commanders alike had no grasp of the importance of radio security. Wherever they arrived, when the station began making contact, they would first send a 'QRC?' — asking the other's whereabouts, so as to determine who was on the main axis and whom to raise first. And when the other answered its location, it did so in plain language, because their forces were so mixed that a common code would not do. In effect they kept reporting to us the movements and dispositions of their troops. From the founding of our station down to the second 'encirclement and suppression' campaign, our chief work lay in this. The enemy's leaks, from the reverse side, reminded us of the importance of radio security; from the very start our army's radio work took note of the importance of radio security in the struggle against the enemy, and over long years of work afterward we formed the habit of paying it close attention."

Feng Wenbin, then political commissar of the newly formed radio unit of the First Front Army, said: "In those days Wang Zheng played no small part. He put it to me that he knew the enemy stations' callsigns, and from these could learn where the several enemy units were stationed. Later, with my agreement, he took down the enemy's telegrams. This played a great part in the second 'encirclement and suppression' campaign. At the time we took down the traffic between the enemy 'suppression' forces and the Nanchang field headquarters. He Yingqin was then commander-in-chief, and we could pick up the telegrams he sent to the several divisions at the front. Because Wang Zheng knew the callsigns of the surrounding enemy stations, and was familiar with the stations of the Gong Bingfan and Hao Mengling units, we used radio to learn the times, places, unit numbers, dispositions, and intentions of the enemy's arriving forces. When we had wiped out the enemy, we captured the enemy codebook as well, and could then decode ciphered telegrams — so we learned the contents of the orders of Chiang Kai-shek and the Nanchang field headquarters and the traffic among the enemy forces at the front. Chairman Mao and Commander-in-Chief Zhu were then delighted, and gave rewards. That we fought so well in the second 'encirclement and suppression' campaign had to do with picking up and decoding the enemy's telegrams. In the very first battle of the second campaign, we got the movement plan of the Gong Bingfan force from the enemy's telegrams, wiped that force out, and captured a large station, a 100-watt one. Later it was sent to the rear, and Zeng San and Liu Yin used this station to begin their contact with the Party Central in Shanghai. In this way we fought all the way to Jianning, Lichuan, and Taining, sweeping across 800 li, and were clear on nearly every enemy order. We fought by the enemy's telegrams, and held the initiative. The station played a most important part."

In the third "encirclement and suppression" campaign in the Central Soviet, three more stations were captured, and the First Front Army now had six. On December 14, 1931, the Twenty-sixth Route Army — a former unit of the old Northwest Army that Chiang Kai-shek had ordered to attack the Central Soviet — rose in revolt at Ningdu, Jiangxi, and joined the Red Army, bringing with it eight radio stations, more than forty radio technicians, and a good deal of communications gear. In this way the First Front Army's stations leapt at once to fourteen, and the Red Army's radio had made a great leap forward.

As the stations grew, so too did the Red Army's radio technicians, rapidly swelling in number. The Red Army's radio personnel came at first from three sources. One was those sent by the Party Central in Shanghai: besides the first batch of Zeng San and two others, after the third "encirclement and suppression" campaign it sent Zhu Bangying, Ban Deyuan, Xu Ping, Luo Guikun, Wang Yiqun, and others. Two was those who came over from the Kuomintang forces. Three was training their own — picking young Party and Youth League members from the ranks to run training classes. The first radio training class, run in February 1931, had twelve people; the second, run soon after, more than twenty; the third, begun at the end of 1931, was renamed the Central Military Commission Radio School. The Soviet's training classes soon grew into several, and later grew again into a single communications school. By 1933 the students at the Red Army communications school in Ruijin had grown to more than a thousand, with separate specialties in radio, telephone, semaphore, and bugle signaling, and a class for equipment men as well.

In November 1931, the provisional government of the Chinese Soviet Republic, chaired by Mao Zedong, was founded at Ruijin, Jiangxi. Next was founded the Central Revolutionary Military Commission, chaired by Zhu De, to lead the whole Red Army in unified command. At the same time the Red China News Agency was founded, fitted with a station to send out the news. By now, links had also been opened with the Fourth Front Army and the Second Army Group.

Radio played a major part in the Red Army's fighting on the Long March as well: "Along the Long March, radio communication was fairly good throughout; the best, as I recall, was the crossing of the Jinsha River. Because the Third Bureau delivered the orders well and the Second Bureau's intelligence was well in hand, we were able to shake off the enemy and cross the Jinsha smoothly. When we were still three days' march from the Jinsha's bank, the enemy was one day's march behind us. In front of the enemy was the Thirteenth Division, whose commander, Wan Yaohuang, feared death and wanted to preserve his strength. When Chiang Kai-shek asked him whether there were Communist forces ahead, he falsely reported that there were none, waited a day where he was, then marched a day back, and to return took three more days — so the gap between us grew to six days, and only then did we have time to move the First Army Group from Longjie ferry and the Third Army Group from Hongmen ferry, all to the Jiaopingdu ferry, to cross the river smoothly. The moving and command of the forces relied entirely on radio, and communication ran very smoothly. Our forces had all crossed the Jinsha by May 8; the enemy reached the bank on May 9, when we had long since gone far away."

Zhong Fuxiang, who did radio-interception work on the Long March, said: "Several stations did the intercepting, each fixing on one or two enemy armies and listening to them at all times. In those days the enemy used cipher for traffic and plain code for voice, sometimes with a little English thrown in. In their voice transmissions they talked of everything: wherever the forces moved, they told each other. So we could at least learn the enemy's movements and stations. And the ciphers the enemy used then were fairly simple, easy to break. After we reached Yunnan, a staff officer of ours was captured by the enemy, carrying some enemy telegrams we had decoded. On May 2, 1935, Long Yun sent an urgent telegram to Chiang Kai-shek reporting the matter, which read: 'Recently at Caojie was captured one Communist-bandit staff officer, Chen Zhongshan by name… on his person was found a bundle of intelligence, being secret telegrams exchanged among the several parts of our army, all rendered into plain text. No wonder the bandits saw our army's movements so clearly and knew where to advance and where to fall back.' On receiving the telegram, Chiang Kai-shek wired Long Yun again on May 3 and 4: 'That our army's telegrams should be stolen and decoded by the bandits is a matter of the utmost gravity,' 'the danger is grave, the shame the greatest,' and laid down that 'a good number of ciphers must be printed and held in reserve, to be changed and used by turns each day, so that each cipher is used at most once in a week, changing with the day…' From then on the enemy changed cipher a day at a time, even a cipher a message, and yet we guessed them all out too."

From the founding of the Central Special Section's first station in Shanghai in 1929, neither the savage ravages of the White Terror in the White areas nor the ordeal of the Red Army's 25,000-li Long March, over mountains and across rivers, could halt the growth of the radio-communications work the Party led. In the summer of 1936, when the famous American journalist and friend of the Chinese people Edgar Snow visited the Soviet area of northern Shaanxi, Zhou Enlai had a long talk with him at Bao'an. Snow found to his astonishment that the Communists had wireless links with all the important cities of China, Shanghai, Hankou, Nanjing, and Tianjin among them: "Although their radio stations in the White-area cities were frequently uncovered, the Kuomintang never once succeeded in permanently cutting off their communications with the Red regions. According to Zhou Enlai, ever since the Red Army set up a wireless-communications department with equipment captured from the White army, their ciphers had never been broken by the Kuomintang. Zhou Enlai's radio station was set up not far from his headquarters. By it he kept in touch with every important place and every front in the Soviet area. He even communicated directly with Commander-in-Chief Zhu De, whose forces were then stationed hundreds of miles away on the Sichuan-Tibet border in the southwest. At Bao'an, the provisional capital of the Soviet in the northwest, there was a radio school where about ninety students were being trained in radio engineering. Each day they took down the broadcasts of Nanjing, Shanghai, and Tokyo, and supplied the news to the Soviet's newspapers."

By the 1980s, looking back on our Party's radio work — on the enormous achievements won by the struggle of a great host of radio workers who braved death and pressed dauntlessly on — Li Qiang was filled with feeling. In his article titled "An Epoch-Making Communications Revolution," he wrote with high spirit:

More than half a century has passed since our Party's radio communications work was born. On this invisible front there appeared many heroic figures of a legendary cast: loyal to the Party, quick-witted and brave, diligent and toiling in obscurity, they made great contributions to the Party's cause, and some gave their precious lives. In looking back today on the hardships of that founding, our aim is to learn from their revolutionary spirit and to carry our communications work one step higher; and, drawing constantly on new scientific and technological achievements, to speed the modernization of that work, so as to meet the demands the new historical period places on it.
