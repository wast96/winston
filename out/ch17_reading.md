## Afterword: Keep to Poverty, Endure the Silence

From November 1927 to September 1935, the Central Special Branch, in its own singular way, lay hidden and fought on for eight years in a Shanghai of bloody rains and gilded dissipation. Eight years hidden, eight years at war — and into them went how much wisdom, how much daring, how much courage and iron will.

At around ten o'clock on the morning of June 16, 2010, a veteran of the Central Special Branch, a national treasure of our Party's struggle on the hidden front, Shen Anna, died in Beijing at the age of ninety-five. Her daughter Hua Kefang sent me a text message saying that the Party's leaders mourned her passing with deep sorrow and had saluted her as a hero of the hidden front who had rendered the Party great service. She said too that her mother had already slipped into a coma at the end, but that even then she kept murmuring, over and over: "Am I blown? Run! Out the back door! Whatever you do, don't let them catch you!" I wrote back to Hua Kefang: "With boundless grief I send my thoughts across the distance to a loyal soldier of our Party's struggle on the hidden front. I have wept until the tears ran dry and then wept blood; my heart is crushed, and the living and the dead alike fall silent. Now that this parting has become a parting forever, I will follow the old woman's receding figure, long and far, with my deepest remembrance." A person who thought of staying hidden even to the point of death, terrified that being blown would do grievous harm to the Party's cause, is a person to honor.

To follow, with boundless grief and deep remembrance, the receding figures of such honored elders is my heart's wish, and it is the whole purpose of my writing this book.

Now that this wish, this purpose, after years of effort, is at last coming to solid ground, the stir in my heart is naturally past all telling. The paper is short and the feeling long; here, all I can say is a quiet "thank you" to everyone I love and everyone who loves me.

Over these years, from Shanghai to Beijing, from Cishu to Gold Wall, his persistence and his support have moved me deeply. Last of all, let me say this. In writing books, and works of history and letters, I hold to one principle: use quotation marks freely, and let the original materials speak as much as they can. This too I learned from Mr. Yang Tianshi. Mr. Yang is a member of the academic division of the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences and a research fellow of its Institute of Modern History, whose chief field is the history of the Republic. Every time Yang Tianshi sees me, he is bound to say: "The fake documentary that fills its gaps with imagination — 'short on sources, so make it up' — I won't buy it and I won't read it, because so many fine subjects have been ruined. Those made-up, cobbled-together things are truly frightening. Who can tell which of them checked the archives and did the verifying, and which just passed on error or invented outright? If we ourselves can't tell, who will be able to tell a hundred years from now? So you may have a thousand tricks, but I have my one fixed rule. I simply look at whether you add a note, whether you use quotation marks. If you don't like it, then add me a note, use quotation marks. If you say your account has grounds, has a source, then put the note in for me and mark it with quotation marks. Make it clear to me. Make clear where your account really comes from. Otherwise I won't believe you. The more vividly you write it, the more you make the flowers rain from the sky, the less I trust you."

Seeing things from Mr. Yang Tianshi's standpoint, I have always laid particular weight, in my own books, on adding notes and quotation marks, striving to get to the bottom of every important fact and to track down its original source. I only want to do a little honest scholarship. What is so, is so; what is not, is not. To know what you know and to know what you do not know — that is knowledge. Never to pander, never to be flighty, never to pass a hasty judgment or draw a hasty conclusion for the sake of an empty name. The *Yan Family Instructions*, in its "Exhortation to Study," has it: "Until you have read through all the books under heaven, you must not lightly wield the correcting brush. What one holds to be wrong, another holds to be right; editions may agree at the source and differ at the branches, or both texts may fall short — you must not put your whole trust in a single corner." That is exactly the point.

Some say the truth cannot be known, that perhaps there never was a truth. I say no. For I believe that so long as each of us keeps to poverty and endures the silence, carries a little more of the will to seek truth from facts and a little less of the wish to court the crowd, and holds to "touching secrets without leaking them, uncovering without sensationalizing," we can draw step by step nearer to the truth, until at the last we walk right into the truth that lies wrapped in fold upon fold of mist.

Ye Xiaoshen

April 20, 2021
