## The Shadowing

Summer was close now. One afternoon, at the foot of the stone steps below the Gion Yasaka shrine, a horse-driver lay dozing deep on the bench of a little open-air tea-stall screened with reeds. All at once he heaved himself upright, drew his eyes down to two threads, and fixed them on one corner of the street. His shaved crown had grown out shaggy and untended. A beard hid half his face; but the light in his eyes, the clean ridge of his nose, the even white of his teeth were proof enough that here was no common horse-driver.

"Granny. Tea money."

He flung the words over his shoulder toward the back of the stall, tossed a few debased coins down onto the earthen floor, and stepped out into the street. Over bare skin he wore a haori, a single loincloth, his hairy shins bound in leggings, and nothing more. Yet for a horse-driver his skin carried none of the highroad's tan. It was Kazama Gohei.

Ever since the day at the hamlet of Kiwano, when Sōkyū's people sprang their ambush, Gohei had fled back to the capital barely alive; and even so, on those same feet, he could not go back to the magistrate's residence. He knew too well what there was to fear in the Kōga assassins. Give them a gap wide enough for a breath of wind, and they came through it, from anywhere. Until he had blotted out his whereabouts, his life stood in no less danger inside the magistracy's walls than out on the road.

(No matter. As things fall out, it suits me well enough.)

So thinking, Gohei shed the samurai for good. At the Maeda mansion his master, Gen'i, kept him on the books as an Iga man and used him on the terms that trade runs by; so if he decamped without a word, Gen'i would raise no needless hunt. That was Gohei's reckoning. A hound will leave its master's hand and plunge into the hills to nose out its game: this was no more than that. Gen'i would take it so, he assumed, as a matter of course.

Gohei had melted into the streets under one disguise after another. Part of the purpose was to throw off the Kōga men who dogged him; but the heart of it was this: let him only pin down, and fast, where Jūzō lodged and what he was about, and lay bare the proof that the man meant to slip into Hideyoshi's stronghold, and Sōkyū's whole plot would stand confirmed at a stroke, and Gohei's own place in the house of Maeda take a great leap upward.

(— It's him. No mistake —)

The samurai in the woven-sedge hat had walked past the front of the tea-shop and off toward Shijō. He wore a gaudy kosode flecked with scarlet, and at his hip a red-lacquer scabbard dusted with gold maki-e; figure and gait alike were past all mistaking: Tsuzura Jūzō. So Gohei read it. Softly he slid one foot forward, and from a few ken behind the man, hunched low, set off picking his way through the shade.

(Jūzō, now — a great slow-moving carcass of a man, and even so he must always deck himself out like one of the kabuki bucks. Naturally he draws the eye. All the easier to fasten a thread to him.)

He spat, well pleased with himself. That one man's person could be worth a thousand koku — the thought was almost too sweet to bear. And at the same time there was something pitiable in Jūzō's dullness, that even now, in this age, never once dreamed of shrugging off the dogged scruple of the Iga trade.

(Wits. It's wits, and nothing more to it.)

Gohei jutted his chin at Jūzō's broad shoulders moving on ahead and gave them a silent sneer. And by some answering reflex, what rose in his mind was Kisaru.

(Kisaru — a pity, a fine woman thrown away. Well, she'll not be among the living now.)

No more feeling stirred in him than at the death of a cat. Only while she lived had Kisaru's body and her craft been of any use to Gohei. Death, for a ninja, was simply the using-up of a tool. A man does not live by the tripe of his belly or the flesh of his heart; by the ninja's reckoning of things, a man exists for his use and nothing besides. That was the one understanding of mankind that Gohei owned, ground into him from infancy through the ninja's long discipline. The haze on the fields, the very stones by the roadside, all things that were in the world existed to be spent to the ninja's ends, and it was in order to be so spent that they held on to their lives — so Gohei believed. Toward a woman he had once desired the law bent not a hair. Let her die, and she was wiped from him clean as a smear rubbed away: such was the strange creature that a ninja was.

(So. He's got wind of it, has he?)

Somewhere about the Shijō bridge Jūzō stopped short, and seeing it, Gohei felt a jolt go through him. Jūzō had made a small show of canting his sedge hat to one side.

While Gohei kept himself hidden under the eaves of a boat-shed, Jūzō stood a good while where he was, gazing off, to all appearances, toward the Shimeigatake heights of Mount Hiei, where the clouds of a rainless rainy season hung; then, at last, he moved off again at an easy pace.

(No. He's not marked me.)

Gohei was easy again. Long years of the work told him as much. Sure enough, Jūzō came ambling up to the wicket of a sideshow booth pitched on the dry riverbed and, one hand tucked in his breast and his left sleeve swinging loose, sauntered in without a care.

"Step along in!"

The booth-tout's voice rang out at him, brisk and full of swagger.

"One for one, right this way, good sir! We're packed to the walls inside, but squeeze on in, squeeze on in! Now then, now then — a giant boar out of Kifune, three hundred kan of him, and miss him today and you'll not see the like twice in a lifetime, not a monster the like of this! Tusks three shaku, two sun long; bristles a full one shaku, six! Carry off a loose hair and paste it to your ridgepole, it wards off fire; paste it at the demon-gate, and no demon comes near — such is the wonder-working power in the beast! Now then, now then!"

"Step along in!"

From under the eaves of the boat-shed Gohei stood off and eyed the coarse straw mat over the wicket where Jūzō had vanished, and cocked his head. He could not fathom what possessed Jūzō to go out of his way to watch so witless a show; but there was no help for it — better to go in after him than to stand puzzling.

Gohei tossed two Eiraku coins onto the tout's stand and flung the mat aside himself. Inside, sure enough, the place was packed to bursting; but there was no trouble picking out the tall Jūzō. He had taken off his hat, of course. His profile stood plain in view. With a look of all but perfect unwariness he was watching the boar's cage set up in the middle of the booth.

Only, Jūzō stood far too tall. Every so often, from somewhere behind him, an irritated bark of abuse flew up — someone, no doubt, driven past patience by that height.

"Here, you — samurai in front. Pull your neck in a little, won't you? There's no telling whether you've come to see the boar, or whether we've come to see Your Honor's back!"

"This any better?"

Each time, Jūzō would smile ruefully and duck himself lower.

"Still your back! Like a curtain hung right in front of my eyes."

"How's this, then?"

Crouching lower and lower, Jūzō at last — out of real consideration, perhaps, for the man behind him — began working crabwise through the press of the crowd. So long as he kept moving, he must have reckoned, he would not plague any one man behind him for long. But as he went, a strange thing: Jūzō's height began, little by little, to dwindle. By the barest degrees, a hair at a time, almost too slight for the eye to hold, Jūzō was shrinking. Before long his head was gone. And in the end even the gaudy kimono sank away and was lost in the black welter of the crowd.

(Hm—?)

Gohei held his breath. It seemed Jūzō had already sensed that someone was on his tail. Gohei did not stir from his place; he honed his gaze to a point and followed where his man went. Jūzō himself he could no longer see, but each time the man shifted, the crowd swayed with him, and that much Gohei could just make out. He had only to watch the wake of that swaying. And the wake was tracing a slow circuit of the booth, like the track a ship leaves on the water.

(So — he means to make for the way out.)

Way in and way out were one and the same wicket. Gohei fixed his eye steadily on the coarse mat at the entrance. Before it the customers passed ceaselessly in and out, churning up an eddy of bodies. A man with the look of an idler came out. Then, a moment later, a peddler-woman went in. A man dressed as a merchant sauntered out humming under his breath; and after him, next, a painter. In the thick of that crowd Jūzō had changed himself over, and cleanly. Now he wore a shabby, plain kosode dyed a dull brown, no sword at his hip, and carried in his arms what looked like a wrapped hanging scroll. It was Tsuzura Jūzō beyond a doubt. Gohei watched that profile and marveled.

(Nicely done. He's everything they call him — first in all Iga, a past master of the shadow art, no less.)

Out on the road, Gohei made his profit of the crowded bustle of the early-summer dusk, slipping from one passer-by's back to the next as he held behind the painter that was Jūzō. Jūzō walked on, unhurried as ever. After Gohei had followed the better part of a small half-hour, it seemed to him that a certain ease of relief had begun to loosen Jūzō's shoulders. By the time lamps were being lit in the houses to either side, Gohei's shadowing had come off. He saw Jūzō turn in at the earthen entry of a small inn that looked to be the man's roost, and marked him down.

(From here it's only to sit and watch, and take my time about it.)

Down both sides of the narrow lane, no more than two ken across, inns with red-ochre lattice fronts stood ranked close as the teeth of a comb. Gohei took a room at the inn directly across from the one Jūzō had entered.

"Landlord. I've money."

The host had eyed the horse-driver Gohei with suspicion, and the first thing was to set his mind at rest. Money, before anything else, is proof of a man's standing. Gohei showed him all the coin he had, there before his eyes, then pressed a single grain of silver from it into the host's palm, and —

"I'm from the Maeda mansion. There's an inquiry on foot, and a man I'm to follow. Mind you don't stint me."
