## Chapter 7. Treading Thin Ice, Never Relenting

### (1) The Bitterness of Failure Is a Cruel Torment

At the start of this year I had been summoned from Tianjin, by way of Shanghai, to Hong Kong; and now I was summoned again, from Hanoi, by way of Haiphong, to Hong Kong. Both were solitary journeys made alone, coming and going upon the sea; yet in mood the two were worlds apart. On the way out I had been full of hope and of boundless confidence; on the way back I was as a man who has lost his bearings, all before him a blur. The bitterness of failure is a cruel torment. I said earlier that I had the courage to shoulder my responsibility, and that was no mere phrase to keep up appearances. In truth, once I knew that I had blundered and disgraced my mission, the blame was mine alone to bear, and there was nothing to do but prepare myself to submit to a review of the work—to be called to account, to be punished, all of it referred up to my superiors for discipline.

This was altogether unlike the affair of 1931, when the sanction of Shi Yousan at Beiping and Tianjin came to nothing, and I, through a complication that grew out of nothing, fled my post from fear of blame. In that case I had felt, in my own mind, that I was being made the scapegoat for the sake of the work; that before the truth had even been looked into I should be saddled with a charge of "kidnap for ransom" (the full particulars are given in Chapter Four of Rooting Out Traitors in the North) was not only a grievous wrong, but something my own heart could not bear. And besides, in those days I was still too green, thinking only to win a little time in which the matter might be cleared up, and heedless of everything else. This present failure was another thing entirely: from the bottom of my heart I owned that I had failed the grave trust of my superiors, and, less pardonable still, that I had bungled a great affair of state, and might even have harmed the future of the War of Resistance. How weighty was the guilt of it! In forming this conviction I believe I had grown a good deal more mature than I was five years before. And it was precisely for this reason that, though I knew full well I should be sternly punished on my return, I bore no grievance and no complaint, and took it all with composure.

The ship I took was a French vessel, half passenger and half cargo, of no great tonnage and but ordinary fittings; its one distinction was that at every mealtime it served, free of charge, a bottle of French red wine. This wine was sour and thin, and to one unused to drinking it the taste seemed exceedingly poor; yet for me, just then, it had two great virtues: it whetted the appetite, and it dispelled a little of my gloom.

After two days and nights upon the sea we reached Hong Kong; there I transferred to a small lighter to go ashore, and, along Connaught Road, found at random a small hotel.

I got word at once to the "Hong Kong District," and they informed me that they had already booked me a ticket with the Eurasia Aviation Company, but that I should have to wait until the evening of the third day for a flight to leave. What was to be done? Though my heart flew homeward like an arrow, I had to master my patience and wait it out.

Just then I was in the grip of a deep sense of shame, and dreaded meeting our old colleagues; above all I was unwilling to speak of the Hanoi failure. That very evening the man in charge of the "Hong Kong District," together with Mr. Zhang, who managed the finances of the "Juntong," insisted out of friendship on standing me a meal. Here was a quandary: to go was to burn with shame; not to go, was that not a little wanting in common feeling? Happily, they were all most understanding; at table they spoke only of the moon and the breeze, and of the Hanoi business let not a single word fall. I felt they were very good to me. Looked at from another angle, this too was one of the fine traditions of the "Juntong."

Idle on my journey, I leafed through old newspapers, and by chance came upon a copy of the South China Daily News published a few days before. This paper was run by the Wang faction; after the "Hanoi Incident" it had put forward six "leads," alleging the deed to be the work of "Blue Shirt Society" men. The six points ran as follows:

One: "The Chongqing side, in order to assassinate Mr. Wang, specially opened an air route from Kunming to Hanoi—on the one hand to make it easy to bring in the killers and their weapons, and on the other, once the deed was done, to carry away both men and matériel by plane."

Two: "They used diplomats as cover, producing red passes, so that the weapons could be freely brought into Hanoi." Three: "A portion of the assassins was dispatched by a Blue Shirt Society organ in Hong Kong, an organ that is a section of the Southwest Transport Company." Four: "The firearms seized by the Hanoi police that day were not of French manufacture, and such weapons have never been found within the territory of Annam."

Five: "A few days before the incident, the assassins rented, at a high price, from a foreigner, a Western-style house opposite the Wang residence, the better to carry out the deed."

Six: "After the murder, the French-language daily of Hanoi reported in large type that it was the work of the Blue Shirt Society."

The facts as the writer understood them naturally did not tally with the six leads the South China Daily News put forward—which is just why a thing seen through a veil cannot be seen clearly; and yet, where the grounding in basic common sense is not sufficient, it can make for a great laughingstock too.

One: that the authorities in charge of communications opened an international air route from Kunming to Hanoi was on account of wartime need; the Juntong had no reason, nor would it make a mountain out of a molehill, to demand that the government open a route specially in order to send a few men abroad. Besides, the opening of an international air route requires the consent of both parties, or of several; one cannot simply fly wherever one takes it into one's head to fly.

Two: the Juntong never used "red passes"; indeed it avoided, so far as possible, carrying "official passports" at all, lest it give a handle to others. Of this, the fact that the three men arrested all used the ordinary passports of merchants is clear proof; even the passport the writer himself used was an ordinary one. Things of this kind they ought to have known: a so-called "red pass" is not to be issued at will, still less would one carry an official passport merely for the convenience of moving weapons.

Three: as for the name "Blue Shirt Society," an explanation has already been given in Rooting Out Traitors in the North, and I need not go over it again here; while the "Southwest Transport Company" in Hong Kong was but another organ with which Mr. Dai had ties. In fact, the assistance rendered to the "Hanoi work" should have come from the "Hong Kong District" under the "Juntong"—a point they could of course make no sense of.

Four: what the South China Daily News means is that, the firearms being not of French make, and never before found on the spot, this proves they were brought in from outside; and being brought in from outside, they must therefore be the work of men sent from Chongqing. Is such reasoning logical? In fact the weapons were brought in by sea, land, and air separately, and among them were products of both America and Germany. But supposing there had also been French goods among them—then how would that be explained?

Five: I do not know on what grounds they were so certain. We did not in fact rent any house opposite the Wang residence. What was, was; what was not, was not. For opposite the Wang residence ran a very, very broad avenue, and in the middle of the road stood old banyans and royal palms, so that one could see nothing however one tried. Had we truly rented a room among the near neighbors of the Wang residence, and watched it over a long time, then perhaps in the end things would not have gone awry.

Six: here again the "Blue Shirt Society" is brought up. Beyond making the "Blue Shirt Society" out to be something formidable, I really can see no other meaning in it.

At last I bore the waiting out to the second evening, and only then was the flight ready. This route from Hong Kong to Chongqing, it was said, must always be flown by night, to avoid attack by the Japanese army of occupation while passing over the fallen zones; but whether that was the true reason I knew the fact without knowing the why of it. Sure enough, we had not been flying long when, passing over the skies of Guangdong, searchlights from the ground swept up at us—happily, no guns were fired.

The plane I took was one of the Eurasia Aviation Company's old crates, very poorly fitted, and giving one very much the feeling of an old ox hauling a broken cart. As we flew over the Shiwan Great Mountains of Guangxi we met with unstable air currents, and the fuselage was flung about most violently, plunging suddenly some tens of feet and then slowly floating up again; in under a minute it would be at it once more—it fairly worked one over. The stewards aboard offered no word of explanation to comfort the passengers, and the people on the plane, not knowing what was going on, were all frightened out of their wits, and truly, that once, laid their states of mind quite bare. Some kept wiping the sweat away; some, palms pressed together, chanted the Buddha's name without cease; some asked this and that and got no answer; some simply shut their eyes and waited in stillness upon Heaven's disposing. This spell lasted, in all, perhaps ten-odd minutes—torment enough.

The night passed in a half-sleep that was no sleep, and the next forenoon we reached the Shanhuba airfield at Chongqing. I searched a long while and saw no one come to meet me; it may be that word had not reached in time, or it may have been a deliberate coldness. Be that as it may, precisely because no one came to meet me, I felt on the contrary a load lifted. By our own inner custom, in the ordinary way, whenever a comrade is about to be disciplined he is "collected" the moment he steps off the plane. Now, since no one had been sent expressly to meet me, perhaps the matter would not be too grave?

It was my first time in Chongqing, and where to turn and what to do I had not the least idea. Just as I was at a loss, my eye fell upon Wang Yunsun. He had been my clerk when I was chief of the "Beiping Station"; I knew him well and we had a friendship, and having run into him now I hastily called out to him, and, coming straight to the point, begged him to settle me for the time being in some place to lodge. He blurted out at once: "Better go first to Elder Sister's; she has empty rooms to spare there." The "Elder Sister" that Brother Yunsun spoke of was his own elder sister, Wang Chiping, who was also the wife of my old friend Brother Wang Zhaohuai; and there was yet a further tie, for Wang Chiping had been the very matchmaker of my own marriage.

Brother Zhaohuai was at this time commander of the "Special Service Regiment"—a unit directly under the Juntong—with his office at the regimental headquarters at Wanglongmen, and his residence too was near Wanglongmen. Brother Zhaohuai and his wife Chiping looked after me exceedingly well. On Brother Zhaohuai's guidance I went to the Bureau headquarters at "Xiaoxiangzi" and reported in, and met Mr. Mao Renfeng, the man who in fact ran "Xiaoxiangzi." In those days, though his title was but "clerk," or perhaps "secretary," he managed everything, and one might say he handled all the Bureau's business as Mr. Dai's representative. Above Mr. Mao Renfeng there had originally been a "chief clerk," Mr. Zhou Weilong, who by this time had sulked and stopped coming to work. Mr. Mao Renfeng said nothing at all—he asked nothing of the Hanoi business, nor spoke of the work—and I thought to myself: "How can he be so cold?"

Now I was "out of a job," with very much the feeling of a man "at leisure." I heard no news from Hanoi, nor knew how things stood on the Tianjin side; even of the state of my own household I had heard not a word. The long, lonely days—dull, empty—I had no notion how to pass. If this was to be the punishment I deserved, it was harder to bear than a prison term.

So far as I knew, Mr. Dai had not left Chongqing, and yet he would not once appoint to see me. What all this was for, there was nowhere to go and find out plainly. Even so, I did not run to the Dai residence and write out my name to beg an audience, nor would I hand in a written report to plead my case; for if he should refuse to receive me, or scrawl a few shaming words across the report, how much more mortifying that would be.

At the end of April and the start of May, when the Japanese devils' planes raided Chongqing, Brother Zhaohuai held fast to his post and could not go home, and I accompanied Elder Sister Chiping in fleeing outside to dodge the air-raid alarm. We had no notion where there was an air-raid shelter, and simply found a great rock to sit upon on a patch of open ground near Wanglongmen. Before long more and more people came, gathering in a dense, thick swarm; and I thought, if one bomb were to fall here, it would be a wretched thing indeed.

It was a half-overcast night, the cloud very thin, letting a few stars show through now and again. After the heart-shaking urgent alarm had sounded, XX fell instantly still—not even the dogs barked; and in a moment a droning came drifting from afar, nearer and nearer, somewhat like the flood-tide at the mouth of the Qiantang River in the middle of the eighth month, coming on as though to shoulder the river aside and pound the sea; and in a flash the planes bore down overhead, and again came a rattling clatter. I lifted my gaze on high to search for them—I could not see them, they were hidden by the cloud. Just then, near at hand, came the rumbling boom of xxxx exploding. And then it sank into silence. A little while more, the all-clear sounded; and hard upon it the babble of voices, and in the din each scattered off again to look after his own household, great and small.

If you had seen with your own eyes the scene after an air raid—above all a corpse scorched black as a lump of charcoal, or a bloody thigh hung upside down upon a telegraph pole—you would feel the after-fright of it, and draw in a cold breath.

For safety's sake I moved, along with Chiping's household, to a farmhouse near Futuguan to lodge for the while; here it was but a few dozen paces to a rather better-appointed air-raid shelter, most convenient—for "fleeing the alarm" had by now become one of the great affairs of daily life.

I had been more than a month in Chongqing; the days went hard, but they passed. One day the alarm was raised again, and in the shelter I met Mr. Dai quite unlooked-for. There was some distance between where we stood, with clumps of people, three here and five there, between; the voices were a din, and unless one shouted aloud even a greeting could not be heard clearly. Mr. Dai and I only exchanged a glance, four eyes meeting for an instant and passing on, and neither of us made any sign. It seemed to me that he was somewhat pointedly ignoring me; and yet it was not a hostile bearing—perhaps I was too stubborn, or truly had not the courage to go up and find a word or two to say.

When the all-clear sounded, Mr. Dai strode grandly off amid the press of his household, and I, who had all along hoped he might pass me some word, or stand a moment waiting for me to come up and meet him, was in the end disappointed. How hard to bear is the taste of being cast aside and left unregarded! What was one to call this? Could it, too, be reckoned a rule of punishment? I was choked with a bellyful of humiliation, and could scarcely bring myself to tell others of what had just passed, for I held it to be a kind of disgrace.

And there was more: certain familiar friends who followed at Mr. Dai's back seemed likewise not to see the person of me at all. A strange thing, truly—could this be that other principle of "success alone permitted, failure never"?

The next forenoon a man came from the Bureau headquarters bearing a letter. I opened it and read within: "By Mr. Dai's decision, Comrade Chen Gongshu is appointed Acting Chief of the Third Section of this Bureau; he is to report to the Bureau for duty this very day." This was a surprise; I had never once imagined he would make such a move.

While at Hanoi, about the first ten days of March, I had seen in the Ta Kung Pao published at Chongqing a notice—no more than a few lines of small type—announcing the writer as "Major-General Chief of the Inspection Office of the Party-Anniversary Garrison Headquarters." At the time I had thought nothing of it, and reflected that perhaps it was a notice put out on purpose, to serve as cover. But there was no need for such superfluous pains! On my return to Chongqing, the post of Chief of the Inspection Office of the Chongqing Garrison Headquarters had already been filled by my schoolmate Zhao Shirui, with Tao Yishan as deputy chief. It was said that at first the post had indeed been meant for me, but that afterward, because I had botched the work at Hanoi, the intention was changed. In truth, it was as well that I was not called to it, for I am not the stuff of which such men are made.

To go on: after I received the notice of "Acting Chief of the Third Section, report to the Bureau this day," that same afternoon, past noon, I went to the Bureau headquarters at Xiaoxiangzi to report in. This place I had visited once before, after my return from Hanoi to Chongqing; the man who received my report then was likewise the Mr. Mao Renfeng mentioned above.

"Xiaoxiangzi" is a place-name, and a fitting one—not even a small motorcar can drive up to the door. The building was two storeys high, a room here, a room there, so mean it scarcely made a shape at all. As I recall it carefully, it seems there was not even a plainclothes guard at the front gate—unlike the later days of strict gatekeeping and armed watch, with all that air of consequence. What it was like below stairs as one came in the door, the impression is already blurred, and I cannot call it up. Going up to the second floor, there was a great hall able to hold twenty-odd people; within it stood a large trestle table, which with a spread of blue cloth would do for a meeting, and with the blue cloth taken off would do for a meal. Round about the hall were several doors; the room that faced the head of the stairs was the largest office in the whole house. The great writing-desk of seven drawers by the window was Mr. Mao Renfeng's; on the right as one entered was a five-drawer writing-desk used by the Chief of the Second Section, Mr. He Zhiyuan; and set at an angle to these two desks was yet another office table with but three drawers, originally made ready for Zhou Weilong, the "chief clerk" of the Bureau headquarters—who, it was said, never so much as sat down at it, but gave it one look and departed with a flick of his sleeve. Now it was assigned for my use, and so became the throne of the Chief of the Third Section of the Juntong.

Who my predecessor in the post had been I never learned, nor did anyone hand over to me any unfinished business; it may be that I was myself the first Chief to fill the vacant seat. For there was not a single document in the drawers, and in such papers as I was able to see, I never once found my predecessor's signature or seal. In the eight years since the writer joined the work, I had always served in the "field," and to sit at an office desk handling documents I was a rank amateur. So I could not but seek instruction of Mr. Mao Renfeng.

By Mr. Mao's guidance I came to understand the charge and authority of the Third Section: the Third Section had oversight of two great lines of business, "Judicial" and "Operations," and was therefore divided below into two sub-sections. The Operations sub-section, as its name implies, dealt roughly with the general administration of operational work—such things as personnel and organization, technical training and the like; but it was a "sub-section" without men, with no business in fact, which is to say it had never been organized at all. The Judicial sub-section had been established many years, and had stood originally on its own; now incorporated into the Third Section, it was as though a further storey had been laid over it. Its chief, Xu Zhongqi, was a thoroughly good-natured man. I was now his direct superior; and yet, a few more years on, he became warden of a detention house, and I in turn became a "prisoner beneath the steps" under his charge. The affairs of the world are hard to foretell—it can happen even so! But this is a matter for later, and I will not dwell on it. Below the Judicial sub-section, besides its internal branches, there were three outside units under its jurisdiction; these three units ought all to have had formal names, but I truly do not know what they were called, knowing only that they were, one and all, places for "holding" men.

Mr. Mao Renfeng looked after me most kindly. Take the matter of clothing: because everyone had to wear a Zhongshan tunic to the office, and I had none, nor could afford to have one made, he could not bear to see it, and gave me a suit he had himself worn. Though there was a small, not very conspicuous hole in the seat, in those days it was a kindness by no means slight. And so too with the handling of documents: he taught me how to sign, how to endorse, how to dispose of them; and at the same time he passed on to me, more or less, that whole art of his—how to wait upon a superior with just the right touch, and how to meet the many demands of subordinates. A pity it was too subtle by half, and hard to learn to any good.

I truly had no interest in sitting at an office desk, nor did I wish to make any showing during my time as Chief. What gnawed at me still was: why would Mr. Dai not see me? What I could never for a moment forget was: how did matters stand at Hanoi? How would my failure in the work be brought to a close? And what tugged at my very entrails was: my home, far off in Tianjin—in what state was it now?

Before long my good friend Brother Mao Wanli—the younger brother of Mr. Mao Renfeng—sent a telegram from Shanghai, informing me that my family had already moved from Tianjin to Shanghai, and bidding me set my mind at ease. I had been from home half a year; how they had been living, with never a letter and I in utter ignorance—and now at last there was word, and no comfort could be greater than this. Do not think it was but a scrap of a telegram, two lines of text; in it Brother Wanli had taken on two liabilities. First, he had sent a private message on the public wire—a breach of discipline. Second, how did he know I was in Chongqing?—a leak of secrets. Both were things not permitted to be done! Yet as a matter of private friendship, this was precious feeling indeed.

### (2) The Comrades Left at Hanoi Had Further Actions Still

Not long after the "Hanoi work" failed, the Bureau collected, within the country (at Kunming or Chongqing), a letter; or rather, to say "collected" is less exact than to say "seized." This was a document of the utmost gravity: a letter in Wang Jingwei's own hand to Long Yun (Zhizhou), then the chairman of Yunnan Province. How this letter came into the "Juntong's" hands not a single man can now say for certain; by the writer's own conjecture, it may have been "seized" in the course of its posting, though whether this was so I dare not vouch. According to what is disclosed in the Complete Works of Mr. Dai Yunong, it was thus: "This letter of Wang's, of such grave bearing, was detected by Mr. Dai's working personnel, photographed, and reported up to the Center." As to how it was "detected"—the words are vague, no more than those three characters.

The writer himself had not known before that such a letter existed; it was only after the Complete Works of Mr. Dai Yunong came out in October 1979 that I saw it in the book. Having read it, I formed a notion, holding that this letter must have been the chief cause that brought about the sanction of Wang Jingwei. And yet, until I had made certain of the date the letter bore, I still had some reservation.

The abstract of this letter is printed in the Complete Works of Mr. Dai Yunong, upper volume (the whole work is in two volumes, upper and lower), pages ninety-six to ninety-eight; beyond the "opening omitted" and "closing omitted," only the middle portion remains, and so no date appears either. In order to fix the date the letter bore, I made a special request of a friend to go himself to the "Mr. Dai Yunong Memorial Hall" of this Bureau and look at the original. Having seen it, he told me: "The hand in this letter is the same as the hand we have always seen; it may be taken for Wang's own writing. The original letter has several pages, folded and set within a picture frame that hangs upon the wall; the whole of the contents, of course, one cannot read entire, but happily the signature on the last page is exposed to view, and its date is 'the thirtieth of the third month.'"

To be quite sure, some months later I asked that friend again over the telephone, and he said with certainty: "There is no mistake—it is the thirtieth of the third month." So it was that the notion I had first held, that this letter was the chief factor in the sanction of Wang Jingwei, was overturned by the facts and could not stand. To make the account plain once more: the action to sanction the man Wang took place on March 21, and this letter was written a full nine days after the action.

I here set down the main content of Wang Jingwei's autograph letter to Long Yun, following the abstract already published, in the original words:

"To the honored attention of Mr. Zhizhou, Chairman:

(Opening omitted) Your letter further inquires of the Sino-Japanese situation. As I see it, it differs little from the circumstances before the twenty-eighth of the twelfth month last year. I once put two points to the Japanese authorities: first, my hope that they would swiftly come to terms with Generalissimo Chiang, I being willing, in my private capacity, to lend help from the side; second, whether the proposals of the Yan Telegram might be accepted. The Japanese authorities' reply was most resolute. As to the first: the proposals of the Yan Telegram, if the Chinese government carries them out in good faith, Japan can surely accept. The more difficult among them is the question of withdrawing troops. By the proposals of the Yan Telegram, Japan's withdrawal must be general and swift. Yet should Japan withdraw, and the Chinese armies—above all the Communist armies—come sweeping back under the name of recovering their several regions, then Japan holds that the withdrawal must wait until the Chinese government has real sincerity, and its military strength can truly maintain order in the localities and carry out the treaty of peace between the two nations; only then will troops be withdrawn. The method of withdrawal would be that Japan draws off by degrees and China moves in by degrees, all according to agreement—such is the principle; as to the technique of carrying it out, that awaits the agreement of both sides. As to the second: Japan can only plan the restoration of peace together with a National Government of good faith, and can by no means again trust Generalissimo Chiang. This is not to doubt Generalissimo Chiang's character, but his former diplomatic course and attitude, and the circumstances in which he stands today, are such that Japan holds no negotiation of peace to be possible. Upon this point I have exhausted my wits and can do nothing. Germany once counseled Japan, why not treat for peace with Generalissimo Chiang; and the result of such peace would be that, without demanding Generalissimo Chiang step down, he could not but step down of himself—yet to this counsel Japan still would not agree.

There is one matter of which I would gravely take counsel with you. My long dwelling at Hanoi has this one meaning only: to be of some benefit to Yunnan—this being for Yunnan's sake, for the sake of the great situation of the Southwest, and for the sake of the Republic of China as well. The brightness of your heart, and the difficulty of your circumstances, I know deeply. Because I know your heart is bright, I dared, at our former meeting, to speak my mind to you openly; and because I know your circumstances are hard, I departed in haste, leaving no trace, to wait upon your leisurely arranging—and now three months and more have passed, and I do not know how you have arranged matters. I harbor no extravagant hope; but could you resolutely and openly declare your agreement with the proposals of the 'Yan Telegram,' I should at once come to Kunming, declaring that in my private capacity I offer what I see, for the reference of the government and the nation. As for me, you need only, by the strength of your troops and police, protect the safety of my life and not interfere with my freedom of speech and action; this were enough, and when the great situation shall in time undergo some change, we shall lay plans for a second step's advance. Thus may Yunnan be made secure, the great situation of the Southwest be made secure, the Republic of China be made secure. My wish is truly bound up in this. I have long dwelt at Hanoi, and it is not that I fear anything; yet living beneath another's roof, my words and acts cannot win the trust of the nation, whereas were I to return to the interior, then my standing would be wholly other. The several quarters that lean and gather have their object; the eyes and ears of the world, too, have their focus; the work would be half the labor and twice the effect. Japan's dealings and negotiations with me, too, would be the more readily made to tell. This is what, three months ago, I dared not ask of you, but which today I begin to ask of you—and I know not whether you can find it in you to respond. Upon this layer I earnestly hope you will consider with care, and give me a decisive reply. For, dwelling shut up these three months, morning and night I crane my neck; my long-standing eagerness has not been brief. If you give me your affirmative, then I will resolutely come; if you give me your negative, then I too cannot but plan to go elsewhere—for Japan, with all its repeated delay, is already in a posture where it can wait no longer …… (Closing omitted)"

The purpose of Wang's letter was to incite Long Yun to desert the front of the Resistance, to wreck unity and solidarity, and to answer to his "Peace Movement," and further to furnish him a base from which to set himself up as a rival to the central government. Do not be deceived that his words are soft and yielding; his intent was most venomous. Any who read it will see this at a glance, and no further gloss is needed.

What the writer marks is this: because the letter contains the three phrases—"now three months and more have passed"; "this I dared not, three months ago, ask of you"; and "for, dwelling shut up these three months, morning and night I crane my neck"—it can be judged, past all doubt, that this letter was written only after the "Hanoi Incident." And on this point, if any should comment that the single failed blow at Hanoi may well have goaded Wang Jingwei toward the extreme, I think it would not be going too far. Though the whole truth is not so simple as that, neither can it be flatly denied. The writer and Comrade Wang Luqiao, in our self-reckoning, said as much too: to do it and fail is worse than not to do it at all. It comes to the same meaning.

Let me next give a brief account of the doings of Wang Jingwei after the "Hanoi Incident."

After March 21, Wang still felt danger lurking on every side, and was uncommonly careful in his every move; though he called himself "shut up in seclusion," he had not in fact given up his treasonous activity—the inciting letter to the chairman of Yunnan Province mentioned above is one good proof of it. As for his collusion with the Japanese side, that of course still went on.

On March 22, 1939, the Japanese government received the report of its consul-general at Hanoi, and forthwith convened the Five Ministers' Conference to devise a plan for handling the matter. In the conference it was first decided to dispatch Kagesa Sadaaki, chief of the China Section of the General Staff, to Hanoi to take charge of receiving and escorting Wang Jingwei to some safer place, and then to hold further consultations. Kagesa, on receiving his orders, at once recommended to the Japanese cabinet that Inukai Ken go along as his aide (Inukai Ken belonged to the Seiyūkai, and was the son of Inukai Tsuyoshi, once premier of Japan, and had old ties with certain Chinese figures); the other attendants included the army surgeon lieutenant-colonel Ōsuzu, the gendarme warrant-officer Maruyama, and the gendarme sergeant Matsuo. He chartered from the Yamashita Steamship Company a cargo vessel, the Hokkō Maru, to make the voyage to Vietnam. This operation, after two weeks' preparation, at last set out on April 7; an hour after sailing, Kagesa announced that the destination was Haiphong in Vietnam.

The Hokkō Maru was some ten days upon the sea, and only on April 16 put in at Haiphong. Because the party were suspicious of every shadow, and because the Japanese consulate-general at Hanoi was deliberately laying false trails, Kagesa Sadaaki and Inukai Ken let two more days pass, and only on April 18, by a stealthy, furtive course, met with Wang. The meeting-place was the third floor of Wang's residence on Gao Lang Street; and the man who carried word between them was Zhou Longxiang, who thereafter served throughout as Wang's interpreter.

The result of their conference was that they reached three points of agreement:

One: that, with the understanding and help of the Annam authorities, Wang should leave Hanoi as soon as could be;

Two: that Wang's opinion be accepted, and he go secretly to Shanghai aboard the Japanese vessel Hokkō Maru;

Three: that questions of future "cooperation" await their arrival at Shanghai, to be deliberated at length.

Thereupon, acting on this agreement, in the dead of night on April 25, under the close escort of the Annam authorities, they left Hanoi. At sea, owing to the problem of joining the small boat to the large ship, there was a further delay of three days, and only on April 28 did they steer for Keelung in Taiwan, to lie a while at anchor; and after taking on fuel and provisions, on May 8 they reached Hongkou in Shanghai. Wang landed in secret and first hid himself on Yuyuan Road in the French Concession, then set on foot the activity of manufacturing a puppet regime.

Kagesa Sadaaki was one of the men of gravest consequence in the manufacture of the Wang puppet regime. During the period when the Wang puppet regime was brewing in Shanghai, in everything Kagesa Sadaaki stood as the Japanese military's representative and the party to be negotiated with. After the Wang puppet regime made its entrance, Kagesa Sadaaki became the first-generation supreme head of Japan's greatest political-espionage organ in China—the Ume Kikan. After the outbreak of the Pacific War, Kagesa was transferred to command a division, and took part in the campaigns of Southeast Asia. When Japan was defeated and surrendered, Kagesa died of illness at Tokyo in September of 1948.

While Kagesa lived, he too wrote a good deal; after his death, his son-in-law Tanigaki Sen'ichi and his secretary Kuraoka Katsuyuki put his posthumous manuscripts in order and published a piece, "Why Did Wang Jingwei Set Up a Regime?" Though its angle differs from ours, it is still not without value as material to consult; and I here excerpt from it the part concerning Wang Jingwei after he went from Hanoi to Shanghai, to spare going over the same ground again. The text runs thus:

Wang agreed to the outcome of the Shanghai Baimei talks with the Japanese side, and on the eighteenth of the twelfth month of 1938 left Chongqing, reaching Hanoi on the twentieth.

Konoe (the premier), on the twenty-second of the twelfth month, published the so-called "Three Konoe Principles" as a policy for adjusting Sino-Japanese relations. Wang, to answer this statement, on the twenty-ninth sent by the Yan Telegram to the Central Party Headquarters of the Kuomintang, to Director-General Chiang, and to the Central Executive and Supervisory Committees a proposal for peace, urging with all his strength the necessity of Sino-Japanese peace …….

On the first of the first month of 1939, the Chinese Kuomintang resolved to strike Wang from the party rolls and to strip him of all his offices. In the wake of this, the agents of Chongqing came also to Hanoi, and on the twenty-first of the third month Zeng Zhongming was assassinated. The Japanese side, getting word that Wang's circumstances were dangerous, decided at the Five Ministers' Conference to send us to Hanoi to help Wang remove to a safe zone. Inukai Ken and I, aboard the Yamashita Steamship Company's Hokkō Maru, reached Hanoi on the seventeenth of the fourth month; Yano (the Foreign Ministry secretary) and Itō Yoshio had already flown out ahead.

On the eighteenth of the fourth month I met Wang at his residence on Gao Lang Street in Hanoi. After Wang had expressed his regret at the outbreak and the growth of the Sino-Japanese conflict, he said: "As to the conviction that China and Japan must have peace, I have discussed it more than once, in writing or face to face, with Mr. Chiang; but Mr. Chiang, on the one hand doubting Japan's true intent, and on the other governed by circumstance, has been unable to carry peace through." He went on: "I am considering giving up the attempt to move Mr. Chiang to change from within Chongqing, and turning instead to spur Chongqing's conversion from without; and it was just then that I learned from the two men Kagesa and Ume of Japan's peace policy. I think that if Japan can but hold firm to this policy, I believe it not impossible to win the support of public opinion." He said further: "The Konoe statement of the twenty-second of the twelfth month is the greatest guarantee for the growth of the Peace Movement."

Wang held: "To remain at Hanoi is both dangerous and without meaning; Shanghai should be made the base, and the Peace Movement developed there." He was concerned, too, that "to set out from Hanoi requires the understanding of the Vietnam authorities," and that he must "study, once at Shanghai, how to open matters by methods a step beyond the original plan." Wang declared again and again that the guiding purpose of the Peace Movement was to enlarge and organize the people's demand and strength for peace, and thereby to spur the Chongqing government to change its policy of resisting Japan—not to set up another government.

Wang, under the understanding of the Vietnam authorities and the guard of the Vietnam security police, on the twenty-fifth of the fourth month set out by French ship from the island of Cát Bà; on the evening of the twenty-eighth, in the northeast of the Baie d'Along, he shifted aboard the Hokkō Maru, and by way of Keelung in Taiwan, on the eighth of the fifth month reached the Hongkou wharf in Shanghai.

Here it is fitting to set forth Wang's thinking at that time:

"The original plan of the Peace Movement was to organize, with Kuomintang members at the core, a peace body, and by speech to point out the errors of Chongqing's theory of resisting Japan, and to proclaim that peace was the one and only way to save China and to save East Asia. Step by step the camp of peace would be enlarged, in the hope of turning Chongqing about. But on close consideration, to turn the Chongqing government by speech alone is a most difficult thing. For though the theory of peace is indeed a love of China, the theory of resistance is the more kindled by the ardor of patriotism. Yet the theory of peace and the theory of selling out the country are also most easily confounded, and hard to win the understanding of the ordinary man; whereas the theory of resistance readily gains men's sympathy. So there was nothing for it but to prove the rightness of the peace theory by Japan's own just and disinterested action. True, if the Konoe statement could be redeemed to the full, the Chongqing government's theory of resistance would lose its ground, and might even yield to public opinion and lean toward peace. But the question is how to make the Konoe statement real. Ought one, then, to change the original peace plan—besides urging Chongqing to awaken by speech, to set up a peace government, and by fact prove the effect of Sino-Japanese cooperation, so as to rouse the people's opinion and hasten the coming of peace? Of course, the setting-up of this peace government is not for the purpose of overthrowing the Chongqing government; it is only to halt the resistance and further peace; and even should the peace government, of necessity, keep an army, it is by no means to make an enemy of Chongqing. If once peace be realized, then whether or not the two governments merge, or take some other form, I (Wang) will by no means meddle, but will resolutely take the blame and step down, to make my heart plain."

In sum, I am certain that Wang's thought was to set up a peace government, as a demonstration of peace with Japan, and by fact to prove the rightness of the peace theory, so that the ordinary people and the Chongqing government, leaning toward peace, might be led to a peace over all the land. Though after the new government's founding it would, on the surface, stand for the while opposed to the Chongqing government—China being formed into two camps, of peace and of resistance—yet the outcome would be a merging, and only by the merging of the two could a peace over all the land be realized. This was the guiding principle of Wang's Peace Movement.

On the sixteenth of the first month of the fifteenth year of Shōwa (1940), Wang, in his peace circular telegram, said: "I hope Mr. Chiang will take the nation and the people's livelihood as the weighty thing …… to halt the war and treat for peace with Japan, …… and Zhaoming, with his comrades, will surely join heart and strength with you, sir, to hasten the coming of peace over all the country ……." Herein one may see a glimpse of Wang's state of mind.

After this there was a long spell of haggling between Wang Jingwei and the Japanese government, and it was not until the middle of June 1940 that some thread of it was got hold of, though not wholly settled. Of the course of the dealings and negotiations between Wang and the Japanese side, there is a passage in Kagesa Sadaaki's "Why Did Wang Jingwei Set Up a Regime?" which runs thus:

In the first ten days of the sixth month of that year, Wang, together with Zhou Fohai, Mei Siping, Gao Zongwu, Dong Daoning and others, escorted by the Japanese side's two Foreign Ministry secretaries Yano and Shimizu, by Inukai Ken and by me, flew from Shanghai to Tokyo. Wang, on reaching Tokyo, at once met with Premier Hiranuma. Hiranuma first urged with all his force the necessity of Sino-Japanese cooperation in the way of righteousness, and then expressed his respect for Wang's ardor in standing forth to resolve the conflict. Wang replied that, for the meaninglessness of this long contention between the two nations of China and Japan, he had resolved to strive for peace. Wang held that the Japanese side had, for resolving the affair, no more than the three following methods: one, resolutely to adopt the policy of taking the Chongqing government for its object and pursuing peace; two, or, outside the Kuomintang, to seek out men of will in private station as its object, and devise a plan of peace; three, or else, without regard to whether in office or out, whether Kuomintang or no, so long as a man is for peace for the sake of the two nations' future, all together to pursue peace and resolve the affair. Wang said: if the Japanese side held the third method to be the fitting one, he had the resolve to serve as the core of setting up a peace government, and would strive with all his might to attain the end of peace. Premier Hiranuma thereupon declared that the present cabinet held firm to the spirit of carrying on the Konoe statement, and agreed with Wang's view; so long as Wang had such resolve, the Japanese side would give its absolute help.

On the fifteenth of the sixth month, War Minister Itagaki, on the command of Premier Hiranuma, set forth the Japanese side's hopes and exchanged views with Wang. In the talks Itagaki, to avoid the impression of meddling in China's internal politics, was uncommonly careful in his wording. The chief points of the two sides' talks at that time were these:

One, Itagaki asked: "The harm done by the one-nation, one-party doctrine of the past—may we not take this chance to make a reckoning of it?" To this Wang expressed his agreement, and said that in organizing a government this time he would draw in men of the various parties and factions outside the Kuomintang, and non-party men, to take part.

Two, Itagaki proposed: "The men of the two already-established governments, the Provisional and the Reformed, have borne much slander to strive for Sino-Japanese peace; if all were now at a stroke abolished, the Japanese side would feel it hard to face them. Might one turn the Provisional Government into a Political Affairs Committee, and the Reformed Government into an Economic Committee, as organs for the partial handling of matters bearing on Sino-Japanese relations?" Wang said: "North China lies far from Nanjing, and to set up a Political Affairs Committee there, to have it handle governmental affairs to a certain degree, is well enough; but Central China has no need of setting up such an organ. As for the men of the former Reformed Government, however, we shall of course consider finding them places."

Three, Itagaki said: "The Nationalism and the People's Livelihood among the Three Principles of the People, many interpret to mean the very doctrine of admitting the Communists and resisting Japan. Of course this was not Mr. Sun Yat-sen's meaning. May this point be revised this time, and the attitude of China and Japan jointly opposing Communism be clearly declared?" To this Wang expressed his complete agreement.

Four, Itagaki said: "Many Japanese interpret the Blue-Sky-White-Sun flag as the ensign of resisting Japan; at the same time, in the military actions of the Japanese army, if the peace government and its forces use the Blue-Sky-White-Sun flag just as the anti-Japanese government does, mishaps can hardly be avoided. As a practical question, may this be given thought?" Wang was absolutely opposed to this notion, but he promised to consider some method of distinguishing themselves from the Chongqing side.

The writer will, so far as he knows, append a brief gloss to several of the personal names and titles mentioned in the text above, for the reader's convenience:

Hiranuma Kiichirō succeeded Konoe Fumimaro as premier of Japan, and had by then become a puppet of the Japanese military clique. This is proved by Wang Jingwei's direct talks with War Minister Itagaki Seishirō.

Yano Seiki and Shimizu Tōzō, two secretaries of the Japanese Foreign Ministry, though their posts were not high, yet played a very great part in the coming Wang puppet regime.

The "Provisional Government" refers to Wang Kemin's in North China; the "Reformed Government" refers to Liang Hongzhi's in Nanjing. From the dialogue between Itagaki and Wang Jingwei, one may also see how many ounces their weight came to.

In writing this piece, Kagesa, I believe, held something back, and his wording too is rather guarded; it is not so frank as the account carried on pages 209 to 213 of the eleventh volume of the complete translation of Chiang's Secret Records. I here excerpt several passages, to make the matter plain:

Wang Zhaoming, arriving in Japan on his visit, met Premier Hiranuma Kiichirō on the tenth of the sixth month, and held separate talks with the former premier and present president of the Privy Council, Konoe Fumimaro, with Foreign Minister Arita Hachirō, and with War Minister Itagaki Seishirō.

At this time Wang Zhaoming, who did not yet understand that the Japanese government's intent was only to make of him a puppet, on the fifteenth of the sixth month, at his second meeting with Itagaki, put to him his "Hopes for the Respecting of China's Sovereignty," the content divided into the several heads of internal government, military affairs, and economy, and asked the Japanese side for a speedy reply.

In this document of his hopes there was included—"(1) That in the central government no advisers be set, lest men be given cause to suspect Japan of meddling in internal politics; matters to be arranged with Japan may follow the ordinary diplomatic channel, through the ambassador resident in China. (2) That in the highest central military organ an advisory committee be set up, its advisers not limited to Japanese, but including Germans and Italians also. (3) That the Chinese public and private property Japan has taken over in the occupied regions be forthwith returned"—and the like.

These demands were in fact wholly incompatible with the fixed policy of the Japanese government, and so during Wang Zhaoming's stay in Japan they got not a single point of reply.

What the Japanese government opposed head-on were the "Three Principles of the People" and the "Blue-Sky-White-Sun-over-a-Crimson-Field flag." The Japanese side put forward the demand: "The Three Principles of the People are to be revised by the Japanese side," "the national flag must adopt a new design." The result was a compromise—the revision of the Three Principles was for the time laid aside, while to the Blue-Sky-White-Sun-over-a-Crimson-Field flag there was added, above, a cloth streamer reading "Peace, Anti-Communism, National Reconstruction."

Against so treasonous a submission to the enemy as Wang Zhaoming's, the National Government, on the eighth of the sixth month, issued an order for his arrest.

On June 28 Wang Zhaoming returned to Shanghai, and, with the help of Satō Kenryō, deputy chief of staff of the Japanese "South China Expeditionary Army," set about the activity of establishing his so-called "new regime" in South China. He worked upon the Guangdong generals Zhang Fakui, Deng Longguang and others to take part; but Zhang Fakui and the rest, standing on high righteousness, were not to be moved, so that Wang Zhaoming could not but give up his attempt to set up a "new regime in South China," and change his course to the manufacture of a regime at Nanjing.

He first set himself up as the "orthodox leader" of the Chinese Kuomintang, and on August 28, at No. 76 Jessfield Road in Shanghai, the residence of Chen Diaoyuan, former chairman of the Anhui provincial government, convened his so-called "Sixth National Congress of the Chinese Kuomintang."

This "congress" ran three days, with two hundred and thirty-three "delegates" present; throughout, it was but Wang Zhaoming alone performing, wholly at his own manipulation. It chose him "chairman," took anti-Communism for the "policy" of this counterfeit "Kuomintang," and resolved to "adjust Sino-Japanese relations and further the normalizing of diplomatic ties between the two nations."

On September 12, Japan, in order to unify the command of all its army forces in China, newly set up a "China Expeditionary Army General Headquarters" at Nanjing, transferred the Japanese army's inspector-general of education, General Nishio Toshizō, to be commander-in-chief, and appointed the former army minister, Lieutenant-General Itagaki Seishirō, chief of the general staff. This was a measure adopted to meet the pushing-forward of Wang Zhaoming's work of setting up his puppet central government.

On October 24, the Japanese government presented to Wang Zhaoming the "Guiding Principles of the Central Political Conference," and demanded that on this basis consultation proceed for organizing the new government. This was a reply to the "Hopes for the Respecting of China's Sovereignty" that Wang Zhaoming had put to the Japanese side on his earlier visit to Japan; its content ran: that, besides the central government, in the departments of finance, economy, and science and technology Japanese advisers should all be set; and that even within the military advisory group no third-country men of Germany, Italy and the like might be engaged—which came near to denying the whole of Wang Zhaoming's hopes. Even Kagesa Sadaaki, charged with the task of negotiating with the Wang side, grumbled: "This is simply a puppet regime!"

To go on to our own side, the Juntong's work against Wang. Though the writer himself was ordered to leave Hanoi first and be recalled to Chongqing, yet our comrades left at Hanoi, during the period when Wang Jingwei had not yet left Hanoi (from March 22 to April 25), still did not give up the activity of sanctioning Wang Jingwei.

Since I had been transferred away from Hanoi, in point of duty I was no longer the man responsible on the ground for the work, and so of their doings I was naturally not clear. What little is set down here I came to know only afterward, in scattered driblets, or verified in the course of some piece of work; the time of my learning it varies—some a year or two after, some at a remove of several decades. I record it all together now, as follows.

The reader may still recall that in the earlier section, "The Soft Action," Brother Yu Lexing and I had once discussed and tried the use of a drug, but afterward gave it up because certain technical problems could not be solved. We had first meant to put the drug in bread; later Brother Lexing brought out a small can, saying that the drug placed within it could give off a volatile gas, enough of itself to kill a man, and more effective still if it met with heat. In the end, because there was no way to set this small can inside the Wang household's bathroom, the matter was laid aside. And what do you think? When, in the winter of 1941, I was arrested at Shanghai and fell into the enemy's hands, and Wang's wife Chen Bijun examined me, she asked: "Tell me, what was that little can you people set in the washroom for?" At this question of hers I was on the contrary struck dumb—what little can? How was it I knew nothing of it—and then all at once it came to me with a start: was it not, after all, Yu Lexing's "masterpiece"?

I heard that Mr. Xu and Fang Bingxi and the rest had a political and diplomatic play of their own as well, the aim being to hem Wang Jingwei in, so that he could not leave Hanoi and could carry on no wider activity. Perhaps it was a matter of their standing, but on the plane of rank their strength was still wanting, and so it bore no fruit. This is borne out when Kagesa Sadaaki, in receiving and removing the man Wang out of Hanoi, said to Wang: "You wish to leave Vietnam, sir; how do you mean to negotiate it with the Vietnam authorities?" The man Wang answered: "The best is to give the Vietnam authorities no provocation of any kind." Seen thus, it was not so simple as leaving whenever he pleased.

Brother Cao Shi'ang, who is living yet, told me this year (1982), face to face and with his own lips: "The weapons my wife and I brought to Hanoi that year—besides the two pistols and the box of cartridges you took away—we still kept back one pistol, to use ourselves when the chance should be ripe, and did not tell you of it at the time." Brother Shi'ang's meaning was that he and his French wife too had borne a mission of direct action! After the work under my command had failed, he received instructions to go on seeking a chance and pressing it actively forward. After many an involved arrangement, and with the sanction of the "Wang residence," on a certain day in the middle of April, his French wife, got up as a journalist, went to Gao Lang Street and "interviewed" Wang's wife Chen Bijun. This was a prelude to spying out the ways in, and she carried no weapon; the step planned was to lay the design after she had had her look. A pity that time was not on their side—within a few days Wang left the place for Shanghai. When I heard Shi'ang tell of it, I thought only then: had we, that year, but asked Mrs. Cao to make one "reconnoitering interview," would not the error have been avoided? This one thought lay buried forty-three years, and could not be raised in time.

And Brother Cao Shi'ang told me further that Brother Tan Tianqian had a scheme of his own besides—the particulars I of course do not know, only that it too, in a small way, had to do with a foreign woman. Whether it was that the working of it was ill-managed, or that Brother Tianqian was greatly at fault, the thing came in the end to nothing, and he was severely punished besides.

When Brother Wang Luqiao was transferred to Shanghai I do not know; but by the time Wang Jingwei came to Shanghai, Luqiao was already there, his mission being, still, to sanction Wang Jingwei.

### (3) Setting Out Anew: A Thousand-Li Pursuit, Valiant to Strike the Foe

I had worked more than a month at the Bureau headquarters, and had come gradually to perceive that this post of Chief of the Third Section was, after all, no more than an "honorary office." One might interpret it thus: unless one won the high regard of one's superiors, there was truly no more of me for having me and no less for lacking me. And in fact, sitting there all day long, one saw not a few documents, and there was no important business needing to be dealt with. Sometimes Mr. Mao Renfeng, seeing me idle and bored, would kindly pass me two or three papers to look over—to speak of it unkindly, just to dispel my tedium.

Of great affairs handled during my time as Chief there were, one may say, none; of odd and curious trifles there were a few. One was a telegram Brother Wang Tianmu sent from Tianjin, in which there actually appeared the words "you turtle's egg"—a rare thing indeed. Another was that a certain comrade, in a fit of derangement, had killed one of our own; and when I was ordered to try the case, it chanced that an urgent alarm came, and amid a burst of exploding bombs, that shook heaven dark and earth dim and set the dust flying, by the time the dust had settled and order was restored, the whole set of prisoners had, to my astonishment, vanished without a trace—making a great joke past all believing. The third was that, blaming myself for not knowing better, when I saw the daily returns crammed with "law-abiders," I actually conceived the bright notion of putting forward a proposal to "disperse" them—that is, I urged that some of the comrades guilty of light breaches of discipline be simply let go. This once I committed a great taboo, and the endorsement I got back read: "We do not need you to play the good fellow." The upshot was that I had asked for a snub and got it.

I was just preparing to write a report requesting a transfer of work, when forthwith I was ordered to enter the Third Term of the Party-Government Training Class of the Central Training Corps for a month's training. Whether it was training with my post retained, or whether my place had already been struck out, neither was ever set down in writing; and thereafter it too passed off in a muddle. But at the start of the class one had to fill out a form, and on the form was a column for "rank." What rank was I? Even I myself did not know, and yet I dared not fill in something at random; and for this small matter I had to go back to the Bureau headquarters to ask. In the end I was permitted to report as "colonel"—though in truth, I knew well enough, this "colonel" was eight parts a black-market office too.

Of the month's training on Futuguan there is much worth setting down; but as it has no bearing on the Wang case, and yet I cannot bear to pass it over with a stroke of the pen, I must make a long story short. What I can never forget is this—

I met with the principal of my middle-school days, Mr. Wang Fei, styled Chongtian, who was also under training. In my school days I was but fifteen or sixteen, and Principal Wang about thirty-five or -six; now I was thirty, and he must have been around fifty—his dignity of bearing not one whit less than of old, and before him I still held a mind of awe and reverence.

Once, when the ranks were being formed up, I discovered again, by chance, our military academy's First Student Corps commander of the Fifth Term, Colonel Shuai Chongxing, among the files. In our school days it was 1926, and now we were under training in the same class—another happy meeting; yet his rank was still colonel.

During the training, the man whose bunk was above and below mine in the dormitory, and whose desk was the same in the lecture-hall, was Mr. Kong XX; his name is great, and I do not mean to give it here. The discipline being then very strict, and life truly hard to the point of bitterness, we could not so much as get a bowl of shredded-pork noodles when the craving took us. This Mr. Kong, naturally, felt the same. At that time the "Guard and Inspection Group" charged with the security of the Central Training Corps was held concurrently by Mr. Dai Yunong, but was in fact run on the spot by the deputy chief Yang Ying. Brother Yang Ying and I were old partners at the "Beiping Station," and had a friendship; he gave us no few conveniences—and far more than a bowl of noodles at that; at the same time he built a friendship between me and Mr. Kong. It was just because of this connection that a piece of trouble was very nearly stirred up. The inside of it I shall write hereafter—though it will be very hard to set down.

There is one thing more worth the recording, touching upon the most disciplined collective action amid the air-raid alarms. This differed utterly, in kind, from the disordered scrambling of the common folk fleeing the alarm. Whenever the warning alarm sounded, the trainees under instruction had still to carry on with the lesson in hand as usual; only if the urgent alarm was then raised did they stop the lesson, and after that muster on the parade-ground, where the general orderly-officer of the whole class gave the word of command, and in order of file and rank, the target place being pointed out, they advanced at the marching pace, whereupon a further order of "double time" was given. On reaching the air-raid shelter they filed in in ranks, still in their original formation, and stood each in his marked-out place. When all was in order, the general orderly-officer gave the word "stand at ease." By the rule: no running about, no talking, and without the word of command one might not even sit down—and thus they waited on until the all-clear. In the eyes of the common folk running for their lives to dodge the alarm, this was marveled at as a strange sight; and that was well enough, but the sorest trouble was the question, unpreventable for want of facilities, of relieving oneself, great and small, which was hard to solve.

Halfway up the hill at Futuguan there was a natural great cleft, like a gaping open mouth, able to swallow several thousand men, and so it became a shelter fashioned by Heaven and set by Earth, and a refuge for tens of thousands.

A month's time, said to be swift, is gone in the twinkling of an eye. On the very evening of the graduation of the Third Term of the Party-Government Training Class of the Central Training Corps—about the end of July 1939, I think—Mr. Dai Yunong sent a man to let me know that I was to come to his residence at seven that evening for dinner. The notice gave no reason; and of the five of us from the Juntong under training at the same time, I alone was invited—which showed it was no invitation of the routine sort.

Mr. Dai and I had parted at Hanoi, and though we had once come face to face in the air-raid shelter without exchanging a word, this was our first real meeting in half a year; with a smile on his face he shook my hand. Also present were the "Residence Secretary" Mr. Pan Qiwu and several others, all men I knew. Out of the ordinary was a woman guest of tall stature, whom I did not know, and whose standing I understood still less. Because a woman was at table the air was not so very grave; and as Mr. Dai himself talked and laughed, we all felt the less constrained.

This meal, with its eating and its drinking, had already run a full two hours; and though I knew in my heart there must be some purpose in it, he let not the least breath of it slip. By the end we had all drunk pretty near our fill, and so grew unbuttoned; and at that woman guest's urging I actually, in a rush of spirits, bawled out four not-very-tuneful lines of "Changbanpo"—and thereby, at least, spat out a lump of the pent grievance I had carried these several months in my belly, and felt uncommonly relieved.

I thought it was over so, and that any business could perhaps be spoken of on the morrow. Just as I was straightening my clothes to leave, Mr. Dai gave me a tug, and at the same time signed to me to go and sit in his study. At first I thought he must this time be about to bring up a review of the Hanoi work, or even to declare the punishment appointed for me. But he was still his old self: before making his intention known, he first, with reserve, kept a spell of silence, and then, in a single word that struck the mark, spoke out his main design. Mr. Dai said: "Our organization in Shanghai has already suffered wrecking by the enemy and the puppets, and up to now we have not got hold of the whole state of it, and it is worsening still. I have resolved to ask you to go and deal with this problem—to clear away the obstacles, to safeguard the continued carrying-on of the work, and to strike at the traitors who are wrecking the organization. I hope you will set out tomorrow forenoon; we shall keep in touch at all times."

His so speaking showed full well that this was the final decision, which of course brooked no further weighing; yet, before I took on a mission, I ought surely to have some understanding of it. So I could not but ask him to disclose a little more of the real state of things. In truth he had long meant to speak, and only weighed how to put it fittingly.

As though he had some hidden difficulty of speech, he first asked me: "You do not know of the Tianmu business, I suppose?" I shook my head—and indeed had heard not a word. But his mention of Wang Tianmu called to mind that, during my time as Acting Chief of the Third Section, I had received a telegram from Brother Wang Tianmu sent from Tianjin, venting a great deal of spleen, in which the words "you turtle's egg" had actually appeared—the sense being to blame the local unit for its unwillingness to do this and that for him—and, besides, some further rambling, idle talk; and at the time Mr. Mao Renfeng and I, having conferred, had filed the telegram away. I guessed it could hardly be on account of this matter?

Mr. Dai knew clearly enough that I could not know; but to bring me into the picture, he went on: "Tianmu's doing this is far outside the common run of things, and above all one cannot fathom what his motive is. On this trip of yours, you must at all costs understand the thing thoroughly and clearly, and do everything possible to persuade him to come back; I can guarantee that his safety will be absolutely protected."

On the matter of Wang Tianmu, Mr. Dai said only this far, and went no further. The chief reason was that even now he did not fully understand the true face of the affair, and did not yet believe the fact as reported.

He paused again, his face graver still. He directed: "You are to carry on the unfinished work of Hanoi, and press forward the sanction of Wang. On this point, once you reach Shanghai, you may make contact with Brother Wanli, and I hope the two of you can work together."

He weighed it a moment more—about a minute, perhaps—and then said: "The situation in Shanghai (he meant our organizational situation) may, before you arrive, undergo many changes; I shall keep in touch with you at all times."

By now it was drawing near midnight, and he had still made no sign for me to leave, when it suddenly came to me that I had an engagement with Mr. Kong the next day; so I asked Mr. Dai whether he might permit me to stay one more day in Chongqing. He asked me why. I could not but tell him truly of the meeting I had fixed with Mr. Kong, that it was a promise, and how could I go without a word of farewell. He heard me out, and then, without the least weighing, said: "The situation just now is pressing, and we must gain the advantage of time; where is there leisure to take part in social doings? Besides, every formality is already settled—even your seat is booked—and word has gone to the comrades on the Hong Kong side to arrange your passage by ship to Shanghai; the fact is there is no way to change the date. I think it needless. Engagements of this sort are best the fewer the better."

I had no ground to press it further; but in my heart I felt it a matter for regret that I could not keep the tryst as fixed. Yet the tie between Kong and me is not thereby at an end; what I am willing to write and what I am able to write, I mean to write in the two volumes, the third and the fourth, of Nameless Heroes, fitting it to the events of the time. To put it grandly, it was a coming-and-going of a fellowship beyond the ordinary, with much in it to move a man.

The talk between Mr. Dai and me was thus broken off, and so came to its end. I had meant, on taking my leave, to go first to the household of Brother Zhaohuai and his wife Chiping to say good-bye and give my thanks, and at the same time to put my traveling-things in order, and see whether, of the few clothes I had left at their house, there was anything fit to wear. But who would have thought that, as Mr. Dai saw me out, he said he had already booked me a room at a hotel, and had charged the man in charge of general affairs to lay in a few things of clothing for me, and hoped I would take a good night's rest, and hereafter redouble my efforts.

This was my last parting from Mr. Dai; and thereafter, save for the coming and going of letters and telegrams, I never saw him again. To recall it now—it wrings the heart to tell!

When I reached the hotel, someone had already fetched over the things I had left at Brother Zhaohuai's house; one could see that all this had been arranged long before.

That night, again, I did not sleep well: there was an excitement not to be told, for before long I should be reunited with my family; and at the same time an unnameable dread, for the compass of the Shanghai organization was so vast, its affairs so tangled, and with this pair of hands of mine, in a wholly strange setting, I must not only solve the inner problems but complete an unfinished task—think of it, and it might well leave a man worrying so he could not sleep. Tossing and turning I wore the night through, and when day broke, being used to rising early, though I was not slept out I had no wish to lie longer abed. Not long after, Brother Wang Yunsun had already come, bringing another comrade to help me gather my things and see me onto the plane.

The plane I took this time was one of the China National Aviation Corporation's, and the whole way was smooth, with nothing to record. On reaching Hong Kong, they had already bought a ticket on the French mail steamer Général Joffre; I stayed one night, and at noon the next day she weighed anchor. Just about midday, when I was already aboard the lighter and about to steer for the Général Joffre, a comrade from Hong Kong came tracking after me, and, seizing a moment when others were not looking, passed me a small slip of paper. I looked at it—and sure enough the situation in Shanghai had changed. This was what my superiors had charged the Hong Kong side to pass on to me; the slip read: "On arrival, do not on any account go straight home; someone you know will come to meet you." What did this mean? Could something have happened in my household? Surely not. It threw me into a muddle.

Over the two days' voyage there was time enough to think; as for the scenery aboard ship, this once I had no heart to take it in. I hoped only that the ship might reach shore a little sooner, so that I might the quicker break this riddle.

The ship reached the Wusong bar; and I know not why, but only after several hours' waiting did she steer into the Huangpu River, and after a long wait again in mid-river before she came alongside the wharf. From far off I had already caught sight of my old friend Brother Hu Yongquan standing on the shore, accompanying my wife. The moment I stepped ashore, my wife hastened over to meet me and told me in a low voice: "I have rented a room and taken the children there; Kangzi and Yinzi come often, and lest it be awkward to be seen, Mr. Mao and the others bade me come to meet you; and I have dragged Hu the Third along too, meaning to ask him to settle you in some place to lodge." Thereupon I clasped hands with Third Master Hu, and together we got into a car, driving first to the Hu household at the Carlton Apartments to sit a while, and then to decide where this person of me was to be put.

I must begin with "Kangzi and Yinzi." Wang Kangzi, also called Chanhong, and Wang Yinzi, also called Chanlü, were the two daughters of Brother Wang Tianmu. In 1933, when Brother Tianmu was sentenced and imprisoned, and in 1935, when the writer suffered for keeping the law against discipline, my wife and the two sisters Kangzi and Yinzi were held in one and the same place, becoming the "bound-along" of their husband and father; and so they were "fellow-sufferers" and formed a close bond. This meeting again at Shanghai, and the talking-over of old ties, was but the common course of feeling and reason.

Then, how did all these people come to be connected up? The particulars would make a long story; but first, Brother Mao Wanli, after being sent to Shanghai, had found my wife; Wang Luqiao, transferred from Hanoi to Shanghai, came under Brother Wanli's command; and Tianmu's third daughter Yinzi had known Luqiao back in Nanjing, and, it was said, meeting again in a strange place, they went about together very closely and had their trysts from time to time—and so, one drawing another, they were strung up together. As regards our organization, this was not permitted, was against the principles; and yet it was a thing hard to avoid in private.

Then to go on with myself. My present case was that I had a home and could not go to it, and I owed much to Brother Yongquan's warm-hearted care. He proposed that I lodge for the while at his place, and go at once to look for a house; once a house was found and the family moved, I could go back, and then would not every problem be solved? Good—so it was settled. In about a day, Brother Yongquan had sublet, from the household of a friend named Hua, a large room on the third floor and a garret-room; and I too had a place to lay my head. This place was on Route Doumer in the French Concession, and only afterward did I learn that directly opposite where I lodged was the Du residence.

I had not been many days in Shanghai, and had not so much as touched the work, when I received a telegraphic order from Mr. Dai: appointing me to take over as "Chief of the Shanghai District." The "Shanghai District" was the largest field unit under the "Juntong"; but at this moment it was in the very midst of "the songs of Chu on four sides," of alarm at the cry of a crane or the rustle of the wind, so that this was far more tangled than my former mission, and I was truly in fear and trembling.

The former chief of the "Shanghai District," Wang Tianmu, was no longer in the post; the acting chief and concurrent leader of the First Action Squad, Zhao Lijun, was on sick leave in hospital, and had besides been ordered transferred to Luoyang. At this moment all the business and affairs of the "Shanghai District," inner and outer, rested wholly on the shoulders of the "Shanghai District" secretary, Brother Zheng Xiuyuan, who bore the real responsibility. Besides him there were but the assistant secretary Hu Shangwu, the intelligence compiler-reviewer Liu Yuanshen, and a few cipher-clerks and courier-liaison men. Brother Liu Yuanshen is also the man who is now the collator of the whole of Nameless Heroes.

Brother Mao Wanli did not belong to the "Shanghai District"; his title was Inspector-General of the Shanghai region. That is to say, apart from the "Shanghai District," he was the inspector-general of all the "Juntong's" work units within the city of Shanghai and its neighboring regions. And in fact, besides directing the general work of the several units, Brother Wanli bore also the special mission of sanctioning Wang Jingwei as occasion offered. Brother Wang Luqiao, transferred from Hanoi to Shanghai, was of course there for the carrying-out of this very task. So he took Brother Wanli's command alone and individually, and did not belong to the local organization—the "Shanghai District."

On what day Brother Luqiao reached Shanghai I cannot find out from the records; but in the "True Record of Three Perils on the Shanghai Shore," written by Brother Zheng Xiuyuan, I found that Brother Luqiao was arrested on July 14, 1939, in the French Concession of Shanghai. The course of it, drawn together and set in order from materials of various quarters, I will tell from the beginning.

In July, I was under training at the Central Training Corps in Chongqing, and of the affair that befell at Shanghai I of course knew nothing; but at that time my household had already removed from Tianjin to Shanghai, and some of the matters my wife had word of, or was even herself present at. The thing was this:

One day, Brother Wanli, Brother Luqiao, the "Shanghai District" accountant Bai Shengzu, and my wife—four of them—were at mahjong, when, before long, Wang's second daughter Yinzi telephoned Luqiao, asking him out to meet her. All hoped Luqiao would not go; his going would make it "three short of one," but that was a small thing—now, in these many-troubled days, when a single evening held alarms enough, if some mishap were to befall it would be no light matter. Luqiao held there could be no trouble at all, and that he would go and be straight back, and not keep them long waiting. Sure enough, in under twenty minutes Luqiao was back; and so all breathed easy and went on with the game, asking no further questions. But scarcely had they played two or three hands when the telephone rang again; Luqiao snatched to answer it, and all, hearing his tone, knew it was again the third daughter calling; Luqiao said nothing, but set down the telephone and made to go. Brother Wanli could bear it no longer, and spoke up too to dissuade him from going, moving him with words of his personal safety and the bearing on the work; but Luqiao held they were making a mountain of this sesame-seed, mung-bean trifle. In the end he went off, set on keeping the tryst. This going, he fell into a trap, and came not back.

Brother Xiuyuan wrote, in the "True Record of Three Perils on the Shanghai Shore": "When I reached No. 71 Maiyang Road, and was just in the midst of perusing the dispatches received that day, of a sudden came a telephone call from Brother Mao Wanli, the inspector-general of the Shanghai shore; in veiled words he told me that Brother Wang Luqiao had been seized by someone, and bade me devise a swift means of rescue. I at once telephoned the leader of the First Group of the intelligence section, Zhu Xiaogu, asking him to make speed to consult the two brothers Liu Junqing and Liu Shaokui (of the International Settlement police-house), to spy out into whose hands Luqiao had fallen, and by every possible means to rescue him."

At first it was known only that Brother Luqiao had been arrested, but not yet into whose hands he had fallen; only afterward was it learned that it was the "West Shanghai ruffians" (as the Shanghai people called the Wang puppet special agents) who, at the instigation of the Japanese gendarmerie, had, through word passed by the Japanese side to the French Concession police-house, cut off Brother Wang Luqiao's way in the street and arrested him. When he was taken to the French police to go through the "handing-over" formalities, the French police-house, having also to put a few questions as usual and keep a record, unexpectedly discovered that Wang Luqiao was in fact a "wanted man" of the Hanoi Wang case. So it was that the French police-house, hitting the mark by a wild stroke, had unwittingly taken a "wanted man," and Brother Luqiao, having all but flung himself into the net, became a "prisoner beneath the steps" of the French. Fortune and misfortune are hard to judge apart: though a term of imprisonment could not be escaped, he yet, in the end, slipped past the enemy-and-puppet's peril of life and death!

In the upshot, Brother Luqiao was escorted back to Hanoi "to answer the case," and sentenced to imprisonment for life; only after the victory in the War of Resistance was he at last released, together with Zhang Fengyi, Yu Jiansheng, and Chen Bangguo.

The great muddled, tangled Shanghai was nowise like Hanoi, so quiet and simple. The fine great chance of before was lost already; and now, to mend the fold after the sheep is gone—who says it is too late?

Wang and his household, and the Wang puppet clique, once come to Shanghai, could be called, compared with Hanoi, a throng of men and a press of power; the strictness of his guard needs no telling. Our action could reach no further than to track along behind him; to say nothing of drawing near, even to gaze at him from afar was hard to do at will.

In the setting of the real world, the place in which we stood was, just so, beset by foes on three sides, in peril doubly great: the Japanese army of occupation, forming a system of its own, with its gendarmerie mad and murderous, marked us for its foe number one, and would not rest content till it had stamped us out; the lackeys great and small newly gone over to the Wang puppets busied themselves solely with dealing against us, to earn their merit; and the authorities of the International Settlement and the French Concession, too, under Japanese pressure, had no choice but to make shift to meet them, and though a part of their police and detectives had sympathy for us, another part were willing to play the tiger's lackey, and were crueler even than the enemy and the puppets. Bound in so, we were as men trussed hand and foot doing mortal battle with a host of powerful foes; not only was the peril of the work increased, but our striking power was greatly canceled out as well.

By the time the traitor Wang had for a long spell taken up residence at Nanjing, our side, besides the local work units, the "Shanghai District" and the "Nanjing District," each charged with the mission of sanctioning the traitor Wang, had the Juntong headquarters and Mr. Dai himself directly disposing of matters, unceasingly making individual or ad hoc assignments, to make good our shortfall. And for this cause there came, one after another and treading in each other's steps, many patriots of high resolve, who went to their peril with a great heart, valiantly gave their lives, and went calmly to their deaths for righteousness—a tale of grief and grandeur that the pen cannot exhaust in the writing.

In the postscript at the very end of this book, a detailed account is given of the life-and-death whereabouts of the nineteen men who took part in the Hanoi work, to record the fierceness of their sacrifice or the misfortune of their fate; and this, indeed, is the final intention of my writing Nameless Heroes.
