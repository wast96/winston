## Chapter Seventeen. The Hour of the Ox, Second Half (2 a.m.)

The common folk come to watch the fun, the performers up on the lantern-float carts, the officials and imperial kinsmen and the envoys of the many nations standing at the edge of the terrace — all of them, as if by one accord, fell silent, waiting for the birth of a wonder worthy of a golden age.

The third year of Tianbao, the fifteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Ox, second half.

Chang'an; the southeast corner of the Xingqing Palace plaza.

Yuan Zai was a rational man. He held that everything in the world could be sorted into two kinds: what one could enjoy, and what one could not. The meaning of life lay in ceaselessly turning the latter into the former.

And so he could never understand how the common people of Chang'an could work themselves into such a state over a Lantern-Crown Red Tally they would never in their lives be entitled to enjoy. Yuan Zai looked coolly at the crowd seething to the very pitch of frenzy on the distant plaza; the mad faces of those foolish women and boorish men struck him as pitiable.

A low rumble came suddenly from overhead. Yuan Zai raised his head and saw that the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower had woken at last. Its body first shuddered a few times, giving off a harsh grinding and creaking of things pressed together, and then several of the outer turning-poles began to move. The twenty-four lantern-chambers began to revolve slowly about the core of the tower.

The Lantern-Crown Red Tally was even now hurrying into the Xingqing Palace; the many tedious security checks could not be skipped, and it would likely take a while yet. So although the tower had begun to turn, its candles were not yet lit, and its huge black bulk, thrown into relief by the torches of the Xingqing Palace plaza, looked not like some true-cultivated immortal but rather carried a hint of the sinister, like Kuafu of high antiquity gazing down upon the multitudes of men.

"A lantern-tower on this scale must have cost a pretty sum," Yuan Zai sighed inwardly, his eyes fixed on it.

Then his gaze sharpened. A human figure and some object came bursting out of the tower, tearing through its outer skin, tracing an arc through the air, limbs waving feebly a few times, and then dropped heavily to the ground — landing, as it happened, not far from Yuan Zai.

The unexpected had happened after all!

The others had not yet grasped what was happening, but Yuan Zai had been waiting a long time for this. His eyes lit up, and in two or three bounds he rushed over. He saw the man lying on the ground, limbs twisted, fresh blood trickling from the back of his skull. He flung himself down, propped the man up, and looked first at his face: a hunchbacked old man.

The old man's mind was already clouding. He lifted a trembling hand: "The qilin-arms... explosion... the turning-gear... the Tianshu." Then his head lolled and he fell silent. Yuan Zai, wholly mystified, reached out to support the old man's neck — and found on it a long, narrow line of blood.

This man had had his throat cut before ever he fell from the tower.

By now a soldier of the Lüben Guards had picked up the object that had fallen out as well. Yuan Zai looked: it was a long bamboo tube of peculiar make. He gave it a shake, and there seemed to be a sloshing of liquid inside. He pulled the stopper from one end, and a sticky black fluid ran out.

"That's fierce-fire thunder!" cried one of the soldiers in alarm; he had taken part earlier in the cordon against the Türk Wolf Guards, and the stuff still gave him the shivers.

Yuan Zai was so frightened that he flung it away at once. He had read the reports: a single barrel of fierce-fire thunder made from Yanzhou rock-oil could level near half a ward. If the thing went off in his hands, what then?

By this time the Longwu Army too had been alerted. The guard-corporal of the checkpoint came over with a few men and asked what had happened here. Yuan Zai flashed his Jing'an Bureau waist-token and said that they were investigating a case, that they had just seen this man and this object fall out of the tower, and that the murderer was still inside.

The guard-corporal leaned close to the old man's corpse and started violently: "Isn't this Master Mao Shun?"

"And who is that?"

"The master builder of the lantern-tower."

At the mention of that office Yuan Zai's mind raced, and in a moment he had thought the whole thing through. He seized the Longwu guard-corporal, his tone grave: "I fear some villain has slipped into the Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower, meaning to wreck it. Look — these qilin-arms are packed full of fierce-fire thunder; once they go off, the tower will be utterly destroyed. Master Mao, I daresay, could not stop them in time, and the Pifu flung him brazenly from the tower."

It was rather a lot to take in, and the guard-corporal, at a loss, hastily said he would go and report to his superiors.

"There's no time!" Yuan Zai barked. "Master Mao has already been foully murdered; the Pifu must be inside even now, making ready to strike."

The guard-corporal was used to obeying orders, but he had no ready answer for so sudden a turn. Yuan Zai went on: "This is the very case we of the Jing'an Bureau are pursuing, and we have brought men enough. Now call up your people — we go into the tower at once!"

"But — this is against the rules..."

"By the time the great Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower is destroyed, the first head to fall will be yours!" Yuan Zai threatened. The guard-corporal went white with fear: if a villain had got into the tower, there was no way he, as its guard, could shrug off the blame. Talked round by Yuan Zai, he had no choice but to call his fellows to shift aside the spiked barrier.

Yuan Zai's mind was just now split in two. One part strove furiously to piece together all the information he had received, trying to reconstruct the whole picture of the attack plan; the other part was swiftly reckoning how much profit he stood to gain this time.

To foil the Pifu's plot to destroy the lantern-tower — if he brought that off, word would go straight up to the ears of Heaven itself; it would be an extraordinary merit unmatched in an age! And bringing along this one petty Longwu guard-corporal would not thin out the credit in the least; on the contrary, at the needful moment the man could serve as shield and scapegoat both.

His plan settled, Yuan Zai braced himself. The Longwu Army and the Lüben Guards had ten-odd soldiers apiece; they joined into one band and charged toward the Mystic Abbey at the foot of the lantern-tower.

Tonight was destined to be the night when I, Yuan Zai, would win merit and make my name!

Zhang Xiaojing and his two guards came back once more to the great hall. It stood empty now of every soul. Zhang Xiaojing said, "My guess is Mao Shun has already climbed up above. It's too dangerous to go up now; you two stay down here as backup."

The two exchanged a glance and said with one voice, "We are under orders to protect you — how could we give up halfway?"

"Very well, then keep up."

Zhang Xiaojing wasted no words but scrambled swiftly up the stairs, the two guards close behind. On the steep, narrow staircase the three of them climbed strung out in a line, one above another. This was the level of the Lingguan Loft, where Li Bi had been held. Zhang Xiaojing was the first to top the stairs while the two behind were still climbing with lowered heads. He whirled around, drew his hand-crossbow, and with a snap-snap loosed two bolts into the hindmost man first, then a second double-shot that struck the guard just behind him.

The order mattered: had he shot the nearer man first, that one, toppling, might well have become a shield of flesh for the hindmost.

Two volleys, four bolts, were spent almost in an instant, and the two guards, caught utterly off guard, tumbled screaming to the foot of the stairs. Zhang Xiaojing had aimed at the crowns of their heads; at so short a range he was fully certain of driving the bolts through. Even if by some luck they were not dead yet, they would never rise again.

"I'm sorry..." Zhang Xiaojing's single eye was thick with sorrow. He reloaded his last four bolts as he went, then turned and climbed swiftly up from the Lingguan Loft toward the crown-loft. Beneath his feet he could feel the floor trembling; the whole Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower had now formally begun to turn, and the power of it in motion was a truly magnificent thing.

The blast he expected from the crown-loft was slow in coming, and Zhang Xiaojing worried that Mao Shun had changed his mind again at the last moment. The damned craftsman blew hot and cold and could never make up his mind; you really could not rest easy without standing over him.

Now at last he had won himself the best of situations. Xiao Gui had already gone down to the Hydraulic Hall to carry out other tasks, the two guards were finished off, and there was no one to hold him back. He had only to reach the crown-loft and force Mao Shun to detonate the qilin-arm, and there should still be time to withdraw.

Soon he reached the crown-loft and kicked the door open, only to find not a soul inside — only the turning-gear clicking and clacking round and round. Mao Shun was not there; neither was the fierce-fire thunder.

A chill ran through Zhang Xiaojing all at once. Where could they have gone? He made a circuit of the room, hurried out of the crown-loft, and looked up into the Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower above. The interior of the tower, its candles not yet lit, was like the vast maw of some great beast, crammed with fangs great and small.

His foot seemed to tread on something; he looked down and found a firestone and some mugwort tinder, and a smear of blood. It seemed Mao Shun had not gone willingly, but had been dragged from the crown-loft.

"Yuchang!" Zhang Xiaojing forced the two words out through his teeth.

The only man capable of doing this was Yuchang! This was his challenge to Zhang Xiaojing, forcing him to come and fight it out.

Zhang Xiaojing turned his head and saw, beside the turning-gear, a line Mao Shun had drawn with soapstone — the marked detonation point. Which meant that even with Mao Shun gone, Zhang Xiaojing could work it himself.

But the qilin-arm too was gone, most likely carried off by Yuchang along with the rest.

Gazing at the turning-gear that drove the Tianshu slowly round, Zhang Xiaojing fought hard to calm himself. Then he remembered: in that row of small cauldrons beside the great hall of the Mystic Abbey there should still be a few left — it was from there that Mao Shun had taken them earlier. When Xiao Gui pulled out he had not carried them all off; go back now, and they should still be there!

Zhang Xiaojing left the crown-loft, went back down that same staircase, and returned to the great hall. The two guards lay collapsed at the foot of the stairs; without pausing to check whether they lived or died, Zhang Xiaojing strode in great steps to the back of the hall. The fires in the six small cauldrons had been smothered out, but in several of them a few qilin-arms still lay aslant.

Zhang Xiaojing picked out one, hefted it onto his shoulder, and ran back from the rear of the hall into the main hall. He was just about to climb the stairs when he heard a "boom" at the entrance of the Mystic Abbey: the great gate was forced open, and soldiers of the Longwu Army and the Lüben Guards came pouring in together, all mixed up.

Ever since he had come off worst against Zhang Xiaojing, Yuan Zai no longer dared to lead from the front, and so the one at the head of the charge was that Longwu guard-corporal. The moment he saw Zhang Xiaojing carrying a qilin-arm up the stairs, he bellowed, "Hold, villain!" and rushed straight at him.

Zhang Xiaojing groaned inwardly: there was no way his present actions could fail to be misread. But time pressed, and left him no room to explain. He drew his crossbow and loosed a bolt straight ahead, catching the guard-corporal square in the thigh. Zhang Xiaojing then loosed three more bolts in quick succession, dropping three men and forcing the vanguard to a halt. He seized the chance to dash for the stairhead.

"Quick! Shoot! Shoot him!" Yuan Zai roared furiously from outside the gate.

The soldiers, waking as if from a dream, raised their arms one after another, and countless bolts flew like a swarm of locusts to nail themselves into the wall on this side. Luckily Zhang Xiaojing had climbed the stairs a step ahead, dodged the rain of bolts, passed through the Lingguan Loft, and came once more to the crown-loft.

Swiftly he set the qilin-arm at the drawn line, drew out the fuse, and then struck hard at the firestone. The government troops outside were already coming up fast; the sound of their feet pounding on the stairs was louder even than the cheering beyond. Zhang Xiaojing thought fate a truly marvelous thing: who would have guessed that the ones now hemming him in here would be government troops of his own side?

Not that they could be blamed: whoever saw a wanted man clutching fierce-fire thunder and bent on blowing up the tower's turning-gear would take it for sabotage. To make them understand that blowing up the turning-gear was in truth to save lives would take a calm, unhurried talk — and Zhang Xiaojing did not dare hope those men would grant him the chance.

Come what may, he had to hold out until the qilin-arm exploded!

Frowning, Zhang Xiaojing listened to the footsteps drawing nearer and nearer outside. Then his wrist gave a sudden jerk, the fire-steel struck out a dazzling spark that fell right on the fuse, and the fuse began to burn with a hiss.

Li Bi had waded a long while through the icy water before he came at last to the mouth of the passage. Here stood four dragon-scale water-dividing pillars, each sheathed in layer upon layer of scales; but one of the pillars had already been broken through, plainly filed apart by someone's hand.

Perhaps this was where Zhang Xiaojing had slipped in, Li Bi thought. Dragging his sodden body, he turned sideways to squeeze between the dividing pillars, grasped the water-weeds on the bank of the channel, and hauled himself up onto the shore. His topknot had by now come wholly undone in the soaking, and his face was in a very bad way, so long steeped in the cold water that not a trace of color was left in it.

Not sparing himself even a breath, he looked up to get his bearings and guessed that he must be somewhere in Daozheng Ward.

This was easy to tell, for from the north came a surging tide of cheers and drumbeats, and that colossal Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower had begun to turn as well. Li Bi bound up his hair roughly with one hand, brushed the water-drops from his face, and ran with uneven, stumbling steps toward where the crowds were thickest, for he knew the time left to him was not much.

If he had guessed rightly, the Pifu meant to break into the Xingqing Palace and drive straight to the inner precincts!

The underground watercourse Mao Shun had dug in the Daozheng Ward canal ran south to north into the lantern-tower, and must of necessity have a northward outlet — and the nearest such place was the Longchi within the Xingqing Palace.

The Longchi lay within the palace gardens on the south side of the Xingqing Palace, deep and broad, wide enough for little boats and painted barges to ply upon it. In the pool were lotus leaves and reed-thickets, and around its margins were planted peonies and willows; the many buildings of the gardens — the Dragon Pavilion, the Chenxiang Pavilion, the Hua'e Xianghui Tower, the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, and the rest — all rose along the pool, and were famed as the 'four seasons, four vistas.'

The water of the Longshou Canal in Daozheng Ward flowed into the lantern-tower's channel and thence drained into the Longchi, forming, all unseen, a tunnel that skirted the imperial guards' defenses and led into the Xingqing Palace. Once the tower blew, the country round would be laid waste for dozens of wards. The Pifu could then seize the moment to stride brazenly into the Longchi, burst into the Xingqing Palace, and launch a second wave of attack against the surviving imperial clansmen, high officials, and even the Son of Heaven himself — which was why they had made ready their water-gear.

If the Pifu's scheme were allowed to succeed, this Lantern Festival would be the most shameful day in all the history of Great Tang.

Stumbling, he ran a stretch along the channel and at last made out, dim and shadowy ahead, several ward-soldiers standing about and chatting. They were the men charged with guarding the Longshou Canal, but the lantern-floats were about to begin, and they were all busy craning their necks to look off that way.

Li Bi rushed toward them, shouting aloud. When the ward-soldiers saw a wild-haired black shape suddenly leap up out of the channel, they started in fright and leveled their spears and cudgels.

Li Bi held up the bronze token Zhang Xiaojing had left him and said, "I am the Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau. Take me at once to the Longwu Army." The ward-soldiers were taken aback by this turn; at last an old soldier took the token and looked it over, and seeing besides that Li Bi's skin was fine and soft and his hands without a callus, and that his robe, though soaked through, still bore the marks of an official's dress, was at last satisfied there was no mistake.

Before long Li Bi had made contact with the Longwu Army deployed at the gate of Daozheng Ward, who were astonished to hear that this was the missing Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau. Li Bi told them they must take measures at once to clear the lantern-watching crowds out of the Xingqing Palace and the plaza.

The Longwu officer, hard put to it, said this was impossible. Just now fifty thousand people were packed tight and immovable on the plaza; the Longwu Army was posted in scattered detachments and could not possibly be mustered. To force an evacuation at such a moment — the people trampling one another alone would leave a fearful toll of dead and injured.

Li Bi knew too that these junior officers had no power to decide the matter, and so he said, "Take me at once to General Chen Xuanli." Seeing how fierce and commanding Li Bi's manner was, the officer dared not dawdle and hastily made ready a horse. The Longwu Army had passages of its own for coming and going; along one of these Li Bi galloped, skirting the plaza packed to bursting, and in one breathless run reached the southwest corner of the Xingqing Palace.

At this hour Chen Xuanli, as commander-in-chief of the imperial guards, was stationed in command before the Jinming Gate.

On the south side of the Xingqing Palace there were three city gates in all: the Jinming Gate to the southwest, the Tongyang Gate due south, and the Chuyang Gate to the southeast, known together as the 'Three Yang.' The Qinzheng Wuben Tower faced the plaza directly at the Tongyang Gate. It was through this gate that the Lantern-Crown Red Tally would, before every watching eye, mount the tower to give thanks to the Son of Heaven and to salute the many partisans on the plaza. Its function was chiefly a ceremonial one.

The Jinming Gate to the southwest, by contrast, was a working passage. The many stores and personnel of the Lantern Festival banquet, officials and nobles who had drunk themselves past all measure, couriers and fast riders from every quarter, the singers and dancers and music-troupes of the Pear Garden, and the like — all passed in and out of the Xingqing Palace by this gate.

So for security, the most crucial point was the Jinming Gate, not the Tongyang Gate. It was no wonder, then, that Chen Xuanli commanded there in person.

Li Bi galloped up before the Jinming Gate and could see from afar Chen Xuanli in full gleaming armor, standing awe-inspiring atop the gate-tower. He turned his head to glance at the still more awe-inspiring Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower: though it had begun to turn, it was still all dark above, its candles not yet lit — a little time yet remained.

"General Chen! Urgent word from the Jing'an Bureau!"

Li Bi, mounted, shouted at the top of his voice — but in a moment, as though someone had seized him by the throat, his voice died away all at once. The horse beneath him, feeling its master haul hard on the reins, gave a grudging whinny.

His eyes went wide: the heavy doors of the Jinming Gate stood half open, and a sumptuous four-windowed carriage came driving hurriedly out from within. A four-windowed carriage ought properly to be drawn by a team of four, but just now only two horses were harnessed to its shafts, and at its rear not even a pennant had been set; had the censors seen it, they could hardly have failed to fault it with a word — 'a breach of due ceremony.'

Li Bi knew it at a glance: it was the heir apparent's own carriage, and the heir apparent himself was inside. More than once he had ridden together with the heir apparent, and he knew that Li Heng, dreading the stuffiness of the cabin, would each time draw the side window open a third of the way and rest his hand, as was his habit, on the window-lattice.

And now, on the window-lattice on the right side of the carriage, there rested that one dignified, opulent hand. Its fingers tapped lightly, betraying a certain unease in their master.

The Lantern Festival spring banquet had only just ended; after the lantern-floats there were still the gathering of the assembled court to view the lamps, the presentation of poems before the throne, the ceremonial drinking of the Grotto-Heaven sacred wine, and other stages to come. Why should the heir apparent, of all moments, choose this one to hurry away? For a moment Li Bi was quite at a loss; he wanted to call out and stop the carriage, but something seemed to stop up his throat.

He reined in his horse and stared blankly as the four-windowed carriage swept whistling past him.

At the same moment, a great burst of cheering broke out in the distance before the Tongyang Gate. The Lantern-Crown Red Tally had already mounted the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, climbed to the seven-story Star-Plucking Hall, and, standing on the outward-jutting terrace, had flung a lit candle with their own hand toward the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower.

Seeing the fuse now lit, Zhang Xiaojing let out a slight breath of relief. The fuse was made of a hemp-vine core soaked in oil; once lit, it would not easily go out. Its one drawback was that it burned rather slowly: it would take a good seven or eight finger-snaps to burn its way into the bamboo tube, and detonation would come, at the least, only after ten finger-snaps.

Zhang Xiaojing threw down the fire-steel, rose, and rushed to the door of the crown-loft, hoping to hold off for a while the pursuers behind him; he need only block them a moment to win time enough for the detonation.

The irony was that this was the second time within a bare half-shichen that Zhang Xiaojing had faced almost the same predicament in the same spot. More ironic still: the pursuers outside on the two occasions stood plainly on opposing sides.

The soldiers of the Longwu Army and the Lüben Guards had already flung themselves to the door. Zhang Xiaojing's crossbow was empty now, and with no other weapon in hand he had only his two bare palms to fend them off. With a great shout he wrenched the door-panel from the crown-loft to serve as a shield and drove it out at a slant, bearing down a whole swath of pursuers on the instant.

But both the Lüben Guards and the Longwu imperial guards were crack troops, one picked from a hundred in the capital. Men kept rushing up from below the stairs, and the pressure mounted without let; though no one of the soldiers was Zhang Xiaojing's match one on one, together they could swarm him. Zhang Xiaojing could only grip the door-panel with his bare hands and, using the narrow corridor, strain with all his might to push them back out. Countless flashing blades hacked into the panel, wood-chips flying, until it looked as though the panel would be chopped into a lattice-fence.

One Longwu soldier, seeing that hacking would not serve for the moment, spread both arms wide and threw his whole weight against it. The others, taking the hint, did likewise one after another. Zhang Xiaojing could neither wound his foes nor stand against the weight of so many men, and all at once found himself pinned back beneath the door-panel, unable to stir.

Only at this point did Yuan Zai come up the stairs. Seeing that it was that new Jing'an Bureau official he had frightened out of his wits before Chao Fen's gate, Zhang Xiaojing shouted out: "It was I who tipped you off to come to the Xingqing Palace — I'm not one of the Pifu! I'm on your side! I'm one of your own!"

Yuan Zai stared at Zhang Xiaojing, his feelings growing ever more tangled. This was the man who had killed ten-odd of his own subordinates before his eyes and frightened him into wetting his trousers — yet it was indeed his tip-off that had brought Yuan Zai to the Xingqing Palace. Could it be that Zhang Xiaojing was truly wronged? But Yuan Zai soon dismissed the thought. Here the man plainly was, clutching fierce-fire thunder and come to blow up the tower, an act done before every watching eye — was that not the deed of a traitor?

Clever as Yuan Zai was, he could make nothing at all of this one-eyed death-row convict's welter of contradictory acts. Yuan Zai resolved to think no more of it: seize the man first, and never mind the rest!

"Do not believe a word he says!" Yuan Zai was just about to clear his throat and issue his next order, but Zhang Xiaojing's voice got in ahead of him.

"This tower is already flooded with fierce-fire thunder — it's about to blow! You must send men at once to stop it!" Zhang Xiaojing bawled from beneath the door-panel until his voice cracked. The words made Yuan Zai shudder, and he hastily raised his head to look into the interior of the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower. But it was too vast and empty within; nothing could be made out.

Good heavens — if this tower were full of fierce-fire thunder, would not the whole Xingqing Palace be blown sky-high along with it? Yuan Zai's mind went blank.

"C-Commander! Look out!" a Longwu soldier suddenly shrieked, pointing at the crown-loft. The door-panel had been pulled away, so everyone in the corridor could see what lay within.

A qilin-arm lay pressed hard against the back of the turning-gear, and its fuse had already burned down into the bamboo tube. That cold premonition of death surged up in Yuan Zai's heart once more. Without a word he flung his arms over his head and rolled down toward the stairs. And the soldiers pinning down Zhang Xiaojing's door-panel, seeing their commander do so, sprang aside one and all.

The qilin-arm's fuse burned to its very end, flashed a few sparks, and then vanished. But Zhang Xiaojing knew it had not vanished — it had bored into the inside of the bamboo tube, and would soon wake a most fearful monster of flame.

He clenched his fists, shut his eyes, and waited for the moment when the flames would sweep over him and set him free.

One finger-snap, two finger-snaps, three finger-snaps... by the fifth finger-snap, the crown-loft was still perfectly quiet. Zhang Xiaojing heard no expected blast; instead he felt a scorching heat on his face. He opened his single eye and saw a fierce mass of fire dancing beside the turning-gear.

This one round of fierce-fire thunder was a dud.

Zhang Xiaojing soon found the reason. The tail of this qilin-arm had been knocked open into a crack during the recent struggle, and the black, sticky fierce-fire oil had run out of it and spilled across the floor.

The secret of making fierce-fire thunder was that the inside had to be packed tight and firm, the oil's force stored fast in one place, before it could be brought to burst successfully. If the seal were broken and the force leaked away, it would only burn as ordinary fire, keeping the fury of the flame but losing the sudden thunderclap of the blast. Earlier, it was precisely because the seals were poor that several rounds of the barrel-borne fierce-fire thunder carried by the Türk Wolf Guards had turned into duds.

Plainly Zhang Xiaojing's luck was not good enough: this qilin-arm's tail was damaged, its force had leaked away, and it had become a mere ordinary-burning round of fierce-fire thunder. Fierce it certainly was as it burned, but on the metal turning-gear it had no effect at all.

In the roaring blaze it went on turning, cold and indifferent, driving the Tianshu round. Zhang Xiaojing closed his eyes in helplessness — he had done all he could; was this, then, the will of Heaven?

The soldiers who had fled below, seeing there was no explosion, made ready to charge up again. Just then a huge wave of sound came surging in from outside: on the plaza countless pairs of hands were flung aloft, and countless voices fused into a single cry: "Lantern-floats! Lantern-floats! Lantern-floats!"

As the crowning stage of the lantern-float rite, the Lantern-Crown Red Tally stood upon the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, and the Son of Heaven would bestow upon him or her a sprig of willow-wood, the first in the palace gardens to have budded that year, while a music-band struck up 'Qingping Yue.' The Lantern-Crown Red Tally, holding the willow sprig, would light it and fling it toward the lantern-tower to kindle the candle-flames — not truly kindling them, but existing only as a rite: cast from this side, and on the other the people in the tower would at the same moment raise their candles, in token that the burgeoning of spring was near at hand.

When the cry of "Lantern-floats!" reached him, Zhang Xiaojing understood that this Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower was about to enter upon its final purpose. Yuchang would light the tower's fire-heads and let the Que-le Huo-duo devour every soul.

But not yet!

To ensure the greatest effect, Yuchang's operation would fall into two steps. First, he would start the mechanism working as normal, letting the twenty-four lantern-chambers light up one after another and drawing the Son of Heaven, the assembled court, and the envoys of every nation to the very edge of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower; then, once all the lantern-chambers were lit, Yuchang would set off the twenty-four rounds of fierce-fire thunder laid in beforehand, letting them burst all together and so touch off the Que-le Huo-duo hidden within the Tianshu.

Which was to say: so long as the twenty-four lantern-chambers were not yet all fully lit, a sliver of hope of life remained.

A dangerous light shot from Zhang Xiaojing's eyes as he struggled up from beneath the door-panel. The soldiers had come charging up a second time, trembling; without a word Zhang Xiaojing shielded his face with both hands and rushed into the crown-loft once more, braving the flames.

The pursuers were astonished: that way was plainly a dead end, and a great fire burning besides — was the man courting his own death? But Yuan Zai dared not make light of this death-row convict, and urged his men to press across quickly and see what was afoot.

Several soldiers rushed up to the crown-loft and found the great fire still burning, the turning-gear still revolving without hindrance — but the man was gone. Hearing this, Yuan Zai ran up himself, looked up, and saw a great big hole broken through the ceiling.

When Zhang Xiaojing had ambushed Yuchang earlier, he had already noticed that this ceiling was very thin, no more than for show; his crossbow bolts had punched four holes through it with ease. Entering the crown-loft again, he took a barrier-knife of Lüben Guards issue that he had picked up and hacked fiercely at the weak zone between the four bolt-holes, soon chopping out a great hole; then, treading on the scalding-hot turning-gear, he climbed up through it into the interior of the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower.

A voice came down from the hole: "The tower is about to be blown up by fierce-fire thunder — raise the alarm, quickly!" Then came a string of footsteps, gradually receding.

The soldiers made to give chase, but Yuan Zai stopped them.

"If that fellow is right, the tower is full of fierce-fire thunder now — it's too dangerous." Yuan Zai narrowed his eyes, looking up into the pitch-black interior of the tower above. His foreboding grew stronger and stronger: on no account could they go on. "We've got to get a warning out to those outside as fast as we can."

"Didn't you just say we shouldn't believe a word he says?" a slow-witted big lug of a soldier objected.

Yuan Zai shot him a glare but did not explain overmuch. In truth even Yuan Zai himself was baffled and did not know what to make of Zhang Xiaojing. If the tower were full of fierce-fire thunder, ought he not to have fled at once? Yet here he was, heedless even of his pursuers, forcing his way inward — could it be he actually meant to stop it? Whose side was he on, after all?

"This man we're chasing — is he a good man or a bad one, in the end?" the slow-witted big lug asked, his own face turned upward too, all bewilderment.

This time Yuan Zai did not snap at him: "I don't know. But one thing's certain — the man's a madman."

The instant the Lantern-Crown Red Tally flung out the lit candle, the plaza before the Xingqing Palace fell all at once into a hush so deep you could hear a pin drop. It was as though some unseen warrior had raised a modao and, at a single stroke, cut through all the clamor. The common folk come to watch the fun, the performers up on the lantern-float carts, the officials and imperial kinsmen and the envoys of the many nations standing at the edge of the terrace — all of them, as if by one accord, fell silent, waiting for the birth of a wonder worthy of a golden age.

The Qinzheng Wuben Tower stood very close to the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower, and the lit candle traced a graceful arc through the air to settle lightly into the upturned-head bracket of the candle-dragon made ready beforehand on the tower.

The Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower stood towering and unmoving, still cold and aloof in the darkness, as though it took no notice of this lit candle knocking at its door. A small ripple stirred through the crowd, and up on the tower the officials fell to whispering among themselves, all fearing that something had gone wrong along the way.

Before long a creaking sound, like the low growl of some great beast, rose from within the tower and dispelled every man's doubt. Together they craned their necks and saw the huge Kuafu-like turning-arms begin to work, driving the twenty-four lantern-chambers around the tower's outer ring in a slow revolution — this one rising as that one sank, wheeling round without end.

The first to turn up to the top of the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower was the 'Benevolence' lantern-chamber. At first it lit only the faintest gleam, dim as a bean of light, by which one could just make out what seemed a figure moving within. Swaying, it passed over the tower's zenith and slipped beneath a trip-lever wrought in the shape of a suanni. As the lantern-chamber moved forward, the trip-lever on its fixed frame flicked open a slanting oil-spout at the chamber's roof.

The spout once open, the lamp-oil within poured out, ran along channels all around the whole lantern-chamber, and at last reached that bean-sized candle-flame. Almost in an instant the oil in the whole channel turned into a line of fire, kindling the several dozen great white dragon-candles ranged along it.

The whole lantern-chamber turned in a flash exceedingly bright, like a brilliant star bursting open against the night sky, looking down in lofty disdain upon the mortal world. Its radiance stood in sharp contrast to the black of the night, and the onlookers could see clearly: within the chamber a man stood with his hands clasped behind his back, nodding again and again; all manner of sparrows and swans and wild geese flew about him, and a great net was raised on three sides, with only one side let down to the ground.

This was the allusion of King Tang of Shang and the 'net opened on one side,' to signify benevolence. That male lantern-figure was Tang himself; the birds beside him were most exquisitely made, fashioned by gluing on real feathers, and the two wings of every bird beat up and down as though they were truly bursting free of the net.

The onlookers gaped, dumbstruck at the scene before them. When had they ever seen the like? Those lantern-figures set high above could move of themselves, lifelike as living things. As the outer lantern-chamber sank slowly down, colored silks fluttered at its four corners, streaming with light and color. The common folk were enraptured, and some even knelt and bowed to the ground, as though worshiping immortals come down to earth.

In the next half-shichen, twenty-three more such wonders would be kindled one after another. Every man held down the excitement in his breast and, breath bated, awaited what was to come.

Zhang Xiaojing, inside the tower just now, was not nearly so excited as the people outside. Trusting to what he had seen earlier, he felt his way toward the Tianshu tier, where Yuchang must be working the mechanism. He had no fear of mistaking the way, for that one Tianshu pillar running through the whole tower would never shift its place and was most conspicuous.

But once the tower had begun to turn, the situation within grew far more complex. The turning-poles, the hanging bridges, and the timber posts and jib-arms everywhere formed an intricate maze — and this maze was, moreover, forever moving and changing. Zhang Xiaojing strained his single eye wide and leapt from platform to platform.

The one comfort was that, as one lantern-chamber after another lit up, the light inside the tower grew brighter, and he no longer had to grope forward in the dark.

Zhang Xiaojing climbed on upward, but soon found his body in no happy state: after running up a few steps he had to stop and catch his breath a while. From the moment he had left the death cells that morning, he had not once rested; he had been wounded several times over, and had snatched only a brief rest at the Cibei Temple. Even a man forged of iron would by now, one feared, be a crossbow at the end of its shot.

Zhang Xiaojing worried that in this state he would be no match for Yuchang. That fellow was the most dangerous of killers, and in so complex a setting he was a very fish in water; Zhang Xiaojing's own odds of winning were in truth very slim — he would have to change his strategy.

He lifted his head and looked up. By now four lantern-chambers had been lit, while the Tianshu tier was still dozens of chi up in the air above. Zhang Xiaojing thought a moment, then threw back his head and roared, "Yuchang! Let's have it out, you and I!"

His voice echoed through the tower, long refusing to fade, yet drew no answer at all. Zhang Xiaojing had meant to use himself as bait to lure Yuchang down, but the other plainly paid him no heed.

Zhang Xiaojing could only clench his teeth, steady himself, and spring upward. But just then the tower shifted: a hanging board slid out of place, and all at once his footing gave way beneath him and he nearly fell. Luckily Zhang Xiaojing was quick of eye and hand and grabbed hold of a thick hemp rope dangling down, so that he hung, very nearly, in midair.

He clamped the barrier-knife between his teeth, freed his other hand, and climbed hand over hand; having barely gained a little height, he swung his body bit by bit and, in midair, arced across to the nearest recess. No sooner had Zhang Xiaojing set foot on it than the rope, unable to bear the strain, tore loose the few boards above it and went clattering down to the bottom of the tower.

With that, the way up was torn away, trapping Zhang Xiaojing outright in this one narrow recess, unable to go forward or back.

The place where Zhang Xiaojing had come to rest was a falcon's-beak jutting out from the tower — an observation-hole the craftsmen used to true the turning-arms. Poke one's head out from here, and one could see the turning-arm sweep past right before one's eyes, and tell at a glance whether the arm's core ran askew. It was named the 'falcon's-beak' for two reasons: first, the foothold here was extremely narrow, like a falcon's bill; second, the falcon's eye is the keenest of all, able to see the smallest error.

Along the path of the turning-arms, at every set distance, there was sure to be a falcon's-beak hole, and the positions of all the falcon's-beak holes were strictly identical. If Zhang Xiaojing wished to climb on, there was only one way: to climb from the inside to a falcon's-beak hole on the tower's outer face, seize the slowly rising turning-arm, ride it up to a higher observation-hole, and leap once more into the tower's interior.

It was an exceedingly perilous route. The tower's turning-arms were all made of thick round bamboo, polished smooth all over and none too easy to grip. One slip, and a man would plunge to the foot of the tower and be dashed into a heap of pulp. Even if by luck he caught hold, whether he could keep his balance amid the ceaseless motion, and whether he could pick the right instant to leap clear, were both unknowns.

By this time the fifth lantern-chamber too had lit, and time pressed harder still. Zhang Xiaojing had no choice but to force his body out and lean over the edge. He was more than forty chi above the ground now, and the people and objects below looked like so many little ants. The night wind howled, almost sealing his eyes shut.

A turning-arm came slowly wheeling round in the distance, and Zhang Xiaojing fixed it with an unblinking stare, silently reckoning its speed and distance. He had not the slightest confidence, but it was the only choice open to him now.

The tower's outer face had eight turning-arms, each driving three lantern-chambers. The surfaces of their poles had been painted black, so that to the lamp-watchers looking on from afar the black arms were swallowed up by the night, and for a moment it seemed as though the lantern-chambers hung suspended in midair. This detail, for Zhang Xiaojing, added all unseen to the difficulty of taking aim.

"Wen Wuji — if you think I am doing right, then bless me, I pray you."

Zhang Xiaojing prayed thus in silence, then tucked the knife behind him and leapt clear out of the tower. He did not wait, nor did he hesitate; both were the most extravagant of luxuries now. Flying into midair, Zhang Xiaojing stretched out both arms to meet the turning-arm. He quickly found that he had judged the direction right but the speed wrong: before his arms could close around the arm, his whole body slammed into it with a heavy 'thud.'

The impact set stars swimming before Zhang Xiaojing's eyes and all but knocked him senseless. Luckily his limbs reached and bent by instinct, and like a monkey he clung fast to the edge of the great bamboo pole, so that he did not, after all, fall. The turning-arm gave a faint creak, shivered a few times, and went on lifting upward.

By now nearly a third of the lantern-wheels of the Taishang Xuanyuan Tower had lit in turn, each dazzlingly bright. The common folk of Great Tang loved above all to watch these scenes of the immortals, and were not in the least sparing of their cheers and applause. Every man's gaze was fixed on these glorious, exquisite wonders of the mortal world, and not one noticed that, near the pitch-black turning-arms, a man who was trying to save them all was climbing up toward the heavens.

After a little while Zhang Xiaojing's sight came back somewhat. His breath came heavy and hoarse, his muscles ached fiercely, yet he dared not slacken in the least. He hung there upon the turning-arm like a drowning man clutching a floating log. A bitter gust of wind blew across and scattered his topknot, already come loose.

With difficulty he turned his neck and saw the tower's outer wall before him slowly sinking; about ten chi higher up there was a protrusion jutting out like the beak of a falcon.

That was his goal.

He had only to wait some fifteen finger-snaps more, and the turning-arm would carry him level with the falcon's-beak hole — the best moment to leap back into the tower. But now Zhang Xiaojing found his posture was wrong: as he was placed now, he could keep himself from being flung off the arm, but could scarcely gain enough purchase to spring up in midair.

Pressed close to the bamboo pole, Zhang Xiaojing worked his body along, gradually easing his two feet loose and throwing all the strain onto his tight-clasped hands, several times all but falling in the process. With great effort he got himself into a posture of hanging by both hands, and began to swing in wide arcs like a weight on a steelyard.

When the distance between the falcon's-beak and himself was at its shortest at last, Zhang Xiaojing suddenly let go with both hands, tore free of the turning-arm, and flew toward the tower. With a soft 'pop' his body actually punched a hole through the outer skin and dropped straight into the tower's interior. Zhang Xiaojing decided on the instant: twisting round, he grabbed the falcon's-beak with his right hand for dear life and hung his whole body fast by it, and so did not fall.

The connecting passage of this falcon's-beak had not been wrecked; Zhang Xiaojing kicked his feet a few times, reached the edge, and then hauled his whole body up over it. Once up, Zhang Xiaojing lay sprawled on the floor, gasping without cease.

He knew time pressed, but he had truly reached his very limit. That string of movements had taken little time, yet had all but drained Zhang Xiaojing's strength away. His right wrist above all, having just borne the weight of his whole body, showed the first signs of cramping muscle.

He raised his head and counted: the lantern-chambers had already lit up to the tenth. The people on the Xingqing Palace plaza had by now caught the rhythm of the great tower's lighting; they would cheer aloud each time a lantern-chamber appeared, then let their voices sink lower and lower until the next one lit. Inside the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, one feared, it was already empty, all the banquet-guests having crowded to the outer railings to gaze at so lovely a sight up close.

"Fifteen, fifteen — so long as I'm up before the fifteenth lantern-chamber lights, there's still time, still time..." Zhang Xiaojing told himself. He truly could hardly hold on any longer and had to rest a moment. But once he stopped, his body did not want to move at all.

Zhang Xiaojing drew his knife and slashed himself savagely across the wrist; the violent pain, like a red-hot iron awl, forced the last of the ferocity in his body to the surface. Gritting his teeth, he propped himself up by main force and staggered off upward.

He was very close to the Tianshu tier now. Zhang Xiaojing looked up and could already see that expanse of slowly turning boards overhead.

The Tianshu tier was the core of the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower, and its most obvious mark was the ring of immensely broad, yellow-brown boards fitted around the Tianshu — so broad that they cut clean across the whole interior of the tower, so that it looked as though the very floor were turning without stop.

Zhang Xiaojing hefted the knife again and made for the stairs leading to the upper level. He trod lightly and held his breath, doing his best to make no sound. But the moment he set foot on the steps, a cold flash of light came without warning. Luckily Zhang Xiaojing had been ready, and thrust out before him a wooden board that had been cast aside nearby, to serve as a shield.

The cold light swept across, and the wooden board was cleft in two on the instant; Zhang Xiaojing seized the moment to leap into the Tianshu tier and slashed with his knife held level. Yuchang, guarding the stairhead, had only one arm to use and could not draw his blade back in time, so he threw himself into a backward roll and escaped the edge of Zhang Xiaojing's stroke.

But strangely, Yuchang launched no counterattack; instead he retreated several steps, a look at once gratified and cruel on his face: "How very good that you aren't dead — I've waited a long time for you." The hoarse voice came against the rumbling din of the Tianshu chamber.

Zhang Xiaojing, too, did not hurry forward; he wanted to buy more time to recover some strength. And so the two of them faced each other, three eyes meeting, a few dozen paces apart, sunk in a silent standoff. The floor beneath both men's feet turned slowly all the while, so that the background behind them shifted like a revolving lantern, the light now bright, now dim, and the expressions on the two faces grew rather subtle.

Zhang Xiaojing suddenly noticed, behind Yuchang, a square wooden stand, its outside painted with black lacquer, and on top of it two conspicuous long levers, one indigo-blue, one crimson-red. That must be the control-mechanism that governed the detonation of the Tianshu. The last step of Xiao Gui's plan had to have someone to work it, and that was why Yuchang had stayed to the end. Destroy it, and this whole plot would be reckoned a failure.

"Why didn't you go and denounce me to Xiao Gui?" Zhang Xiaojing asked.

"No use. That fellow would never have killed you. I'd rest easier doing it with my own hands." Yuchang licked his lips, murderous intent brimming in his eyes.

"So instead of denouncing me, you killed Mao Shun?"

"Just so. With Mao Shun dead and the qilin-arm gone, if you wanted to settle this matter you had no choice but to come up the tower after me. That way I could work the mechanism in the tower with an easy mind and wait, into the bargain, for you to come up and throw your life away — neither of the two tasks need be neglected."

Zhang Xiaojing frowned: "Then do you know that Xiao Gui meant, from the first, for you to die as well?"

He had thought these words would shock and enrage Yuchang and so make him give up blowing the tower, but Yuchang answered in earnest: "What of it? I promised to do ten things for him; this is the last of them, and I'll not leave it half done just because he means to kill me."

Zhang Xiaojing had not thought Yuchang so honorable a keeper of promises. Yuchang stretched out his hand and stared at him like a wild beast, making ready to strike. Zhang Xiaojing tried to coax him: "Stop the mechanism first, and I'll agree to go outside and duel you."

"No — right here is perfect!"

The words were scarcely out before Yuchang came rushing in like a phantom. His speed was extreme; Zhang Xiaojing could not dodge and could only swing his barrier-knife to meet him head-on. Through the Tianshu chamber rang a jangling, more than ten crisp notes of metal clashing on metal.

Yuchang's manner of attack relied above all on speed and set great store by taking a man off guard. So when Zhang Xiaojing settled his mind and defended with all his strength, Yuchang could for the moment find no opening. Having attacked several times to no effect, Yuchang suddenly broke off and, using his advantage in footwork, drifted up onto the lantern-frames near the Tianshu tier.

The bamboo scaffolding hereabouts crisscrossed this way and that, denser than a wild mountain forest. Yuchang darted and leapt among it, and Zhang Xiaojing soon lost his trace, looking left and right without knowing from what angle this dangerous killer would launch his attack.

Zhang Xiaojing was rich in battle experience and knew that in such a case he must on no account let his opponent seize the rhythm. He thought a moment, then suddenly darted back several steps, set his back against the inner wall of the tower, and braced both feet against two jutting bamboo-nodes.

The whole Tianshu tier, save for the Tianshu itself, kept its floor slowly revolving. With his back to the tower's inner wall and both feet off the turning floor, Zhang Xiaojing made sure, first, that no enemy could come at his back; and second, that his own body would not turn with the floor — so that he need only wait a little, and the wooden stand controlling the mechanism would revolve of itself round to face him.

His aim had never been to kill Yuchang, but to destroy the mechanism-stand. By taking up such a stance, Zhang Xiaojing could hold the initiative and meet every change with changelessness. Yuchang must either face him in open combat, or watch helplessly as the mechanism-stand turned round before Zhang Xiaojing and was destroyed.

Sure enough, once Zhang Xiaojing stood so, Yuchang saw the situation clearly and realized he had no choice but to show himself. With a few bounds he suddenly pounced down viciously from the bamboo frame at an unthinkable angle. His back to the tower wall, Zhang Xiaojing easily made out the quarter of the attack, swung up his barrier-knife, and with a crisp clang blocked the surprise blow once more.

Yuchang, used to the surprise strike, would by habit withdraw at once when a blow failed to land. But Zhang Xiaojing gave his long knife a twist, entangled his opponent, and dragged him bodily into the rhythm of a grappling fight. The two were each at once better and worse off: Zhang Xiaojing's disadvantage was that his strength was spent and his blows lacked force; while Yuchang had one arm wounded — and for the moment they fought on even terms.

"How much longer can you hold out?" Yuchang said as he fought.

"Same to you." Zhang Xiaojing bared his teeth in a grin.

By now fifteen of the lantern-chambers overhead had lit, leaving only nine that had not yet turned to the zenith to be kindled. If Yuchang could be kept pinned here the whole time, there would be no one to throw the mechanism and set off the qilin-arms of all twenty-four lantern-chambers.

And so neither of these two men could afford to be delayed.

Seeing the wooden stand about to come round, Yuchang quickened the attack in his hand, trying to bear Zhang Xiaojing down. Zhang Xiaojing, not to be outdone, gave back blow for blow. In a gap between the storm-and-tempest of the assault, Yuchang's other, maimed hand suddenly shook its sleeve, and several drops of green green-vitriol oil flew out from the cuff and scattered toward Zhang Xiaojing.

But Zhang Xiaojing had long been guarding against this trick; he held his long knife level and turned his wrist half round with the motion. The broad back of the barrier-knife lashed hard at the flying green liquid and whipped it back the way it had come. One drop of the green fluid struck Yuchang square on the left shoulder and gave a soft hiss on the cloth.

A stab of pain went through Yuchang's shoulder, and his brows twitched despite himself. As a killer who worked in the shadows, he had very rarely fought anyone thus, head-on and clinging, and he was ill at ease with it. The fellow before him was like a paste of glutinous rice steeped in baiji-juice: his swordplay might be nothing so very fine, but he clung on to the death and would not let go, tough and stubborn to the last.

Yuchang felt this could not go on. He turned his head aside, saw that the wooden stand was nearly upon them, and threw himself into a posture of mutual destruction, rushing straight at Zhang Xiaojing.

Seeing him carry on so, Zhang Xiaojing opened his mouth and laughed aloud.

At a glance he saw through it: Yuchang was bluffing. What killer ever had the resolve to perish together with his foe?

In a situation like this, only the man with no fear of life or death could win.

Zhang Xiaojing planted both feet firmly and swung out another stroke. Seeing that his opponent was unmoved, Yuchang could only pull his force back midway and drift swiftly off. The wooden stand was by now less than three chi from Zhang Xiaojing, and the two wooden levers upon it could be seen clearly, one side indigo, one side crimson-red.

"Do you know which side to destroy?" Yuchang's voice came down maliciously from above.

Zhang Xiaojing's long knife, already raised, stalled in midair.

He knew nothing of the art of building mechanisms; who could say whether this stroke would bring blessing or disaster? Indigo, or crimson-red? And if he struck wrong and instead set off the explosion early, what then? Zhang Xiaojing had not thought of any of this before, wanting only to hack down once and have it over; but pricked so by Yuchang, it became a demon in his heart, and he could not bring the knife down.

In the instant Zhang Xiaojing hesitated, the mechanism-stand had already swept past in front of him and was drawing away. Zhang Xiaojing hastily leaned his body forward and reached out to grab it — and his back left the tower's inner wall at last.

This one small opening was seized on the instant by Yuchang, who had been coiled and waiting. He leapt down from the scaffold all at once and thrust flying at him. Zhang Xiaojing must either grab for the stand and be stabbed dead, or bring his knife back to save himself and watch the stand recede.

By now twenty-one of the lantern-chambers had lit, and Zhang Xiaojing had no time to wait for the stand to come round another full circle.

Zhang Xiaojing knew this full well in his own heart, but faced with the two colors, indigo-blue and crimson-red, he had no way to begin. Gritting his teeth, he first brought his knife back to block Yuchang's sudden thrust — and thereby missed his chance of reaching the mechanism-stand.

The turning floor bore the mechanism-stand steadily away, farther and farther off.

Yuchang made no sound, but his eyes flashed with excitement. The outcome of this struggle would come clear at last. He suddenly found that not to kill the fellow, but to let him slide down into the abyss of despair, would be sweeter revenge than killing him.

Yet after this bout of grappling, Yuchang knew too that the fellow would never give up so easily.

Sure enough, seeing his holding strategy fail and feeling the pressure of the hour, Zhang Xiaojing simply flung himself forward. This time he threw all caution aside and charged straight for the wooden stand.

High at the zenith, the twenty-second lantern-chamber flared alight — twenty-two now ablaze in all.

Zhang Xiaojing's charge was like a wild boar's, heedless of all around him. Yuchang seized the chance to strike: a cold flash, and he laid open the right side of Zhang Xiaojing's ribs, sending up a spray of blood. But the wound did not slow Zhang Xiaojing's speed in the least.

Yuchang struck once more, this time gashing his left shoulder. Zhang Xiaojing gave a tiger's roar and charged on, blood streaming all over him, paying his wounds no more heed than if they were not there.

Yuchang's expression froze. From his opponent rose an aura that filled him with an incomparable dread — the first time in his whole life he had felt such a thing. Yuchang had a premonition that, even were he to cut the man's throat now, his foe would first tear him into several pieces before dying himself.

A panic out of the shadows of his childhood rose unbidden in his heart. It was in the year he was seven: alone and adrift on the grasslands, he had been trailed by a lone, wounded wolf. Man and wolf had faced each other off for half a night, until, luckily, herdsmen came up and drove the wolf away. But those green, glinting eyes had left on Yuchang a nightmare-mark he could never forget.

That nightmare had today taken flesh again as Zhang Xiaojing and appeared before Yuchang. For the first time Yuchang lost his composure; he had a violent urge to retreat and hide.

He gave a low growl, struggling desperately to shake off these tangled thoughts — but Zhang Xiaojing was already upon him.

Yuchang no longer wished to face Zhang Xiaojing head-on. Mastering the urge to flee, he swept up his knife and cut through a length of yellow bamboo frame beside him. The heavy wooden wheel, robbed of one support, sank down a few fen on the instant, and the lurch threw the charging Zhang Xiaojing's body to one side. Yuchang hastily cut through another bamboo frame, and the wooden wheel canted over a few fen more.

Zhang Xiaojing saw the level path before him turn suddenly into a tilted upward slope. He could only heave up his steel knife and quicken his pace, running forward. Yuchang, like a man gone mad, raised his knife and cut through a third support.

With a great clatter half the wooden wheel of the Tianshu tier collapsed, wood-chips flying. Zhang Xiaojing's strength was near rock-bottom, and with his wounds too grievous he could not for the moment keep his balance, but slid all the way down to the edge of the wheel. He tried to grab at the things around him, but his arms were sore and without force, and his whole body slid out into the air at a stroke, held only by one hand hooked with all its might into a groove at the rim. That barrier-knife spun a few circles in midair and dropped into the abyss at the foot of the tower.

At the same moment, a twenty-third lantern-chamber lit — twenty-three now ablaze.

Yuchang burst into a fit of mad laughter; he rarely so lost his composure, but today was the exception. This duel, in the end, he had won after all. Zhang Xiaojing, that wild beast, had been beaten by him at the last.

He walked to the edge of the wooden wheel and set his leather boot down on Zhang Xiaojing's five fingers, grinding them with a crunching sound. Zhang Xiaojing's body swung helplessly in the air, his face contorted, yet he would not, for all that, let his fingers go.

"In the end, you couldn't save a single soul."

Yuchang looked down on his beaten foe. He could kill Zhang Xiaojing easily now — yet all at once he changed his mind.

Zhang Xiaojing's madness just now had made him feel fear. Simply to kill the wretch was no longer enough to wash away such humiliation. Only by letting this enemy stew a good long while in despair and anguish, and then die, would the rage in his heart be stilled.

He ground down on Zhang Xiaojing's fingers no longer, but pointed at the mechanism-stand and walked over toward it. Zhang Xiaojing roared, "Come and kill me, then! Only don't throw that mechanism!"

Yuchang cocked his ear and slowed his steps: this wailing was sweeter than any tune of the entertainers' quarter, and he meant to savor the whole of it. Zhang Xiaojing clung one-handed to the groove, his eyes bloodshot, his voice as hoarse as a cracked gong: "Don't throw it — you'll regret it!"

Amid these cries upon cries, Yuchang stepped slowly up onto the wooden stand. He reached out and grasped the two levers, then lifted his head and looked up toward the zenith.

The last lantern-chamber, 'Discernment,' lit.

The twenty-four lantern-chambers upon the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower were, at this point, all kindled at last. Twenty-four brilliant, enormous masses of lantern-fire, set against the night sky, were like the very constellations come down to earth.

With a grand, irresistible momentum they revolved in turn, forming in midair a bright and perfectly round orbit of eternal recurrence, looking down in lofty disdain upon the hundred and eight wards of Chang'an. Every lantern-figure within the chambers wore a solemn, sacred aspect, as though the gate of all mysteries opened from this very place.

At the very top of this tower, some ten-odd extremely long hemp ropes were drawn slantwise in different directions, hanging suspended in the air, hung all over with gauzes and colored banners of every hue. When the lamps were unlit, these ornaments were nothing to look at. Now, with the lantern-chambers all alight together, the gauzes trembled and rustled as one, filtering the lamplight into a wealth of colors — scarlet, grape-purple, jade-sprout green, ocher-yellow — and wrapping the whole tower, within and without, in a haze of strange and wondrous colored shadows, like the phantom realm of the immortals.

Whether humble folk who measured their grain by the peck or noble scions of the imperial line, how many had ever beheld immortals descend to the mortal world? Yet today every man's dream had become a scene before his very eyes, an experience worth talking of for many years to come. Cheering like a tempest of towering waves came beating in from all directions. The music-bands made ready long before, within and without the Xingqing Palace, struck up together the tune 'Shangxian You.' The Lantern Festival revels of Chang'an had reached their very height.

Yuchang gave Zhang Xiaojing a glance and deliberately turned his body aside so that the man could see his action clearly. With a press of his wrist, he pushed the crimson-red lever all the way to its end.
