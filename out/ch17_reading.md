## Chapter 17. Each Has a Sky of Its Own Warp and Weft

Under the dim lamplight the red-lacquered floorboards looked a deeper crimson still, and Ronghua and Muci conferred in secret in the little loft above the Huamei Bookstore. Photographs of a stack of “classified military maps” lay spread on the small tabletop; in the upper right corner of each map ran the words “Military Secret — South China map, scale one to fifty thousand — Nanchang stock number,” and in the lower left, “Land Survey Department, Army General Staff.”

“Last year Japanese imperialism brazenly launched the September 18th Incident. Chiang Kai-shek gave the order for ‘absolute non-resistance,’ and the Northeastern Army yielded up the city of Shenyang without firing a single shot. The Japanese army pressed its advantage; in a little over four months the three provinces of Liaoning, Jilin, and Heilongjiang fell entirely. Look at these photographs — the Japanese will not rest until they have devoured our China. They want not only our three eastern provinces; they mean to swallow the Central Plains as well.” Ronghua’s expression was grim as he squared the photographs one by one.

“Where did these photographs of the maps come from?” Muci asked.

“They were drawn by Japanese spies long planted in our country. Our agents obtained the photographs from the hands of the Special Higher Police. The Japanese intelligence organ known as the Liyang Society set up its Eastern Institute on Kunshan Road in Shanghai years ago. The Japanese spymaster Doihara Kenji has sent a great many agents slipping into our country, and among them are a good number of women. He likens them to cherry blossoms drifting down onto the mainland, and to brilliant stars that shine with the very light of the sun and the moon.”

“Hmph. Shining with the sun and the moon? The cherry blossom is short-lived in the end.”

“Well said. Come and look at this one.” Ronghua straightened a photograph. “It shows the main streets and harbors of Shanghai; the map is remarkably clear and exact.” Muci studied the photograph closely. On the sheet was drawn: General Staff Headquarters, Land Survey Bureau — China Expeditionary Army — “Imperial Flower” Survey. Shōwa 4.

“Shōwa 4? 1929,” Muci murmured.

“Our superiors have ordered us, in the interest of the nation, to pass these photographs to the Nationalist government as quickly as we can, in the hope that they will round up every Japanese spy hidden in Shanghai in a single sweep. And there is more. The Third Communist International is about to send a senior delegate to Shanghai to take part in an enlarged meeting of the Central Special Committee, and we are charged with the receiving and escorting of those who attend. Fang Zhitong, Secretary of the Central Special Branch, is the direct commander of this mission of ours. Is that clear?”

“Clear.” Then Muci turned back suddenly to those words, Shōwa 4, and said, puzzled, “This handwriting — where have I seen it before?”

“Surely not?” said Ronghua. “Unless you have met this Imperial Flower in the flesh!”

The French Concession, Shanghai. A Japanese tearoom.

The tearoom was exquisitely appointed. Its private rooms were divided one from another by large, broad folding screens of black-lacquered Tang-style work, well fitted and handsome in design. The screens were painted in gold tracery and flying lacquer with scenes of the exchange and growth of the tea cultures of China and Japan. The style was elegant, breathing an antique fragrance at every turn, steeped in the flavor of the past.

From a little porcelain pot much in the Japanese manner, tilted just so, a smooth thread of water poured down into the bottom of a teacup. A pair of hands, reserved and savoring, lifted the cup with ice-cold fingers; a pair of alluring lips touched the jade-green tea, and a tongue came out, equivocal, to test its warmth. Then, calmly, she waited for her visitor.

The visitor came, though he came most unwillingly.

Han Zhengqi pushed open the low door of the private room and stooped to enter. A thick, pungent scent of perfume filled the whole room without restraint. The light over the tatami was dim, and beneath an orange-red lamp sat Xu Yuzhen, bolt upright and correct — his mistress of former days.

A woman who had once saved his life.

In the tearoom the last embers gave off a faint heat, and the tea had cooled to lukewarm.

“Sit,” said Xu Yuzhen.

Han Zhengqi sat down, his face without expression.

“Do you still remember? The fragrance of tea come by night?” the woman asked tenderly.

How could he not remember? The warm, soft fragrance of that one night had made him rue it his whole life long.

“Why do you say nothing?” Xu Yuzhen watched his eyes with close attention.

In the depths of his eyes she was like a night-blooming cereus past its prime, luring him with her flaunting airs, her gorgeous vulgarity. A nightmare he had cut away years before began once more to bare its wicked smile — this was his old transgression, his sin, the devil he could hardly face and yet was forced to face.

Xu Yuzhen brewed the tea for him. Across the lifeless surface of the porcelain bowl there spread a green ripple, brimming with the softness of spring, as if dead ashes had rekindled. The finely ground tea-dust rose to the rim of the bowl, and at a light scrape of the lid it spun round and round, showing its true face, greedy and self-pleasuring.

She turned the tea bowl round one and a half times and handed it respectfully to Han Zhengqi, giving him an enticing smile — a fawning, affected smile that let a faint, decadent allure spill out.

“I have not come looking for pleasure,” said Han Zhengqi, and drank a mouthful of tea without so much as a glance aside.

“And I am not a woman any man may have,” Xu Yuzhen retorted, her face turning grave. “How is the tea?”

“Thin and flavorless as water.”

“That is because your heart is too thin of feeling.” She lit a cigarette. “What are you staring at me like that for?”

“You — you did not used to smoke,” said Han Zhengqi.

“People change,” Xu Yuzhen said with a forced smile. “The years alter a life.”

“You did not ask me here simply to mourn over old times, I take it?” Han Zhengqi said, his face set hard.

“Is there any past between you and me worth the mourning?” Xu Yuzhen countered.

“So much the better!” said Han Zhengqi. “Lately you have done a great many things ill-suited to your station. It puzzles me. How could a woman who is a mother commit so monstrous a crime — the murder of her own son?”

“You do not understand. There is no one more anxious to let matters rest than I. But it was they — they who would not let me be. I never meant to blow them to pieces; I meant only a warning, a warning and nothing more.”

“Three people died, and two of them had nothing whatever to do with this affair! Killed for nothing, purely innocent!”

“Then their luck was bad!” Xu Yuzhen drew herself up stiff and rigid, her feelings roused. “I did not want it either.”

“They were innocent, all of them! It is you who deserve to die! You should have died twenty years ago! All of this, every last bit of it, is my sin! My sin is beyond pardon!”

“Those who deserve to die are not always guilty, and the guilty do not always deserve to die.”

“You killed my woman.” Han Zhengqi’s eyes spat fire, the muscles of his face began to work, his teeth all but cracking. “What did you promise me twenty years ago? You said you would never harm my woman. Now you have killed her — killed her!”

“What was intended beforehand and what came of it afterward are too far apart. Neither of us wished for such an outcome. The one I meant to kill was not her at all! It was she — she who, as if driven by some demon, went looking for her own death! Her dying — better she should die than that we should die, no? Do you imagine this is the situation I wanted? As things stand now, it is very much against us both, you and me alike.”

“Stop saying ‘we,’ ‘we,’ with every breath — I have nothing whatever to do with you!”

“You mean to cast me off? To destroy me? You think that because you have made yourself a petty deputy chief of the police bureau you can blot out the sky and cover the sun? Do not delude yourself. Without my help in those days you would long since have starved to death in the gutter. Do not forget what manner of man you are. Do you imagine you have sloughed off every last trace of the outlaw’s rankness, the savage stink of the low-born? No. You have not. It is not possible. Do not dream! A newly widowed woman fancies that once she has fanned dry the earth on her husband’s grave she will turn into a bride fresh from her wedding! One who betrays faith and honor will never be born anew — unless he dies! I think it necessary to remind you: you owe me a debt of passion. I am an exacting creditor, and every pleasure you ever wrung from my body I mean to reclaim from yours, in full and to the very letter.”

“And I tell you this: I will be manipulated by you no longer. I do not fear how ruthless you are — I will stake my very life against yours! Drag out the whole moldy ledger of years past if you like, it is all one to me! Twenty years ago at the Ciyun Temple, it was you who laid the snare, you who drugged me. By the vilest of means you stripped me of every shred of honor. It was you who, in a single night, ground my self-respect, my very character, to powder. It was you who, with your own hands, destroyed my happiness, my love.”

“This was your destiny.”

“No — you owe me a debt of life!”

“No — by it you scooped up high office and a fat salary.”

“What I gained was never what I wanted.”

“What one cannot have is always the best of all.” Xu Yuzhen smiled, a strange, self-satisfied smile. “The threads of warp and weft run to a thousand ends, and each finds its own fitting place. Do not let your greed carry you too far, or in the end you will come to a death with no ground to be buried in.”

“The one who dies with no ground to be buried in ought to be you.” Han Zhengqi rose abruptly and drew on a pair of snow-white gloves. “This time I will not be soft of heart or gentle of hand.”

“What do you mean to do?”

“I mean to snap your neck and be done with it once and for all!” Han Zhengqi all but flung himself upon her and clamped his hands about Xu Yuzhen’s throat. It came so suddenly that in an instant she had the worse of it; she struggled with all her might, gasping. He gave her no chance to draw breath, but throttled her windpipe hard. “One light squeeze now and I send you back to the next world. You are shrewd, you know how to calculate. I will not go in circles with you: I mean to make you evaporate into the air like so much water, to dissolve, to vanish. I can do it. I am not the little pawn I was twenty years ago; I hold the power of life and death now. Come here. Come and look.” His five ice-cold fingers gripped her and dragged her to the window. With a rasp the curtain was pulled back, and Xu Yuzhen saw that outside the Japanese tearoom stood a solid line of policemen on guard, and that the bodyguards she had brought were all pinned down against the foot of the tearoom wall. Plainly Han Zhengqi had come prepared, resolved to kill the “thief.”

But it was no use; the thing was beyond his power to undo.

Onto Xu Yuzhen’s face there forced its way a strange, unfathomable smile. Such a smile set Han Zhengqi shivering though he was not cold; such a smile he had seen once before, twenty years ago, and that once he would remember all his life.

“What are you smiling at?”

Xu Yuzhen signaled to him to loosen his grip on her throat. Han Zhengqi let go, and she coughed violently. “You — you truly are a savage.” She worked at her own windpipe to ease and relax it. “I shall not die so easily, you know. Not unless you are willing to sacrifice that precious son of yours.”

“What did you say?” Something in Han Zhengqi’s breast flinched hard before he could stop it. “Say that again?” He drew his pistol and leveled it straight at Xu Yuzhen’s head, his eyes gone bloodshot as he roared, “Do you think I won’t put a bullet through you here and now!”

“Splendid — one shot, two lives. One the mistress you ‘ravished and then murdered,’ the other your own flesh-and-blood son. I can afford the wager. Do you dare to lay it, Deputy Chief Han?”

“Making a great show of bluster, are you?! Don’t try to bluff me — I wasn’t raised on scares.” But Han Zhengqi’s fierceness had already spent itself, an arrow at the end of its flight.

Xu Yuzhen’s quick eye caught the subtle change in Han Zhengqi’s face. Here, beyond doubt, was her opening. “Make a telephone call. Try it,” she urged, with the greatest earnestness. “See whether it is true or false. Twenty whole years, and how are you still so rash? You think that a last stand with your back to the river is enough to bring me to my death? Then you make far too little of me. I was able to walk in here with nothing to shield me, and I can as easily walk out again without a hair harmed. Put the gun away — mind it does not go off. Two lives.”

“You wait here.” Han Zhengqi put the gun away, went over to an elegant antique-style telephone, and cranked the handle. Curtly he said, “Get me the Customs House.”

In a moment the call went through.

“Kindly fetch Han Yu to the telephone.” Within a few seconds the light went out of Han Zhengqi’s eyes. He slumped against the wall. His heart ached, a needle-prick of an ache; that gnawing, frantic pain only a parent can truly understand.

His only son, Han Yu, had not come to his post at the Customs House that morning; on their way to work his colleagues had found his uniform hanging on a tree. Han Yu had vanished.

“What has happened to my son?” Han Zhengqi’s face was streaming with sweat. His nerves were at the very edge of breaking.

“He is quite well. So long as I am safe and sound, he is sure to live to a hundred. Look at you — sweating with anxiety from head to foot.” Xu Yuzhen made to wipe the sweat from him.

“Do not touch me.”

“You are too weak. You need my help, do you not? You need my pity. Do not be in such haste to be rid of me. Think — if I had not saved you in those days, what would have become of you? Matted hair and a filthy face? Bowing and scraping like a slave? Coarse tea and plain rice?”

“What is your purpose in summoning me here today?”

“I want your repayment.”

“I have already repaid you.”

“Have you?”

“You are still alive — is that not proof enough?”

“That is not enough. I want you to declare your position plainly, and under your own colors. The Golden Dragon Society must not be allowed to rise again from the dead.”

“In the affairs of the gangs I am powerless.”

“Are you not afraid of losing the child you love best? You must make a wise choice — just as you did twenty years ago. Choose me, or choose her. And now: do you choose a young master whose day is past? Or do you choose your own son?”

For Han Zhengqi the terror of losing his beloved child was far, far greater than the loss of a man’s honor and good faith. His heart heaved in its anguish.

“Well?”

“No.” Han Zhengqi was breathing hard.

“No?” Xu Yuzhen was taken aback; she had no wish to lose this trump card that had never once failed her. “You should understand that what you sever with such a clang is not only the natural bond of father and son, but the very bloodline of your house of Han.”

“It is for that very reason that I refuse to choose.”

“You must choose.”

“I cannot choose. Twenty years ago you made me choose: you held the life of the woman I loved best over my head; you seduced me with your body, with your beauty; you forced me to choose. You staged a murder scene and framed me, you compelled me to choose. Now, twenty whole years gone by, and still you would have me choose. No. I will not. Never mind that I still hold some power in my hands — even were I now the meanest of common wretches, I would never choose again. At the worst, let the fish die and the net be torn; it is you or me!”

“Who do you imagine you are? You are nothing but a dog of the house of Yang. Do not take yourself for a man. You think that if you keep him safe he will forgive you? You have been to bed with his father’s woman.”

“I have not.” Han Zhengqi flatly denied it.

“You betrayed his elder sister.”

“I did not!”

“You have deceived him!”

“I have not!”

“He will surely kill you! Rather than die at his hands, better to kill him and buy back your son’s life.”

“Be silent!” Han Zhengqi cut short Xu Yuzhen’s overbearing assault. “Hold your tongue, you viper of a woman! He is your son, is he not?”

“He is not!”

“He is Mr. Yang’s son!”

“He is an impostor!”

“He is the leader of the Golden Dragon Society.”

“Wipe him out, and he is nothing at all.”

“You madwoman.”

“I am not mad,” said Xu Yuzhen. “It is you who are mad. Which matters more — your own son’s life, or one who bears the name of Yang?”

“I will not go on compounding one wrong with another!”

“You must make your choice.”

“Then kill me.” Han Zhengqi abruptly gave up his ferocity and leaned weak and spent against the wall for support. “Kill me, and let my son go.”

“Your life is worth nothing,” Xu Yuzhen said, her eyes full of contempt. “Choose.”

“I will not.”

“You must.”

“I will choose for him!” The words rang out clear and bright, coming all at once from behind the folding screen.

Before the voice had died away, the black-lacquered Tang-style screen behind Han Zhengqi was thrust open with force, and before either he or Xu Yuzhen could so much as blink, A-Chu stepped forth in a plain unfigured gown, his bearing splendid.

Han Zhengqi was thrown into confusion and dismay;

Xu Yuzhen’s face was all suspicion.

The air was strangely still.

“To have a viper in your grasp is not the same as holding the whole board,” A-Chu said, wreathed in smiles. Behind the black-lacquered screen stood the brothers of the Golden Dragon Society, and with them Huang Sanyuan, boss of the Hongmen.

Huang Sanyuan was at once a senior detective of the French Concession police and, in the world of the gangs, the chief of the Hongmen. With a foot in both the black road and the white, his power reached to the skies; on the Shanghai waterfront he was a great man whom no one dared to cross.

That A-Chu should have laid this tea-trap here and now meant, beyond any doubt, that he had done his groundwork thoroughly. Han Zhengqi’s mind went blank: so A-Chu had had him followed for more days than one.

Xia Yuechun and Young Tang, both in black Western suits, stood to one side of the screen. Young Tang, lighting a cigarette, said, “Madam Yang, your methods are altogether too black, do you know that? That day I very nearly got blown up myself. My, but you are ruthless.”

Huang Sanyuan thumped his own chest. “Brother Yang, by the rules of the Hongmen: he who defiles another man’s wife or daughter shall be struck down by the five thunders, and she who lets the red apricot bough stray over the wall shall die beneath a rain of blades. If you cannot bring yourself to do it, you need not scatter any banknotes — one cough from you, and your elder brother will do it for you.”

“There is no need, elder brother. This is my own family’s business, and I should see to it with my own hands.” As he spoke A-Chu gave a strong pull and drew the black-lacquered screen back into place, shutting off Huang Sanyuan, Xia Yuechun, Young Tang, and the rest. A brief calm returned to the private room. The three of them stood face to face, each nursing designs of his own, glances darting like darts, the demon way and the holy way contending for the mastery.

“You had best see clearly: this is the Concession, this is a Japanese tearoom, this is Japanese ground. Do not think to run wild here,” Xu Yuzhen said, forcing herself to seem composed.

“And you had best not forget: this is a world that belongs to the Chinese!” A-Chu stepped forward and tore the curtain down with one strong wrench. It was full afternoon, the red sun high and blazing; the policemen outside the tearoom had long since vanished, every one of them replaced by patrolmen of the French Concession and by the brothers of the Golden Dragon Society.

“One who comes is a guest, and to taste tea and talk quietly is a refined thing. Why take up shield and spear? Would you not agree, Mr. Chu?” Xu Yuzhen put on a smile she did not feel, and her color was very bad.

“It is Mr. Yang,” A-Chu corrected her. “Yang Muchu.”

“What a coincidence — Mr. Yang’s name happens to be the very same as that of my late worthless son.”

“Is that so? Though I have heard that Madam Yang was in truth a chamber maidservant by origin, and a concubine risen from a bondmaid has no standing to call her own husband’s child her ‘worthless son.’ You ought to call him young master, ought you not?”

“I should never have thought that a doctor schooled overseas would hold such old-fashioned notions.”

“That my notions are old-fashioned is a thing you should count yourself fortunate for. The reason I am still willing to hold a conversation with you is that you were once the woman of Mr. Yang Yubo. Otherwise I should have lowered this conversation straight to abuse.”

“I am still, to this day, the woman of Mr. Yang Yubo,” said Xu Yuzhen.

“Is that so?” Yang Muchu ran a deliberately strange, sidelong gaze over her, and said, with a needle hidden in the silk, “The woman of Mr. Yang Yubo, so far as I know, has kept him company on the road to the Yellow Springs these twenty years past. Are you some relic dug up out of the earth? Or is it that your own hour of death draws near?”

“Relic dug from the earth or hour of death drawing near, it is all one. That I should chance to meet you today may be counted a bond of fate between us. I should like to borrow this tearoom to invite you to tea, to renew our acquaintance and talk over old times — I wonder what Chu— no, what Mr. Yang would be pleased to say?” Xu Yuzhen kept her head in the face of danger, and had about her something of a great commander’s bearing.

“This cup of tea you have been brewing for twenty years; were I not to drink it, would that not be discourteous?” A-Chu gave his gown a shake, flicked up the hem, and sat himself cross-legged, neat and clean about it.

“You sit too,” A-Chu said to Han Zhengqi, who had stood dumb and rooted for a long while.

Drained of all strength, Han Zhengqi came over and said, “This subordinate will respectfully take the lowest seat.” He sat down, ill at ease in mind and spirit.

Xu Yuzhen began to brew tea for the two of them.

One could see the finely ground tea leaves struggling in the white Tenmoku bowl. Under the buffet of the boiling water the flesh of the tender leaves curled up, each one a sleek green figure of sorrow, like broken clouds heavy with rain.

“Please.” Xu Yuzhen offered the tea respectfully to A-Chu; in the performance of the tea art her own state of mind was gradually steadying. “How does it taste?” she asked.

“Utterly without savor,” A-Chu answered.

“The drinker has no heart in it, and so it has no savor,” said Xu Yuzhen.

“When the one who brews it is not clean of heart, not still of heart, then the tea has no quality,” said Yang Muchu.

“And the art of the tea?”

“It has ‘art’ but no ‘Way,’ form but no spirit — all outward show and nothing more.”

“A yellow-mouthed child, and he presumes to know the Way of tea?” Xu Yuzhen could bear it no longer.

“Are you a Chinese?” A-Chu asked, springing it on her.

“I am — of course I am.” The muscles of Xu Yuzhen’s face quivered slightly.

“Since we are all of us Chinese, why should we taste tea after the Japanese Way?”

“Because this is a Japanese teahouse, and we do no more than follow the customs of the place we are in.”

“But the Japanese teahouse does its business upon the soil of us Chinese; it is they who should follow the customs of the place, and not we.”

“Can the Chinese Way of tea even be spoken of in the same breath as the Japanese?”

“Hmph.” A-Chu gave a cold, mocking laugh. “Do you know of what country the founder of the Way of tea was? He was a Chinese — Lu Yu of the Tang. ‘I covet no goblet of gold, I covet no cup of white jade; I covet not to enter the ministry at dawn, nor at dusk to mount the terrace of office; a thousandfold, ten thousandfold I covet only the waters of the West River, that once came flowing down past the walls of Jingling.’” A-Chu laughed, and there was great pride in his laugh. “China’s tea was carried back to Japan by the Japanese envoys to the Tang court; the Chinese Way of tea and the Japanese Way stand as master to disciple, as father to son. Do you understand? A disciple, meeting his master, must know to keep a disciple’s courtesy.”

Xu Yuzhen tilted up her face in a cold sneer. “Oho — mighty big talk. I too have some twenty years’ study of the Japanese Way of tea behind me. What they call ‘harmony, reverence, purity, tranquility’ I know to my very bones. The samurai of old Japan prized nothing so highly as the dignity of the Way of tea. At one and the same gathering, from one and the same cup, should a man fail to drink the same tea as another — can you guess what they would do? To uphold the dignity of the Way and the dignity of the warrior, they would cut open their own bellies and let the blood spatter the ground where they knelt. Not like you… not like us Chinese, who spoil the art of tea as carelessly as we please, with tea bowls set out at every street corner and lane, the tea itself as muddy and foul as river water. It is a downright insult to the spirit of the Way. When the master has sunk of his own will into degradation, what is there in the disciple worth revering?”

“Degradation? And what is degradation? A Japanese warrior, because he cannot drink good tea, must kill himself. That is not upholding the dignity of the Way of tea — that is a sickness of the mind! It is a spiritual self-torment that runs clean against the very culture of tea. That is what it is to sink of one’s own will into degradation! Tea, besides being a thing to be savored, serves just as well to quench thirst. The art of tea is a pleasure of the human spirit, a refined delight and interest in the life of mankind. It is never a weapon for the mastery of men’s minds. The Japanese Way of tea, from the boiling of the water to the passing of the cup, prescribes every single step to the death — for all the world like dead zombies going through the mechanical motions of wooden puppets, without life, without meaning, and handed down generation after generation, none daring to step one pace beyond the line. I suspect they suffer from a mania, from madness.”

“You—” Xu Yuzhen’s face went iron-blue, and she coldly held her feelings in check.

“The Chinese Way of tea has none of the artifice and hypocrisy of the Japanese. What the Chinese Way of tea cares for is the ‘oneness of Heaven and man’!”

“And what is this ‘oneness of Heaven and man’?”

“Heaven — Heaven’s nature is pure and true. Man — what is called…” A-Chu took up a cup of tea and, pointing at it, went on. “The saucer is Earth, the lid is Heaven, the cup itself is Man. Heaven is great, Earth is great, and Man is honored above all!” And with that, with a sharp rap, he set the cup down. Lifting his head, he said sternly, “Harmony — the genial breath of spring, a kindly face and pleasing mien, with harmony held most precious; Oneness — not one mote of dust upon it, not one false thought within it. This is the Way of tea of the Chinese: it takes man as its root, it profits the thought and opens the wit, it returns to the plain and comes home to the true.”

“It all hangs together very neatly — but I wonder how your tea-craft stands? All talk and no practice is a sham performer’s trick.” Xu Yuzhen threw down the challenge openly.

“In that case I shall try my small blade on this ox,” A-Chu said, taking up the challenge.

“Do not grudge me the lesson,” said Xu Yuzhen.

“I should be glad to behold the gentleman’s mastery!” Han Zhengqi, who had said nothing for a long while, put in a word.

A-Chu said to Xu Yuzhen, “I shall be sure to instruct you to your full satisfaction.” Then he called out loudly, “Change the tea things!”

At once a Japanese waitress came trotting in, head bobbing and back bowed, and asked in stiff Chinese, “What sort of tea things does the gentleman wish to change to?”

“Any sort will do, so long as they are Chinese tea things,” A-Chu said, ringing and firm.

“Hai!” The Japanese waitress, her face all painted white powder and rouge, bent her waist lower still until her head nearly dropped below her knees, and withdrew with a bow. In a little while she brought in, held in both hands, a set of purple-clay Yixing tea things of Chinese make, and said politely, “Please.” Then she slid the door shut after her.

A-Chu rolled back his snow-white cuffs and, with easy grace, went through the steeping and the pointing, the skimming of the froth, the rinsing of the pot, the scalding and the rolling of the cups; a fine thread of water, a bloom of leaf afloat, and cup and pot brimmed over with fragrance. Then he poured and stippled low, going several times round in “Lord Guan patrolling the city wall,” dashing high and sieving low, back and forth in “Han Xin numbering his troops,” until he had poured out three cups of tea of the same color, the same fragrance, the same flavor, the same measure. His movements were exact and true, deft and quick; the whole set of skills was like drifting cloud and running water, done through in one continuous breath, and gave the senses an exquisite, delicate pleasure, so that the two lookers-on stared open-mouthed and could not but admire him utterly.

From the age of twelve A-Chu had bought and brewed tea for Rong Sheng in the Rong household, and by fifteen or sixteen, steeped in the Fourth Madam’s tea lore, his tea-craft had grown accomplished indeed: the measuring of the leaf and the watching of the water came readily to his hand, and the steeping and infusing followed his every whim. When Rong Sheng went abroad in those years, it was not only at the Fourth Madam’s insistence but at the young master’s own firm wish that he was taken along — and for a good half of it the reason was his mastery of “tea.” In the fine-grained life Rong Sheng led, the pleasure of tasting “tea” was one indispensable link that could not be spared.

And so A-Chu’s tea art was out of the common run — only he had kept it deep hidden and never shown.

“The Chinese Way of tea is broad and deep; the tea art of China springs from a far source and runs long. I, A-Chu, have done no more than sip at the surface, showing but one corner of the iceberg of the Chinese Way. Yet the small may make the great plain, and the little let the large be seen. The master is, after all, the master; the disciple is still the disciple!”

“Splendid indeed, in both sound and show,” Han Zhengqi said in praise.

“Well? Not to your taste?” A-Chu said to Xu Yuzhen.

“You look at your own reflection in the running stream and cannot shed the airs of a woman. In the end you are nothing but a mad dog trained up by Yang Mulian — you are no man. Were you a man, you would fight me out in the open, above board, and not go about it as you do now, working your tricks behind my back by stealth, to pen me up dead fast in this place.”

“Trying to goad me, are you? Afraid to die?” A-Chu burst into a hearty laugh. “You may scold the wind and curse the rain, but you’ll snatch no advantage of wit from me!”

“Do not dream of coming back to life in a borrowed corpse. Unless I acknowledge that you are Yang Muchu, unless I let you into the house of Yang, you will not get so much as a single copper.”

“Is that so?” Step by step A-Chu closed in upon Xu Yuzhen. “I, Yang Muchu, swear here this day by Heaven and by the sun: I will walk in through the great gate of the house of Yang openly and with my head held high! I will take back every last copper you plundered from my hands — remember, every last one. And every debt of life you have run up must be paid back with your own blood! I mean to destroy you utterly — in your fortunes, in your spirit, in your very flesh. Go back and tell Yang Yuhua to make ready three coffins, for I will take the three of you, your whole family, and tear you apart one by one and lay you inside them. Do you hear me clearly?” A-Chu’s handsome face was darkened a little with rancor and grief.

“No — not three coffins, but four, for there is your brother too, my son. We four are one family; if we die, we die assuredly together, and if we are buried, we shall be buried in one place.”

“Is that so? Is there anywhere in this world a mother who would bury her own son? You are simply not human.”

“Whether there is anywhere in this world a mother who would bury her own son, I do not know. But this I do know: from ancient times down to our own, China has never wanted for cases of brother destroying brother. ‘They boil the beans by burning the beanstalks, and in the cauldron the beans weep: we sprang, the two of us, from the selfsame root — why scald and sear each other so hard?’”

“Set your mind at rest — I will be sure to fish that weeping bean out of the cauldron first.”

“Nothing could be better.”

“I will surely go home smiling!”

“On your way home, mind you are not led astray by ghosts and beset by demons,” Xu Yuzhen said with a cold laugh.

“On my way home, let a ghost cross me and I kill the ghost; let a Buddha bar my path and I kill the Buddha!” The cold smile froze on A-Chu’s gloomy face.

“Then I shall go home and wait for you — sweep the chamber, burn the incense, and stand ready in full array.” Xu Yuzhen was pinning her hopes now on getting clear away.

“Very well — though you need not go to overmuch trouble. I like to cut away the excess and keep to the plain. Set your mind at ease and see to your own funeral arrangements.” On A-Chu’s long, fine lashes there hung both a smile and contempt.

“No — she cannot go, sir.” Han Zhengqi cut in suddenly. “My son is in her hands.”

“No — your son is in my hands.” A-Chu turned his head, fixed both eyes upon Han Zhengqi, and said very distinctly, “Han Yu is in my motorcar. Our own account we shall settle slowly, in good time.”

Han Zhengqi’s face was numb. Every sentence, every word A-Chu spoke went thread by thread cold to the very marrow of his bones, and he felt a fear and a desolation such as he had never known before.

For Xu Yuzhen it was as though a sliver of sky had opened before the very gate of the netherworld; without loss of grace she bowed her head to A-Chu in thanks. “Until we meet again.” Just as her leading foot crossed the threshold, A-Chu spoke. “Wait, Madam Yang.” He came close to her and said with a smile, “I forgot a moment ago to tell you: the four men you sent to kidnap Han Yu — I have already cleaned them up for you.”

Xu Yuzhen’s heart was wrenched violently tight. She was aghast. “You — what did you say?”

“I said, I have cleaned them up for you.” A-Chu, worked up now, spoke slowly and clearly. “This morning I sent men to bring young Master Han here for tea, and as it happened the men you had sent arrived first. So my people invited them to take tea at my humble house beforehand. They told me the TNT that went to the Tongji Hospital was sent by them, on your orders, and that they had all had a hand in it; and I thought, a thing to be done sooner is a thing to be done later, so I did for them first, and threw the bodies into the Huangpu. Forgive me — I forgot to send you word of it. Only…” He leaned close to Xu Yuzhen’s ear and lowered his voice; plainly there was this that he did not wish Xia Yuechun and Young Tang, next door, to hear. “I had their hands and feet taken off, every one, as a gift to mark our meeting — for you.”

Xu Yuzhen was utterly stunned; her breath came quick and thick.

“Come — bring in the gift I have prepared for Madam Yang.” A-Chu gave the order to Liu A-Si and Lu Liangchen, his men standing at the door. These two had formerly followed Rong Chu, making their living out in the world of the gangs.

Liu A-Si placed a black leather suitcase in Xu Yuzhen’s hands. Her hands were trembling; the black case was heavy and laden, and blood kept seeping out of it. A thick, rank stench oozed from within, enough to split heart and gall.

“Among them, it seems, was a Japanese — one Sakai Ichirō.”

Xu Yuzhen vomited, fouling the floor.

“I have always been one to offer courtesy before force. This precious gift I beg you to pass on to Mr. Yang Yuhua, as a token of my resolve to come home. And I beg you, Auntie, to tell my uncle that the last day is near at hand, and that he should enjoy this last light of spring to the full. Thank you. You may go.” With more than usual courtesy A-Chu bowed her out.

Xu Yuzhen herself did not know how she left the tearoom in such wretched disarray; her hands and feet were stiff, and she was for all the world a walking corpse. Liu A-Si hired a rickshaw for her, set the so-called “gift” down at her knees, and then let her go.

Xu Yuzhen was weeping.

Her hand stroked the black case. In it was the blood of her “first love,” his hands, his feet. He was the first man of her life, and he had gone like this, without a sound, without a word of explanation. For the sake of her cause he had crossed the far oceans; he had existed for the sake of her own existing. His hands had once so warmly caressed her hands, his toes had once lain folded against her toes; their blood had once mingled, they had clung to each other as lips to teeth, licking each other’s wounds in the dark, howling like wolves beneath the light of the moon, sharing a wild and savage love between them. Now all of it had become so much smoke and mist before the eyes.

Last night still warm and tender beneath the quilt; this afternoon sundered by the barrier of the quick and the dead, the one gone, the other living on.

She moaned in her pain, her heart convulsed and shuddering, her teeth grinding audibly against one another; and even were she herself on the very brink of destruction, she would, in the one instant before she was destroyed, destroy this whole city.

In the wind Xu Yuzhen’s pale, clear face looked darker and more sombre still.

In the air of the tearoom a few faint threads of tea-fragrance lingered and gathered, like a sullen haze that would not disperse and could not be undone.

Han Zhengqi felt that there was no longer anywhere for his body and mind to hide.

A-Chu seemed to have an excess of energy about him; the wronged rancor he had carried for twenty years had burst out all at once, and he could not quite master himself. There was something within him verging on madness.

The curtain, torn wantonly aside, lay sickly and limp upon the floor; the sunlight, its screen gone, split into a grinning, flaunting mouth and drove straight in from beyond the bright window, its strong and powerful rays drowning in an instant all the gloom within the tearoom.

The black-lacquered Tang-style screen was now pushed gently open, and the two cramped tearooms became one long rectangular private room. Huang Sanyuan, Xia Yuechun, Young Tang, and the others came one after another into Han Zhengqi’s view. Huang Sanyuan’s contemptuous gaze and Young Tang’s mocking grin put Han Zhengqi to great shame.

“Uncle, it is all right; Han Yu and we are good friends, and no one is going to harm you.” Xia Yuechun did his utmost to comfort Han Zhengqi; he had no wish for this affair to injure the bonds of fellowship among schoolmates, though he knew something of the “rules of the gangs.” Still, to his mind, these so-called “rules” ought to bend from one person to another.

A-Chu knew that these few words of Xia Yuechun’s were meant for his own ears.

That Xia Yuechun and Young Tang had come here to take tea had a particular cause behind it. A month before, the Chunhe Hospital that Xia Yuechun’s family ran had received notice that the government meant to requisition its land. The hospital was the family’s ancestral property, and Xia Yuechun had ten thousand reasons why he could not move. He had run high and low to several government offices, said a great many fine words, laid out a great deal of money, worn a great many smiles, and at last won a sliver of room to maneuver in the way of favors: he was told to enter into a joint venture with the Japanese, and so long as the Japanese held shares in the Chunhe Hospital, the Chunhe Hospital might go on standing towering and unshakable where it was, as immovable as if no thunder could touch it. It so enraged Xia Yuechun that he fainted clean away on the spot. Young Tang drove him to A-Chu’s house, and the moment he was inside he threw his arms about A-Chu and wept aloud, unable to master himself, with even a mind to hang himself.

Young Tang too, moved as the fox mourns the dead hare, was all for going and risking his own life for Xia Yuechun, and let his mouth run away with him: he would go to Tokyo and cut down the Emperor, he would wipe out the little Japanese; until at last, worn out with his ranting, he lay sprawled on the sofa foaming at the mouth and calling for a pipe to smoke.

A-Chu kept no hired servants at his house; it was usually the nurse-amah A-Yue who saw to the meals and the daily needs, and now and then A-Chu did the cooking himself, so that there was no one to spare to wait upon this opium-fiend. A-Chu could not help but set to it with his own hands and cook Young Tang two pellets of opium. Only with much trouble did he win the house a moment’s quiet.

That same evening A-Chu took his own calling card and went to the French Concession police station to “pay his respects at the wharf.” The “gift money” being handsome, Huang Sanyuan received this “rising talent” of the Shanghai gangs with great courtesy. Neither had looked for it, but the two found their words met well and each felt for the other the sympathy of like for like; and so they opened the incense hall and swore themselves brothers.

Before long the matter of the Xia family hospital’s removal took a sudden and dramatic turn. The government’s Land Administration Bureau notified Xia Yuechun that the two camphor trees within the grounds of the Chunhe Hospital were “relics of the former Ming,” national property in urgent need of protection, so that not only need the hospital not move now, but should the government ever draw up plans to rebuild, those plans would have to go around it.

When the good news came, it was for Xia Yuechun like finding one life amid a hundred deaths. In secret he was grateful to A-Chu and would have shared out a portion of the shares to him, but A-Chu declined. Only, A-Chu bade him be sure to invite the “man who had seen to the business” to a cup of tea, and how should Xia Yuechun dare not gladly obey? The whole of it was arranged by A-Chu’s own single hand.

The party of them had reached the Park Hotel in the morning and spent two or three hours at their socializing. They were on the point of breaking up when A-Chu proposed that they go to a Japanese tearoom to hear a piece of Japanese “kabuki” and all make merry a while. Xia Yuechun had been unwilling to go and was hauled along by main force; and only once there did he learn that this proposal of A-Chu’s went beyond “the pleasure of helping others” — that the factor of “helping himself” was very much to be suspected in it. Quietly they had sat through a whole “grand play behind the screen,” and had come, altogether against their own will, to know the private secrets of A-Chu’s family.

To be sure, had A-Chu simply told him of the matter outright, he would never have believed it — it was too much like a tale out of the Arabian Nights.

But now, even had A-Chu not said a single word, he had the whole shape of the thing more or less clear.

Come what might, he was wholly unwilling that A-Chu should harm Han Yu’s father, though he knew that in A-Chu’s eyes Han Zhengqi’s heaped-up sins were many indeed.

“To err is human. My advice is that it’s best to forgive and forget,” Xia Yuechun said to A-Chu, in English. A-Chu smiled and did not answer. “I hope you will give favorable consideration to my suggestion,” Xia Yuechun pressed on. “You might do well to think of a saying you are always quoting yourself. To err is human, to forgive divine.”

“This is no mere mistake, Yuechun,” said A-Chu. “This is sin!”

“A-Chu knows his measure; let us not meddle in another man’s family affairs.” Young Tang came over to smooth things over, and said politely to Han Zhengqi, “Good luck to you, Uncle.” Taking the chance, Young Tang pulled Xia Yuechun along and made straight for the door. “A-Chu, we will wait for you downstairs.”

“Very good. Young Tang, Yuechun, see Mr. Huang out for me.” A-Chu, seemingly all mildness, arranged for Young Tang and Xia Yuechun to see Huang Sanyuan off first.

Once more the tearoom fell utterly and completely still.

“Sir, I beg you, let my son go.” The sentence Han Zhengqi had been holding pent in his throat forced its way out at last; his sweat had never once stopped flowing.

“I did not kidnap Han Yu by design; it was my men who snatched Han Yu back from the hands of Xu Yuzhen’s people. You are no longer under anyone’s coercion — you ought to thank me, not fear me,” A-Chu said calmly.

“Then does the gentleman mean that he will grant my son and me a way to live?”

“I am no philanthropist.” A-Chu smiled coldly. “Your own son, of course, you must save yourself.”

“Then what is the gentleman’s meaning?”

“The truth! What is the truth? I mean to know the whole truth!”

“The truth? Have I not already told it to you? The only thing I have held back is — is that unspeakable, filthy tale of how the blame was fastened onto another.”

“You, the amah A-Yue, my elder sister — Xu Yuzhen among you — the tale of the past that you have all told me: I have gone over it from beginning to end, again and again, in dead earnest, every chapter and every link of what you told me. And frankly, every one of you has lied! Lied, each in his own degree! My dearest sister among you. Even she kept part of the ‘truth’ hidden from me.”

“Sir?”

“To speak truly, I cannot be at peace; I cannot make myself calm. Every time I wake from a dream at midnight, in that haze between sleeping and waking, all the hidden images come thronging one upon another. All the shattered, broken fragments of memory knit themselves together again. Do you know what it is that I see?”

“You…?”

“The one who sells you out is, more often than not, the one closest to you.” Suddenly A-Chu dealt Han Zhengqi a backhanded blow with a teapot — the stroke was vicious, the attack fierce. The purple-clay teapot shattered against Han Zhengqi’s forehead, and he was struck to the ground. “The one you trust most, the one on whom you rest your highest hopes, is more often than not the one who wounds you deepest from behind!” A-Chu’s face was flushed with the violence of it. “When you were at the end of your road, destitute, it was by the grace of my house of Yang that you found a place to settle body and life. My father used you well, and you attended him closely for years — how did you dare to carry on with my father’s woman? For one gust of spring wind you sold your very soul, gave silent leave to evil to happen, and looked on with folded arms like a passerby in the road! My poor sister, breaking out alone, in peril all the way. Driven to it, she chose to live by going toward death, and married herself down to a husband of rotten wood, made herself a concubine in a lesser hall! And pity her — to her death, to her very death, she still hid in her heart a sincere and ardent love for her sweetheart; to the very moment of her death she never knew that her lover had betrayed her twenty years before and had chosen fat salary and high office! You, drifting and getting by, forever anxious over your own shadow — what manner of man are you, in the end? Well?” A-Chu poured out his feeling to the full, so that Han Zhengqi’s mind went blank and something in the pit of his heart convulsed.

“Sir!” Han Zhengqi knew that an irreversible nightmare had at last become reality. This was no dream from which, waking at midnight, a man might lightly erase and blot out of his mind all the harrowing things he had lived through — no, it was not possible at all. “You do not understand, sir. There are some facts that can never be made clear.”

“In this world there are only ‘facts’ that men will not tell, never ‘facts’ that cannot be made clear. Would you not agree? You have been lying all along! A lie to blot out the very sky! You fabricated for me one lie after another, and what astonishes me is that you should truly have thought I would swallow it whole. You put my patience to the test, thinking me a man of great patience! I tell you now: I am a man with no patience whatever for hearing lies told over and over.”

“But — but the real truth is cruel; it can never bear the light of day. For you, not to know is ten thousand times better than to know. Once the truth is laid bare, you may well not be prepared for it in your heart, sir.”

“Since it cannot be set openly and honorably upon the table and spoken, then of course there is some bitter difficulty behind it that you dare not, will not, would not, cannot tell. And what, in the end, is that bitter difficulty?”

“I was at the end of every road, with no plan left to me.”

“I should like to hear it — how a man at the end of every road, with no plan left to him, went back on his word and betrayed his faith in those days,” A-Chu said, nearly cold.

Han Zhengqi knew that any vain plea, with the facts plain and the evidence ironclad, would turn pale and powerless. With the balance of force so uneven, the one way to loose himself from his plight was to tell the truth without rousing the other man’s anger. “I will tell you the truth. The so-called truth is in fact a single sentence.” Once more his windpipe gave out a rising and falling, trembling sound. “Xu Yuzhen is your own birth mother!”

A secret buried too deep for words; too deep for words.

So that was it.

A-Chu gave a contemptuous, jeering laugh.

This time Han Zhengqi felt, truly and vividly, how terrible A-Chu was. He had thought that once these words were out, mountains would fall and the earth split open. A-Chu’s natural reaction ought to have been first shock, then fury; or else to be caught between advance and retreat; or else to be shaken to the very soul.

His own elder sister had made of him a tool of vengeance, meaning him to kill his own birth mother with his own hands. Though her wish had not been fulfilled, still, twenty years of a sister’s and brother’s love had been, after all, an illusion… ought he not to have grieved?

His own birth mother had killed his own birth father with her own hands, and for her single selfish desire would not spare even her own son — ought he not to have felt sorrow?

The very people he had striven with all his might to avenge himself upon were, every one, his own kin — ought he not to have been in an agony that made life not worth the living?

However he might have shown it, he ought by no means to be as he now was — so unmoved, so perfectly at his ease — unless the man were a madman. The trouble was, A-Chu was no madman.

“You feel that my manner, on hearing this ‘truth,’ is very much out of the ordinary? Is that it?” A-Chu came forward himself to unriddle it. “There is but one reason: this so-called ‘truth’ is merely a front, a smoke-bomb. The real ‘truth’ is ‘betrayal,’ is ‘murder,’ is a ‘swindle,’ a ‘conspiracy,’ designed and meticulously plotted from the very start.” A-Chu walked up to meet Han Zhengqi, crouched down low as if stooping to him, and said close by his ear, “I mean to tell you a wholly opposite tale. The frame of the story is not yet perfect, and at first hearing it may seem absurd, past believing. But I tell you, every word I speak is fact — incontestable fact.” Slowly A-Chu straightened up and said, a word and a pause at a time, “I am indeed, most certainly, the son of Xu Yuzhen. That I do not deny.”

“Sir?” Han Zhengqi’s face was full of doubt.

“But my mother must already, twenty years ago, have had her fragrance snuffed out and her jade shattered.”

“Twenty years ago? When?”

“On a certain night of a certain day of a certain month twenty years ago — at the very time you were going to bed with this present ‘Xu Yuzhen’ — my own birth mother disappeared. She vanished; she was cruelly murdered; she met her death.”
