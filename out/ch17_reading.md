## The Du Yuesheng I Knew

{d} As told by Yu Yongfu

I had been a shop hand at a fruit firm by Changsheng Bridge, in the old walled city of Shanghai, selling fruit as a rule and, in autumn, sugar-roasted chestnuts. Around 1922 I went to Hong Kong to trade in fruit, and in 1925 was caught by the great Canton-Hong Kong strike; life in Hong Kong was hard, so I went back to Shanghai.

In my thirty-eighth year — 1927 — Du Yuesheng's accountant came looking for me. He said Du had heard I had a real knack for cooking and rolling opium, and wanted me to come to the Du house to prepare his pipes; I agreed. From then on I went to the Du house at first light every morning and did not get home until deep in the night, for Du kept late hours, and I had to wait upon him until he had drawn his last pipe before I could leave.

When I entered the Du household, the residence on Rue Wagner (today's Ninghai West Road) had stood less than two years. The plot, I was told, had originally been Huang Jinrong's; Huang made a present of it to Du and Zhang Xiaolin, and the two of them each built a house on it. From the front each house had its own gate, but inside they were joined through.

The second and third floors of the Du house were where his wives, senior and junior, and his children lived. On the ground floor was a large room, the "dining hall," where he received guests and did his business; it was very handsomely appointed, with steam heat in winter and cooled air in summer. Behind the dining hall was a smaller room, called the accounts room, which was really where Du smoked his opium. Only his closest friends and disciples could go in; ordinarily just three of us were inside — Du Yuesheng, Wan Molin, and myself. Du and I would lie stretched out facing each other across the couch, while Wan sat guard at a little table beside it, his sole business to place and take Du's telephone calls. Wan had done this work for years and had by heart every number that had anything to do with Du; at a single word he would pick up the receiver and put the call through, so everyone called him "the walking telephone book." Every morning, when Du came down from upstairs to smoke in this room, someone would already have drawn up a list of where there were meetings that day and where there were social engagements and laid it on the opium tray; Du would look it over and, drawing on his pipe, tell Wan Molin which places he meant to go to himself, what gifts to send where, and where a telephone word would do — and Wan would see to it at once.

In this way I served in the Du mansion for more than ten years. Not long after the Battle of Shanghai broke out on the thirteenth of August, Du took refuge in Hong Kong. I did not go with him but stayed in Shanghai, keeping in touch all the while with Wan Molin and Huang Guodong (Du's accountant), from whom I drew my living. After the victory in the war Du returned to Shanghai, first staying at Gu Jiatang's house on Beijing West Road, and later in Room 705 of the "Eighteen-Story Building" (his family still lived on Rue Wagner, while he himself lived in the Eighteen-Story apartments with Yao Yulan and Meng Xiaodong); he sent for me again to cook and roll his opium, and so it went until early 1949, when Du left Shanghai for Hong Kong.

His going was decided in a hurry. He had sent word for me to go with him, and when I got the message I rushed to the wharf; but the ship's gangway had already been drawn up and there was no way aboard. That was the end of my connection with Du.

### I. Du Yuesheng's Origins

What I know of Du Yuesheng's early life I heard from his friends and his junior disciples, talking in the Du mansion.

Du was a native of Gaoqiao, in Pudong, born in 1888. He lost both parents as a small child and was raised by his elder sister. Even as a boy he loved to gamble, and would often steal money from his brother-in-law to game with; when the man found out, he quarreled with the sister. In the end, seeing that the two of them simply could not abide each other, the sister asked someone to find Du a trade. When he set off for Shanghai to apprentice at a fruit firm, his sister had made him some new cloth clothes — but on the way he fell to gambling again, and by the time he reached Shanghai the clothes had already been lost to someone else.

Some say Du was raised from childhood by his maternal uncle, but that is not so. He did have a maternal uncle, surnamed Zhu, a mason by trade. Du once tried to borrow money from him and was scolded and turned out for his pains, so that after Du came up in the world he would take no notice of the uncle whenever he called. It was not until 1925, when the Rue Wagner house was going up, that — so I heard — his senior wife, "Lao Si," said to him, "This time you might do something for your uncle," and only then did he have the man come and oversee the building work. Later he even promoted his own cousin, Zhu Wende — this same uncle's son — who came to wield no small influence within the Du establishment.

In Shanghai, Du apprenticed at the Zhang Hengda fruit-and-produce firm by the Great Wharf in Nanshi. When his three years were up and he had risen to "counterhand," the proprietor let him go, for he was forever losing money and running with the hoodlums, up to anything and everything. He went back to his home village, and with no way to make a living took to hawking fried dough-sticks and flatcakes in Gaoqiao town; but he could not keep himself even so, and returned to Shanghai. Huang Wenxiang, the accountant at the Zhang Hengda firm, took pity on him and, behind the proprietor's back, would pass him the poorer fruit at the price of spoiled goods. At the same time Du fell in with the hoodlums and loafers around the money-shop guildhall on Li Xiangua Street (then called Xiexing Street) — Hangzhou A-fa and the rest — and would loiter about the Shiliupu wharves; when a fruit boat came in they would find a way aboard and, half by theft and half by force, carry off some of the fruit, then hawk it all through the streets and the teahouses, opium dens, and gambling houses. So in the hoodlum world he became known as "Fruit Yuesheng" (in his early days he was called Yuesheng, written 月生; the present 月笙 came later).

Because of this bond with Huang Wenxiang, after Du moved into the Rue Wagner house Huang became an honored guest there, right up until his death during the war. Huang had two sons. The elder, Huang Guodong, was set by Du as accountant in the Rue Wagner mansion (the accountant before him had been Jiao Wenbin; after Jiao died it was a Ningbo man surnamed Yang, an old hand of Du's, who was moved up to assistant manager once Du opened the Zhonghui Bank, whereupon Huang succeeded to the accounts). When Du went to Hong Kong and Chongqing during the war, Huang stayed on in Shanghai the whole time, managing Du's property and accounts together with Wan Molin; and when Du left for Hong Kong before liberation, Huang too stayed behind and did not go. Huang Wenxiang's second son, Huang Guofa, was placed by Du in the Zhonghui Bank.

In his "Fruit Yuesheng" days, Du not only stole fruit with Hangzhou A-fa and the others but ran gambling stalls and flower-lotteries with them, and would often gather to gamble at the coal warehouse on the China Merchants' Jinliyuan wharf. One of the hoodlums who ran with Du in those days told me later that once, on Du's thirtieth birthday, his gang brothers each put in a dollar to hold an evening feast in his honor — but in a single afternoon Du gambled away every cent they had chipped in. Du's fondness for gambling was famous among his fellows even then.

It was in this period that Du took the Green Gang boss Chen Shichang as his "old man." In those days the Small East Gate quarter was full of opium dens, gambling houses, and every sort of ground-stall for cheating and swindling the common people, and it was there that Chen Shichang had got his start. He began by making his living at a ring-toss game: holding three bamboo pegs in his hand, he would have people pitch cash coins at them, and whoever ringed the peg with a red thread tied below it could win a piece of fruit or a pack of cigarettes or the like — a swindler's trick. Later Chen became the Green Gang boss of the Small East Gate district; he was of the "Tong" generation and Du of the "Wu." After Du had made himself a great hoodlum in the French Concession, Chen lived off this disciple of his. He lodged at Fuchang Lane near the old Baxianqiao and would drop in at the Du mansion now and then; everyone called him the Old Master. Every Spring Festival he would keep a gambling house running at the Heng Society for three days, gathering in Du's junior disciples to game; over the three days he could rake off tens of thousands of yuan in house cut, most of which went to Chen Shichang.

In the year Du turned thirty, he shifted from Nanshi to the Zhengjia Wooden Bridge quarter of the French Concession, standing guard over the gambling floor — as a bodyguard, that is — and serving as a strong-arm man. Before long he came to know Huang Jinrong, who by then, backed by the strength of the French Concession police, had become a noted hoodlum chief. Du first served Huang as a follower, picking pockets and lifting purses; being quick and clever, he won the great favor of Huang's wife, Sister A-Gui. It was said that around this time the Shanghai garrison commissioner He Fenglin and others had privately put up money for a company, chiefly to traffic in and sell opium. Du Yuesheng got wind of it and told Sister A-Gui; through Huang they insisted on taking a share, and in less than two years Huang had drawn more than two million yuan from it. Sister A-Gui had Huang reward Du with 50,000 yuan, and this became the capital on which Du got his own start: with it he and Huang opened gambling houses and opium dens together. Later, when Fan Huichun, who had held the opium-vending monopoly in the French Concession, changed trades and gave it up, Huang put Du forward to take it over — and from then on Du's affairs grew grander and grander. By the time Chiang Kai-shek and the Nationalists' power reached Shanghai, Du had thrown in with them too, mounted the political and economic stage, and become one of Shanghai's "men of mark," his standing outstripping even Huang Jinrong's.

### II. Du's Life and Family

When Du Yuesheng was in Nanshi he had a wife, who died young. Later, in his days with Huang Jinrong, Sister A-Gui found him another, commonly called "Lao Si," who ranked as his senior wife. Afterward Du heard that Lao Si had been carrying on with her own cousin, and he had Jin Tingsun send men to break both of the cousin's legs and to put out both eyes of Lao Si's chauffeur; Lao Si herself he shut up in a small room, cut off from the world. Only years later, when her son Weifan married, was she let out. Du next took as concubine a certain Chen, of courtesan stock, commonly called "Lao Wu"; these wives all lived on Rue Wagner. Besides them Du took Yao Yulan as his fourth wife. Yao Yulan had started as a singer in the all-female opera; her foster-mother, Xiao Lanying, was a noted actress in old-male roles. Yao had, besides herself, a foster-sister called Yuying; the two were on the boards at the Gongwutai Theater when Du took a fancy to them. He had meant to take Yuying, but she fell ill and died, and so he married Yulan. After Yulan went to Du, Xiao Lanying became a Buddhist nun. Yao Yulan did not live on Rue Wagner; Du rented two houses for her at Lafayette Lane on Route Lafayette (today's Fuxing Lane on Fuxing Middle Road) for a lesser establishment, and on both of his later trips to Hong Kong Yao went with him.

Du Yuesheng had in all eight sons and two daughters; those who remained in Shanghai after liberation were Weifan and Weiren. One daughter married the fourth son of Jin A-san (that is, Jin Tingsun). Du arranged in advance which wife would have which night, and for years there was no strife among them. His children ordinarily stood in some awe of their father and were seldom in his company; what the old man was really about, they did not understand. I remember that once they asked me about some matter concerning Du Yuesheng. I said, "For a thing like this you have only to ask your mother — how is it you come asking me about your own father's affairs?"

Meng Xiaodong had once sung at the Great World, and had since become a noted actress in old-male roles. After the victory in the war she came to Shanghai, sought out Yao Yulan, whom she addressed as "elder sister," and lived with her at the Eighteen-Story Building on Changle Road; there she took up with Du, and it was not until the eve of liberation, when Du left for Hong Kong, that he announced their marriage.

Once Du had become a "man of mark of Shanghai," he always wore a long gown or robe at home, the very picture of a gentleman. Among his junior disciples, men accustomed to short jackets and rough ways would still have to throw on a long gown when they entered the Du mansion, or Du, catching sight of them, would give them a scolding.

Before Du opened the Zhonghui Bank he rose very late and had a heavy opium habit. Beside his smoking-stand hung ten pipes of every kind; I would always get there early, work up the pellets, and load all ten, so that the moment Du came downstairs and entered the room he could set to puffing out clouds. After the Zhonghui Bank opened, his public affairs grew busier and busier: he took to rising before nine each morning and no longer smoked so much. On getting up he would take a cup of chicken broth and two slices of bread, draw two or three pipes, and go out. For a time he spoke of breaking the habit, and a doctor of his, Pang Jingzhou, made much of preaching to him that lecithin could cure the craving; Du took Pang's advice and from then on smoked less and took the substitute instead. After he returned to Shanghai following the victory, his habit gradually grew heavy again, for his asthma was worsening and he could less and less do without the pipe.

He drank little, but was fond of gambling; only, reckoning that he now had a position to keep, he no longer gambled with his junior disciples, nor at the public gambling houses. When the great gambling house at No. 181 Avenue Foch was opened, he set aside a secret room within it for himself and a few men of standing to gamble in. After No. 181 closed down, he set up another secret gambling place outside, of which I do not know the exact whereabouts. As for the Rue Wagner mansion, only now and then, at New Year or a festival, would he get a few friends together for mahjong. As a rule he did not gamble at home, nor would he let others gamble for money in his house.

Du seldom entertained at home. At ordinary meals his wives and children ate upstairs, while he, Wan Molin, and I — three of us — ate at one table in the accounts room. Sometimes his closest retainers, such as Zhang Yishu, or such partners and disciples as Gu Jiatang and Ye Zhuoshan, would happen to come into the accounts room at mealtime to talk over some matter with him, and then a little extra food and wine would be set out and they would eat together.

Du as a rule did not drink; I, on the other hand, would take a large cup of rice wine at every meal. They always ate first, and I would sip on slowly by myself. When Zhang Yishu and the others were at table drinking, Du would keep them company with a glass or two of grape wine.

Besides our table, six more tables of food had to be laid downstairs for the drivers, bodyguards, servants, and maids. Du himself had two motorcars, each wife had one, and there was besides one for general use on the mansion's business, so there were six or seven chauffeurs in all. He kept eight bodyguards: two guarded the main gate, two stood watch inside the house, and the other four, in two shifts, followed his car to protect it. When I first came to Rue Wagner, Du still had no bodyguards, only one strapping, long-faced fellow who attended him. After the bank opened, everyone urged him to take on a few guards. At first Du thought them unnecessary, but he could not withstand the repeated urging — and a battalion commander then in official employ took the lead in recommending them, sending over a batch of guards, so that he had to accept. From then on he came and went with an escort about him, and his consequence swelled at a stroke.

Besides the Rue Wagner residence, Du Yuesheng had in Shanghai, at the corner of the former Route Doumer (today's Donghu Road) and Route Paul Henry (today's Xinle Road), another tall and spacious Western house, which Jin Tingsun had built for him. Colluding with the Nationalist government, Du held the monopoly on the "aviation lottery" of six provinces, Jiangsu and Zhejiang among them, set up the Dayun Company with Jin Tingsun as general manager, and scraped in a great deal of money; out of the Dayun Company's profits Jin took US$450,000 to build this house on Route Doumer and made a present of it to Du. But the house was scarcely finished when the war broke out; Du left at once for Hong Kong and never lived in it a single day. After the victory he sold it to a foreigner for US$600,000 (by one account US$800,000).

Besides these, Du had houses in Hangzhou and at Moganshan; during the war the furniture and everything else in both was scattered and lost. After the victory he shipped a great quantity of costly furniture up from Shanghai — I saw with my own eyes several Simmons beds sent off to Hangzhou, and all the summer sofa-covers were made of Taiwan matting. These two villas he used chiefly to receive Nationalist notables, the warlords of the various provinces, and other guests, and they had to be kept up and waited on the year round by a staff of Chinese and Western cooks and attendants.

### III. Du Yuesheng's Extravagance

Du's life of reckless extravagance may be shown by two instances.

The first was the "dedication ceremony" he held at the Du Family Ancestral Temple he built in Gaoqiao. This affair, in 1931, caused a stir throughout the country, and for days the Shanghai papers carried full accounts of it. To speak only of what I had a hand in was startling enough. Some days before the ceremony, Du told me to lay in extra opium, so I boiled up 5,000 taels of raw opium beforehand and brought in twenty-eight men from the various gambling houses to help me cook it and roll the pellets. Once we were at the Du temple, I set ten men to wait on the famous opera singers who came to the banquet as they smoked, and a dozen others to wait on the ordinary run of so-called honored guests; as for the "old guns" among Du's junior disciples, they were given the paste and pellets to cook for themselves and needed no waiting on. Even so, the dozen and more of us were run off our feet. In those three days I was too busy to sit down to a proper meal, and never saw a single opera. In the end the 5,000 taels of opium paste were smoked out by the second day, and another 3,000 had to be boiled up in haste before we could barely keep up.

Over those three days, seven or eight hundred tables of banquet had to be laid each day; several dozen cooks were brought in, and Du had more than forty of his junior disciples who were practiced in such things put in sole charge of arranging the feasts. The accounts were kept by fourteen men, all seconded for the occasion from the Zhonghui, the Commercial, and other banks connected with Du.

To lure the farmers of the Gaoqiao district to this "ceremony," Du had laid in a great stock of washbasins, towels, and the like. Everyone who came bearing a gift, however small, was given a commemorative badge, on the strength of which he could freely eat at the banquets and watch the operas for three days, with opium supplied to any who cared to smoke; and besides this, each was given a washbasin, a towel, a few bottles of prickly-heat lotion, and a lantern, all printed with the words "Dedication of the Du Temple." In this way not a few farmers were drawn to come with gifts and take part.

Du had two motor launches of his own, one named the Yuebao and the other the Botao; and in those days, besides these two, many more steamers were brought in from the China Merchants company and other shipping lines expressly to carry the well-wishers back and forth between Shanghai and Gaoqiao. A hoodlum gentleman of Nanshi named Mao Zijian went so far as to borrow from the City God Temple five gold-topped grand sedan-chairs, meant for the City God himself to ride in, to bear Du's ancestral tablets in procession to Gaoqiao.

The other instance was the lucky money Du gave out at the Spring Festival. As far as I know, every year by the twenty-fourth of the twelfth lunar month, Tian Hongyuan, the accountant at the Commercial Bank, would make ready for him two thousand "red packets" of two dollars each, for the general run of drivers, servants, patrolmen, and so on; and, besides, two hundred gold "quarter-pieces" — gold coins — for the children whom friends and disciples brought along on their New Year calls, and fifty gold pieces struck especially for his godsons and godgrandsons. On New Year's Eve, Du would bathe and everything would be got ready; and from the next morning on the house was thronged like a market, callers coming in wave after wave, people of every kind — his junior disciples kneeling and knocking their heads before him, the rest bowing or making obeisance with clasped hands. Those who came by car generally had to be given three or four "red packets" apiece, bodyguards included. The most went to the patrolmen, who would sometimes come forty or fifty in a troop and cry at the gate, "We've come to wish Mr. Du a happy New Year!" — whereupon someone would ask them to line up outside the main gate, and each would be given a "red packet"; that done, they would call out a "Thank you, Mr. Du" and go. There were petty hoodlums and street toughs, too, who came in droves at the New Year to pay their respects, and each of these was likewise given a "red packet." Usually, before the third day of the New Year, the two thousand packets would be gone, and Tian Hongyuan always had to send over another eight hundred before the demand could be met.

Du had to go out and pay his own New Year calls as well. His first stop was always Huang Jinrong's place at Junpei Lane, his second his "old man" Chen Shichang's; everywhere else he merely sent in a New Year visiting-card.

By a rough reckoning, Du spent tens of thousands of yuan on New Year lucky money alone each year. What the whole year came to, his accountant Huang Guodong might know better; Wan Molin and I once made a rough count of it too, and leaving aside his large outlays on hospitality and the gifts he sent the provincial warlords and the like, the running of his mansion alone came to no less than two million yuan a year.

### IV. Where His Money Came From

Du spent money so wildly — where did it all come from? As far as I know, the first source was the opium trade. Back in his days following Huang Jinrong he had already gone into the monopoly purveying and transport of opium, and had opened an opium shop. Later he joined with Huang Jinrong, Zhang Xiaolin, and others to open the "Sanxin Company" (Shanghai's great monopolistic opium firm), which held the monopoly on the purveying and transport of the opium and other narcotics that the warlords of the southwest, the northwest, the northeast, and abroad shipped into Shanghai. The narcotics the provincial warlords sent to Shanghai to sell yielded great profits, and the Sanxin Company took a large cut of them — sometimes at three-sevenths, sometimes at four-sixths. Tang Yulin of Rehe, for instance, had opium worth several million yuan shipped to Shanghai every year, all of it sold on his behalf by Du Yuesheng. All told, the Sanxin Company's yearly income ran to tens of millions of yuan. This money was divided by "three big shares, six middle shares, eight small shares": the three big shares were Du Yuesheng, Huang Jinrong, and Zhang Xiaolin; the six middle shares were Gu Jiatang, Jin Tingsun, Ye Zhuoshan, Rui Qingrong, and the like; and the eight small shares were several of Du's and Huang's chief disciples. Beyond this, a large part had of course to be shared out to the foreigners of the French Concession Municipal Council and the International Settlement Municipal Council, and a part to the officials of Shanghai; and what was left over still had to keep their "sure-footed" junior disciples, the newspapermen who were in the gang, and so on.

The next source was the money from running gambling houses. For a time the Shanghai French Concession was full of gambling houses — first at Shenji Lane on Rue Montauban (today's Xizang South Road), and later at No. 181 Avenue Foch (today's Yan'an Middle Road), which was then the largest gambling house in Shanghai; Gu Jiatang, one of Du's chief lieutenants, stood guard over the floor, while Jiang Zhaoming and others acted as managers. I went to that gambling house a good many times. Inside there was Western food, Chinese food, and pastries, everything one could want, all free of charge, and cigarettes, opium, fruit, and fine Chinese and Western wines supplied gratis besides. Those who came by car were given five yuan for the chauffeur and five gallons of petrol free for each car. All this was "bait," to make you part with your money willingly. The chief games inside were roulette, fantan, and pai gow, and the stakes ran high; there was a pawnshop on the premises, so that when a man had gambled away his ready cash he could pledge his gold ornaments, his watch, and the like on the spot, and after that strip off his very clothes, layer by layer, and pledge them too. I saw not a few people there leave cleaned out to the skin. To get away once you had won was no easy matter; and to go merely to eat for free without gambling would not do either, for the place was full of red-eyebrowed, green-eyed men keeping watch on you. I was the exception, for they all knew I was of the Du mansion and had come only to skim a little off the top.

Next after this came the taking on of lawsuits and extortion by kidnapping. Of this I know very little, but there is no doubt that Du raked in a great deal of money that way.

Later Du became chairman of the board of the Commercial Bank, and opened the Zhonghui Bank of his own, whose capital was chiefly the money skimmed off the opium. He reached out, step by step, into shipping, into the flour trade, into the cotton mills, and became one of the "magnates" of the commercial and financial worlds, so that his income grew greater still. As far as I know, after the victory in the war he held the title of chairman or director of fifty-six enterprises, factories, and bodies, to say nothing of the standing directorships, directorships, and trusteeships beyond counting; a chairmanship brought, on average, at least a thousand yuan in salary or carriage-money, and there were dividends on his sleeping shares and other incomes besides — reckon it up, and he was taking in a hundred or two hundred thousand yuan a month.

Now and then he would do a "good deed" as well. In summer, for instance, he would give out free bottles of "Shidezhi" heat-rash lotion, and through charitable bodies he doled out coffins. Besides this, at his own gate he would give a little charity to some of those who came begging his help; but everyone who came begging had first to be looked into and questioned by Wan Molin and me to make sure his story was true, and was then given a card, on the strength of which he could draw a few dollars at a native bank Du named, or fetch a padded jacket and trousers or a padded quilt from the Xierenxiang clothing shop on Caishi Street.

Du Yuesheng's money came easily and went easily. Each time he was to go out on some social round, Tian Hongyuan would first stuff a wad of bank drafts into his pocket. If, say, he went to dine at the Cathay Hotel in Sassoon's building, he would pull out several drafts on leaving and hand them round to the waiters, the elevator boy, the gatekeepers, and the like. A set of officials leaving their posts always had to come and take their leave at Du's; and the moment Du heard that "Director So-and-so has come to see you," he would pick a draft from the drawer and, tucking it up his sleeve, go to receive the man — ten thousand yuan at the most, three or five thousand at the least, according to the caller's repute and standing and the depth of his friendship with Du — and, wrapping his words gently, present it as travel-money for the road.

Du Yuesheng had several strongholds, and, counting his kinsmen, friends, and disciples, his monthly outlay was no small thing. Setting aside what his household expenses and the salaries and carriage-money from his fifty-odd chairmanships offset, his yearly outgoings, with all his other uses added in, came to more than four hundred thousand yuan. In Du's heyday, disciples drawing fifty to a hundred yuan a month were past counting. The anti-smuggling battalions of every quarter, and men on public business, all had to share in his bounty. Apart from the two great outlays of opium and gambling, he spent no money foolishly. He kept company, as a banker, with the great merchants and industrialists, but he never bought antiques or curios. Except for the antiques others presented to him, which the accounts-room hangers-on would appraise for him, the antique dealers could do no business with him — for they knew, in fact, that he was no judge of such things, and sent him nothing good.

### V. His Friends, Disciples, and Retainers

That Du Yuesheng's ties to Chiang Kai-shek were close goes without saying; he had dealings, too, with the other so-called "dignitaries of the party and state" among the Nationalists. Of these, H. H. Kung's ties with him were deep — I remember Kung himself coming to Rue Wagner. He had dealings with T. V. Soong as well; and men like Sun Ke, because they ran banks in Shanghai, did business, and kept women, could not but stay on good terms with Du to gain his protection. Within the Nationalist party, the one closest to Du was Dai Li, and after him Yang Hu and Chen Qun — all of them his sworn brothers. Among the party officials of Shanghai, Wu Kaixian was closest to him; he had first been Du's disciple, it was said, and later returned his pledge-card and became his friend instead; the rest, Pan Gongzhan and the others, were all on intimate terms with Du. The warlords of the various provinces were all in league with Du too, mostly for the sake of a joint hand in the opium trade.

In the days of the Shanghai Local Association, Shi Liangcai came to the Du house almost daily, and the two got on very well. When Shi was assassinated, Du grieved at the news and was cast down for several days. Huang Yanpei, too, often went to Du's in this period.

When Zhang Xueliang and his wife Yu Fengzhi came to Shanghai, Du treated them with the courtesy due to honored guests. Zhang was then sunk in narcotics, his face haggard and his spirits low, and Du generously planned a cure to rid him of his affliction. So, soon after reaching Shanghai, Zhang moved to No. 181 Avenue Foch, where a doctor set apart for the purpose cleansed him of the drug and broke his craving, and after a time he slowly won free of that sea of misery. Afterward Du said of him to others, "Hanqing has resolve."

Du Yuesheng kept seven secretaries at his mansion, and one of them was Yang Du, foremost of the "Six Gentlemen of the Hongxian." His opium craving was heavy, and on coming he would always stretch out on Du's smoking-couch for me to cook his pipes; Du gave him a thousand yuan a month. He did not come every day — Du sent for him only when there was some weighty matter to talk over.

Among the others who served as his secretaries down the years, one was a man of Wuxi who had been chief of the third section under Xu Guoliang, the assassinated commissioner of the Shanghai police — his name I have forgotten. Another was a man surnamed Chen who had been headmaster of the Zhengshi Middle School. Guo Lanshi was a secretary of his too, and entered the Du establishment around the time of the war.

Du was originally illiterate, and though he later took to practicing his characters a little at home, he wrote very poorly. His dealings with the foreigners of the French Concession Municipal Council and the International Settlement Municipal Council were close, and were managed chiefly through the good offices of Zhang Yishu, who all along served as a Chinese councillor of the French Concession Council; every dealing between Du and the French Concession authorities passed through his hands, so that he stood high in the Du mansion. As for Du's meetings with Frenchmen, the interpreting was done by Li Yingsheng (a Cantonese), who kept a jewelry shop on the Rue du Consulat (today's East Jinling Road).

Among his retainers, besides those already named, there was a certain Gu Ao, a "meritorious servant of the Hongxian," whom everyone in the mansion called Secretary Gu; he too came seldom, and every time he came he would smoke a great fill of opium — Du gave him several hundred yuan a month. Zhang Shizhao, too, was a friend Du respected, and came and went often.

Chen Shichang, Du's "old man," came to Rue Wagner very rarely, and when he did he would leave as soon as he arrived. After the victory he went a few times to the Eighteen-Story Building. A son of his had once been an assistant manager at the Zhonghui Bank, and afterward took over a small native bank and lost the whole of it. Chen Shichang brought the son and hurried to Du's by night, and, recounting how it had come about, pointed at the boy and said, "This little devil was doing very well at the Zhonghui, and then, on a sudden whim, went and started that cursed native bank, and lost the lot clean." Then he asked Du for a loan of 25,000 yuan. Du said, without turning a hair, "It shall be sent over in full tomorrow." Little did he know that Chen's son was an incorrigible wastrel, who in five days had squandered the whole of that sum too, and then went to Du to borrow another 20,000. That cut off the road for good; Chen no longer came to the door, and was fairly worried to death by his son. This was after the victory.

Du's men may be divided, roughly, into two camps. One was the old camp — Gu Jiatang, Ye Zhuoshan, Jin Tingsun, and the rest — who were not his disciples but might be called his underlings, and it was they who did all the opium and gambling, the kidnapping, the brawling, and the killing. Besides these there were Rui Qingrong, Gao Xinbao, and others, who were his strong-arm men. Gao's son was made over to Du as a godson and was on fairly close terms with him.

During the occupation of Shanghai, not a few of Du's junior disciples turned collaborators, and after the victory Du got most of them cleared of the traitor's charge through his connection with Dai Li; only a few, such as Doctor Xie, who had probably failed to keep up their ties with Wan Molin and the others during the occupation, went uncleared after the victory. One day, when Du and I were both in the accounts room where he smoked, and Du was bent over the couch practicing his characters, Xie Baosheng slipped quietly in and called out, "Mr. Du." Before he could get out what came next, Du raised his head, gave him a glance, and said coldly, "You've made your fortune now — what do you come to see me for!" Seeing how the wind lay, Xie slipped away. Under the enemy and the puppet regime, Xie had been chief of the puppet Suzhou police, and had scraped together a good deal of money; he had a house on Xinwen Road in Shanghai, and had opened the Xinwen Hotel, the Carter Bath, the Ciro's dance hall, and the like.

Du's other camp was of formal disciples, the claws and teeth of his political and economic doings, men like Lu Jingshi. After the war began, Lu became Du's most favored disciple, and was besides a mainstay of the Nationalist party and a boss of the yellow labor unions. There were also Luo Qinghua, Tang Jiaozhi, and others, who were the feelers by which Du reached into the commercial and industrial world.

Among the notables and men of mark in Shanghai, those closest to Du were Qian Xinzhi and Huang Renzhi; and Shi Liangcai, before he was assassinated, had been a good friend of Du's as well. Yu Qiaqing came to Rue Wagner from time to time, chiefly to exchange bank drafts with Du — that is, to swap his own long-dated or uncovered checks for near-dated drafts of Du's to meet a pinch. Wang Xiaolai was a regular caller at the Du mansion, and Du gave him 1,600 yuan a month; Wang served besides as general manager of the fish market and manager of the Jiangsu Long-Distance Bus Company, both posts arranged by Du to provide for this old friend and old partner. Even the so-called "three elders" among the traitors — Wen Lanting, Lin Kanghou, and Yuan Lüdeng — were all close friends of Du's.

### VI. Du's Character and Tastes

After Du Yuesheng opened his bank, he spoke in soft, gentle tones, mild and refined, neither haughty nor servile, doing all he could to strike the pose of a "superior Chinese." Someone joked that he had just come out of the "cold-air room." That he had reached such perfect polish, one may judge from this.

To my mind, Du's knack for handling people did not begin only after he grew rich. In his days loafing about the Jinliyuan wharf, whenever the company had no money to eat, so long as he had a twenty-cent piece on him he would pull out the whole of it and give it to you, saying, "Take it and get yourself a meal; pay me back when you've the money!" — and sometimes he would go hungry a whole night himself for it. Huang Jinrong was of a different stamp from Du Yuesheng: short-sighted, and not open-handed. Let any disciple make him a present of a hundred-odd dollars to use, and he would feel very pleased and look on the man with special favor.

On ordinary days, when he was not laid up ill, Du Yuesheng's temper was good enough, and he neither struck nor cursed those about him. When there were no outside guests, the three of us — he, Wan Molin, and I — would talk of everything under the sun, rambling on at random. Du's affairs were on a grand scale and his acquaintance wide, and from morning to night he was kept extremely busy with his social rounds. Some said he kept a lacquered notice-board by his smoking-couch, listing the households he was to call on that day. In fact no such board was hung up; each day the accounts room would sort into a queue the invitation cards that had come in, ranking them by the host's standing and the face owed, in order of weight and precedence, draw them into a list, and, once Du had looked it over, tell the drivers, bodyguards, and the rest to act on it as it stood.

I saw with my own eyes how Du received a caller: first he would incline his ear and listen to what the other had to say, and only when the man had finished would he ponder a moment, then open his mouth and say a few words, slowly. The art of it was to say much in little, striking to the very point, so that the listener was moved. Some said inspiration came to him at the touch of Heaven; in truth it was a method of his for dealing with men. Du had been utterly unlettered to begin with, but after he became chairman of fifty-six firms and factories he practiced the two characters of his own name, "Du Yong," morning and night, until, sure enough, he wrote them stroke by solid stroke; and after a while, having signed his name so many times, he grew all the more practiced, so that there was even something bold and stirring in the flourish of it.

As a rule Du's constitution was sturdy, and he seldom took injections or medicine. Whenever he was ill he would have a doctor surnamed Chen feel his pulse. During the war, when Du was in Sichuan, he always saw a Chinese doctor when ill. Once he had a pain in the belly, appendicitis most likely, and called in an old Chinese doctor of fifty-odd to treat him; the doctor used no knife but prescribed to the symptom, and a single dose cured it. From then on Du trusted Chinese medicine all the more. While Du was living in the Eighteen-Story Building, H. H. Kung came sometimes, and, seeing Du unwell, showed the deepest concern, saying, "Whatever medicine you want, I have it all at my place."

Du Yuesheng took little interest in dancing, unlike Yu Qiaqing, Wang Xiaolai, Zhang Xiaolin, and the rest, who never tired of it. I remember the first time Zhang Xiaolin went into a dance hall to dance: the floor was very slippery, and, in his long robe, he lost his footing and took a tumble, at which Du and the others burst out laughing. The Lido Garden, where Du often went, he went to mostly for dinners and social rounds; as for dancing, it was no common thing with him.

Du was an opera amateur and loved to sing, being especially fond of such roles as Zhao Yun in Yellow Crane Tower and Huang Tianba in Luoma Lake. When there were dress rehearsals at the opera clubs around the Zhengjia Wooden Bridge, he would always have a hand in and show his face. When there was a benefit performance for relief and someone came to ask him to take the stage, he would not refuse if he was in the mood. Once, when Wang Binyan of Subei asked Du to help out at a charity benefit, the tickets went for as much as fifty yuan apiece. An old gentleman said to me, "Fifty yuan to see Du Yuesheng sing is not dear at all, really. His singing, to a professional eye, is of course nothing much; but his costume is splendid, and that turn of acting and singing he gives on the stage is enough to split your sides with laughing — worth a good deal more than fifty yuan!"

Du died in Hong Kong; of the circumstances at the time I know little. As Du lay dying, Meng Xiaodong, striving to secure her standing as his wife, had Du's children call her "Madame" (English for "mother"). The instant Du breathed his last, no one would put up any money, and in the end they had to wire America, from which US$300,000 was remitted; so each widow got US$50,000. What Du suffered was a stroke. On the afternoon it struck him he was still talking with the family at one o'clock; by two his hands and feet had gone suddenly numb and he could not open his mouth to speak. A doctor was hastily called to save him, but was told it was of no use; they did what human effort could, put in some medicine, and on the sixteenth of August, 1951, he died.

For more than twenty years I came and went at the Du house, morning and evening at Du's side for a long stretch of years; what I knew of his affairs is roughly this. Much of it I do not remember in full, and there may be things I have set down wrong; I hope the gentlemen who once knew Du well will freely set me right.
