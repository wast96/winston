### 1. The Ministry of Justice Case: Twenty Years of Bearing Disgrace
After the founding of New China, under the leadership of the Chinese Communist Party, the whole country carried through land reform and the socialist transformation of the private ownership of the means of production, and launched the Three-Antis, the Five-Antis, the Campaign to Suppress Counterrevolutionaries, and the War to Resist America and Aid Korea. Marked gains were made on every front, the system of exploitation was abolished, and the task of socialist transformation was in the main complete.
In these conditions a certain number of Communist Party cadres began to grow complacent and to nurse a taste for privilege. To meet this, in April 1957 the Party Center decided to open a broad and searching Rectification Movement throughout the whole Party. The masses answered the Party's call and offered a great deal of useful criticism of the way Party and government worked and of the thinking and conduct of Party members and cadres. But in the course of rectification a tiny handful of bourgeois Rightists, under the banner of helping the Communist Party rectify itself, seized the chance to attack the Party and the newborn socialist system, hoping to supplant the Communist Party's leadership and overturn that system. The Party Center issued a directive, and an Anti-Rightist Campaign was carried out across the country. For the sake of stabilizing the newly built socialist system and holding to the socialist road this was wholly necessary; but in the methods of the campaign a grave error of over-extension was committed.
After the Anti-Rightist Campaign, from the winter of 1957 on, industrial and agricultural production entered a new upsurge. But because the Party erred in its guiding line for economic construction, "Left" thinking gained ground. Mao Zedong and the Party Center did try to lead the whole Party in correcting the errors they had noticed, yet "Left" thinking kept rising and spreading.
In June 1958 the Center convened the Fourth National Conference on Political and Legal Work. It met when "Left" thinking was in full flood.
At this conference the Ministry of Justice and the Ministry of Public Security took different views on how to weigh the achievements and errors of the Campaign to Suppress Counterrevolutionaries. The State Council's Political and Legal Affairs Office therefore set the delegates from the Ministry of Public Security, the Ministry of Justice, the courts, and the procuratorates upon nine comrades of the Ministry of Justice: Zheng Shaowen, secretary of the ministry's Party group and vice-minister; Chen Yangshan, deputy secretary of the Party group and vice-minister; Wang Huai'an, Party group member and assistant minister; Wang Ruqi, Party group member and director of the Notary and Lawyers Bureau; Wang Yuechen, Party group member and director of the General Office; Liu Shangzhi, Party group member and assistant minister; Tang Jinshi, director of the Personnel Bureau; Song Zicheng, director of the Education Bureau; and Luo Zhiguang, deputy director of the General Office. They were harshly denounced. Their speeches, talks, reports, and instructions given at meetings up and down the country were gathered up, then lopped top and tail and quoted out of context; by the methods of "great democracy," of speaking out freely and airing views in the open, everything was raised to the level of principle. The Ministry of Justice Party group was charged with "opposing the dictatorship of the proletariat; defying the Party Center's line for building the judicial system; opposing the leadership of local Party committees over judicial work; and shielding the Rightist Wang Huai'an" — a political error. The material was worked up and sent to the Central Political-Legal Group with a recommendation of severe punishment.
With the backing of the Central Political-Legal Group, the Ministry of Justice Party group was labeled an "anti-Party clique." Zheng Shaowen, Chen Yangshan, Liu Shangzhi, Wang Yuechen, Wang Ruqi, Tang Jinshi, Song Zicheng, and Luo Zhiguang were all stripped of every post inside and outside the Party, demoted, and given Party disciplinary penalties; Wang Huai'an was branded a Rightist and expelled from the Party.
Dong Biwu, then president of the Supreme People's Court, took a different view of how the matter was handled. He held that of the many problems brought to light, some were matters of understanding and some were errors of work, and that to define them as an "anti-Party clique" was improper. But the Central Political-Legal Group insisted on the label. When the case was referred up, the Center approved the finding of an "anti-Party clique," and the Ministry of Justice was abolished.
Yet the facts proved that of these four charges forced upon the Ministry of Justice Party group, some had no basis whatever and some stood truth on its head. For instance, in the spring of 1957, after Chairman Mao's "On the Correct Handling of Contradictions Among the People" was issued, the Ministry of Justice Party group had said: "The scope of contradictions among the people has widened. A great many ordinary criminal offenses are still contradictions among the people in character; not every criminal offense is a contradiction between the enemy and ourselves." This view was plainly correct, yet it was ruled to be "opposing the dictatorship of the proletariat and defying the line of the Party Center." Again, the Organic Law of the People's Courts provided: "In trying cases the courts exercise independent judgment and are subject only to the law; where a local Party committee disagrees with a verdict, it may apply to a higher level for review." But in practice the Party committees of some provinces broke this rule and interfered at will with the courts' independent handling of cases. When the Ministry of Justice Party group moved to stop this, it was plainly upholding and carrying out the Party Center's line for building the judicial system, in keeping both with the law and with the Party's organizational principles; yet this too was ruled to be "opposing the leadership of local Party committees over judicial work," and made one of the group's "crimes."
It is plain that the Central Political-Legal Group's grounds for calling the Ministry of Justice Party group an "anti-Party clique" had no basis in fact and could not stand at all. But the whole Party was then waging its struggle against "Right deviationism," "Left" thinking was rampant, and so the group's case was gravely over-extended, and nine cadres loyal to the Party's cause who had done real work for it, Chen Yangshan among them, were wrongly labeled an "anti-Party clique." Chen Yangshan was stripped of every post inside and outside the Party, demoted three grades, and placed on one year's probation within the Party, and was sent down to be deputy manager of the Qinghe Woolen Mill in Beijing.
In April 1959, once the penalty was announced, Chen Yangshan's standing in politics and in daily life changed overnight: his secretary and driver were transferred away, his car was withdrawn, and when he went in to work he was met with cold indifference and no one would deal with him. Chen Yangshan was an old Party member, and in the face of all this he did not utter a word of complaint; he handed back his housing and furniture of his own accord and moved out to live and work at the Qinghe Woolen Mill, in the town of Qinghe in Changping County.
When Chen Yangshan was punished, his family was drawn in too. His youngest son, Chen Zhenyu, was still a boy, and could not bear to see his father struck by so sudden a blow and treated as an outcast; his nerves gave way and he fell into mental illness. More than fifty years on he has still not recovered: it ruined his whole future and marred his life for good.
When the Ministry of Justice Party group "anti-Party clique" case was passed down among cadres of provincial and army rank across the country, many who had worked with Chen Yangshan and knew him were dismayed. They did not believe he could turn against the Party, and many wanted to speak up for him. But he himself was large-minded and took it calmly, and never voiced a grievance. Beyond lodging an appeal with the organization by the proper procedure, he did nothing that broke the organization's rules. It showed his high character and revolutionary integrity: he did not let his merits and his wrongs war within him, but bore it with the breadth of spirit of an old Communist.
In 1960, once the year's probation was lifted, the Central Organization Department, on Li Kenong's proposal, transferred Chen Yangshan to the Central Investigation Department. But when the work was assigned, he met with contempt and obstruction from the department's acting head. Knowing full well that Chen Yangshan's strength was his command of intelligence work, the man assigned him to office administration instead. Chen Yangshan said he knew nothing of office administration and would surely make a poor job of it, and asked the organization to think again; but the man would not hear him. Only when Li Kenong learned of it and overturned the wrong decision was Chen Yangshan given the work of training intelligence officers.
Both Chen Geng and Li Kenong cared greatly about setting down the history of our Party's intelligence struggle. In July 1961 Li Kenong wrote a report to Deng Xiaoping, General Secretary of the Party Center, and to Yang Shangkun, director of the General Office of the Central Committee, proposing that a team be organized to investigate, gather, sort, and compile into a volume the history of the struggle of the Central Special Branch under the Party's Central Special Committee. The aim was that the nameless heroes who had given their lives in that struggle should rest in peace, and that those still living, the widowed and the orphaned and the solitary, the old and the young alike, should each find their place. He proposed that Chen Yangshan be drawn in to take charge of the work.
Li Kenong's report was reviewed by Zhou Enlai, Deng Xiaoping, and Peng Zhen and approved. Li Kenong at once formed the team and named Chen Yangshan head of the group investigating the historical materials of the Central Special Branch.
Li Kenong proposed Chen Yangshan for the task because Chen Yangshan was one of the Special Branch's longer-serving members, who had worked in it from its founding to its close. He knew this stretch of history and had made a singular contribution to it: the first counter-intelligence connection the Central Special Branch ever established, Bao Junfu, had been brought in through him. Li Kenong trusted Chen Yangshan absolutely, and now, in setting down the history of the intelligence struggle, put him in charge.
Chen Yangshan had long meant to write the history of the Central Special Branch's work. Chen Geng, before he died, had charged him to supply the historical materials of the intelligence struggle to those studying Party history. But at the time Chen Yangshan was under slander and heavy blows, his position was straitened, and it was not easy to act on the plan. That Li Kenong should now assign him the very task was a happy chance, and he took it up gladly and set to writing the history of the Special Branch's struggle with a will.
On August 28, 1961, led by Li Kenong himself, Chen Yangshan, Pan Fang, and the rest went by train from Beijing to Shanghai to investigate and gather materials. The trip was short, only a little over two months, but the yield was not small: a good deal of very precious historical material was collected.
Back in Beijing, Li Kenong told Chen Yangshan to press on with the sorting and writing. With deep reverence and boundless attachment for his comrades-in-arms, Chen Yangshan turned it over in his mind again and again, and worked through the gathered materials heedless of sleep or food. It showed how wholly he answered for the historical record and how wholly he kept faith with what his old comrade had asked of him.
On February 7, 1962, Li Kenong sent for Chen Yangshan to ask how the work was coming, and gave him many detailed instructions. Two days later, on February 9, Li Kenong died suddenly. Chen Yangshan was stricken with grief. To honor Li Kenong's memory he wrote on night and day, and over three years, at the cost of his very heart's blood, produced the Draft History of the Central Special Branch, more than a hundred thousand words, along with biographical materials on a number of martyrs. It was a sorrow that neither Chen Geng nor Li Kenong lived to see this precious record of the intelligence struggle. That Chen Yangshan, under grave slander and heavy blows, should bear his disgrace, work for the Party with such diligence, and see the history of the Special Branch through with such devotion, won him the deep respect of his colleagues.
### 2. Ten Years in Ningxia: True Colors in a Chaotic Age
From mid-January to early February 1962, the Party Center held an enlarged Central Work Conference in Beijing, also known as the Seven Thousand Cadres Conference. It made a first summing-up of the basic lessons of socialist construction since 1958 and analyzed the chief shortcomings and errors of the past few years. Mao Zedong gave an important speech, stressing above all that the Party's democratic centralism must be made sound. He called for restoring the Party's fine ways of seeking truth from facts and following the mass line, for a healthy inner-Party democratic life, and for stronger central unity.
At the end of that April the Party Center, acting on the spirit of the enlarged Central Work Conference, issued its "Circular on Speeding Up the Review of Party Members and Cadres." Those wrongly criticized and punished in the struggle against "Right deviationism" then had their cases reviewed and their verdicts reversed.
The Ministry of Justice Party group "anti-Party clique" case, approved by the Center in 1959, was among those taken up for review. On examination the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection found that the nine leading members of the Ministry of Justice had made mistakes in their work but had committed no acts against the Party, that to define them as an "anti-Party clique" was wrong in character, and that the verdict should be quashed. But the Central Political-Legal Group would not agree to change the label; it agreed only to lift part of the penalties. Referred to the Central Secretariat, the case drew the ruling that the original finding would stand for the present, though the penalties might be suitably lifted.
On the strength of that ruling, in October 1962 Chen Yangshan was made deputy dean of a branch campus of the College of Foreign Affairs. In February 1966 the Central Organization Department transferred him again, to be a vice-chairman of the government of the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region and to sit in on the meetings of the regional Party committee's standing committee.
Chen Yangshan was a man of strong Party spirit, who always obeyed the organization's decisions without reserve. Though he knew he had severe emphysema and chronic bronchitis and that his body would not take to Ningxia's climate or way of life, he said not a word against it, and after the Spring Festival went off to take up the post. He was by then an old man of sixty, in poor health and in need of care, but he took no family with him and went to Ningxia alone.
Chen Yangshan reached Yinchuan in the middle of February 1966 and took up his post as a vice-chairman of the Ningxia government. In May the Great Cultural Revolution began. The rebels were forever coming to Party and government offices to make trouble, and much work could not go on as it should. Once, while the regional government was in session, the rebels came to disrupt it; Chen Yangshan, beside himself with anger, sharply rebuked them for it. This so provoked them that he was hauled up for struggle, and became the first of the region's leaders to be attacked. It was his good fortune that he had only just been posted to Ningxia; the rebels knew nothing of his past, and after a few sessions the matter petered out.
In January 1967, under the sway of the Shanghai rebels' seizure of power, the Ningxia rebels on the 27th seized power from the regional Party committee and government; the leading cadres were all dismissed and made to register with the seizure organs. But the rebels had no capacity to run Party and government work, and within two or three days Ningxia fell into utter chaos.
In view of this, in February the Party Center decided that the Ningxia Military District should step forward and set up a "Production Command" to lead Party and government work throughout the region, and named Chen Yangshan to sit on it as a representative of the veteran cadres. At the end of July the rebels wrecked the "Production Command" too and carried off those they called "royalists." By this time every province in the country was preparing to form a revolutionary committee. The Ningxia "Production Command" gathered the representatives of the various mass organizations and took them to Beijing for a study class.
After more than three months of argument, a preparatory group for the revolutionary committee of the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region was formed, with Kang Jianmin as its head. Chen Yangshan was chosen as a member. Before long the revolutionary committee was set up, and Chen Yangshan was elected to its standing committee and later raised to deputy chairman.
In May 1968 Premier Zhou Enlai brought Chen Yangshan to Beijing to join special-case work, assigning him with Li Qiang to examine Bao Junfu's history and to press Bao for an account of how comrades of our Party had been held in the Nationalists' Nanjing Reflection Institute.
Between 1928 and 1931 Bao Junfu had done many useful things for our Party. But after 1932 he went over to the Nationalists and served for a time as head of a Reflection Institute; during the War of Resistance he went over again, to Wang Jingwei, and became a collaborator, a man with a counter-revolutionary past. Chen Yangshan and Li Qiang knew Bao's history, and so Zhou Enlai put the two of them in charge of his case. In January 1970 Bao Junfu died suddenly of a cerebral hemorrhage, and the case was closed. About this time Chen Yangshan fell ill with colon cancer; weak after his operation, he was allowed by Premier Zhou to convalesce in Beijing.
In 1972, recovered, Chen Yangshan returned to work in Ningxia. The Party committee of the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region had by then been established, and he was elected to its standing committee, with charge of culture and education, science, sports, and public health.
Chen Yangshan played an important part in advancing the region's culture and education, science and technology, sports, and public health, and the state of these fields improved under his hand.
In January 1977 Kang Jianmin died, and Huo Shilian succeeded him as first secretary. By this time the Gang of Four had been smashed, and a campaign to expose and criticize them was under way across the country. Chen Yangshan, Ma Xin, Ding Yimin, and others played an important part in rousing the masses to expose and criticize the Gang of Four and in grasping revolution to promote production.
Throughout his years in Ningxia, Chen Yangshan often went down to the grass roots to investigate; it was his lifelong habit. He not only looked into how things stood at that level but did all he could to help settle real problems there. Cadres and masses alike held this way of working in deep regard, and he stood high in the people's esteem; everyone fondly called him "the good old man." In 1974 and again in 1978 the people twice elected him a deputy to the National People's Congress. In October 1978, when Ningxia marked the twentieth anniversary of the region's founding, he came back at their invitation to take part, and cadres and masses gave him a warm welcome.
### 3. Full Rehabilitation: At Last the Sun Rises over the Deep Mountains
After the Great Cultural Revolution the Party's work shifted its center of gravity to economic construction. To make that shift go smoothly, cadre policy had first to be put right: unjust, false, and mistaken cases had to be thoroughly reversed, problems left over from the past resolved, and veteran cadres restored freely to their posts and their good name.
In this setting, in the spring of 1978, the Central Organization Department, the Ministry of Public Security, and the Supreme People's Court, following the instructions of the central leadership, reexamined the Ministry of Justice Party group "anti-Party clique" case. Their finding read: "The reexamination shows that the Ministry of Justice Party group did not in fact oppose the Party's line or policies. But in the struggle against the enemy and on the question of Party leadership there was a grave error of Right deviation, in neglecting the Party's unified leadership." They recommended quashing the original "anti-Party clique" verdict and the penalties imposed. Referred to the Center, the reexamination report was approved.
Chen Yangshan was accordingly restored to his former grade, transferred out of the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, and called back to Beijing for a posting. In the summer of 1978 he was made a deputy procurator-general of the Supreme People's Procuratorate and a member of its Party group. Twenty years of wrong were set right, and Chen Yangshan was overjoyed. He threw himself heart and soul into building up the procuratorial cause; heedless of age and frailty, he kept full working days, looking carefully into and settling the problems that arose in staffing and work as the procuratorial organs were rebuilt, and made an outstanding contribution to their recovery and growth.
The Sixth Plenary Session of the Tenth Central Committee, held in June 1981, adopted the "Resolution on Certain Questions in the History of Our Party Since the Founding of the People's Republic." In light of it, since the 1978 reexamination of the Ministry of Justice Party group "anti-Party clique" case had come before the Third Plenary Session of the Eleventh Central Committee, when the "Left" guiding line had not yet been cleared away, it was held that although the Party group had committed no act against the Party, a "grave error of Right deviation" remained.
On the spirit of the Sixth Plenary Session of the Tenth Central Committee, in 1983 the Central Organization Department, the Ministry of Public Security, and the Supreme People's Court reexamined the Ministry of Justice Party group "anti-Party clique" case once more. Their finding was that before 1959, in criticizing old legal thinking, setting up a system of lawyers, spot-checking mistaken cases, carrying out the Organic Law of the People's Courts, and carrying out the educational line for the political and legal colleges, the Ministry of Justice Party group had followed the line, principles, and policies of the Center. In particular, from 1956 on, it had fully supported and firmly carried out the assessment of the domestic situation made by the Eighth National Congress and the line of strengthening socialist legality, and no fact of opposing the Party's line or policies could be found. The 1978 reexamination report had rightly quashed the original "anti-Party clique" verdict and penalties, but its retention of a "grave error of Right deviation" had no basis and should be negated.
Referred to the Center, approval was given to quash the "Report on the Anti-Party Clique in the Ministry of Justice Party Group," approved by the Center in 1959, and the decision to punish the nine comrades, Zheng Shaowen and Chen Yangshan among them.
With this, the Ministry of Justice Party group "anti-Party clique" case, a wrong borne for a full twenty years, was at last thoroughly reversed.
That the case could be thoroughly reversed showed that the line, principles, and policies of the Party since the Third Plenary Session of the Eleventh Central Committee were wholly correct. It proved once more that the Chinese Communist Party is indeed a great, glorious, and correct Party, one that dares to own its errors openly and set them right. This is a sign of the Party's strength, and one of the marks of a cause that can flourish and thrive.
### 4. Giving His Remaining Warmth: Devoted to the People to the Last
In the spring of 1980 Chen Yangshan fell ill with stomach cancer. He was seventy-four by then, and weak after the operation, no longer able to keep full working days. For a man of his strong sense of duty and of purpose, this was a great distress. Chen Yangshan's Party spirit was strong; he could not abide drawing a high salary and not going in to work, living in ease at home; he could not abide holding a high post entrusted him by the Party and the people and doing nothing for the people. So of his own accord he wrote a report asking to resign the office of deputy procurator-general and retire, saying that even in retirement he would still do what he could for the Party.
But once the Center had granted his request, he went on as if still in office, going in every day to read documents and ask how things stood, and attending meetings and major public occasions as before. Each day he set aside time as well to travel to other provinces and cities to look into things and investigate.
Chen Yangshan was diligent in study. In daily life he had no indulgences; apart from work there was study. He rose at seven each day, and it was either work or study, day in and year out, Sundays and holidays no different. To the end of his life he pored tirelessly over Marxist-Leninist theory and central documents, or wrote articles and memoirs, and never went idly about letting the time run to waste.
At the end of 1985 his wife, Zhang Suzhen, who had been at his side for fifty-eight years, died of stomach cancer, and he grieved deeply. He himself had by now fallen ill with lung cancer and was in hospital under treatment. He knew his time was short, yet there was much he still meant to do: the charge Chen Geng and Li Kenong had laid on him in their lifetimes, to supply the Party with the historical materials of the intelligence struggle, was not yet fully carried out, and it weighed on him like fire. So the moment his condition eased a little he hurried out of the hospital. Home again, he wrote up the historical materials night and day, heedless of sleep or food. Many of his old comrades and his family urged him to take care of himself, but he would not listen, and paid his illness no mind at all. He was set on completing what Chen Geng and Li Kenong had asked of him. He drafted an outline for his memoirs. Because some of the old events had grown hazy to him, he planned, once his eldest son, Chen Jianyu, had retired, to have the son go with him to the places where he had once fought, to interview and then write it all up and offer it to the Ministry of State Security. But in 1989, just as he was about to carry out the plan, his lung cancer worsened; careful treatment was of no avail, and on February 22, 1991, the illness took his life.
That he could not carry out the plan was a great regret. But it is some comfort that the more than ten pieces he wrote in his lifetime did leave the Party's history a store of precious things: "Investigative Leads on Jiang Qing's Defection After Her Arrest," "Exposing Kang Sheng's Crime of Murdering Party Cadres in the Soviet Union in the 1930s," "Comrade Yun Daiying Was the Man Who Led Me to Revolution," "Security Work in the Early Days of the Party," "Some Facts About Our Party's Underground Organizations," "On the Central Special Branch," "Some Facts About the Closing of the Central Special Branch," "Recollections of the New Sichuan News Agency," "Intelligence Work in the War of Resistance," "A Brief Account of Intelligence Work in the Jin-Sui Border Region," "Comrade Li Kenong Was an Outstanding Leader on Our Party's Intelligence Front," "A Few Views on Training Judicial Cadres," and "My Life."
In the early days of the War of Liberation Chen Yangshan had held leading posts in the intelligence and public-security organs of the Jin-Sui Border Region. To sum up that stretch of history, in the spring of 1986 he organized and led the comrades who had done intelligence and public-security work there in the old days — Pei Zhouyu, Jin Zhaodian, Qu Rixin, Feng Jinchen, Zheng Xiaoxian, Gu Yizhi, and the rest — in gathering, sorting, and compiling a volume of more than a million words, the Draft Historical Materials on Intelligence Work in the Jin-Sui Border Region. He also wrote testimonials of history for many intelligence cadres whose cases had not yet been put right. It showed his deep and particular care for the political lives of intelligence cadres.
In 1986 Chen Yangshan also accepted an engagement from the Ministry of State Security to revise the Draft History of the Central Special Branch afresh.
In the autumn of 1987, at the invitation of the Zhejiang provincial departments of state security and public security, Chen Yangshan went to Hangzhou, Ningbo, and elsewhere to lecture on the history of intelligence. Heedless of his years and frailty, he took up the invitation gladly. In each lecture he spoke with ease and relish, and above all when he told the tempestuous, hard-fought episodes — the rescue attempts for Peng Pai and Luo Yinong, the elimination of the traitors Bai Xin, He Jiaxing, and He Zhihua, and the struggle against the great traitor Gu Shunzhang — he was all fire, painting every scene as if he had it at his fingertips. He would speak three or four hours at a stretch each time, unlike a man of eighty and more. This devoted spirit won the admiration and esteem of the younger generation.
While he was lecturing, he heard that the daughter of the martyr Rou Shi was still living and about to take up work in Ninghai County. He asked the comrades looking after him to set aside time to make a special trip to Ninghai to pay homage at Rou Shi's old home and call on his family.
In Rou Shi's old home, Chen Yangshan sat a long while in thought in the room where the martyr had worked, and with tears brimming told the leaders of Ninghai County who had come with him: "The martyr Rou Shi was a man of letters, and a revolutionary too. In 1931 he and Lin Yunan, He Mengxiong, Li Qiushi, and some twenty others, twenty-four comrades in all, went to a meeting in Shanghai. Before it the Central Special Branch had learned that the enemy meant to arrest them at the meeting, and had reported it to the Party Center. But the Center was then in the grip of the followers of Wang Ming's 'Left' line, who were bent on their factional schemes. Because the comrades at the meeting opposed the 'Left' line, they deliberately failed to warn them to take cover, and so all twenty-four were arrested and killed."
He charged the leaders of Ninghai County to see well to the martyr's family, showing his boundless attachment to Rou Shi. When he had paid his homage, Chen Yangshan talked warmly with the family, asking what difficulties they had in their lives and work, and charged the county leaders to help resolve them. On leaving he had a photograph taken with them as a keepsake, showing his deep sympathy for a martyr's family and his kindness toward them.
In August 2005 my colleagues Hu Weihua, Jiang An, Zhou Jianjie, Rong Xuan, and others went to Mengzhou in Henan to call on and interview Wu Huai'e, the younger brother of Wu Hujing. Wu Huai'e, close on ninety, was much moved, and when the talk turned to Chen Yangshan he was more grateful still, and handed us the letter Chen Yangshan had written him in his own hand on January 22, 1983.
In the letter Chen Yangshan said: "We once planned to seek out the families of the martyrs who died working in the Central Special Branch, so that their home districts might know of these comrades who fought bravely for the Party and made glorious contributions. Their revolutionary histories should be spread widely among the people and set down in the annals of the Party and of the county, and honored by the people. Their families and descendants should be treated as martyrs' kin; and where the families are in hardship, the government should give them suitable care."
The letter went on: "Of Comrade Wu Hujing's revolutionary history I am still not clear; I know only of his time in the Central Special Branch. . . . It was probably in the summer or early summer of 1933 that he was made head of the Central Special Branch. The Special Branch of those days was the organization that guarded the Party, gathering all manner of intelligence and suppressing traitors and enemy agents, and it did much important work. Because of Wang Ming's 'Left' opportunist line, errors of the 'Left' were committed in security work too, and by the summer of 1935 the Shanghai Party organizations were broken almost entirely by the enemy, many comrades could no longer survive there, and the Central Special Branch had no choice but to close — this too was the doing of Wang Ming's line. As the leaders of the Shanghai Central Bureau were arrested and turned traitor several times, he could stay in Shanghai no longer, and so probably went to Moscow in the spring or summer of 1935. In the Soviet party purge of 1936, Kang Sheng, with intent, slandered Wu and others as traitors, agents, and Trotskyists, and had them killed. Three other comrades of the Special Branch were killed with him. In exposing Kang Sheng's crimes, it was confirmed that Kang Sheng was the murderer of many comrades, a criminal against the Chinese revolution."
On the investigation of the martyrs' deeds Chen Yangshan wrote further: "Please have your county committee carry the inquiry further among all quarters, so as to reflect his whole history, and be sure to keep to the truth."
After Chen Yangshan reported to the Party Center Kang Sheng's crime of destroying our Party's cadres in the Soviet Union in the 1930s, in June 1988 the families of two of the victims wrote to him, saying that the units concerned would not investigate the martyrs' historical cases.
Chen Yangshan took the letters much to heart. He wrote at once to the Central Organization Department and the Ministry of State Security, proposing that the Center press the departments concerned to investigate the historical cases of the fallen martyrs and reach fair conclusions in keeping with the facts, and that martyrs' families in real hardship be given financial aid. This report of his showed his boundless reverence and attachment for the comrades who had lived and fought so long at his side, Wu Hujing, Xiao Taihuang, He Changzhi, and Ouyang Xin, and his deep care for the martyrs' families. It showed too his exactness in this piece of history and his readiness to speak out for what was right.
In the autumn of 1988, at the request of Liu Fuzhi, the procurator-general, Chen Yangshan went to Shandong Province to look into work. Sparing himself no fatigue and heedless of his years, at every stop he went down to the grass roots to investigate, and held many discussions to hear the views of the working people at that level; afterward he would sit knee to knee with cadres and masses to learn the problems and difficulties in their work and lives.
Back in Beijing, he wrote up the report himself, setting before the Party organization what he had learned and his own views on it, and showing his absolute answerability for the Party's cause.
In the twentieth century a whole host of heroes who fought imperialism and feudalism rose up across the land of China; holding high the banner of truth, they led hundreds of millions in bloody struggle, one falling and the next stepping forward, and wrought a world-shaking work. It was into that storm of blood and hail of gunfire that Chen Yangshan threw himself without hesitation, taking the revolutionary road with resolve, to become the hero of the secret war that later generations praise.
From a shop clerk who believed in national salvation through industry he became a steadfast proletarian revolutionary, along a hard and broken road. His bold search for truth and his diligent study of the Marxist-Leninist classics wrought a deep change in his thinking and step by step built in him a communist view of the world; the tide of the great revolution, the cruel setting of the White Terror, and fierce struggle tempered him and stiffened his resolve to see the revolution through.
For the sake of the people's liberation, through years of unbroken gunfire, he gave every ounce of his strength in valiant struggle; in the spirit of one who is first to bear the world's cares and last to enjoy its pleasures, he kept a high and selfless integrity, held himself always to a strict standard, worked hard, kept his hands clean, and served the people with a whole and loyal heart. He was, in truth, a man of upright spirit who followed the Party and gave his all for the people to the last.
Chen Yangshan's life was indeed a hard one, and yet a glorious one. His leaders, his comrades-in-arms, and his colleagues thought highly of him. Qin Jie and Lian Chengyi once said to me: Chen Yangshan was a model of seeking truth from facts, a model of absolute loyalty to the Party; we will cherish his memory forever and forever hold him up as an example.
After Chen Yangshan died, the People's Daily published an article calling him "an outstanding member of the Chinese Communist Party, a loyal communist fighter tested through long trial, a proletarian revolutionary, and an outstanding leader on our Party's political and legal front." He was worthy of it.
