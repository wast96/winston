## Chapter 6. The Palace Blossom Falls and Is Already Dust

It was a sultry, oppressive afternoon.

Muci had been sent to Instructor Li’s office to fetch the latest issue of Telecommunications Technology, for Yu Xiaojiang wanted him to acquaint himself with all the new techniques in the field. Coming out of the instructors’ room, he made straight for the rear building behind the school parade ground; secluded though it was, it made a good place to read.

At the foot of the rear building behind the parade ground stood a few faded plum trees, looking much decayed in the soft, willowy spring wind. Muci was on his way to read on the round wooden bench where he usually sat, when he saw two lovers, holding each other and weeping bitterly, huddled together on it. He knew them both. The man was called Guo Ziqiong, a temporary recruit from the telecommunications school; the woman was He Yashan, said to be descended from the old Qing loyalists and their heirs. Withdrawn, reserved, and silent by nature, she was known to everyone as “He Gege,” a nod to the former glory of her surname. She had come straight from a modern school to sit the entrance examination, and her aim was simple: to escape the feudal marriage her family had arranged for her, to choose love freely, and to follow Guo Ziqiong here without a backward glance, bent heart and soul on marrying him.

This pair of lovers had long been no secret in the school. From their very first day they had been all but inseparable; students said that in the dead of night the two of them often met in this sparse plum grove, never fearing to be seen, more brazenly sure of themselves than a married couple. The instructors, too, seemed to grant their attachment a tacit consent, and no one came forward to interfere—so it was no wonder Muci should find them here. What was strange was that two people, well and whole, should be weeping in each other’s arms; Muci quite admired them for their leisure of time and their wealth of feeling.

Muci sat down against the wind on the steps of the rear building’s passage. He had no intention of eavesdropping on their talk—yet he would not deny that, with the wind at his back, he was listening to it.

“I feel such anguish, as though I had woken to find myself adrift on a vast ocean, tumbling with the muddy waves, unable to save myself, unable to escape this sea of bitterness. It is truly so far from what I first set out for when I left home; I have all but lost every power to resist. I don’t even know what tomorrow will bring.” He Yashan wept as she poured out the misery in her heart.

“Do you really think you’d be happier married to a man you don’t know than following me?”

“At least there’d be no crisis of survival. Right now I’m terribly afraid… wretched and unsettled day and night.”

“Shan?” Guo Ziqiong was plainly doing all he could to soothe her.

“So that my first-budding love should not become a gift to be handed round among relatives and friends, I came here with you. I thought this was a school to train police officers—I never dreamed it was a school for spies. I don’t want to go into this trade full of terror and blood. What is it we are to learn, in the end? Assassination? Demolition? Conspiracy? I feel I cannot sink any further into this; we cannot go on waiting passively like this. If it goes on, we’ll die—and even if the body doesn’t die, the spirit will.”

“Calm yourself, calm yourself, Shan.”

“I can’t be calm. I’ve no way to be calm any longer. I’m going to have a child!”

“What?” Guo Ziqiong was astonished. “Is it true?”

“Yes. I don’t know how to face this little life. Take me away! Let’s run! China is so vast—where can’t we go? For the child’s sake—no, save the children!”

“Save the children?”

“Qiong! I beg you!” In that moment the fierce fire of He Yashan’s love turned into a boundless strength; she meant to save her own family, to keep this three-person world about to be born forever far from the smoke of battle. She wanted a quiet place, to greet a beautiful new world for the child not yet born.

“Shan. I promise you. For the child, and for you—the first chance we get, we’ll run!”

“Have you a way to do it?”

“The night sentries stand thick as a forest; it’s not easy to chance it, and besides, you’ve a child in your belly. One careless step into a marsh, and you’d be left here to admire the scenery forever. We’ll look for the right moment, and try to get out by day. Once we’ve slipped past the sentries and through the barbed wire, the highway lies just beyond; it’s easy to catch a passing lift by day, and then we can begin our truly happy life.”

The two young people, so full of confidence and longing for happiness and the future, held each other tightly, as though the gates of heaven had already opened to them…

Slip past the sentries? Through the barbed wire? Catch a passing lift? Where was it so easy as that? Muci thought. Still, for the child’s sake, it was worth a desperate try, and he did not oppose their decision to take the risk. For Muci reckoned that they were, after all, only students—children who had seen little of the world—and he believed no one could have the heart to send them to their deaths.

But he was wrong.

In the next morning’s criminal-investigation class, Du Luning expounded on the simplest ways to put a prisoner to the torture. In the park, at the inn, at home, he said, there is always “water” to hand—and as the saying goes, water and fire have no mercy. If you want a prisoner you’ve seized to speak at once, then turn on the “tap” and let the water pour straight from his nostrils into his lungs; the sufferer will be in agony, and many a weak-willed man will submit on the spot. To demonstrate the process of extracting a confession, Du Luning used a student. He picked out Guo Ziqiong! Guo Ziqiong was made to faint dead away on the spot—and fainting away with him, of course, was his sweetheart, He Yashan.

“You must learn to endure the torture!” said Du Luning. “It’s just like swimming in a river: you’ve lost sight of your goal and can’t get to shore for the moment, so it’s no great matter to swallow a few mouthfuls of water. The greatest danger is choking on it!”

Just then Guo Ziqiong, who had fainted, came round again, gasping and coughing without a stop.

“Once water is choked into a man’s lungs, his life is in danger; the coughing throws his breathing into worse disorder, and in bad cases the lungs bleed slowly.” Du Luning stepped up close to Guo Ziqiong and said, almost cruelly: “Student Guo, let’s try once more. This time, I hope you can hold out a little longer…”

No one dared step forward to speak; everyone feared bringing trouble down on himself.

Guo Ziqiong coughed so hard he could scarcely speak. Unable to refuse or resist, he let Du Luning drag him to the edge of the water tank, ready to undergo a second bout of inhuman torment.

“Wait.” Muci walked out from the crowd. “Teacher, let me.” His words were few and to the point; without waiting for Du Luning’s response, he first took off his uniform tunic, tucked in his shirt collar, drew a deep breath, and plunged headfirst into the tank.

The students could not help bursting into applause.

“Once you’ve gone under, don’t panic; you must let it come naturally. A minute or two without breathing won’t harm your organs; you’ll come to no hurt at all. But if it goes on too long…” Du Luning stared fixedly at the tank—one minute, one second; two minutes, ten seconds; three minutes, thirty seconds… Suddenly he reached in and hauled Muci out. Muci broke into a loud fit of coughing. Du Luning said coldly, “If it goes on too long, the water seeps into the lungs, and a man dies!”

With his fellow students’ help Yang Muci got to his feet. He went first to see how Guo Ziqiong was; He Yashan, just come round, was at his side now, and the two of them thanked Muci in low voices. Seeing they were in no great danger, Muci shook the water from his wet hair, picked up his uniform tunic, and walked off without a backward glance.

“Where are you going?” Du Luning asked.

“To get a breath of air,” Muci said loudly.

“What do you mean by that?”

“I mean the foul air of this place is about to suffocate me.” Yang Muci strode out of the classroom in great steps.

For once in his life Du Luning made no response at all, saying only, mildly, “Then let us go on…” In his heart Du Luning admired Muci’s untamed defiance more and more.

At the midday rest Yu Xiaojiang telephoned and called Muci to her office; there were some documents that needed his help to sort. Muci agreed at once, without a word.

Only when Muci took these so-called “documents” from Yu Xiaojiang’s hand did he understand that the “documents” were nothing but private letters written by the students of the class to their families and classmates. Muci felt this a hard thing; they were, after all, other people’s private affairs—why should one pore over them with relish?

“Is there really any need for this?” Muci asked Yu Xiaojiang, whom he took to be a fairly reasonable person. “These are all other people’s private matters; we’ve no right to pry into them.”

“Here there is no privacy.” Yu Xiaojiang gave her instructions without so much as looking up. “I’ve already dealt with some of them; help me sort the rest into categories first.”

“Sort them how?”

“Letters home to parents in one pile, letters to wives and children in another, love letters in another, letters to friends and classmates in another; the mass-produced love letters written to film stars, don’t bother sorting—just tear them up. Understand?”

“All right.”

Muci took out the letters one by one, reading each carefully, and sorting them after he read. All at once he caught a rich breath of perfume; the intoxicating fragrance came from a letter bound for the Hongxia Girls’ Aristocratic School in Shanghai. With a small knife he slit the envelope open carefully along its mouth, the better to restore the letter quickly afterward.

He read a letter full of apology, and at the same time full of hope for the future. The writer was He Yashan, and the letter was to her younger sister, He Yashu. It ran thus…

Yashu, my dear sister,

How are you.

Before we knew it a month has passed since we parted, and I miss so much the days we spent together—carefree, lively, and bright. Though Father lay day and night sunk in the sorrowless, desireless mists of the opium haze, it did not keep you and me from growing up in a quiet and comfortable place. That home, in those days, is what I miss most; I long for the elegance of “sleeping pillowed on a book beneath the high sunlit window,” and I long still more for the romance of “a thousand years of moonlight shining upon my bed.” But little did I dream that these romantic days of youth would slip away as heartlessly as running water. Our parents would actually trade my love as a bargaining chip for a comfortable life; they would actually marry me off into a family wholly strange to me. The bridegroom, they say, is a wanton by nature, keeping serving-girls by force at home and a prostitute besides outside. I could never, never consent to this match! And so I chose to flee the marriage.

When I was at the modern school I came to know young Guo. He is a very kind man, but he is of poor and bitter origins; half the money for his schooling his father earned by working the boats, and half he earned himself by work-study. When he learned of my unhappy plight, he poured out to me his love for me, and for the sake of my own lifelong happiness I chose young Guo. To escape our family’s harassment, Guo and I sat the police-school examination together, hoping that by the school’s special privilege we might escape all our misfortunes. But I forgot that there is still you, my dear sister—you who are helpless and alone. I dare not imagine whether our selfish, self-serving parents might make you take my place and marry in my stead. If it should come to that, I would be a sinner. I can never sit by and watch such a thing happen; I would be tormented and reproached by my conscience all my life. So, my dear sister, you must find some way to drag things out, and wait for me to come back and take you away with me.

Guo and I live very happily here in this school; we already have a little baby soon to be born, and for the child’s sake we have decided to leave the school and go back to Shanghai to work, so that the child may grow up in a quiet and peaceful place. We have set the date of our return for this very month; I believe that in no more than a week we shall see each other.

In the days to come, we can live together, free and happy; I hope that you, Yashu, like me, will look forward to the coming of the light.

What followed was He Yashan’s signature and the date. Little did Yashan dream that a private letter she had already dropped into the post-office box would, at this very moment, find its way back to the school by roundabout ways. Still less did she dream that someone had already opened and read every secret of her heart. Muci thought for about half a minute, then put the letter into a new, unremarkable envelope, took out his own fountain pen, and copied out a fresh address, changing only the form of address for the recipient, to read: To be opened by Miss He Yashu in person. Then he placed the letter among those to wives and children. He hoped that things might truly turn out as He Yashan had written—that they would have a fine future—and hoped, too, that her sister might receive the letter in time, and not sink into the mire of life.

Just as he was bent over his work, he heard Du Luning’s voice.

Du Luning seemed to have just come over from his own office; the moment he entered, he told Xiaojiang to fetch him the “New No. 7 file”—though, seeing Muci rise at the sound, he was rather taken aback all the same.

“Sit.” He waved a hand at Muci, then turned to ask Xiaojiang: “What’s this—too much for you to manage?”

“Yes,” said Xiaojiang. “The students lack amusement in the evenings, so nearly every one of them writes letters—about all manner of things, every kind under the sun. I really can’t keep up; I’m run off my feet.”

“Nearly every one of them writes letters? Yang Muci, have you written any?”

Muci stood at attention and answered: “No!”

“Why is it that everyone else writes letters home, love letters, to while away their loneliness, and you write none—not even a letter home to say you’re safe?”

“Because I have no time to while away loneliness.” Muci seized the chance to vent his discontent. Du Luning did not answer; he picked a letter at random and read it—but read it very carefully, and with much feeling. “‘…I cannot see the color of the sky, cannot see the natural forms of flowers and trees; I am like a colored soap bubble in the air…’ What sort of nonsense is this?” Du Luning set the letter down.

“That is a new-style poem, Teacher. He cannot see the color of the sky because the students are very despondent.” Muci suddenly raised his head and looked Du Luning straight in the eye. “He cannot see the natural forms of flowers and trees because the growth of the flowers and trees has been twisted.”

“And what do the flowers and trees stand for?”

“Love.”

“And what is love?”

“Love is the most precious, most sincere, most ardent, most unadorned feeling of humankind.”

“How long can love last?”

“As long as heaven and earth endure.”

Du Luning burst out laughing. “Sometimes I really can’t make out whether you’re too naive, or too deep.”

“He must be still naive about love, but very deep in his feeling.” Yu Xiaojiang put in, teasingly. Then she handed Du Luning a sealed file, and said: “Chief, here is the material you asked for.”

Du Luning took the file and said: “These letters—especially the letters home to parents, wives and children saying all is well—if they touch on no secrets of the Party or the state, send them off for the writers as soon as you’ve checked them; the rest of the letters and the love letters, destroy them all.” Du Luning walked to the door, then suddenly turned back and said: “Xiaojiang, you said just now they lack amusement?”

“Yes.”

“We ought to adjust the students’ curriculum at once—say, ballroom dancing and music.” Having said this, Du Luning turned to Muci: “This ought to be your strong suit—aren’t you a scion of a distinguished house?” Without waiting for Muci to answer, he turned and went.

Ballroom-dance music soon struck up across the school grounds. It was a bright, sunlit morning, and the bare parade ground became an important place of amusement for the students; the classmates, after their long and cruel training, walked all at once, relaxed in body and mind, into a wonderful world of music, more or less exhilarated. Under the instructors’ guidance their dancing grew more and more correct, their movements more and more practiced. But the instructors shook their heads more often than they nodded, for the students lacked one thing indispensable to dancing—an air of refinement.

Du Luning watched the students dance a long while, and his own hands began to itch; so he asked Yu Xiaojiang to dance a waltz. The students made way on every side, forming a great ring, so that all might have a good angle from which to watch their teachers dance.

Muci noticed that Guo Ziqiong and He Yashan were nowhere to be seen.

The sentries all around were watching the dancing from a distance, and the guard had indeed grown lax; Muci hoped the two of them could get clean away. For the child’s sake, they truly ought to go.

As the dreamlike music rose, the waltz danced by several instructor couples drew every eye; Du Luning and Yu Xiaojiang moved together beautifully, spinning swiftly, lovely as tops, tender as spring vines twining a tree. Whether in the circling of the turns or the smooth advance, their colorful steps kept always to the beat of the music. When the piece ended, applause broke out on every side.

Among the students watching the dance, Muci kept up his usual proud aloofness. In his heart he was reckoning that he ought to do what little he could for that pair of lovers—that is, to draw the guards’ attention away once more, and buy these lovebirds precious time to “escape.” So he deliberately curled his lips a little, putting on an air of disdain. Du Luning noticed this inborn arrogance of his, and came walking toward Muci. “You seem to have quite a distaste for my dancing.”

“Distaste is putting it too strongly. It’s that you, Teacher, don’t quite understand the ‘waltz.’ Your dashing style and forceful, ringing step are better suited to the ‘tango.’”

“So far as I know, the ‘waltz’ too joins the soft and the hard.”

“Just so; as they say: the soft can subdue the hard, and only with softness does the force of the hard come. That is exactly what you, Teacher, lack.”

“Then you come and give everyone a perfect demonstration.”

The gramophone struck up again, pouring out a warm, flowing, moving melody. Yang Muci walked to the middle of the parade ground and asked, very courteously: “May I ask the young ladies—is there any among you who studied at the Hongxia Girls’ School in Shanghai, or at St. Mary’s Girls’ School? If so, please step forward.”

Both were famous schools, the first choice of Shanghai’s aristocratic young ladies, so on the parade ground there was no woman’s voice in answer at all. Just as Muci felt a little disappointed, in the hush of the parade ground one girl suddenly stepped boldly forward of her own accord. “My name is Xin Lili. I am a young lady bred at the Mingchen Girls’ School in Shanghai. I should be glad to be of small service.”

“I could not be more honored.” Muci sent out his invitation gracefully. Though the two of them wore trim uniforms, the aristocratic hauteur that seeped from their every gesture conquered the whole field. In the same instant every eye turned to them in a gaze of salute.

Yang Muci and Xin Lili began to dance to the beautiful, moving music; their lovely reverses, their smooth turns, their supple poses carried everyone off into a realm brimming with the mood of poetry and painting.

Their flawless performance turned the whole broad, empty ground into a flourishing world of flower-scent underfoot and wine-fragrance in the air, and set everyone dreaming.

And in that ravishing, gorgeous moment, a crisp gunshot rang out…

Everyone started at it. The students reacted differently: some cried out, some ran, some looked on. Most scattered of their own accord, to keep themselves safe.

“Who’s firing?” Du Luning bellowed.

“Don’t shoot! She has a child in her belly!” Muci knew that this pair of lovebirds might not get away after all.

“You know who it is! And I know now who she is, too!” Du Luning shoved Muci angrily. “No wonder you were so eager to steal the show today! You think you’re helping her? You’re killing her!”

Just then Yu Xiaojiang handed Du Luning a makeshift megaphone.

Du Luning asked loudly: “What just happened?”

The guard on duty at the sentry post answered through the megaphone: “Reporting, Chief—students have been electrocuted! They tried to climb over the barbed wire and were killed by the current. The brothers have already put rounds into them to make sure.”

Electrocuted, and then finished off with gunfire. Plainly, they had already left this beautiful world. Everything went black before Muci’s eyes, and his body dropped straight to its knees. He could not bear to imagine He Yashan’s pallid face and ice-cold body; he felt fresh blood spreading all around him, coming at him dripping and raw.

Expressionless, Du Luning said to Yu Xiaojiang: “Take Yang Muci to look at the bodies. I want him to remember forever the way they looked in death. And that goes for all the students present, of course—at two o’clock this afternoon, everyone is to assemble.”

Du Luning left. Muci sank into grief.

In the cramped passage of the sentry box, Muci saw the bodies of Guo Ziqiong and He Yashan, their faces full of pain. The guards said the two of them had tried to pass through the barbed wire, but the wire, most unfortunately, had been electrified. Guo Ziqiong had died of the shock, and He Yashan, seeing her beloved lying dead on the spot, had been beside herself with grief. The guards fired warning shots, but He Yashan flatly refused to leave, and so resolutely followed him.

The flower fell, the person was gone.

Muci felt he had become an accomplice to murder. He should have stopped them fleeing, not helped them. He had no tie to them of any kind—yet in his heart he grieved deeply. Through it all Yu Xiaojiang said not a word; she only cut out two butterflies and laid them on the bodies, not yet cold.

“Will they be reborn?” Muci asked.

“Those who love each other never die.” Yu Xiaojiang said mildly. “Let’s go. Perhaps they would rather not have their peace disturbed. And you have a still crueler reality to face.”

Somehow Muci had always felt a fondness for Yu Xiaojiang; at this moment he was grateful for her reminder—that he still had a task unfinished, that he must graduate with distinction, and so achieve the end he had come for.

This was a “battlefield,” not a “field of love.”

Muci resolved to start over from the beginning. He must gather up his feelings; he must win Du Luning’s trust; he must get out of here alive.

When Du Luning returned to his office, his mood was uncommonly good; his suspicions of Yang Muci had at last, this day, come to a stop. No—not merely a stop, but an end, a coda.

Earlier, Du Luning had made all sorts of guesses about Muci’s intentions and motives in coming to the school; the most dangerous guess of all was that Muci was an undercover agent sent by the Communist Party. He had himself broken up more than one such “grit-in-the-sand” scheme of theirs. So this time Du Luning had taken no one lightly.

Any blind action on the students’ part would lead the school to a deep investigation of their histories. Nationalist? Or Communist? Left-leaning? Or right-leaning?

If Yang Muci were a Communist, he would never have acted so! This foolish business of helping fellow students to flee would only trap him in another blind alley. If he were a Communist, he would need to lie low for the long haul, to fight the long fight. He must graduate from here with excellent marks, and then plunge into the enemy’s heart like a steel blade. That was what he ought to do—and certainly not to set himself against the school!

Yang Muci had scarcely once considered that after the incident he risked being weeded out or assassinated; this proved he still did not understand “the organization,” that he feared nothing. And that was precisely what proved his background clean and free of anything suspicious.

The first time the students made trouble over a trainee’s drowning, Muci had not taken part, which had puzzled Du Luning greatly—why was he not carried away? Now it appeared he was the most impulsive of the whole lot of students.

And there was one most important point that moved Du Luning to his decision to lift his suspicion of Yang Muci: he had already finished reading the whole of Yang Muci’s student record, copied out by the intelligence apparatus from Tokyo Imperial University in Japan, which confirmed that everything Muci had said was fact.

The judge of horses had met his thousand-li steed at last.

Just as Du Luning was sitting there content with himself, Yang Muci came. Muci’s face was pale, his eyes moist; his gray, dim expression was a far cry from Du Luning’s frame of mind just then.

“Been to see the classmates you helped?”

“Yes,” Muci answered.

“You seem to be taking it badly.”

“Yes.”

“Never seen a dead man before?”

“Yes.”

“Are you frightened at heart? Or grieved?”

“I feel guilty.” The tears burst from Muci’s eyes.

“Their deaths were of their own making! They’ve nothing to do with you.”

“I would rather the one who died were me.”

Du Luning shook his head. “No…”

“It was I who killed them—killed all three of them, a whole family! I beg you! Shoot me at once! The anguish in my heart is truly too much; I can hardly bear this bone-gnawing, heart-piercing remorse.”

“The anguish of our trade is far beyond what any layman can taste. But once you’re in this trade, you’ll go on discovering its fascination, an endless stream of it.”

“The fascination of killing?”

“A shallow view.” Du Luning countered.

“I want to withdraw.”

“Why?”

“Because you fill me with dread; there’s simply no communicating between you and me. Or is it that you despise me from the bottom of your heart?”

“Why should I despise you? Are you a man who can be despised?”

“But you’ve been squeezing me out all along—no, not just squeezing me out, but rejecting me. You suspect me, distrust me, even want to seize the chance to do away with me… I don’t understand what wrong I’ve committed.”

“The greatest wrong you’ve committed is that you like to act on impulse, to take a point for the whole, to judge the whole by a part. No—but the most important reason goes beyond even that. Coming here all this way with you, I’ve felt your moods swing too greatly, too unsteadily. Now flashing into view, now sparing of your words…”

“Teacher…” Muci wanted to argue his case.

“There’s no need to dress up your words and deeds in front of me; it’s most unwise. To put it another way, you must learn to control all your words and deeds freely in front of anyone at all—to weep or to laugh, both are weapons for you to wield. You are outstanding, remarkably outstanding among this lot of students, head and shoulders above the rest. You have keen powers of observation, superb intelligence—a top student, after all. The one thing that makes me suspect you is: why have you come here? What is it that made you choose this dangerous profession? Why turn so utterly against your former life?”

“I want to do what little I can for the country.”

“You can serve the country just as well in finance.”

“I was curious.” Muci said it bluntly. This time, Du Luning’s expression began to ease. He lit a cigarette and said: “Go on.”

“I really was curious. I badly wanted a change of surroundings.” For the first time Muci felt a tangle in his heart. “My parents—my relations with my parents have never been good. I’m very lonely; from childhood to now I’ve never had their true care. Everything I say is true, though people surely won’t believe it—that the young master of a great banker could be so poor as to live on a single apple a day…” Muci began to choke up.

“Since when?” Du Luning asked.

“Since the time I first knew anything.”

“How very unfortunate.” Du Luning said, looking into Muci’s eyes. “Have you never tried to change the feeling between the two of you?”

“I’ve tried—but the estrangement between us runs too deep. Besides, I was grown by then; they are, after all, my own parents, and in the matter of my education they did me no wrong. Only, I didn’t want to work in my father’s bank.”

“So you’ve been a deputy manager in the business department of an English bank all along?”

“Yes. I don’t like that dull, insipid work—all day reading the papers, waiting on clients, forcing smiles, poring over ledgers, playing the stock market, even sitting like a fool in a café watching the scenery. Too boring, and too dull. I don’t want to live that flat sort of life. I wanted to seek excitement, to choose a life of thrill and danger such as I’d never known. And so today I have come to this.”

“Regret it?”

“Yes.”

“So you want to withdraw?”

“Yes.”

“But this trade has its own rules! This trade has one stern house-rule that runs: come in standing, go out lying down. I think you know, too, that this is no scare and no threat; these words are a rule, and a rule is not to be broken. I think you’re a clever man; you’ll make the wisest choice.”

“I know that withdrawing is a lovely, extravagant hope—and so my anguish is all the greater! Teacher. I don’t want to go on disguising my feelings; living like this is truly so wearying.”

There was, surprisingly, a trace of a smile on Du Luning’s face. “Now you have it. I am your teacher; you needn’t hide your thoughts from me. From now on, I need you to answer all my doubts sincerely, and not fob me off as a matter of routine.”

Yang Muci knew that now, between Du Luning and himself, trust was beginning to be truly built. “I’ll do my best, Teacher.”

“Your best? What does ‘your best’ mean? Your best not to lie, or your best to patch up a lie?” The words were sharp, but Du Luning’s tone was very mild.

“I’m not used to it.”

“Used to what?”

“To the manner in which you speak to me, Teacher, and the roughness of your conduct.”

“How would you have me act? Kind and gentle? Or open-hearted and sincere? I am your teacher…”

“A teacher is one who imparts learning and resolves doubts; you are not. You make me feel oppressed at every moment.”

Du Luning said carelessly: “To honor the teacher and revere his teaching is a Chinese tradition. A tradition is hard to form, yet uncommonly fragile, and easily destroyed. I should not like to see you be the first to destroy that tradition here.” Du Luning turned away, and in a hard tone commanded: “In view of all your rash conduct today, I am putting you in solitary confinement. For the whole time you are confined, you may not say a single word to anyone! Talking to yourself included! Until you are willing to obey my every order. To put it another way—until you find me no longer oppressive. I need you to release your cleverness and wisdom in your studies, not to strut and struggle to outdo everyone in front of the instructors. Have you understood me?”

“Yes, Teacher.”

Just then the bell for emergency assembly rang out…

The clock pointed to two in the afternoon.

“Go and assemble on the parade ground first. Await my order.”

“Yes.” Muci walked out of Du Luning’s office and let out a long breath.

The parade ground, bright with spring sunlight that morning, had turned uncommonly grim now that two young corpses lay stretched across it. Their blood had congealed in the spring wind; their lives had ended amid the boundless colors of spring. Two paper butterflies kept them company, a sign to all the students present that they had died for love, that they had given up their own lives, given up the spring.

“Unbelievable—unbelievable! The flower of youth, years like blossom, cast away here so lightly and so easily! Whom to blame? No one is to blame; blame themselves, self-righteous, reckless of their own limits! To be sure, dying for love is poetic; I don’t deny it. But poetic things are too misty, too void. And so there is one thing we must do: to forget—to forget the poetic, to forget all those so-called beautiful, impractical people and things. To come back, wholeheartedly, to reality. Reality is cruel! Let us take it so, at least. You who have come here, whatever your purpose—to serve the country, to seek knowledge, or to find a steady rice bowl—now that you have come, the first thing you must learn is to survive. To gain your end by any means whatever! As for so-called love, you may keep it in your dreams.” From on high Du Luning surveyed the look on each student’s face; he saw the fear in their hearts and the shock on their faces, and he had gained his end. “They died an ugly death, a death utterly without worth. This is a tragedy, a tragedy past all mending. But this tragedy happened so that no tragedy of the kind should ever happen again! You have all seen it: better to be a glorious martyr than a shameful deserter, put to death. From this moment on, I want to see your steps drilled uniform and even, to see your survival skills tempered a thousand times over, to see your composure at the very brink of life and death.”

On the parade ground there was no sound; it could not have been more still. The students stood in the wind, each of them enduring some torment past all naming.

“If you all agree with my view, then today, those who have died shall not have died in vain. Yang Muci, step out of the ranks.” At Du Luning’s command Muci strode forward one step and stood at attention.

“Because of your well-meant, reckless help, an irreparable wrong has been done. And so you must be punished. From this moment on I am putting you in solitary confinement for one week, to take effect at once. I hope you will use this week to reflect well on your past words and deeds. In a week’s time, I want to see a Yang Muci reborn, remade to the very bone! Can you do it?”

“I can.”

“Louder—I can’t hear you.”

“I can do it!” Muci answered in a loud voice.

“Good. Now everyone is dismissed.”

The students scattered quickly; the smell of blood on the parade ground filled them with weariness and dread. In the instant the guards led Muci away, he resolved to show Du Luning his sincerity once more. He called Du Luning to a halt.

“Teacher, I beg your forgiveness.”

Du Luning said lightly: “The words come a little late—but there’s still time.”

“From this day on, your student will follow the Teacher’s lead in all things!”

Looking at the dashing, spirited Yang Muci, Du Luning’s mood at last cleared from cloud to sun. “Those words come a little early—but I’m very glad to hear them.”

That night, Guo Ziqiong and He Yashan were buried in haste beneath the plum trees of the school; every scrap of material concerning them was destroyed, and they vanished utterly from this world.

After nearly a month of tossing at sea, A-Chu, Rong Sheng, and Lishui had at last returned to their long-lost home—Shanghai. This homecoming meant a great deal to Rong Sheng. After long, lonely years, he had put a lasting end to the beautiful love he had once possessed.

For he could no longer go on living so blindly and so wretchedly. Through the extreme selfishness of his feelings he had lost his own loving father; he had not fulfilled a son’s basic duty, had not tended his father in old age nor seen him to his grave. The pain of his own lost love was not one ten-thousandth of the pain his mother had borne for his sake. Whenever Rong Sheng thought of this, he longed to sprout wings and fly at once to his mother’s side, so that they might comfort each other and ease each other’s pain and grief.

A-Chu’s mind was at peace. He had already come to accept fate’s arrangement for him; whatever the future held, he would never cast off Fourth Madam’s care and love for him. The one thing he wanted to do now was to go home—to a home that was not his own, yet could hold his soul in longing dreams and fill him with warm memories.

A-Chu and Rong Sheng each had his own thoughts; Lishui, though, was the exception. This time she had, after all, not failed her aunt’s charge—she had brought Rong Sheng back safe and whole—and, feeling well pleased with herself, she was radiant as the spring.

No sooner had the three of them come out of the harbor than they heard someone shouting: “Young Master! Young Master!” At once a great crowd of reporters came chasing and heading them off, flashbulbs going off one after another, everyone crushing into a knot, fighting for a shot.

As it turned out, ever since receiving the telegram that Rong Sheng was soon to return, the Rong family had sent people to wait at the harbor every day; and the news world, learning of it, had likewise gone to great lengths, every newspaper posting reporters to lie in wait at the harbor, each hoping to be the first to photograph the venerable face of the Rong house’s head. Because all three were dressed in Western style and especially eye-catching, the Rong family’s driver, A-Fu, spotted them at a glance; his excited, booming shout startled all the reporters, and in a moment there was a lively din of cheering people and flashing lights.

“Young Master Rong, may we ask whether on this return you will take over the family business entirely, and become the new-generation head of the medicine trade?”

“May we ask why you went abroad in those years? Was your wife’s death from illness an important reason for your leaving home?”

“Young Master Rong? Young Master Rong, may we ask whether the young lady who returned on the same boat with you is your fiancée?”

“Young Master Rong, how is your health?”

“Young Master Rong, are you already married abroad?”

“You must be the young son of the Rong house? Would you let us take a photograph?”

“As a doctor of medicine, you gave up a well-paid post abroad and came home to your motherland to serve the nation. May we ask what your true feelings are in your heart?”

“Miss, we’re from the Women’s Vanguard magazine—would you grant us an exclusive interview?”

Rong Sheng was plainly unused to this fierce stab of light; under the cover of A-Chu and Lishui he walked forward with his head down. The driver A-Fu had long since been squeezed out by the tide of reporters, and stamped his feet in a fret outside the crowd.

“Quiet, everyone! Quiet! Please don’t crush, don’t jostle, don’t panic.” To ensure Rong Sheng’s and Lishui’s safety, A-Chu had to step out and take the situation in hand. “We’ve only just come back, and haven’t yet found our feet; your excess of warmth will sweep us back into the sea.” The company laughed. “Your questions—it’s not convenient for us to answer any of them just now. A state has its laws, a family its rules. Travel-worn and out of breath, before we’ve even called upon our elders, to hold a grand press conference here in this reckless way would be against all propriety! Friends, friends—you’ve worked hard, and we’re deeply grateful. Once we’ve gone back and seen our elders, we’ll give you all a satisfactory answer. Now, please make way—A-Chu is much obliged! Much obliged!” With a beaming smile A-Chu made the company a deep, long bow, and one of the reporters caught the shot.

Suddenly a woman caught in the very middle of the crowd was knocked down by the clumsy heft of a reporter’s camera; she tumbled out from among the people and fell right at the feet of A-Chu and Rong Sheng.

Everyone cried out in alarm.

A-Chu hurriedly crouched down, laid the woman’s head level, and gave her a quick examination. Blood was seeping from her forehead, and her fingers, slender as spring scallion sprouts, spread open pale and limp…

Rong Sheng seemed to see a beautiful flower falling and withering before his very eyes.

“I know this person.” a reporter said, pointing at the woman on the ground. “She comes here every day to wait for her sister; every day she waits until the last boat has docked before she leaves.”

“How is she?” Rong Sheng asked.

“Nothing serious—she’s badly anemic, undernourished…” Before A-Chu could finish, Rong Sheng, from who knows where he found the strength, lifted the woman up in his arms all at once, and said to A-Chu: “I’ll take her to the hospital; you go on back first.”

Someone at once helped hail a rickshaw. Paying no heed at all to A-Chu’s and Lishui’s efforts to dissuade him, Rong Sheng climbed onto it with the woman in his arms and swept off in grand style.

The reporters snapped away, and a whole procession of them, from every quarter, went sweeping after him.

Because A-Fu couldn’t squeeze into the ring for the moment, he found a public telephone to report that all was well. “First Madam, my congratulations, First Madam—my joy to you, First Madam. The Young Master is back. The Young Master is back. The Young Master is in fine spirits. His health—good. Fourth Madam? Fourth Madam may set her mind at ease—Young Master A-Chu is back too; I saw him with my own eyes. Miss Lishui is well—they’re all back. The Young Master, the Young Master’s been mobbed, sir, by whole crowds of reporters, sprung from who knows what little earth-god’s temple, more than you could count… The eldest Young Master? The eldest Young Master?” All at once A-Fu caught sight of Rong Sheng and a woman passing right before his eyes in a rickshaw. This was no small matter; he shouted at the top of his voice: “Young Master! Young Master! I’m over here.” At the other end of the line First Madam grew anxious, and asked: “What’s going on? What’s happened to the Young Master?” “First Madam, the Young Master’s gone off in a rickshaw with a woman in his arms.” “Then—then hurry after him!” “Right away.” A-Fu hung up the phone. Turning to come out, he ran straight into A-Chu and Lishui.

“Brother A-Fu!” Though A-Chu had not seen A-Fu for some years, by A-Fu’s round, portly frame he could pick him out of a crowd at a glance.

“Young Master A-Chu!” A-Fu, greatly moved, scooped A-Chu up at once and swung him round in a circle. Lishui laughed too. The three of them, talking and laughing, stowed the luggage and got into the car. Only then did A-Fu remember the eldest Young Master again. “But the Young Master? We’re leaving—what’s to be done about the Young Master?”

“You’re afraid he’ll get lost in Shanghai?” said A-Chu. “Let’s go back first and set the madams’ minds at rest; once the Young Master has finished his business, he’ll come back on his own. No need to worry.”

The car started off.

Lishui said suddenly: “A-Chu, how do you think tomorrow’s papers will write it up?”

“Write it up how?”

“Young Master Rong, hero of the Shanghai Bund, saves a beauty!”

A-Chu shook his head.

“Then what will they write?” Lishui asked.

“Young Master Rong and a mysterious woman check into the same hospital room…” Before the words were out of his mouth, Lishui dealt him a blow head-on, and A-Chu laughed without stopping as the car sped off in a cloud of dust.
