## 6

On the day Lin Nansheng left the hospital a sudden squall came down; autumn in Hong Kong was still as fickle as summer, and a heavy reek of the sea hung in the air. The army surgeon Suzuki Masao saw him off under an umbrella, and the whole way neither of them spoke. Over these long eight months they had become friends, playing go or drinking tea in the disabled soldiers' recreation room, sometimes talking of books in English, more often teaching each other Chinese and Japanese.

Outside the hospital gate, Lin Nansheng glanced at the sentry standing rigid in his rubber raincoat, and asked, suddenly, in Japanese, "How many Chinese have you killed?"

Suzuki Masao was taken aback. "I'm a doctor," he said, in Chinese. "I only know how to save people."

Lin Nansheng took the leather case from him. "Very well, then. Goodbye."

Suzuki Masao put the umbrella into Lin Nansheng's hand and said, in earnest, "Pang-san, that you can walk out of here on your own two legs is a miracle."

Lin Nansheng gave a slight smile, turned, and under Suzuki Masao's gaze climbed into a pedicab, saying to the driver only three words: Public Square Street. That was the lodging Gu Shenyan had left him, on the second floor of number 373, its window giving onto a square that filled each night with jugglers, fortune-tellers, and small hawkers. The first time Lin Nansheng came, he had only just learned to stand up out of the wheelchair and walk. Zuo Qiuming had driven him to the foot of the building, pointed up at the window, and said, "I'll wait for you in the car."

It cost Lin Nansheng great effort to climb to the second floor, and in the instant he opened the door and saw Gu Shenyan, he thought of Zhu Yizhen. He had turned it over a long time; still he asked, "What became of the person who made contact with me that day?"

Gu Shenyan lay in a rattan chair, a cigar in one hand, a folding fan in the other, and looked at him a good while. "You shouldn't ask that."

Lin Nansheng lowered his head. "I want to know."

Gu Shenyan thought a moment. "To forget is the finest way to remember."

After a long silence Lin Nansheng raised his head. "Then let me go back to Shanghai with you."

Gu Shenyan shook his head, left the chair, and went to the window; he drew the curtain and looked down at the square, and after a bitter laugh said, suddenly, "Would you betray the Party and the state?" Lin Nansheng started. But Gu Shenyan did not wait for an answer. As if speaking to the clear pane, he said, "A rebel is never trusted."

A few days before, in the moment he took headquarters' telegram ordering him back to Chongqing, this was the very thing he had said to himself. Gu Shenyan had settled everything in Shanghai and chosen to return by way of Hong Kong and Guangxi, through Nanning. It was not, in truth, to visit the student who had come through against all odds. He meant only to see one man, and to hand down an order both of them had waited years for.

When he saw Lin Nansheng to the door, Gu Shenyan took the key from the table and put it in his hand. "Stay in Hong Kong, then. I've arranged it all for you."

With that, propping himself on the doorframe like an old man, he watched Lin Nansheng work his slow way down the stairs, then shut the door and lay back in the rattan chair. He lay there till nearly noon before he rose, opened the wardrobe, took out a leather case, and left.

Gu Shenyan went to Blake Pier in Central and boarded a fishing boat, and the boat hoisted sail and put out.

Meng Annan had laid wine and food on the low table in the cabin. But Gu Shenyan's eyes stayed the whole time on that lean, dark face, and only when Meng had filled the two cups did he speak. "It's been ten years, hasn't it?"

Meng Annan nodded. "Time has all but made me forget who I am."

When Gu Shenyan first took him in, he had been a boy of not yet twenty, an intern at the Chinese legation in Hanoi. Gu Shenyan served four years there as military attaché, and in that time made an agent of him and gave him the name Meng Annan. But on the way home he had left him behind in Hong Kong. Now Meng Annan edited the current-affairs page of Ta Kung Pao, and sat on the board of the Hong Kong Seamen's Union; his other, deeper identity was that of a member of the Indochinese Communist Party.

This time the task was to find a way into northern Jiangsu, into the heart of the New Fourth Army. Gu Shenyan set down his cup. "Now you have what it takes."

Meng Annan thought a moment. "Yan'an has been at its Rectification campaign all this while, and that wind reached northern Jiangsu long ago."

Gu Shenyan nodded. The great body of men headquarters had sent over in 1939 had by now been all but cleaned out, he said; that was what made this an opening. He looked Meng Annan in the eye. "You're not like any of them. Your ten years here have dyed you red already. And in northern Jiangsu you'll have no line above you and none below. What you are to be is a stray stone, a dormant piece." As he spoke he unfastened from his wrist a Meihua-brand watch that had no second hand and laid it on the table. "If one day you see a watch like it, with no second hand, that is the man I've sent for you."

"If I'm a stray stone, a dormant piece, then no third person need know."

"And if this time is a parting for good?" Gu Shenyan said. "I don't want you to become a kite with its string cut."

Meng Annan lowered his head and looked at the half-cup of wine. "Ever since my parents died, I've been a kite with its string cut."

Jiugong Shipping stood on one side of Victoria Harbour. To all appearances a Japanese kabushiki-gaisha, it was in fact a Juntong receiving station for the Hong Kong district.

Lin Nansheng was back at his old trade, carrying a briefcase to the office each morning and home each night, sifting and sorting the intelligence that came in, then sending it out by fishing boat to the open sea, where the Americans transmitted it from a set aboard a ship. Out of care for his body they had put a leather reclining chair in the office, but he never once used it. He would rather sit at his desk until the numbness spread from his spine through the whole of him, as if the blood were setting hard. Often enough he even hoped he might topple forward onto the desk like that and slowly die.

Once, at a check-up, he asked Suzuki Masao, "If a man has lost all feeling, what's left to tell him from a dead one?"

"At least you can still see with your eyes," Suzuki Masao said, "and think with your head."

"So long as I'm alive, that day will come, sooner or later." Lin Nansheng laughed all at once, and looked for all the world like a drunk who had had too much.

Now, on many a deep night, he went to the bars along Queen's Road and lost himself among prostitutes, gamblers, and opium peddlers, drinking the raw spirit run from sugarcane. Then he went home drunk and lay on the bed with his head splitting. It was the one way left him to feel any pain at all.

But one night, on his way home, Lin Nansheng found he was being followed. The man wore a flat cap and kept an unhurried pace behind him, as if he meant to be noticed.

Lin Nansheng sobered in an instant and turned quickly into a lane. The man was in no hurry either, and came on at the same slow pace. When Lin Nansheng stepped out suddenly from behind him, there was not the least surprise on his face.

The one taken aback was Lin Nansheng. As the man turned slowly around, his eyes went fixed and staring.

Ji Zhongyuan took off his cap. "Mr. Lin. We're hardly strangers, I think."

So Ji Zhongyuan had not died after all. That day, the moment he found the shop watched, he had set off the first grenade, the surest way to send the signal that a man is blown. When the agents of No. 76 charged in, he set off the second, and slipped away in the confusion through the hole it tore in the wall.

He showed Lin Nansheng into a car parked at the curb. "I never thought to come out alive."

"To die takes courage," Lin Nansheng said, mildly.

"I died because the work needed it. That I'm alive again, the work needs just the same."

Lin Nansheng gave a cold laugh. "You feigned death only to give her room to draw me over."

"But she didn't carry my orders out to the letter." Ji Zhongyuan's voice went dry all at once; he looked away to the empty street beyond the glass. "In the two years we were married, not one day did she forget you."

"Then you shouldn't have married her."

"It's the two of you who shouldn't have had a past." Ji Zhongyuan turned back, his eyes glittering in the dark. "We're both in this trade. You know better than I that we don't so much as own our own lives."

After a long silence Lin Nansheng raised his head and asked, in a level voice, "Where have you buried her?"

"By our intelligence," Ji Zhongyuan said, "two coffins were carried out of the Renji Hospital that night."

"What do you mean?" Lin Nansheng's eyes went wide; he glared at him. "What are you trying to hint to me?"

"I'm only passing on a piece of our intelligence."

"You went to all that trouble just to tell me this?" Lin Nansheng said.

Ji Zhongyuan shook his head. "We need the movements of the Japanese army at Guangzhouwan and on the Leizhou Peninsula... these you can manage."

"Without an order from above, I'll give you nothing."

"The invaders won't wait for your orders from above."

"I'm a soldier." As he spoke, Lin Nansheng pushed the car door open; then, after a moment, he added, "I obey my superiors alone."

Ji Zhongyuan caught hold of him and fixed him with a hard, insistent stare. "Your intelligence can save a great many lives."
