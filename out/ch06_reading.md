## 6

On the day Lin Nansheng left the hospital a sudden squall of rain came down; autumn in Hong Kong was still as fickle as summer, and a heavy reek of the sea hung in the air. The army surgeon Suzuki Masao saw him off under an umbrella, and the whole way neither of them spoke. Over these long eight months they had become friends, often playing go or drinking tea together in the disabled soldiers' recreation room, sometimes talking of literature in English, but more often teaching each other Chinese and Japanese.

Passing outside the hospital gate, Lin Nansheng glanced at the sentry standing rigid in his rubber raincoat and asked suddenly, in Japanese: How many Chinese have you killed?

Suzuki Masao was taken aback, and said in Chinese, I am a doctor; I only know how to save people.

Lin Nansheng took the leather case he was carrying and said, Very well, then. Goodbye.

Suzuki Masao passed the umbrella he had been holding into Lin Nansheng's hand and said, in earnest, Pang-san, that you can walk out of here on your own two legs is a miracle.

Lin Nansheng gave a slight smile, turned, and under Suzuki Masao's gaze climbed into a pedicab, saying to the driver only three words: Public Square Street. That was the lodging Gu Shenyan had left him, on the second floor of number 373 on that street, its window giving onto a square that filled each night with jugglers, fortune-tellers, and small hawkers. The first time Lin Nansheng came here, he had only just been able to stand up out of the wheelchair and walk on his own. Zuo Qiuming had driven him to the foot of the building, pointed up at the window, and said, I will wait for you in the car.

It cost Lin Nansheng great effort to climb to the second floor, and in the instant he pushed open the door and saw Gu Shenyan, he thought of Zhu Yizhen. After long deliberation he asked all the same: what became of the person who made contact with me that day?

Gu Shenyan lay in a rattan chair, a cigar in one hand, a folding fan waving in the other, and stared at him a good while before he said, You should not ask that.

Lin Nansheng lowered his head and said, I want to know.

Gu Shenyan thought a moment and said, To forget is the finest remembrance.

After a long silence Lin Nansheng raised his head and said, Then let me go back to Shanghai with you.

Gu Shenyan shook his head, left the rattan chair and went to the window, drew back the curtain and looked down at the square below, and after a bitter laugh said suddenly, Would you betray the Party and the state? Lin Nansheng was startled. But Gu Shenyan did not wait for his answer; as though speaking to the clear pane of the window, he said, A rebel is never trusted.

A few days before, in the moment he received headquarters' telegram ordering him back to Chongqing, this was the very thing he had said to himself. Having settled everything in Shanghai, Gu Shenyan had decided to return to Chongqing by way of Hong Kong and Guangxi, through Nanning — and it was not, in truth, to visit this student who had come through against all odds. He meant only to see one man, and to hand down an order that both of them had awaited for years.

When Gu Shenyan saw Lin Nansheng to the door, he took the key from the table and handed it to him, saying, Stay in Hong Kong, then; I have arranged it all for you.

With that, propping himself against the doorframe like an old man, he watched Lin Nansheng make his laborious way downstairs, then shut the door and lay back down in the rattan chair. He lay there until nearly noon before he rose, opened the wardrobe, took out a leather case, and left the room.

Gu Shenyan went to Blake Pier in Central, boarded a fishing boat, and the boat hoisted sail and put out.

Meng Annan had set out wine and food on a low table in the cabin. But Gu Shenyan's gaze rested the whole time on that lean, dark face of his, and only when Meng had poured wine into the two cups did he say, It has been ten years, has it not?

Meng Annan nodded and said, Time has all but made me forget who I am.

When Gu Shenyan took him in, all those years ago, he had still been a lad of not yet twenty, an intern at the Chinese legation in Hanoi. Gu Shenyan served four years there as military attaché, and in that time made an agent of him and gave him the name Meng Annan. Yet on the way home he had left him behind in Hong Kong. Now Meng Annan was an editor of the current-affairs page of Ta Kung Pao, and a director of the Hong Kong Seamen's Union besides, while his other, better-hidden identity was that of a member of the Indochinese Communist Party.

This time the task Gu Shenyan gave him was to find a way into northern Jiangsu and into the heart of the New Fourth Army. Setting down his cup, he said, Now you have what it takes.

Meng Annan pondered a moment and said, Yan'an has been running its Rectification campaign all this while, and that wind reached northern Jiangsu long ago.

Gu Shenyan nodded and said that the large body of personnel headquarters had sent over in 1939 had by now been all but cleaned out, and that this was therefore an opportunity. Looking Meng Annan in the eye, he said, You must understand that you are unlike any of those men: your ten years here have dyed you red already, and once in northern Jiangsu you will have no line above you and no line below — what you are to be is a stray stone, a dormant piece. As he spoke he unfastened from his wrist a Meihua-brand watch that had no second hand, laid it on the table, and added, If one day you see a watch likewise without a second hand, that is the man I have sent to find you.

Since I am a stray stone, a dormant piece, there is no need for a third person to know.

And if this time it is a parting for good? Gu Shenyan said, I do not want you to become a kite with its string cut.

Meng Annan lowered his head, looked at the half-cup of wine on the table, and said, Ever since my parents died, I have been a kite with its string cut.

Jiugong Shipping stood on one side of Victoria Harbour; to all appearances a Japanese kabushiki-gaisha, it was in fact a Juntong intelligence receiving station for the Hong Kong district.

Lin Nansheng had gone back to his old trade, carrying a briefcase to the office each morning and home each evening, sifting, analyzing, and sorting the intelligence that came in, then sending it out by fishing boat to the open sea, where it was transmitted from a wireless set the Americans kept aboard a ship. Out of regard for his body, his superiors had a leather reclining chair placed in the office, but Lin Nansheng never once used it. Every day he would rather sit at his desk until the numbness spread from his spine through his whole body, as though the blood were setting hard. Often enough he even hoped he might just topple forward onto the desk like that and slowly die.

Once, at a follow-up visit to the hospital, he asked Suzuki Masao: if a man has lost all feeling, what is left to tell him from a dead man?

Suzuki Masao said, At least you can still see with your eyes, and think with your head.

So long as I am alive, that day will come sooner or later. Lin Nansheng suddenly laughed, and the look of him was that of a drunkard who had had too much.

Now, on many a deep night he would go to the bars along Queen's Road and lose himself among prostitutes, gamblers, and opium peddlers, drinking the raw spirit privately distilled from sugarcane. Then he would go home drunk and lie on the bed feeling his head split with pain. This was the one method left him of making himself feel pain at all.

But one night, on his way home, Lin Nansheng found he was being followed. The man wore a flat cap and trailed unhurried behind him, as though meaning to be noticed.

Lin Nansheng sobered in an instant and stepped quickly into a lane. The man seemed in no hurry either, still walking at the same unhurried pace. When Lin Nansheng abruptly stepped out from behind him, there was not the least surprise on his face.

The one taken utterly aback was Lin Nansheng. As the man slowly turned around, his eyes went fixed and staring.

Ji Zhongyuan took off his cap and said, Mr. Lin, we are hardly strangers, I think.

So Ji Zhongyuan had not died after all. That day, the moment he found the mounting shop under watch, he had set off the first grenade — the most thorough way of sending the signal that one is blown. When the agents of No. 76 charged in, he set off the second, and then, in the confusion, escaped through the hole blown in the wall.

After showing Lin Nansheng into a car parked at the roadside, he said, I never thought to get out alive.

Lin Nansheng said mildly, To die takes courage.

I died because the work required it; that I live again the work requires just the same.

Lin Nansheng gave a cold laugh and said, You feigned death only to give her room enough to draw me over.

But she did not carry out my orders to the letter. Ji Zhongyuan's voice turned all at once dry; he looked away at the empty street beyond the window and said, In the two years we were married, not one day did she forget you.

Then you should not have married her.

It is the two of you who should not have had a past. Ji Zhongyuan turned back, his eyes glittering in the dark: We are both in this trade — you know better than I that we do not so much as own our own lives.

After a long silence Lin Nansheng raised his head and asked, in a level voice, Where have you buried her?

Ji Zhongyuan said, By our intelligence, two coffins were carried out of the Renji Hospital that night.

What do you mean? Lin Nansheng's eyes went wide all at once; glaring at him, he said, What are you trying to hint to me?

I am only passing on to you a piece of our intelligence.

Lin Nansheng said, You went to all that trouble just to tell me this?

Ji Zhongyuan shook his head and said, We need to know the movements of the Japanese army at Guangzhouwan and on the Leizhou Peninsula... these you can manage.

Without an order from above, I will give you no intelligence whatever.

The invaders will not wait for your orders from above.

I am a soldier. As he spoke Lin Nansheng reached out and pushed the car door open; then, after a moment's thought, he added, I obey the orders of my superiors alone.

Ji Zhongyuan caught hold of him, looked him full in the face with a compelling stare, and said, Your intelligence can save a great many lives.
