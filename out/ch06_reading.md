## Chapter 6. Earth and Water

The ninja arts of Japan have their wellspring in the province of Iga. From Iga's southwestern corner the mountains run away to the southwest, range folding upon range, and make the backbone of the Kii peninsula. First the Ōmine range, then the Kii range, and at the last the Hatenashi range, until the whole mass pitches down into the sea in the south.

Where that mass of mountain spills toward the Kawachi plain in the north, a river runs east to west, nearly in a straight line, across a quarter of the peninsula's breadth. This is the Kino River.

Even Mount Kōya, a holy place on earth since Kōbō Daishi founded it and the longing of every soul, is no more than a single swell of ground at the western foot of the Ōmine range, where the range comes tumbling down.

In its middle course the Kino skirts the northern foot of Mount Kōya, meeting its tributaries and parting from them, and runs on west and ever west through the warm lowland, laying down a broad bed of stones. Today it empties into Wakanoura at the northern edge of Wakayama city. But in the old days the main channel was the Waka River, which flows east of the present city, and Saigazaki stood at its mouth.

The Yoshino River threads down between sheer cliffs on either hand; and soon after it takes the name of Kino, just at the foot of Mount Kōya, it bends in a half-circle. There, on the height above the bluff, lies a stretch of country strongly fortified.

This is Kudoyama.

Keep Kudoyama in mind. Long after our Goemon has left this world, Sanada Yukimura will raise his fort here, and here, at the head of such famous ninja as Sarutobi Sasuke and Kirigakure Saizō, cut his great figure. This place is the central stage of the sequel to the present book, The Sanada Ninja Band. I beg the reader to hold it in memory.

Now, as I have said before, the river winds along like a great serpent moving at its ease; and where it comes directly beneath the Kishū Fuji on the south bank, there on the north bank stands the famous Kokawa-dera.

It was founded in the first year of Hōki, the year 770, older than Mount Kōya itself. The court held it in deep devotion, and by Tenshō 13, the year to which I am now conducting you, its splendor had reached its very peak. Its lands ran a ri east to west and better than a ri north to south; its precincts spread over fifteen thousand tsubo and more; and it held the seven halls of a full monastery, with two thousand seven hundred halls, pagodas, and priests' lodges besides.

But that magnificence, a bare month after I have shown it to you here, was burned to the ground by Hideyoshi's hand. What stands today is little more than the Great Gate and the Middle Gate, the Main Hall, the Dōnan Hall, the Nenbutsu Hall, the Taishi Hall, the Fudō Hall, and the Jizō Hall.

The temple sits with its back to the peaks of Katsuragi, where En no Gyōja is said to have done his austerities. Seventeen chō from the temple stands the Gyōja Hall, and in it a standing wooden image two shaku high, said to be the work of En no Gyōja's own hand. It is a Tendai temple, a temple of the yamabushi and the mountain ascetics, and so, it need hardly be said, bound up closely with the ninja arts.

Two ri and ten chō downstream from the Kokawa-dera, the Kishi River, running up from the south, joins the Kino. And a ri north of that meeting stands the Negoro-ji, head temple of the Shingi Shingon sect, as I have told you before.

And, as I have also told you, a ri and a half up the Kishi River, south from where it meets the Kino, at the foot of Mount Tokika, lies the hamlet of Nagao, where Maki's people farm.

Goemon, Maki, and Goichi, gone from Hamamatsu, had nowhere to turn but here.

Here, holding a scant six tan of field, lived Maki's father Yagoemon, now fifty-two; her elder brother Gisuke, twenty-two; and her little sister Kazue, fifteen.

The mother, Kiino, had died the autumn before, of a sickness that filled her belly with water.

Yagoemon was a good-natured man, and split off two tan of the field for Goemon and his wife.

The soil here was far richer than Iga's. But two tan was hopeless. By Yagoemon's reckoning, counting the barley and even the buckwheat raised after it, the most it yielded was one koku four to. The land was the Negoro-ji's now, wrested from the Kokawa-dera and taken into temple domain; so the Negoro-ji levied six to in rice-tax, and labor-service rice on top of that, and what remained came to a bare five to. Two people eat two koku in a year: the shortfall was one koku five to. So they had to labor at taro and hemp for all they were worth. And still it fell short, and they hired out by the day besides, dredging the Kino and the Kishi, digging out ponds, to make up the rest. So the year round they lived on chagayu, a gruel of coarse grain boiled in ten times its measure of water. To Goemon, bred to the easy life of a samurai, all this came as a shock.

What a wretched thing the life of a peasant was!

And yet, shouldering hoe and mattock and going out to the field, bathed in the sun, with the larks crying shrill overhead as they climbed and dropped, hoeing between the rows of barley not yet in ear, he would think there was no happiness on earth to match it.

The hamlet of Nagao held forty-two households. Thirty-one of them worked under three tan. Even with no rice-tax at all, no labor-rice, it could not have fed them.

The thing was wrong from the very start. What kind of madness was it, when you came to look? True, there were no samurai here. But in their place there were the priests. Priests great and small, swarming past counting, who never grew a single grain of barley nor a lump of taro, and lived ringed about with gold and silver and treasure.

What on earth were they there for?

They read the hard sutras brought over from India; they held the reasons of the world's beginning and its end and its turning; they knew the spells, so it was said, for every want a man has: peace in the realm, a rich harvest, safety in the house, sickness healed, long life.

These are things any man alive would wish for. But the priests would not lift a hand unless the money came first. Every temple in Japan, from the beginning, was raised on land the court or the warriors who held it had given away, by way of a fee paid in advance for the charms, or of thanks for them; and it lived on the yield of that granted land, peasants and all.

The Negoro-ji itself was a gift of the Retired Emperor Toba; and Mount Kōya, its parent house, was granted by the Emperor Kanmu. Its neighbor the Kokawa-dera is older still: in the year 770 a magnate named Ōtomo no Kujiko built it for the prospering of his own house, and it grew great once it had won the devotion of such men as the Retired Emperor Kazan.

Not one charm would they work for you free.

Then were the poor left out of it? No, it did not fall so. Every man, when he dies, must set out across a hundred thousand million lands to the Pure Land. And for that he must have a funeral, and be given a death-name, and be laid in a grave. Without it he cannot so much as cross the River of Three Crossings. That is the river every ordinary soul, once dead, is held bound to cross, and a demon husband and wife stand guard on it.

When it comes to that, that the souls of your dead, your parents and your brothers, should have nowhere to go and stray in the darkness of unknowing, their bodies flung into a ravine for the crows to feed on, there is no one who would not flay the skin off his own back to wrap up an offering for the priests.

And once a temple was a landlord, it was no different from a warrior landlord. The peasants on its land had their rice-tax taken; they owed their days of forced labor; and when sickness or the like kept a man from working, he had to render rice in its place to make the loss good.

The temples of the land kept armed priests, to stand against the warrior lords on their borders and to hold their own people down. These were the sōhei, the warrior-monks.

Far back, in the age of the Genpei wars, the warrior-monks of Mount Hiei and of Mii-dera were famed for their ferocity. They would not bend even to the court's command, and it was they who wrung from the Retired Emperor Shirakawa his famous word: "The things that will not go as I would have them: the waters of the Kamo River, the fall of the dice, and the mountain-monks of Mount Hiei."

In the diary of Kujō Kanezane, under the seventh day of the eleventh month of Bunji 1, the year 1185, he grieves over the violence of the warrior-monks: "In this world of the five defilements, this age locked fast in strife, do such revolts come treading one upon another's heel without end? It is to be lamented. It is to be lamented."

There is a legend that Minamoto no Yoshitsune, as a boy in training on Mount Kurama, learned the art of war from a crow-tengu. (The crow-tengu wears a crow's face, with feathers sprouting down its back; it flies where it pleases, goes dressed as a yamabushi, and carries a feather fan.) It means nothing more, of course, than that he learned his fighting on Mount Kurama, a Tendai training ground, from the yamabushi or the warrior-monks.

The warrior-monks of temples deeply bound up with the yamabushi and with Shugendō, temples like the Tendai and the Shingon, naturally studied the ninja arts that had grown out of yamabushi warcraft; and from China they had taken instruction in kenpō, the art of the empty hand. By the last years of the Warring States they had been quick to get matchlocks out of Tanegashima, and had gone on to make their own.

The Negoro-ji, which was also the eighth training-ground of Katsuragi Shintō, had warrior-monks to the number of thirty thousand, and their ferocity rang through the realm.

Not all of them, of course, were priests who had truly trained. Many were masterless samurai and drifters washed in from the provinces. And there were local strongmen and big farmers from round about who, at the first sign of trouble, would run to the temple and turn warrior-monk.

The mark of a Negoro warrior-monk was his long hair, worn loose like a horse's mane and hanging halfway down his back.

And one thing more. The temple was rich, after all, so it kept a great store of fine weapons, and its armor, chased with gold and silver, was gaudy enough to startle the eye.

The day after Goemon came to settle in Nagao, Gisuke took him to see the Negoro-ji.

The precincts spread out along the mountain's skirt, vast and broken with steep rise and fall, and splendid pines stood thick and tall across the whole of it.

Among the pines the buildings lay scattered, and there were many of them.

Shingi Shingon, mingled with Shugendō and with Shintō: a Gongen hall here, a Kasuga shrine there, a Bishamon hall, a Kyūsha Myōjin, a Konpira shrine, a jostling throng of them.

Goemon climbed the slope, passed the Great Gate, went up the stone steps, threaded his way among the buildings, and came out into a broad open space where the main cloister stood. Across the way, at the Kōmyō-den, the Mitsugon Hall, a great many priests were hurrying to and fro. To its left lay a pond; and between the Kōmyō-den and the pond stood a small hall, three bays to a side, from which smoke came billowing up in clouds. You would have thought the place afire.

Out of the smoke came the fierce voices of men chanting a spell at full pitch.

"That's the Gyōja Hall," Gisuke said. "They're burning goma in there."

Goemon could not master his curiosity. Half braced for someone to shout him off at any moment, he stole up respectfully and peered in through the lattice.

Gisuke came up behind him. "Goma's a word from Tenjiku, that's India, and they say it means to burn," he whispered. "I had it from Father."

In the middle of the little room stood the goma altar, heaped with splinters of wood and blazing hard. Iron plate had been fixed around the altar and on the ceiling straight above it, and everything in the room, every last thing, was smoked a sooty black.

Five or six men in yamabushi dress were packed in there, working thick prayer-beads between their palms, over and over, and bawling out the spell at the top of their voices.

Peering through the smoke, fixing his eyes hard, he made out at the far end a strange figure seated, near six shaku tall.

He started; and staring closer, he saw it could be nothing but a seated image of En no Ozunu, founder of Shugendō, the very forefather, you might say, of the ninja arts. A staff in the right hand, a scroll in the left, two-toothed clogs on the feet, a long white beard hanging down, and a face gentler than he had looked for.

Before the forefather of the art he had been drilled in since childhood, Goemon bowed his head without thinking and pressed his palms together.

Then, keeping the main cloister on his left, he pressed on deeper, where the pines grew thicker still; and all at once a building rose up before him. It was a pagoda of a strange shape, the like of which he had never once met in all his long years in Kyoto and Osaka.

Put simply, it was a two-storied pagoda. At its foot sat an earthen platform packed hard with pure white plaster, shaped like an offering-cake, and on it rose the first story, square, five bays to a side. The tiled eaves swept up and jutted far out; and above them, upon another rounded white cap of plaster, sat the second story, round this time, crowned with a great roof that reached out nearly as far as the first. At the very top, a huge nine-ringed finial and its jewel stood up into the sky, massive with its own weight.

"It's a tahōtō, a Many-Treasures pagoda, but everyone hereabouts calls it the Great Pagoda of Negoro," said Gisuke. "They say it's a hundred and eighteen shaku to the top of the finial."

"What's enshrined in it?"

"Oh, a whole crowd of Buddhas live in there. More than twenty of them, Nyorai and Bosatsu both. But the most blessed thing of all is the jewel stuck up on the very tip of the finial. There's a nyoi hōju set inside it, a wish-granting jewel, and they say worship it and there's nothing you can't have."

Seeing Goemon stare wide-eyed at the pagoda, as though it were drawing him in, Gisuke grew prouder still of his explaining.

But past that, Gisuke himself knew no more.

This pagoda had been raised by Kakuban Shōnin, founder of the Negoro-ji, in the fourth year of Daiji, 1129, after the model of the Konpon Daitō of Mount Kōya. It stood forty-two shaku lower than that Daitō's hundred and sixty; but its form was by far the graver and more solemn of the two, and had an elegance about it besides.

The tahōtō form, with its two rounded shells of white plaster, was itself patterned on the inverted-bowl stupa of India, and had been brought over from Tang China by Kōbō Daishi, the founder of Mount Kōya.

But Mount Kōya is a place of much lightning; and in the old days, before the lightning rod was invented, a pagoda a hundred and sixty shaku high, topped with iron rings, made fine prey for it. Time and again the lightning came down, and burned not the Daitō alone but the halls and cloisters across the whole mountaintop.

It had been finished in the lifetime of Kōbō Daishi, who died in 835; but lightning struck in 994, in 1149, and in 1521, and each time it burned to nothing. So the Konpon Daitō that stood in Goemon's day was a newer one, built after the old had burned down sixty-three years before he came to gaze at the Great Pagoda of Negoro.

Later still it burned by lightning in 1630 and in 1843; and after that, for ninety years, nothing remained of it but the base stone, until in the ninth year of Shōwa, 1934, it was rebuilt in concrete.

Where the Konpon Daitō of Mount Kōya lost its first shape over and over in this way, the Great Pagoda of Negoro, from its founding in 1129, came through unscathed. It keeps its old form still, not in Goemon's age only, but down to the present day.

All the woodwork had once been painted with red ochre. But by the time Goemon looked on it, four hundred and fifty-eight years since its founding, the paint had flaked and been washed off by the rain, the grain standing up sharp, until the wood had gone white as bare bone. And that was fresh and clean past all telling.

What it was in the pagoda that so drew at Goemon's soul, there is no saying. But draw at it it did. He felt his own heart being sucked up into the tower.

That night he told Maki he had business at the temple, made ready a Yoshitsune torch, and slipped into the Great Pagoda.

Once inside, he saw twelve round pillars ranged in a circle on the plaster base. So the pagoda had begun as a round tower, and the outer wall of the first story and the roofs of both stories had been Japanized into square. That much was plain.

And at the center of the circle the pillars drew, a central pillar some ten shaku around drove up through the darkness at the top of the tower.

A Yoshitsune torch is made in this way. A white ox horn is shaved thin, until it turns clear; twenty quills of goose feather, cut to about a sun apiece, are pushed inside; and the quills are packed with mercury. It stands against wind and rain, and has the virtue of burning long.

By its faint light he could see, at the four corners of the hall, which measured five ken to a side, the four bodhisattvas standing: Monju, Fugen, Miroku, and Kanzeon.

He climbed a ladder set upright among the thick, intricate crossing of the timbers, and found that what was two-storied from without was four floors within. Buddha images stood everywhere, great and small.

Among them were dubious ones too, a Kangiten here, a Gozu Tennō there.

There had been a time when Goemon lived up in the tower of the Nanzen-ji gate in Kyoto, buried among a host of such images. Out of the sight of terrible men, keeping company with the still images, his heart had been deeply at rest. That was how he had come by his fondness for them.

Goemon climbed a narrow ladder that ran up higher still from the fourth floor. At its dead end, in the side, was a small hole, and moonlight was falling through it.

He thrust his head out and looked around. This was the topmost point of the roof, and the hole opened in the roban, the base-plate at the foot of the nine rings where they stood driven up into the sky.

Working a shoulder through, he found he could just squeeze out. One slide of the foot, and he was through and onto the roof, which curved away below him in a shallow arc.

However bright the moon, at night no one below could make out his shape.

East of the pagoda stood the Great Denbō Hall; to the north a pine-covered hill pressed close behind. From the hill the faint sound of water dropping into a distant pond came to him.

Breathing the clean night air, Goemon looked out over the sleeping precincts.

Just then, out of the depths of the hill, piercing the stillness, a sound began to reach him now and again: ton-ten-kaan, ton-ten-kaan.

"That's a smithy. What are they making at this dead hour of the night?"

Goemon came down from the tower and pushed up into the hill. By the four-legged practice he became a dog, killed the sound of his steps, and closed on the noise.

Some ten chō on, in a valley, five or six huts stood in a row behind a stout bamboo palisade, with watchmen posted at several points. The ring of iron came from within.

Watchmen or no watchmen, it was easy work to slip in by the heaven-hiding art, using the moon, and spy out what went on inside a hut.

Though it was the end of the second month, a dozen-odd men, naked but for a loincloth, lit by the blue flames of the forge, stood at their labor streaming with sweat, as if they had bathed in water.

At the big furnace, one man clung to a prop and drove the bellows with his foot. Another beat out a strip of iron glowing red, hammering it long and thin as a ribbon. Another wound that red ribbon around an iron core and hammered the seam shut. Another plunged a finished barrel into water, and it threw up a great gout of steam.

They were making guns.

In another hut, files rasped and shrieked, cutting the screw-thread for the breech.

In another still, molten lead was run into sand molds to cast the shot.

It was now the forty-third year since Tsuda Kenmotsu Kazunaga, head of the Suginobō, had carried back to Negoro one of the two matchlocks that first came ashore at Tanegashima.

When he went to Tanegashima he had been a young man of twenty-five; now he was an old one of sixty-eight. The aged ascetic in white robes seated in the corner of the first hut was surely Tsuda Kenmotsu himself, for the men at work waited on him with reverence.

If he truly was Tsuda Kenmotsu, then here was a monster of another kind. The hair left at the back of his skull was pure white, and a white beard hung from his chin; but the eyes at the bottom of their sunken sockets burned bright, and even allowing for the fire's glow, his face was a deep, angry red.

One glance was enough to read the strange, driving zeal he brought to the making of guns.

He was the moving force behind the arming of the Negoro-ji.

Against the rulers who came one upon another, Nobunaga and then Hideyoshi, the Negoro-ji had held from first to last to a posture of defiance.

When Nobunaga attacked Ishiyama, it went to Ishiyama's aid; and around the time of the battle of Nagakute, whenever Hideyoshi moved east, it would sally at once toward Kishiwada, setting itself to threaten Osaka and Kyoto, and so keep a hand on Hideyoshi's arm.

Why was this?

For one thing, the notion that Buddhism was the enemy, planted since Nobunaga's day, had struck deep root. But it was not a matter of religion alone. The lands the temple had won from the court over long years, the high-handed samurai rulers would surely never let it keep in peace. A warrior ruler was no such pliant adversary as the court. The court you could steer by a religious vision of the world, or by the merits of esoteric prayer; but the warrior houses, realists to the bone, were not to be moved by any of that.

The samurai, whose trade is dying: would not he, of all men, wish to believe that death is not the end of everything, that there is a world to come?

No doubt he would.

But their "trade is dying" meant, in truth, that their trade was killing; and when the killing went wrong, they were killed in their turn. The idea that a man must not fear being killed was what they dressed up in the words "our trade is dying."

Call it the Age of the Latter Law, call it the realm of the Asura, set loyalty to one's lord up as the highest of all virtues, and still you cannot make the killing of men into anything but evil.

So a samurai who made his trade of killing must reckon that, if there is a next life, he will fall into hell; or, short of that, that paradise at least is surely beyond him. And so most of them, where the world to come was concerned, chose to leave it alone and not touch it. But the more philosophical among them, whom that leaving-alone left uneasy, chose instead the doctrine of transmigration.

It was a doctrine the Indians, with their harsh order of caste, had thought out: framed to explain the fact that, once a man is born into a given class, no human power can ever break him free of it, and to offer some comfort in the face of it.

Every living thing in this world is born again and dies again, and transmigrates forever. That a man has been born into this class, in this life, is the karma of what he did in a former one, and nothing now can change it. But if in this life he lays up good causes, that becomes the ground that fixes what he will be reborn as in the next. One would like to settle it, that such a good earns rebirth as such a thing; but settle it as you may, there is not a single fact that can be proved. His former life, and karma heaped up out of an infinity of ages, all bear upon it, so that from the deeds of this life alone no outcome can ever be reckoned.

The use of the doctrine, in short, lay in this. It gave a man resignation, that it was the karma of a former life and could not be helped; and it gave him hope, that in the life to come he might be reborn as something better.

The Japanese samurai reasoned thus: "That I was born a samurai into a world like this is the karma of a former life. Driven by that karma I go on heaping killing upon killing, and so in the next life I may be reborn as something worse. But if I now lay up good causes, then one day, in a life to come, I shall be reborn as something better, and find happiness."

But there were samurai who could not bear the thought of floating and sinking for all eternity in that sea of transmigration, born a dog, a snake, a tiger, a man, and then a fish again. To escape the sea, they cast off their armor and took Buddhist vows, killed every feeling and every sense, and, if only on the plane of the mind, made world and man into the Void, seeking by that to win release from the wheel.

There were a very few such. But the great mass of samurai took no interest in the next world at all.

For proof, look at the Noh, the art the samurai of the Ashikaga age created. Read the utai chants that are its words.

Every one of them turns on the same nihilistic theme: the world is fleeting, so much dust and rubbish, one grief heaped on the next. And when it grows too bleak, with no comfort in it anywhere, a Kanzeon Bosatsu or the like appears at the end. But the figure is a hazy, blurred thing, and the salvation it promises is never made plain.

For all these reasons the samurai, by and large, had no need of religion. Many turned to Zen; but that was for the cultivation of the spirit, for the disciplining of mind and body.

There was, besides, a complex toward the priests, the men of learning, the intellectuals: a sense of their own inferiority.

And so, more and more, the priests were hateful to them.

The priests, for their part, held an unshakable sense of their own superiority: that they alone pursued learning, that they alone had sounded the deep philosophy of the universe.

On top of that they had a special skill. Using Sanskrit, the language of India, which no ordinary person could follow, and elaborate rites and ceremonies, they performed the esoteric prayers. By this, it was held, they could press the whole company of Buddhas and gods into service, and sway a man's fate across this world and the next. In short, they passed for magicians.

The rulers of this world had need of the magicians, both to have the prayers said first of all for themselves, and to hold the ignorant masses in awe.

Just as the chieftain of a primitive age was himself a magician, or else kept magicians close about him, so at a king's side there is always a magician.

Japan's Shintō was too primitive by far; and Buddhism had come in piecemeal, carried back scrap by scrap by the great priests who crossed to Tang, so that the sects stood apart and quarreled among themselves. Suppose it had been drawn into a single sect, the way Europe was unified under Christianity, where all religious power gathers into the Roman Pope, who holds from Christ himself the keys to the gate of Heaven. Then not the lords of Japan alone, but even the King, would have had to do as the kings of Germany and France did. To bring rich tribute, put on a beggar's clothes, and make the pilgrimage all the way to Rome. To climb the stone steps before the Vatican palace on their knees until the knees wore raw. To plead not to be cast out of the Church. They would have had to fall on their faces and shuffle forward on their knees to the court's own magicians, to the Ise Shrine, or to Mount Hiei, or to some head temple, and beg forgiveness for their sins.

But the great priests of Japan held no such trump as the Roman Pope's single grip on the keys of Heaven's gate; and so against the ruling warriors they had no recourse but to fight it out by force of arms.

This was how the warrior-monks arose.

Against the great priests, who lived hand in glove with the court and the nobles of the central government, stood the anti-court religious powers that had struck root among the common people: the yamabushi, the shugenja, the gyōja. These had often to hold off the attacks of the court's armies; and in the doing of it, driven by need, they worked out the ninja arts, and raised warrior-monks of their own.

And it was those powers, in their earliest days, that legend fused, most likely, into the single figure of En no Gyōja Ozunu.

Kishū, for its part, was a vexing country for the central government. Its backbone, the mountains of Yoshino and Kumano, had from of old been a bolt-hole for elements set against the court. And Shikoku, cut off across the water from the mainland, was where magnates with no taste for bowing to the central government dug themselves in; there was constant traffic between the two.

The warrior-monks of Kumano and the Kumano fleet had thundered through the land from ancient times; and the fishermen of Saiga had been hardened by turns joining that pirate band and fighting it.

Those fishermen among them, the landed men, the local strongmen, and the peasants of the whole tract from the coast up to the Kishi River were called the Saiga-shū, and were feared as men who, at the least trouble, would take up arms and resist.

Here the Ikkō sect had been strong from early on, and so was at odds with the Tendai and Shingon houses, with Mount Kōya, the Kokawa-dera, the Negoro-ji. Peasants like Maki's own family, on the border between the Kishū domain and the temple lands, at Ryūmon and west of the Kishi River, often had their houses burned over their heads and were driven off. And yet, faced with a common enemy like Nobunaga or Hideyoshi, they would cross the lines of sect and form a common front.

So it had been at the war of Ishiyama castle. So it had been at the battle of Nagakute.

Once a body of armed men has come into being, the wish grows to make it mightier and mightier still. And once they are warrior-monks, driven by the thought that they fight the enemies of the faith and the enemies of mankind, that they brandish in Fudō Myō-ō's stead the keen sword that subdues the demons, the tendency can only grow the fiercer.

And this man of the Suginobō was the very type of it.

What his birth had been, no one knew. But from what he had done at the Negoro-ji, he was a man of powerful ego by nature, whose pleasure was to feel his own strength by beating others down. Exclusive, and narrow.

Since the self came into everything with him, he was all but unable to think in any universal or objective way.

To think so, a man must be able to set the self aside, and that he could not do. He knew the trait in himself. There had been times when he meant to mend it. But finding he could not, he chose instead to own it as a merit. Seeing that by it he gained a powerful will to act, he let it run loose. By now the thing was fixed past changing.

His drive was so fierce that men held their tongues and obeyed. And so a power built up with him at its center.

Yet all of it, looked at closely, was born of the envy that nested at the root of his nature. Racked and writhing under that helpless passion, he had made himself, in the end, into this.

A fair number of people saw through to the secret, and looked on him with pity. Yet for all that, knowing what trouble it was to cross him, they bore his high-handedness in silence and did as he bade.

For three-and-forty years the gun, that new tool for the killing of men, had utterly stolen away his soul.

To watch it split the air with its roar, and gout flame, and hurl a ball that drove clean through any armor at all: however often he saw it, it sent through him a shudder past all words.

Forty years back he had raised a forge in this valley, gathered the smiths of the domain, and called in Shibatsuji Seiuemon of Sakai besides to try the making; but every cast barrel burst. Then, catching word that Nobunaga was having guns tried at the Kunitomo smithies in Ōmi, he sent a gyōja of his own to steal in and carry off the method: to work the steel into a ribbon and wind it up into a barrel.

But the screw for the breech was beyond him.

Then, hearing a rumor that a man in Sakai had brought off the making of the breech screw, he sent Shibatsuji back to Sakai to steal the method Yasaka Kinbei had learned at the price of his own daughter.

From then on, at the forge in this valley, he made guns in quantity. Some filled the arsenals of the Negoro-ji; the rest went down to Sakai, and through the merchants' hands were sold off to the daimyō.

So those guns flowed into the very hands of the samurai, the enemies of his faith. It was the same thing exactly that the merchants of death do today, selling arms to the enemy as well, and turning their profit on the spilled blood of their own countrymen; the same thing as the Roman Pope holding a great block of shares in the companies that make the stuff of war.

And in the course of his going back and forth between Sakai and Negoro, he fell in with Torii Moriichirō, who served in the house of the Sakai merchant Ikenaga Kurō Chōa.

The narrow, cross-grained Suginobō felt a kinship with this Kirishitan youth, the man with no forefinger on his right hand and a yellow cast to the whites of his eyes. He questioned him too about the Kirishitan doctrine.

In the end Suginobō put it to him: would he not come to Negoro and live with him a while? That the one was Shingon and the other Kirishitan was no great matter to either of them. With Hanzō's leave, Moriichirō came to lodge at the Suginobō of the Negoro-ji. It was just before Goemon moved to Nagao.

Hanzō had given Moriichirō the task of spying out what went on at the Negoro-ji, and it was on that account that he allowed him to go. But Suginobō, of course, never dreamed that Moriichirō was tied to Tokugawa Ieyasu.

Now, when Goemon spied out the gun-works in the valley, Moriichirō was there too, in the bullet-making hut. But Goemon had never met him. So naturally he did not know that this Moriichirō was the very man who had handed over the cargo at Sakai, back when Etegi was running saltpeter from Sakai to Hamamatsu; nor that the man was here now.

But Moriichirō knew Goemon by sight. Just once, when he had gone to Hanzō's house, Hanzō had told him: "That man is Goemon."

One of the gifts of a man like Moriichirō was that he never forgot a face he had once seen. So if he came upon Goemon again now, he would know him at once.

And so Nagao, too, had ceased to be for Goemon a safe haven wholly cut off from the past. But Goemon knew nothing of it, and went home with his heart racing at his discovery of the gun-works.

He had taken a great liking to the Great Pagoda of Negoro. Being inside it, he told Maki, quieted his mind; and so that she would not fret over his going out, he told her plainly, and went there almost every night. For the space of a koku, some two hours, he would sit dozing inside it, or climb to the very top and look down on the world below through the hole in the platform.

Last June, in 1963, I visited the Negoro-ji myself and climbed this Great Pagoda. The tower stood as it had three hundred and eighty years ago, unchanged from Goemon's own day. On the eaves and the plank walls of the first-story roof, many marks still remained of the matchlock balls fired into it when Hideyoshi came against the temple.

As luck had it the head priest was away, but a young monk kindly fetched a flashlight and lit the way for me. Timidly I went up the vertical ladder, half of a mind more than once to give it up midway, and reached the top. From the hole beneath the platform I got my head and shoulders out, but I hadn't the nerve to climb clear onto the roof. And since it was a hole I could only just force myself through even then, Goemon, I should think, was no great big man, but rather a lean one.

Now, having won a lull in his quarrel with Ieyasu, Hideyoshi resolved to bring first Kishū, and then Shikoku, to heel.

Kishū had once been the domain of the Kanrei Hatakeyama Motokuni, together with Kawachi and Izumi. But in Tenshō 2, in the time of Akitaka, the ninth lord after Motokuni, a retainer named Yusa Kawachi-no-kami Masakata rose in revolt and bore down on Akitaka's seat, Takaya castle in Kawachi province. Akitaka was beaten, and took his own life.

For the eleven years since, with no lord of real power, the province had fallen into deeper and deeper disorder. As I have said, the shrine-lands of Kumano and the temple domains of Mount Kōya, the Kokawa-dera, and the Negoro-ji seized land as they pleased, hoarded gold and silver, drew in masterless men on the strength of that wealth, built up their forces, and set themselves in open defiance of Hideyoshi.

So Hideyoshi's first move was to name his younger brother Hidenaga governor of Kii province.

It was on the third day of Goemon's visits to the Great Pagoda of Negoro that Hidenaga sent his general Hori Kyūtarō to bring the Negoro-ji an ordinance under the signature of Maeda Gen'i.

That day the Negoro-ji could talk of nothing else.

By what Goemon gathered, the ordinance ran thus: "From this time forward, not the temples alone, but townsmen and peasants down to the meanest, shall bear no swords. Every sword now held, to the last blade, shall be surrendered."

The priests read the ordinance and burst out laughing.

"Not a vermilion-seal letter from Hashiba Hideyoshi, no, nor even one from the Yamato Dainagon Hidenaga. Just an ordinance from some Maeda Gen'i, is it?"

"What it means is this. They know well enough we'll never heed it; and had they put out a seal-letter from Hideyoshi or Hidenaga and we made waste paper of it, they'd have no way left to back down."

"What do you say? Rather than crush it dead, let's tell them we've made a clean sweep and this was all we found, and hand over two or three blades."

"Ha, ha, ha. He'll go scarlet with rage, I'll wager."

And so the Negoro-ji handed Hori Kyūtarō a bare three swords.

This was no more than a prelude to the Taikō's great sword-hunt, carried out in full in later years.

Ten years before this, Shibata Katsuie, hard-pressed by the Ikkō risings of Kaga, had taken the swords from peasants and townsmen alike. That was the beginning of the sword-gathering.

Katsuie, some books record, had farm tools made from the weapons he seized and gave them to the peasants, and forged iron chains to strengthen the pontoon bridge over the Kuzuryū River; so that the peasants were greatly pleased.

Hideyoshi, of course, never supposed the Negoro-ji would surrender its swords without a word. What he wanted was a pretext to attack, in their refusing the order.

On the tenth of the third month, the court made Hideyoshi Minister of the Interior, and gave his mother the title of Ōmandokoro and his wife that of Kita-no-Mandokoro.

His spirits high, Hideyoshi set out to take Kishū at a single stroke. He named Hidenaga general of the vanguard and Hidetsugu his second; for samurai-captains he named Hori Kyūtarō Hidemasa, Nagaoka Yoichirō Tadaoki, Gamō Chūsaburō Ujisato, Hasegawa Tōgorō Hidekazu, and Takayama Ukon-tayū Nagafusa; and he mustered an army of thirty thousand.

When the rumor of it reached Negoro and Saiga, the whole countryside fell into a great ferment.

In the gray of dawn on the thirteenth, the great bell of the Negoro-ji was rung, and the landed men, the strongmen, the village headmen, and the peasants filled the broad precincts.

These were days with no such thing as a loudspeaker. One man's voice could never carry to so vast a crowd.

The rough monks scattered to their places about the Mitsugon Hall, the Renge-in, the Great Denbō Hall, the Bishamon Hall, and the Great Gate: Suginobō, Sugimotobō, Iwamurobō, Iwasakibō, and the rest, each taking his stand on a high spot, the crowd divided among them.

Goemon was mixed into the throng before Iwasakibō, who stood on the veranda of the Daidenbō-in, beside the Great Pagoda.

Four monks blew conch-horns for all they were worth. This Iwasakibō had once been a retainer of the Kyōgoku house of Ōmi; but when his lord's house was destroyed by Nobunaga, he had come to the Negoro-ji and turned monk. Near fifty though he was, his face was well-fed and greasy, and he was famous for a bawling, barrel-chested voice and a brazen, roaring laugh.

He spread his two hairy hands and bellowed. "Quiet! Quiet! Hear what I have to say!"

"A question, if you please, for His Reverence Iwasakibō!" came a shrill voice out of the crowd. Everyone turned to look.

There was the face of Hyōe-Saburō, headman of Nagao, a full head above the rest. He passed for a good-natured simpleton. His thin little eyes hid under swollen upper lids, so that, as now, you could not tell whether they were open or shut. And that squeaking, high-pitched voice, so ill-matched to the big frame, made him look all the more half-witted.

"Now hear me. What I tell you now, I saw with these eyes and heard with these ears, in Osaka, only the day before yesterday. You follow? Osaka is packed with soldiers as I speak. Soldiers to come and cut down Negoro and Saiga."

And Iwasakibō, his opening thrown off its stride, glared toward the voice. "This that they speak of, that the Naidaijin, Lord Hideyoshi, is to send his soldiers against the Negoro-ji: is it a settled certainty?"

"Certain it is. It is because it is certain that I have called you all together here."

For all the menace in Iwasakibō's words, Hyōe-Saburō blundered scatterbrained on.

"It may be a rash thing to ask, but: might we not, on the face of it, obey the Naidaijin's command, and put off for the moment the ruin right before our eyes, and then, by and by, go on just as before?"

Iwasakibō's voice turned rough.

"Talk sense, you fool! This whole business is them calling us to account for handing over just three swords to the sword-hunt order. No more of your dodges will serve now."

Then he pitched his voice higher still.

"All of you, listen, and listen close. Our Negoro-ji, from the far-off day of its founder Kakuban Shōnin down to this hour, four hundred and forty years, has stood a holy seat of the secret Shingon consecration, and never once let provincial governor or shugo lay a hand within its bounds. From the reign of the seventy-sixth Emperor to the hundred-and-seventh today, through two-and-thirty Emperors, not one has ever meddled inside our borders. And now, Hidenaga! Who is he? A name never before heard in the land. Some upstart sprung up of late, flinging his orders about this way and that. Hideyoshi's younger brother, they call him; and this creature has the gall to name himself lord of Kii province, and dares, past all reverence, to carve up the pure dwelling of Fudō Myō-ō, the very homeland of Dainichi Nyorai! Sacrilege! Sacrilege! It is just such a one that we call an enemy of the Buddha. And to this enemy of the Buddha are we to bow our heads, and tamely give ourselves up, and go meekly to hand over our swords? Is that what you imagine we could do?"

He was, after all, a man who lived by his tongue. The smooth words came pouring out like water. The people were as if drunk on them; their excitement mounted, and the corners of their eyes drew up.

"And more, hear me, more! That you, the people of our Negoro-ji, have lived in peace all this while, never harried by lawless governors and shugo, is owing to one thing alone: that our temple has kept the demon-subduing sword honed, and stood, unbending, against the samurai. When those samurai went subduing the country round about with bow and gun, and reached out to seize even the ground of the Buddha and the ground of the gods, and made plunder their trade, we gathered before the Kirimomi Fudō Hall where the Venerable Fudō is enshrined, and made ourselves the very lasso of Fudō, and fought them without end. When Nobunaga's army fell upon Ishiyama, we crossed the lines of our own sect and went to Ishiyama's aid, and broke Nobunaga's forces times past counting. When Hideyoshi, coveting the realm, warred with Lord Takeda and Lord Tokugawa, we threatened his rear at every turn. We and Hideyoshi are fated, in the end, to fight it out. And should we be beaten, and all Kishū fall into Hideyoshi's hands, what then? Of every three measures of the harvest you bring in by your sweat, two will be taken from you in tax!"

"Eh? Two of every three?"

"Two of three, in tax?"

A great stir went through the peasants.

Iwasakibō, seizing his moment, raised his voice.

"Yamato, Yamashiro, Ise, anywhere you please! Go and look at any land that has fallen to Hideyoshi. Without one exception, it is everywhere the same."

The four monks blew their conch-horns all together to fire the crowd the higher. Iwasakibō flung both hands about in the air.

"And more! In the places that took the sword-hunt order and meekly gave up their blades, they'd have nothing but bamboo spears now, even if they wished to rise. Can bamboo spears best the bow and the gun? So the standing or the falling of the Negoro-ji touches the life and death of every one of you peasants. You'll be wrung dry, weeping tears of blood. And such as are left will be cut down like dogs by the samurai!"

Again the conch-horns sounded, and a monk shouted at the top of his voice.

"Headmen, gather at once before the Bodai-in!"

Another monk bawled out:

"All who know the use of a gun, gather before the Kirimomi Fudō Hall!"

The kokujin and the hunters who knew guns gathered before the Kirimomi Fudō Hall. It took its name from an old tale: when Kakuban Shōnin was set upon by the scholar-monks of Kōya, the Fudō there took the master's place, and was bored through the thighs with a drill, and bled, and was flung into the ravine below the Great Gate. From the hall the men were led on to the gun-armory in the valley, and each was handed a gun, a powder-flask, a bullet-pouch, and a length of match-cord.

"Hidenaga's army will march out of Osaka three days from now at the soonest. We will get ahead of them, and throw up forts at Sengokubori, Shakuzen-ji, and Hama castle by Kishiwada, and lie in wait there."

In the plaza before the Mitsugon Hall, a monk named Sugimotobō, fearfully thin and very tall, was bawling away.

"Since last night the landed men, the peasants, the masterless men of Izumi and of Saiga have been streaming out to the front. To Sengokubori, under Yamauchi Saburō-tayū, Takayanagi Kenmotsu, and Takamatsu Tōnai; to Hama castle, under Suzuki Magoichi and Ten'i Hamazaemon; to Shakuzen-ji, under Tsuya Magokurō and Nakamura Magoheiji; five and six thousand to every place, they have gone. Any man who would go and be first into the fight, speak up here and now! Swords, spears, guns, we'll put them in your hands!"

The young men who raised their hands and pushed forward were enrolled there on the spot. All about the temple stood knots of people, each group talking over the disaster that had dropped on them from a clear sky.

When Goemon came down the slope before the Great Gate and reached the bank of the Kino River, he found the men of the neighboring village of Uenoyama and the men of Nagao gathered on the dry riverbed, talking together.

Uenoyama lay some twenty-five chō northeast of Nagao. Its paddies were narrow but rich, and for that its rice had been seized time and again by the Negoro-ji, and it had suffered badly. Fervent Ikkō believers besides, its people could not stomach fighting under the Negoro-ji's command.

Genzaburō, forty-five, Denbei, thirty, and Gennojō, twenty-one, were all men whose houses had at one time or another been burned by the Negoro-ji's monks. The grudge unhealed in them, they had no heart to go and fight for the Negoro-ji or its priests.

Young Gennojō, the veins standing out and writhing at his temples, cried out in a shaking voice.

"The priests live in the lap of luxury off the taxes they've wrung out of us. Now's the very time for it: let Fudō Myō-ō in all his majesty fling a rope round Lord Hideyoshi, or Lord Hidenaga, or the both of them, and truss them up in a wink. That'd do me fine."

At this Denbei too threw up his head, the head with the left ear a Negoro rōnin had sliced away fifteen years before. "Those Buddhas packed in rows and decked out grand inside that temple, are they blockheads, then? Those hard sutras, those prayers, are they good for nothing? And now, with our backs to the wall, they can tell us to lay down our very lives! Well, the brass of it!"

In the hearts of the Nagao men too the same thought was seething. But they knew they could not give themselves over to the grudge alone.

The headman Hyōe-Saburō above all, who had been more than once to Sakai and to Kyoto and Osaka, knew well how overbearing the samurai could be.

"Now then, men of Uenoyama. True enough, the temple lives in luxury and splendor. But everyone says the temple's rule is a milder thing than Lord Hideyoshi's. Once we're folded into the samurai's rule, and the door shut on us, there's no getting by the way we have till now, not by a long chalk."

"Bah. Even now it's a life no better than a ground-worm's—" Gennojō burst out.

"And I'm telling you it'll come worse than the ground-worm's," Hyōe-Saburō shot back.

An old woman named Yoshino, who lived next door to Goemon and, unlike the rest around her, was of the Shingon sect, was pleading hard with the men of Uenoyama.

"You men of Uenoyama carry your stubbornness a mite too far. Years back, in Lord Nobunaga's day, when Ishiyama castle was under attack, we of the Shingon sent supplies too. And plenty of us went off to fight besides."

Then Maki's father Yagoemon came forward and spoke.

"Men of Uenoyama, now, just hear me out. I'm Yagoemon of Nagao. As you know, I'm an Ikkō man, and from a boy I've had worse done me by the men of Mount Kōya and the Negoro-ji, over and over, than the mouth can tell. They burned my house once, too. But look you. The samurai won't come at us drawing any fine line between Shingon and Ikkō. Mount Kōya, Ise, Kumano, the gods and Buddhas that have stood since the beginning of time, they'll fall on the whole lot, be sure of it. And that is why this old man too means to shut himself in the temple and fight. You of Uenoyama, hear reason. It's a great disaster right on top of us. Let's make one heart of us all, and fight the enemies of the Law and the enemies of the Buddha. Eh? What do you say?"

Goemon had cast off the samurai name of Saeki Dennai he had gone by at Hamamatsu, and become plain Goemon once again; but now, forgetting he was a newcomer here, he found himself shouting out.

"That's the truth of it! The samurai are the evil ones! Worse than the priests by a long way! Sooner fall into hell than live ruled by samurai!"

Genzaburō of Uenoyama spoke at last. "There's no saying no to fighting the enemies of the Law and the Buddha. Only, from this day on, we want the temple-men of Negoro to lay a hand on their own hearts and think hard. That from now they do no more unreasonable wrong to us, who keep to the nenbutsu and nothing else; and that when drought comes, or flood, they promise to ease the tax according to the crop. That we must have."

Hyōe-Saburō nodded deeply. "That's only just. We'll take it up with the temple-men, never fear. Well then: you're with us?"

The men of Uenoyama looked at one another, and then, one after another, said: "Right. We're with you."

"The day the samurai come to rule us, we've lost stock and root alike. We're with you."

And the groups set off home, each his own way.

When Goemon, Yagoemon, and Gisuke had crossed the Kino River and were coming to the foot of Ochaya Gotenyama, there were Maki and Kazue, who had been anxious, come out to meet them.

At the sight of Kazue, Goemon caught himself up short.

There was a reason behind it.

Kazue was fifteen this year. She had lived at hard field-work since she was a little child, so she was bigger-boned than Maki, and browner. And there was something keener about her altogether. Her eyes were large, the dark of them shining jet-black; her lips were set firm and tight, as though she were holding something back. Her brows, to put it unkindly, were black and thick as a centipede; but that too gave her a boyish, guileless look.

And this girl had set her heart on Sakai.

Headstrong she was, but sound at the core, a good, honest girl.

Maki had let fall, without thinking, that Sakai was a fine town. To Maki, for all that she had been made to ply a shameful trade there, Sakai was a place of noise and brilliance, not to be named in the same breath with the likes of Nagao.

Sakai was, in truth, the one place in all Japan where the rare treasures of the world and its strange foreigners came thronging together; to any Japanese alive it was a sight to open the eyes wide. That a country girl like Maki should be dazzled, and want to speak of nothing else, was not to be wondered at.

And in the heart of Kazue, forever at her work caked in earth, Sakai had taken its seat, alluring as a country out of magic.

At the end of Tenshō 1, twelve years ago now, Goemon had gone to Sakai, and there, in a brothel in the back streets, first set eyes on Maki. He fell headlong for her, bought her free of her master, made her a woman of her own, and set up house with her in a back-street tenement. And for five years after that he lived in Sakai.

Deep as Goemon was in those days in his life with Maki, five years is a long stretch of time. It was not that he had made no acquaintance at all. With the family of Yamatoya Tarōbei, in fact, who kept a draper's shop in Kobayashidera-machi, he grew very close, going there now and again to buy Maki's clothes.

He had asked a man with business in Sakai to carry a letter for him. The day before yesterday the answer came: they would take Kazue as a maid for two or three years. Since Maki's coming had added a pair of woman's hands, one fewer mouth to feed suited Yagoemon's household as well; so yesterday it had been settled that, on whatever day fell right for Goemon, he would take her down to Sakai.

"Say, brother, Sakai's a grand place, now, isn't it?" Kazue asked again the question she had asked so many times. In her head there seemed to be room for nothing but Sakai.

"Aye, a fine place, where neither samurai nor priests throw their weight about. There's a moat and a wall clean round the town, so the samurai can't get in."

"It's where you and sister first met, and grew sweet on each other."

"That's so, that's so. A place your sister and I both hold dear. A fine place. Silks and stuffs from far countries too, from Ming and the Southern lands and Portugal, no end of them. And, you know, Sakai's a bare thirteen ri from here. Brother will come and see you now and again, never fear."

In all Kazue's poor life she had never once known what it was to dream of something. Now, for the first time, a dream of dazzling colors opened in brilliant flower. It made her forget the whole world; she drifted, empty and adrift, in a happiness that gripped her by the chest.

That day Kazue's first blood came. And so the setting-out had to be put off until it was past.

That the delay of those few days should throw her whole fate awry: how unknowable a thing is the lot of a human being!

Kazue and Goemon set out on the twenty-first.

It being a young girl's pace, he meant to take the road in two days. They left the house in the morning, went down along the Kishi River, and crossed at its meeting with the Kino. A little way west of the crossing, Goemon caught the sign of someone trailing them.

To catch the sign of a follower is, for a ninja, an elementary art, drilled into him from childhood. Without once turning his head, Goemon could tell that this was a ninja with a certain training behind him, and, moreover, a young man still.

Hm, one of Hanzō's men, up from Hamamatsu, Goemon thought.

To kill me? Or only to spy out what I do and carry word back to Hanzō?

But the man was no adept skilled enough to kill him. Far from it: a good deal of a bungler. Were Goemon alone, shaking off such a one would be a thing done before breakfast; but with Kazue beside him there was no help for it. Follow as you please, then, thought Goemon, and crossing Oyama Pass he took the road north.

The farther they went, the more the country seethed. Peasants with their eyes drawn up, as though the war were right upon them, went running north and ever north along the highroad, each with some weapon snatched up in his hand.

The coast road was hopeless at this rate; better to keep to the hills. Goemon turned off to the right and wound on between low, pine-thick knolls. Somewhere along the way the sign of the follower faded and was gone; and as he walked slow to match the girl's step, the mild spring wind stroking warm at his head, a carelessness must have stolen, all unnoticed, into his heart.

They had sat down to their midday meal when, all at once, from behind a little hillock to the right, thick with pine, came the sign of two men grappling.

"What's that? Wait here for me, now."

Goemon got to his feet, ran across, and hid in the lee of the hill.

By now the sign of grappling had shifted into the pine wood one stand further on. Again Goemon gave chase.

Four times over the thing repeated. That Goemon, of all men, failed to see it for what it was, a form of the Iga school's art of the shifting decoy voice, came of two things: first, as I have said, the carelessness in his heart; and second, that the war was drawing near, so that it would have been no wonder for Hideyoshi's vanguard to show itself anywhere at all.

"Tricked. Drawn off!"

The instant he understood, Goemon raced back to the spot; and sure enough, Kazue was gone, the half-eaten lunch scattered on the ground.

Grinding his teeth at his own carelessness, Goemon ran about like a man out of his mind. And as he ran, a whirl of thoughts turned in his head.

If the one who snatched her was Hanzō's agent, would they carry Kazue off to Hamamatsu? To what end? Revenge, for their running away? Or was it Kazue for bait, to draw him in? She could hardly be made a stand-in for Maki. Or had a healthy peasant girl simply roused the man's lust? But was he truly Hanzō's man at all? And if he was not, then who under heaven was he?

There, in the mottled patchwork of light and shade, in the cover of a clump of grass, a young man had Kazue pinned beneath him.

Her hair was torn loose, her clothes ripped to rags.

Goemon swept the dagger from his breast into his right hand and flung himself at the villain's back. But the man too was a ninja. He shrank and rolled aside, so that the blade did no more than gash his left shoulder.

In that instant, the face Goemon glimpsed was one wholly strange to him, its wicked eyes glinting up as from the bottom of a pit.

"Who are you? And why this girl?"

He shot out his long arm and caught the villain by the collar. But by some contrivance the collar he had hold of slid clean off the garment and came away in his hand, and the man sprang sideways and was free of him.

The Iga school has no such trick of the garment. Then was the fellow a ninja of some other school? Goemon does not know that this man is Moriichirō, son of Torii Kihachirō of Ōhara, and so a ninja of the Kōga school. Still less can he know that the man is of that breed who, like certain novelists and film-makers of present-day Japan, hold sex to be the chief of all human functions and give themselves up wholly to its practice. But not even the Kōga school cuts a garment in any such collar-slipping fashion.

That trick was Moriichirō's own, the practice of a theory he had reasoned out from his own experience.

Suppose that, while he had a woman pinned, he had the ill luck to be caught at it. What would the one who caught him do with the collar? Nine times in ten he would grab the villain by the scruff and try to drag him off. And so, if the collar came away, his own body was left free. That was the theory.

By his own count, in the past, Moriichirō had thirteen times gotten his body clean away.

Just a year ago, at the battle of Nagakute, in the copse on the hill that looks down over the hamlet of Ōkusa, he had set upon the woman who passed for a ninja, Hatsuko, and failed; and that was because the woman herself had stabbed him from below. Had it been a mere bystander who caught him, he would never have blundered so shamefully.

So long as the one who caught him was no ninja, the collar-slipping trick would leave the other dumbfounded for an instant, and in that instant he could always roll aside and get clear. The rolling escape was all reckoned in.

But today's man was a ninja. And when he made to stand, Goemon's hand had closed on the hem of his hakama. He rolled a good three ken—

Even the resourceful Moriichirō had not gone so far as to invent a hem-slipping method. He was dragged bumping along, and just as the second blow was coming, and his life with it, there was a clatter of running feet, and a band of men came into view.

One warrior in full armor, and six or seven brawny foot-soldiers with guns and jingasa helmets.

Turned out so, they were plainly no peasant league, but Hideyoshi's army, at a single glance.

Before Goemon could get out a word, Moriichirō was shouting.

"Is this Lord Takayama's force?"

"It is. We're men of Lord Takayama Ukon-tayū. And who are you two?"

"This fellow is one lately come to Nagao, which lies under the Negoro-ji's rule; an Iga ninja, in truth. He is out on a scouting trip toward Sakai and Kishiwada. He works for the Negoro-ji."

"And the girl?"

"A companion I brought along to hide the scouting."

"And you, then?"

"I am a man of Hattori Hanzō, under Lord Tokugawa Ieyasu."

"I see. Well done. A chō or so back you'll find our vanguard. Go there and get your wound seen to. Men! Take that villain!"

Resist as he might, there was no escaping it. Goemon threw the last of his strength into it and fought like a madman.

The grappling art of ninjutsu is built on the kenpō handed down from the continent, with a whole repertoire of feints laid over it: feint a jab to the head and kick the groin; feint an advance and pull back; feint right and slip left; feint a drop and spring up; feint a fall backward and roll to the side.

Earth-hiding, water-hiding, man-hiding: not one of the arts could be worked here. He fought on with all his failing strength. It was broad daylight.

Had he carried a blinding-powder or a smoke-ball, he might have used it; but he had brought no such thing.

And now, Moriichirō must have given the word, fifteen or sixteen samurai came swarming up at a run. Kazue, hoisted on the shoulders of several soldiers, dwindled into the distance before his eyes.

"Kazue!— Kazue!" Goemon cried, and cried again.

He flung the whole of the six throwing-stars he carried against just such a moment. They wounded six men; but the enemy were many now, packed close and tight all round him.

There was no saving Kazue now. The despair of it filled his head to bursting. What a girl fallen into soldiers' hands would be made to suffer, in a war like this, needed no telling.

Let heaven and earth turn over, then: Goemon went berserk.

He had just two things in his favor. The enemy, ashigaru though they were, were in the heavy armor of real battle; and they meant to take him unhurt.

In the press he wrenched loose an enemy's long-sword that came to his hand, and laid about him with it. And by the time he had at last hacked one blood-path clear and won free, his clothes, down to the loincloth, had been stripped from him, and he stood as naked as the day he was born.

Once he was sure the enemy were not on his heels, he plunged into a little pond he came upon, washed the mud from his body, and bound up his loosened hair.

Then he went out into the open fields, watched his moment, and slipped into an empty peasant house, and there took a garment flung down in a corner of a room, and a rope and a hoe left piled on the earthen floor. He belted the rope round him, shouldered the hoe, swung wide to the east, and came near to Shakuzen-ji once again.

All that while, one prayer was in his heart.

"They said they were Lord Takayama Ukon-tayū's men. Lord Takayama Ukon is a Kirishitan daimyō famous through the land. The captain who set on me a moment ago wore a great crux, a cross, on the breast of his armor too. The Kirishitan forbid the violating of women, and sternly. So it may be that Kazue is safe. Namu, Lord Kirishito, keep Kazue from harm..."

And Goemon, who knew no way to pray, prayed for the first time in his life to Kirishito.

But as he neared Shakuzen-ji, the clouds of war already stood up black above it.

On a low hill stood a fort that was no more than a moat freshly dug, an abatis of felled branches set out, and an earthwork thrown up; and there the Negoro monks held command, with the peasants and fishermen of Saiga shut inside.

The Taikōki sets the number at fifteen thousand; but the truth, of course, was half that or less. Still, the attacking force, counting the strength of Nagaoka Hyōbu-tayū and his son, of Gamō Chūsaburō, and of Takayama Ukon together, must have topped ten thousand easily. And all of it pressed and shoved and fought in that one narrow patch of ground.

The battle was only just joined. Each side raised a war-shout like the roaring of beasts; from among the besiegers a little band would dash out now and again and fire their guns, and from behind the earthwork the defenders would fire back. That was the stage it had reached.

But the besieging soldiers had spread out across the whole neighborhood, ringing the fort round and round, burning the peasant houses down, and cutting off, hard and close, all passage between the fort and the world outside.

The smoke of the torched houses stood up to heaven in column after column, toward Sengokubori and Hama castle as well; and the people who had lost their homes wandered without aim, their eyes wrenched wide with terror, or stood weeping and wailing.

The barley fields, not yet come to ear, were trampled to wreck; every vegetable fit to eat was pulled up, or gone already into the warriors' bellies.

Bodies of men cut down as suspicious lay fallen here and there.

At every fort there was only skirmishing still, and the sun leaned slowly to the west.

That Hideyoshi's great host should waste its time on three such little forts was, in truth, a ruse. In the dark before dawn that day, Hideyoshi, at the head of the hundred thousand the Taikōki names, with Hidenaga's army of thirteen thousand for vanguard, marched out of Osaka. He swung east from north of Kishiwada, gave the three forts a wide berth, and, forcing his way over fifteen ri of road, drove straight for the Negoro-ji.

The scheme, that is, was to fall on the main temple-castle while the Negoro monks and the Saiga-shū were away at the front.

Goemon, working round to the east of Shakuzen-ji, ran full into a band of kamari, the scouts, at the head of this force under Hidetsugu.

To be taken would mean trouble, so Goemon veered further east, toward the hills.

But there too a force under Hidetsugu came pushing on, its kamari out in front.

In the end Goemon threw away the hoe and fled at a dead run back to Nagao.

When Maki saw Goemon crumple in the doorway, she was aghast.

Yagoemon and Gisuke had both gone off to the Negoro-ji, and Maki was keeping the house alone.

Goemon flung himself down on the earthen floor and wept aloud. He was blood all over, from the raw wounds on his body.

"Oh! Hurt like this— what has happened to you?"

"Kazue— they've taken Kazue!"

"What? How did it happen?"

"How? By whom?"

Goemon, forcing the words out through his sobs, at last told her how it had come about.

And Maki too threw herself down on the earthen floor and broke into loud weeping.

"Kazue— Kazue— what is becoming of her now—"

"When the fighting dies down, I'll go to Lord Takayama's camp once more and find out! I'll get her back, I swear it! Lord Takayama is a Kirishitan daimyō, so Kazue is safe. She must be."

Just then he caught the sign of someone, and snatching up a stick of firewood that lay by him, hurled it at the beam overhead.

And down from the beam dropped a man in ninja garb. Goemon sprang at him.

To set out that instant a little more fully, it went like this.

The man on the beam was a ninja too, for all that. The moment he judged that Goemon had marked his presence, he seized the initiative, dropped straight down onto Goemon, and made to pin him then and there. And in the very instant his body left the beam, the firewood Goemon had flung up came flying. Caught in the air, he could not twist aside, and took the wood full in the flank. The blow spoiled his fall, and he wrenched his left ankle.

And so the struggle was settled in the blink of an eye, and the ninja lay pinned beneath Goemon.

"Maki! The rope! Bring the rope!" Goemon shouted.

With the rope Maki tossed him, he bound the man tight.

"Who are you? On whose orders did you come to spy?"

Knowing well enough that it was useless to ask, Goemon ground the man's face hard into the dirt. And he tore the mask away.

At the face that came up from under him, Goemon started. It was a face he had never once seen at Hamamatsu.

This fellow, if he was not from Hamamatsu, then where in the world had he come from?

The face was a sickly lead-gray, and the left eye was crushed shut, gone to a dirty, puckered scar.

Goemon groped in the ninja's breast, drew out the ninja-pouch, and looked through it.

Inside was a full set of Iga-school gear: kunai, tsubokiri, shikoro, iron caltrops, kurogaki, and the rest.

Hm. An Iga ninja, and out for me. His face I don't know, but he can only be a Hamamatsu man—

But the man would not open his mouth.

"I'll send you back to Hamamatsu. Come, out with it!"

"Listen well. Me, I've clean put Hamamatsu out of my mind. So I've no thought of taking revenge on any man. You hear me? In my heart there's no Ieyasu now, no Hamamatsu. So only confess, and I'll turn you loose. I'll send you back to Hamamatsu. Come, out with it!"

All at once Goemon turned savage.

"You won't talk, damn you! Right, then, I'll make you talk!"

He snatched the dagger the ninja carried and made to slash the man's face open. Maki caught at his arm.

"Stop! S-stop, don't do that!"

"Hah! Out of my way!"

He took the ninja by the collar, dragged the face close, and looked hard at it— and was thunderstruck.

The lead-gray of the face had rubbed off when he ground it into the ground a moment ago, and ordinary skin showed through; the scar over the one eye had peeled away, and a sound, open eye had come out.

"Ah! Damn you! You're one of them from that night!"

He was one of the three who had seized Maki and carried her into the castle.

Among the arts of man-hiding there is one called the art of the changed shape. It is the art of turning one's clothes inside out and changing them in a flash; of carrying always a false beard and false brows to put on; of mixing colors into white face-powder, of darkening the complexion with soot, of shaping a scar from clay kneaded with grease.

Nothing so fine, of course, as an actor's make-up today; but in a poor light it served well enough.

The man's name was the Purple Scale. His skin was rough, as if scaled over, and so he was the Scale; and Hanzō, for a jest, had tacked on the genteel color-word "purple."

Maki, who had come close to stop him, heard Goemon's words and drew back with a start.

"What did Hanzō order you to do? Where did you carry Maki? How many times did you carry her? Speak! Speak! Will you not speak!"

He throttled the man, jerked him this way and that. But the Purple Scale set his jaw and would not open his mouth. And presently the bright blood came welling out of it in a stream.

"Ah— you've bitten off your own tongue! Damn you! Damn you!"

Goemon dashed the man's head against the floor, and dashed it again; but before long the man went limp, and his breath stopped.

"He's gone and died. The fool, there was no need for him to die at all—"

Goemon muttered it, as if his wits had left him.

"Someone might come. We must hide it, quickly—"

said Maki.

"Mm. Right, then. Sink it in the pond out back."

Goemon stripped the ninja garb from the corpse, shouldered it, went to the little marsh-like pond just behind the house, tied a great stone fast to it, and sank it.

As the body went down, churning up its bubbles, a thought struck him.

"That's it. Hideyoshi's great army is passing the three Kishiwada forts by and coming straight south. The temple must be told."

"I've sunk it in the pond. Wipe the blood off the floor. I'm going to the temple a moment," he told Maki, and ran off to the Negoro-ji and made his report.

The temple, which had reckoned there was still time while the three forts held, was thrown all at once into an uproar.

The smoke of the goma fires scorched the sky; spells and sutra-chanting filled the air. The great bell was struck; from village after village people came streaming in without a break; and the temple became one solid mass of ringing, roaring noise.

The day went down, and a clouded, starless night came on.

The dark is the ninja's hour of work.

Kazue, and the sooner the better, thought Goemon, seizing on it. He went home, told Maki of it, and put on the ninja garb he had stripped from the Purple Scale a while before.

He had stripped it from the corpse so that, if the body should ever float up, there would be no telling who it was; and now it served him at once. Dressed in ninja garb for the first time in a long while, the ninja-pouch he had also taken from the Purple Scale tucked into his breast, he felt his whole body draw taut.

Even were he seen, slipping away would be nothing at all: the confidence rose in him. He came upon the kamari many times over. But go as far as he might, he never met the army marching south.

He tore north, and further north, along the road he had traveled that day.

At last, in the hills east of Kumatori, he found the great host bivouacked. So that was it, he understood. Fearing that, if they drove straight on the Negoro-ji, the soldiers of the three forts might take them in the rear, they meant to pass the night here for now; to wait for the three forts to fall in the morning; to cut down the beaten defenders as they came fleeing this way; and only then, with all their forces joined, to fall upon the Negoro-ji.

He circled still further east, went into the hills, and came out behind Hideyoshi's army.

The three forts at Kishiwada were still ringed close, but only now and then did a gun sound; the real fighting, it seemed, would come before dawn.

Takayama Ukon's force lay encamped for the night around Shakuzen-ji.

But nowhere was there any sign of Kazue.

To wear a cross on the breast, perhaps, was the fashion of that day; there were any number of commanders who did.

Dodging the glow of the kamari fires, running this way and that, he heard at last the first cock crow.

And at that signal, the gun-covers were flung open with a rolling roar, the conch-horns brayed, the drums beat, and officers and men sprang up as one.

This time the temper of the fight was nothing like the day before. With fearful force they stormed in from all four sides, tore out the abatis with cries of "Ei! Ei!", trampled the earthworks flat, loosed fire-arrows, and set the watchtowers blazing.

And that done, they threw open an escape to the south of a sudden, and let the terror-driven defenders flee as they would.

Now the beast in man stood bared, and raged.

With it come to this, there was no hope of finding Kazue.

Still, thinking she might have been carried off to the rear, he changed himself into a peasant and searched as far as the outskirts of Sakai. But of her there was no trace, none at all.

The three forts had fallen already; the dead lay strewn thick, the burned watchtowers smoldering. Whipping on his worn-out body, he turned and started south again.

The bodies of warrior-monks and peasant-soldiers who had fled the forts lay scattered in pitiful shapes; set upon, no doubt, by the army that had lain in wait, bivouacked east of Kumatori the night before.

Goemon bore east and went south along the mountain paths as though on wings.

The Negoro-ji was already ringed round tight. It was the very moment before the gun-battle broke.

The night had fully broken, and the twenty-third was some hours old; but the sky hung heavy with lead-gray cloud, a gloomy, darkened day.

By the back hill, where the enemy were still thin, he passed from pine-top to pine-top and slipped in among the temple grounds.

He hurried to the armorer, got himself a gun, and hid, unseen, inside the Great Pagoda.

On the instant, with a roar like a hundred thunderclaps falling together, the besiegers' guns opened fire.

On the crown of every great pine that grew thick across the precincts, peasants sat astride, firing down on the attackers.

Let me set before you how the Taikōki, that book I keep citing, tells of the fighting at this point, rewritten to read more easily.

"Meanwhile the besiegers, thick as cloud and mist, raised the war-cry till it rang through heaven and earth, 'and to call them a multitude were too weak a word'; for the brave men of Yamato had come on in their full numbers, and they loosed their guns and raised their arrow-cries and pressed the attack. Within the temple too the powder and shot had long been ready; and since gunnery was an art first handed down from this very mountain, there were masters and adepts in plenty. They flung down great trees and great stones, and held the ground as though it were the last stand of all. From the start of the Hour of the Dragon, eight in the morning, to the Hour of the Horse, noon and past, they attacked without pause for breath; and the monks too, holding it the great matter of their lives, spared neither life nor limb, spent their whole strength, and crushed their bones to hold; so that the place was not easily stormed, and very many of the besiegers took wounds from arrow and ball. Numerous though they were, the assault dragged on; and Lord Hideyoshi, seeing the hours slip by, at once had the banners and horse-standards furled, and, feigning to be baffled in the attack, drew off. The multitude within the temple marked it keenly. 'Let a Hideyoshi come at us with thousands upon thousands: this is the homeland of Dainichi Nyorai, the pure abode of Fudō Myō-ō; once in the Myō-ō's binding-rope, they can stir neither one way nor the other. After them, and cut them down to the last man!' And no sooner said than, led by rough monks such as Han'nyo, Rendatsu, and Unkai, above two thousand leveled their gun-muzzles and charged with a roar; and the besiegers' ranks broke. 'Chase them, chase them! Turn them back, a great turning-back!' came the command; but then, 'To be hemmed in by a host is ruin: back, quickly, within the palisade, and defend it!' At which the multitude, aghast, saw how neatly they had been gulled. 'The shame of it, the shame!' But before the words were well out, the men of Yamato came shouting on, and the Negoro congregation was routed, and some, having no other course, cut open their bellies.

"The besiegers drove the pursuit hard; and in that moment the two great monks called out their word of counsel, 'Quick, back into the temple and bar the gate,' and stood to meet the attack. These two were men famed through the age for their strength. Six shaku seven or eight sun in height, about two meters and five, stout and heavy, the muscle standing in ropes on arm and shin, and the reddish-black of their skin showing between the armor-plates, they looked for all the world like the guardian Niō at a temple gate come loose and striding forth. From among the besiegers one warrior in black-leather armor stepped forward and cried in a great voice: 'You two who stand there seem the foremost rough monks of Negoro. To be hailed heroes for facing deer-hunters and woodcutters, it is enough to split a man's side. My strength is the less, but my blade cuts keen. Come, I'll hew you through at a single stroke!' And with a four-shaku sword whirled up, he sprang at them. Unkai, taking his chance, swung his naginata round to cut him down and leapt in. Gen'uemon, to fight from surer footing, took the parrying stance and gave ground; and Unkai, thinking, So, Amano is for running, is he? gripped his glaive afresh and roared: 'Among the congregation of Mount Negoro, I am Unkai of the Akai-bō; Awaji-born, and of the Minamoto; five-and-thirty years of age; and of the Kurama-school swordplay handed down to me from Kiichi Hōgen Kenkai, no man holds it but I. The secret art of the glaive I had from Matsumoto Bizen-no-kami of Kashima in Hitachi, a pupil of Iizasa Chōi. Come at me, then, Amano Gen'uemon!' At which Gen'uemon, a short-tempered man, flung back: 'Kiichi or Matsumoto, prate what names you please; I keep no pedigree of the martial arts. To win is the whole of it, is it not? To win—' and so saying he whirled up that great sword and came slashing in. Unkai laughed lightly, wheeled his glaive like a water-wheel, and cut at him, left and right, opening and closing, and they fought some seventy or eighty passes. Both were masters and adepts, and which was the lesser no man could have told. Yet Amano was small of frame and quick; he drove in and struck home hard. With one stroke he cut Unkai's shoulder; and as Unkai reeled, Amano, seizing the moment, swept the blade level once more and cut deep, six or seven sun, twenty to twenty-three centimeters, into Unkai's flank. So sore a wound he could not stand against, and down he crashed with a shaking of the ground; and Amano, quick upon him, struck off his head. 'The Unkai that was but now a demon-god, Amano Gen'uemon has taken his head!' he cried; and Rendatsu, aghast, and thinking the day past saving, ran in through the gate, dragged the abatis across, and held it—"

As I have said more than once, the Shinsho Taikōki is a novel got up in imitation of the Chinese Water Margin and its kind. Take the other Taikōki: the Kawasumi Taikōki, by a Kawasumi who had actually served Hideyoshi, or the Hoan Taikōki, by Oze Hoan, who served him after. For all that these too contrive to make of Hideyoshi a hero, and a hero again, they keep at least a good deal nearer the truth; the Shinsho Taikōki is a far more manufactured thing.

And yet, how should the Shinsho Taikōki, which I pored over as a boy, not have its turn here? Let me follow its telling a little further. A lie, and known for one; but good reading, in its way, for all that.

Around four in the morning, some three thousand who had escaped the slaughter at the three Kishiwada forts came fleeing back. By Hideyoshi's order a gap had been left open, on purpose, in the ring on the back-hill side, and these men were let into the temple. It was a ruse: to pen them there and cut them all down.

Evening came on.

"Lord Hideyoshi gave the command: 'The hour is ripe. Fall on, and quickly!' And Hidenaga's thirteen thousand, planting up their shields and their bamboo mantlets, stormed the attack with cries of 'Ei! Ei!'; the van dragged the abatis aside and pressed jostling to break in, and the rear, not to be left behind, shoved on after. The old monks ran into the Main Hall, bound up Fudō Myō-ō with coarse rope, and, in goma smoke thick enough to blind them, worked the rite of subjugation in a frenzy, back and forth, and prayed, pressing and pressing: 'Now, now, show forth your power!' But it availed nothing; the besiegers broke the palisade and were already massed below the wall. And whether it was that the monstrous rite of subjugation had roused its wrath, the flame at the Myō-ō's back of a sudden deepened to crimson and streamed out; and, marvel of terror, the altar of the honzon shook, and fire blazed up on every side, so that the congregation, aghast, went leaping and leaping into the raging blaze and burned to death black as coal, in a sight no different from looking on the torments of the Burning Hell itself.

"Here two wicked monks, Han'nyo and Enkaku, whirling their great glaives, cut down and mowed under whatever came within reach. Enkaku hewed his way, never turning his face, into the very midst of the Tsutsui force, slashing the cross-cut this way and that, breaking through and springing back, cutting open and dashing clear. Out from the Tsutsui ranks came Shima Sakon, forty-six years old this year, his fighting-blood at the full, and a shrewd hand; and Enkaku, for his part, was the foremost swordsman of Negoro, a rough monk six shaku seven sun tall, strong enough to shift alone a stone that fifty men would move. Chasing and chased, they pushed at one another the better part of a watch, an hour or so; then Enkaku flung his glaive clattering aside and, crying 'Come, let us grapple!' spread his great arms and sprang. Sakon, that he might not be grappled, played him off, bobbing and sinking, and cut, chop, and cut, and opening, cut with a hiss. A swordsman of renown he was, after all. Pushing and pushed in the grapple, Enkaku was cut deep at the shoulder; and as he flinched, Sakon, seizing his chance, drove in, pinned him at the last, and took his head. Han'nyo, seeing Enkaku slain, gave his own life up for lost, cut into the Tsutsui force, scattered them to the four quarters and flung the eight into confusion, and fought; but he too was hemmed in by numbers, and cut down in the melee—"

This is the stuff of the war-tales with which, in later ages, the reciters, up on their platforms in the towns, slapping the desk with their paper fans and lifting their voices, set the blood of the pious men and women aboil.

But the truth was no such gorgeous thing.

It was terror and madness, shrieking and roaring. It was a pitiless stretch of hours in which men had turned back into beasts.
