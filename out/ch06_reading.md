## Chapter Six. The Hour of the Monkey, First Half (3 p.m.)

At that same moment, a crossbow bolt came flying from the other side and struck the earth just beside Cao Poyan's foot. Zhang Xiaojing's figure vaulted into the courtyard, and with one swift roll came to rest on the open ground some thirty paces from Cao Poyan.

The third year of Tianbao, the fourteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Monkey, first half.

Chang'an; Chang'an County; Guangde Ward.

Xu Bin leafed through the records scroll by scroll, his fingers sliding over the rough edges of the paper, the inked characters leaping line by line into his eyes.

A moment ago the Deputy Director had let fall an angry word: "Everything that can be set alight, search the whole of it through for me!" And that had struck a fresh spark of thought in Xu Bin: what could touch off a fire need not be oil alone.

Of the goods carried into Chang'an every day there were, at the least, several hundred kinds, and no small number of them would burn. Following this line, Xu Bin called up the customs declarations of the past several days and went through the classified catalogues, to see whether there was any suspicious bulk shipment of the inflammable.

But search as he might, and long, he came away with nothing.

Inflammable goods there were, and many of them traded in bulk; but when Xu Bin thought it carefully over, he found them all impractical. Firewood took up too much room; paper and reed caught fire and went out as readily; bamboo and timber were too troublesome to haul; candle-tallow, cloth and taffeta, silk and hemp cost too much. To start a fire with such things was easy enough, but to burn the whole of Chang'an to ash in short order was far too hard.

The Jing'an Bureau had earlier run a study of the properties of materials, and had found that oil, and oil alone, was the best means of setting off a great fire fast. It was easy to move in secret, it flowed well, it caught readily, and it burned with a savage heat. If the Türks meant to burn Chang'an this very night, oil was their only choice.

This, at bottom, was still the conclusion the Jing'an Bureau had reached long before.

Dispirited, Xu Bin pushed the documents away and rubbed his aching eyes, telling himself he had simply gone mad with wanting to rise in office. He was about to order a servant to clear away the files when he lifted his elbow, knocked the inkstone at the desk's edge to the floor, and with a crash it shattered into several pieces. The ink flew, splattering everywhere.

Xu Bin stared blankly at the floor, then all at once clapped himself on the head and seized a servant by the arm. He rattled off a string of catalogue numbers and had the man fetch the named files at once. Xu Bin crouched down, but did not pick up the inkstone; instead he ran his fingers through the ink spilled on the floorboards, and his fingertips were soon smeared jet-black. Despite himself, Xu Bin's lips curled upward, and his eyes lit.

The Jing'an Bureau's files were stored in good order and easy to call up. In no time the servant had brought the documents he wanted. Not even waiting to undo the binding-cord, Xu Bin tore it open and skimmed through in haste. He soon found what he was after; and his face passed first to delight, then to astonishment, and at last grew grave.

Documents in hand, he hurried from his seat to the sand-table. Li Bi still stood beside it with his brows knit tight, passing his fly-whisk over and over from his left hand to his right, and from his right hand back to his left.

Xu Bin came up and cupped his hands: "Deputy Director." Li Bi did not so much as raise his head: "What is it?"

"Your servant may... mm, has more or less guessed... ai, ai, what the Türks may be about." Xu Bin said it with a touch of diffidence, though it took nothing at all from the excitement in his voice.

The words moved Li Bi at last, and he turned his face round: "Speak!"

A drumbeat came throbbing from far off, dong, dong, dong, dong, and one watchtower after another took up the same rhythm, the sound drawing nearer by degrees. It was a drumbeat of marked character, low and clear, and it carried far. These were lizard-skin drums, brought in on purpose from Persia and used only for the Jing'an Bureau's dispatches, never to be confused with the tempo-drum, the street-drum, the petitioner's drum, or the like.

As though he felt it in his blood, Zhang Xiaojing's single eye flicked open. Fresh news was coming in, and the drumming was long, which was most unusual.

By now Cui Qi had led the Lüben Guards off to scatter through the wards on their search, and only Yao Runeng was left at Zhang Xiaojing's side. He doubled as their interpreter of the code, and the moment he heard the drums he sprang up and listened with his whole attention.

This dispatch was uncommonly long, and Yao Runeng had to listen and, with his foot, scratch a record on the ground as he went. It was as well that each segment of the message was repeated three times, so that nothing was lost.

The dispatches of Chang'an's watchtowers came in two kinds. The first was the fixed code: three quick beats and one slow, say, meant "reinforcements at hand," five quick and two slow "hold your position," and so on. The second was the rhyme code, built upon the Tang Rhymes compiled by Sun Mian after the twentieth year of Kaiyuan, indexed in order by scroll, rhyme, and character: "two-sixteen-six," for instance, meant scroll two, the sixteenth rhyme, the sixth character, and a glance at the Tang Rhymes told you it was the word "heaven."

The fixed code was fastest, but limited in what it could say; the rhyme code could convey matters a little more involved; and for anything more complex still, a rider had to be sent galloping with a letter.

After a moment a long, lilting horn-note sounded from the watchtower, signaling the dispatch was done. The yellow earth was covered now with a long string of numbers. Yao Runeng drew from his waist his little pocket-copy of the Tang Rhymes and swiftly turned them into words:

"A quantity of Yanzhou rock-oil was declared into the city today as ink-stock; its whereabouts are unknown."

Zhang Xiaojing swept his eye over it and his face changed on the instant. Yao Runeng, somewhat at a loss, hastily asked what the matter was, and what rock-oil might be.

Said Zhang Xiaojing: "When I soldiered in the northwest, I once saw a kind of water. It seeps out from the crevices of the rock, with a skin of black oil floating on its surface, sticky and greasy to the touch, much like the fat of pork, and so it is called rock-oil. The people there gather that floating grease off the top with straw scoops and use it to light fires and give light, and very bright it is."

"So it will burn, then?" said Yao Runeng in wonder. Zhang Xiaojing answered: "Rock-oil does not catch easily; it must be refined by a secret art, and then lit off with burning lard or castor oil to draw the flame. But once it takes, it will not rest until it is done. When we held a fortress in the Western Regions, one jar of rock-oil poured down could carry off dozens of lives at a breath: the grease clings the raging fire fast to a man's body, and no matter what he does he cannot shake it off or put it out. I never saw a fiercer fuel. And so in the army we called it fierce-fire."

For a thing to move even a man as hard as Zhang Xiaojing, the scene of that day must have been ghastly indeed. Yao Runeng drew a sharp cold breath, and then his face altered fast: "Do you mean to say the Türks have already brought a thing so dangerous into the city?" Zhang Xiaojing nodded heavily.

With rock-oil in great quantity, to burn the whole of Chang'an to ash in a single night was entirely possible. The Que-le Huo-duo the Türks spoke of might well be this very thing.

"A thing so dangerous—how could the gate-guards let it in as they pleased?" Yao Runeng cried out.

Zhang Xiaojing said: "Rock-oil is produced only in such places as Jiuquan, Yumen, and Yanzhou; only the local folk and the garrison soldiers know anything of it. The common people of Guanzhong—yourself, for instance—have likely never so much as heard the name. And besides, when the Türks brought the stuff in, they played a trick..." His finger pointed to the two characters "ink-stock."

"Ink-stock?" Yao Runeng did not follow.

"When rock-oil burns, its black smoke is very thick. So over in Yanzhou they commonly make ink from the soot of it, and the Yan ink they produce is of no small fame."

Yao Runeng, well versed in paperwork, understood at once. Rock-oil could be burned, and it could also be made into ink; so when the Wolf Guards came into the city to make their declaration, they had deliberately declared it as "ink-stock." And by the rule of Chang'an, raw material and finished product were filed under one and the same category. And so the customs record of this rock-oil had been openly and respectably classed among the inks.

The Jing'an Bureau was straining every nerve to trace oils and other combustibles, but it never crossed anyone's mind to look under inks—for ink, of all things, will not burn!

The Türks had cunningly turned this blind spot of the mind to account, and slipped the whole thing past under the very heavens. Even had someone set out to look, it would have been hard to catch the fraud in the customs records.

"These fellows really are too crafty—they can think up even so vile a stratagem." Yao Runeng sighed out in indignation. Hearing the remark, Zhang Xiaojing knit his brows, with a vague sense of something that did not fit. He had been a buliang chief for many years, and his nose for a contradiction had always been keen.

But now was no time to dwell on such things; the urgent business was to find where the Wolf Guards had gone to ground, and find it fast.

"By your account, rock-oil should be a black, sticky grease; if any spilled on the ground, it ought to be quite plain to see. Shall we look for spill-marks on the roads hereabouts?" Yao Runeng proposed.

Zhang Xiaojing shook his head. Since the Türks had the means to bring the rock-oil in, they would surely have guarded against just this. A few layers of dry straw packed under the sealed wooden casks would be enough to ensure nothing was spilled.

"Then... what's to be done?"

Zhang Xiaojing patted the hound at his side: "Rock-oil gives off a stinging smell, heavier still when it burns. So it will do only for torches out of doors; you cannot burn it for a candle indoors or cook a meal by it—no help for it, it is far too choking. We can try to hunt out an odd smell nearby."

Yao Runeng's face brightened, but a doubt came to him at once: "The dog has to have something to go by before it can search. Where are we to get it a sniff of rock-oil?"

Zhang Xiaojing put out his hand and pointed west: "The Jinguang Gate."

The Jinguang Gate stood at the midpoint of Chang'an's west side, one street east of the West Market; it was the road every caravan coming from the west had to pass. A convoy hauling rock-oil, coming from Yanzhou, must certainly have entered the city by it.

"By the inspection routine, the guards run a long spear down into each cask to make sure no one is hidden inside. The stuff is hard to wash off; have the gate-guards find that spear, and it will be enough," said Zhang Xiaojing.

The Jinguang Gate was a long way off, and at the word Yao Runeng at once mounted to ride there, only to be stopped by Zhang Xiaojing: "You need not go. If I guess right, the Jing'an Bureau's express rider should be here soon, and will bring us the thing we want." So saying, he looked toward the empty end of the street, full of confidence.

"You're so sure of it?"

"Because the Deputy Director has no choice but to do it," said Zhang Xiaojing lightly.

Yao Runeng made no secret of his reverence for Li Bi: "The Deputy Director is a genius born of heaven! A trap as clever as this rock-oil-for-ink, and even that he saw through."

Zhang Xiaojing smiled faintly and did not correct him. The seeing-through of the rock-oil was surely Xu Bin's doing. The two of them had eaten together in the old days, when he had spoken of the ways and customs of the army in the Western Regions and had mentioned, in passing, this curious stuff called rock-oil. He had never thought Xu Bin's memory so good that he would remember it still.

He had not many friends in Chang'an, and Xu Bin was the one he had known the longest. If the fellow could win great merit by this chance, and cast off his commoner's cloth for an official's robe and rank, it would fulfill a wish he had cherished for years.

"I only hope we're in time. We've lost far too much of it." Zhang Xiaojing murmured, gazing at the sky as it slowly dimmed. Seeing the anxiety on his face, Yao Runeng was touched in spite of himself. He had begun full of suspicion toward this condemned man, but through one thing after another he had found that he was wrong: whatever might be said for or against Zhang Xiaojing's every move, there was not a trace of self-interest in it, and he had come near to losing his life for it besides.

Yao Runeng hesitated a moment, then all at once cupped his hands and dropped to one knee: "Your servant harbored much suspicion of you before, Commander Zhang, and asks to be punished for it. I beg that you, Commander Zhang, will not, for one man's fault, hold a grudge in your heart and let the great work of the Jing'an Bureau suffer for it."

With no little interest Zhang Xiaojing regarded the young man's flushed face: "You feel, don't you, that my throwing myself into this heart and soul is not quite natural—is that it?"

"Yes. Your servant took it that you spoke against your heart, Commander Zhang, and were surely after something." Yao Runeng owned it straight out. For the safety of all Chang'an? Had Li Bi given the reason, he would have believed it; but from a condemned man who bore a grudge against the court, it rang all too false.

In his eyes, Zhang Xiaojing's pursuit of the case was a screen, and his watching for a chance to flee the truth of it; that was what accorded with the common run of human nature. But now... Yao Runeng felt his cheeks burn and sting. He wanted to flee the awkwardness of the scene, and yet could not; for unless he apologized to Zhang Xiaojing frankly, Yao Runeng feared he would never in his life forgive that foolish self of his.

Zhang Xiaojing neither raised him up nor spoke a word of mockery; he only stroked the crown of the slender hound at his feet and slowly lifted his head. His gaze passed over Yao Runeng's shoulder to the great and towering Wild Goose Pagoda in the distance, and for a moment his eyes grew deep.

"Runeng, have you ever climbed to the top of the Wild Goose Pagoda, around the time of Grain Rain?"

Yao Runeng started, not understanding why he should bring this up so suddenly.

"There's a little novice up there who keeps the pagoda; give him half a string of cash and he'll let you sneak up to the top and look your fill on all the peonies of Chang'an. The little novice never spends the money he saves on anything foolish; he always buys river-fish on the sly to feed the kittens by the Ci'en Temple." Zhang Xiaojing spoke slowly, a thread of a smile at the corner of his mouth.

Yao Runeng was about to open his mouth to ask, when Zhang Xiaojing went on: "In Shengdao Ward there's an old Uyghur who makes nothing but bilo-cakes; he picks out great fat sesame seeds, so the cakes are wonderfully fragrant fresh from the oven. When I was on duty in the old days, I'd hurry over first thing and wait at the ward gate, and buy a few the moment it opened." He clicked his tongue, as though savoring it yet. "And there's the wild-rice at the Puji Temple, only to be had on the first and the fifteenth; the monks slip in a little meat-fat on the quiet, and it tastes uncommonly good."

"Commander Zhang, what is all this...?"

"Aluoyue of the East Market is a fine hand at breaking camels; his life's dream is to set up a business in Anyi Ward, take a wife, raise children, and put down roots in Chang'an for good. In Changxing Ward lives a Court Music player surnamed Xue, a man of Luling, who on every cloudless clear midnight must go up onto the Tianjin Bridge to play his flute, only that the moonlight may wash the sound of it; I covered for him more than once when he broke the night-curfew. And there's a dancing-girl who lives in Chongren Ward, called Li Shi'er, who burns to rival the Lady Gongsun of the old days. She has danced her heels raw at her practice, till she has to bind them in red silk. Oh, and yes—when they float the river-lanterns at the Ghost Festival, the whole river is candlelight. If you walk along the Longshou Canal, you'll see a blind old woman hawking folded paper boats along the water, saying she's saving for a pair of bronze hairpins for her granddaughter; but I know that her granddaughter died of sickness long ago."

As he told over these people and things that hung together not at all, Zhang Xiaojing's voice ran long and his single eye shone: "I was a buliang chief in Chang'an for nine years, and the folk I dealt with every day were people just like these, and the life I heard and saw every day was a life just like this. To the great and the noble these people count for nothing at all, and these matters are the merest commonplace; but to me, this is the living Chang'an, the Chang'an that no monster has yet devoured. It is only at their side that I feel myself alive."

Here his voice sank a little lower: "If the Türks have their way, the first to lose their lives will be people just like these. For the sake of these people who count for nothing, living their commonplace lives, I will do all that I can. What I want to protect is a Chang'an like this—can you understand me, when I put it so?"

Faced with this sudden candor, Yao Runeng felt his heart surge and had no words to answer. The man's cast of mind was strange indeed: resentful of the court, yet full of pity for the common people of Chang'an. How was one to reckon the two words "loyalty and honor" in his case?

"Have you... always thought this way?"

Zhang Xiaojing bared his teeth in something between a smile and none: "Ten years a soldier in the Western Regions, nine years a chief in Chang'an. What do you think?"

Just then hooves came pounding in the distance, dust rolling up, and the two men returned at once to the business at hand. In a moment a rider flew up and handed them the message-tube at his waist and a long, wooden-hafted spear. Yao Runeng took the spear; the point was indeed spotted with black stains, and one sniff of it was rank and stinging. Zhang Xiaojing opened the message-tube and drew out a slip of paper covered with writing.

"Headquarters has it clear now: the hauling was done by Su's Cart-and-Horse Company. They came into the city somewhere around the hour of the Horse, but then vanished without trace; the foreman, the drivers, and the carts never reported back to the firm." Zhang Xiaojing crushed the slip into a ball and said heavily: "I'd guess they've most likely been silenced. The carts have been wiped clean of any trace too; look for them now and you'll not find them."

This time Yao Runeng did not swell with the same indignation. For one thing, he thought that fellows who hauled cargo for the enemy deserved to die; for another, after these hours of hard running about, he had grown numb to the cruelty of the Wolf Guards.

Zhang Xiaojing let the hound sniff the spear-point and patted its head. The hound first gave a displeased sneeze, then craned its neck, worked its nose, and barked several times fiercely in one direction. Had Zhang Xiaojing not held the leash, it would have bolted.

"No time to lose; I'll go on ahead. You wait for Commander Cui to gather his men and follow after, with yellow smoke for the signal."

Yao Runeng looked around him and only then realized they had made a middling sort of blunder. Cui Qi, in his eagerness to redeem himself by merit, had just broken the Lüben Guards up into small parties and scattered them through the surrounding wards. To gather the troops together again now would take a while.

Which was to say that, until it was done, Zhang Xiaojing would stand alone and without support.

"You're wounded, and going alone besides—isn't it too dangerous?" Yao Runeng was somewhat worried.

"Every man has to answer for his own choices."

Zhang Xiaojing answered simply and loosed the leash. The hound shot away, and he strode out at a great pace, hard on its heels. Yao Runeng watched man and dog vanish round the corner of the ward-wall, and for an instant his mind went blank.

The smell of rock-oil was especially sharp, so the hound tracked it without a moment's hesitation. It threaded and turned through the ward, running flat out, and Zhang Xiaojing had to run with all his strength to keep up. The passers-by round about watched man and hound with curiosity, taking it for some new bit of tumbling, and there were even some at the sides who cheered.

The hound ran more than two li at a stretch, though it was checked several times along the way. It knew only to follow the scent in a straight line and understood nothing of going round; more than once it plunged headlong into a dead-end alley and barked at the high wall, and Zhang Xiaojing had to haul it out and cast about afresh.

When at last, with much labor, they had chased the trail to a certain ward gate, the hound stopped, scuffed back and forth over the ground a few times, and whined dispiritedly.

The smell gave out here, and the hound could track it no further; too long a time had passed, after all.

But this was enough.

Zhang Xiaojing hurriedly put the leash back on it and, with a fine cord, bound up its long muzzle; if this were truly the Wolf Guards' hiding-place, its barking might startle them.

Zhang Xiaojing glanced at the wooden board hung before the ward gate, on which stood the three characters "Changming Ward." Weeds grew thick everywhere along the foot of the wall and before its sill, and the dirt road at the gate bore few cart-ruts—plainly the households here were few, and the place was desolate and still. This ward did not even have a watchtower of the Jing'an Bureau's own; the budget, after all, was limited, and priority had to go first to the thickly peopled wards of the north, so a bleak ward like this was, for the moment, left out of account.

Which meant that, should anything happen, there was no way to send word out in time.

Zhang Xiaojing cast his mind back, but could not recall any notable building in this ward—if only Xu Bin were here; that fellow remembered everything. He slackened his pace and walked slowly in. There was not a single guard near the ward gate; they had all, no doubt, gone off to keep the Lantern Festival. Changming Ward stood now wholly open, and anyone at all might come and go as he pleased.

It was a superb place to hide, and no mistake. Once inside the ward, Zhang Xiaojing let the leash half-slack in his left hand, holding the hound in check as it edged forward a little at a time, while his eyes searched left and right and his right hand gripped the pocket crossbow, ready to loose at any instant.

If the Wolf Guards had really stored the rock-oil here, then he had by now entered the enemy's ring of scouts. But Zhang Xiaojing was not greatly worried; should anything be amiss, a smoke pellet flung out would mark the spot. Even if the Türks themselves fled, there would be no time to carry the rock-oil away.

Without the rock-oil, the Türk Wolf Guards were no more than a pack of cornered thugs at the end of their road.

Before Zhang Xiaojing lay a crossroads. In the northern city this would have been the busiest of spots, the streets sure to be lined with shops. But this crossroads in Changming Ward held only a scattered few earthen huts, screened by a great stretch of bare-branched pagoda-tree wood. Among the trees were a few roving peddlers, their pack-mules and handcarts strewn every which way, and there were more selling wares than strolling to buy. To the right of the wood stood an earthen rise, and on its top a small courtyard with a great gourd hung at its gate.

Rather than a residential ward within the city of Chang'an, the place might better be called the open country of the far outskirts.

In so desolate a spot, a great convoy of carts coming in ought to have been conspicuous indeed. Zhang Xiaojing was minded to go closer and make inquiries, when all at once the hound dropped its forelegs to the ground and gave out a low, growling snarl. His single eye sharpened, and he marked three figures drawing in nearby.

Swiftly Zhang Xiaojing slipped a hand into his breast and drew the pocket crossbow half out, every muscle taut and gathered to spring. Only as the figures came near did he see them clearly: all of them in the garb of beggars, each in a ragged old robe and tattered jacket, hands tucked into their sleeves, sallow-faced and thin.

That famished, greenish look was not to be got up without months without meat; it was assuredly no disguise thrown on for the moment. So Zhang Xiaojing let his shoulders ease a little, though his hand still gripped the crossbow's catch. These beggars fixed their eyes on Zhang Xiaojing, coming no nearer and drawing no further off, but keeping always a distance of a little over twenty paces as they followed close behind.

Zhang Xiaojing gave a cold snort and quickened his step, and the beggars came on after him. He stopped all at once at a stall selling fern-root cakes and bought one; the beggars hastily halted where they were and pretended to gaze about them. Zhang Xiaojing tossed the vendor a few copper cash and turned into an alley ahead, walled in half-collapsed brick.

The beggars followed hard behind; but the first of them, no sooner round the turn, was startled to find that all that remained in the alley was a dog trailing its leash.

He looked about him in some bewilderment, wondering where in the world the man had got to. In the next instant a burst of grey powder flew suddenly into his face, forcing him to screw his eyes shut. And at that a figure dropped down from the wall-top and chopped a hand-blade down on the back of his neck, so that he pitched flat to the ground and could not stir.

This grey powder was plant-ash, which Zhang Xiaojing had scooped up off the stall just now, in passing, when he bought the fern-root cake. Fern-root eaten raw would give one a bellyache, and had to be boiled together with plant-ash to draw out the poison, so the vendors of fern-root cakes always kept some by them.

To deal with such petty riff-raff, there was no need yet to bring out crossbow or steel blade.

The two beggars behind, seeing their fellow set upon, had for their first thought to turn and run. Zhang Xiaojing bent and caught up two bricks, swung his arm, and struck them full on the backs of their heads, so that the two of them tumbled to the ground one after the other. The hound rushed over and savagely tore at their sleeves. The beggars cried out in alarm and flailed the bamboo staves in their hands to no purpose.

Zhang Xiaojing walked over, drew the steel blade in his hand, and slowly leveled it at the throat of one of them, as though waiting for something. And at that moment a voice came urgently from among the trees: "Hold your blade, I beg you!"

A smile of deep meaning showed at Zhang Xiaojing's lips; he drew the blade back three inches and turned his head aside, to see a beggar in a flowered-gauze lined futou standing under a tree not far off, looking his way.

"They only did it on another's charge, and have no quarrel with you, sir. Let their three dog-lives go, and Jia Shiqi will surely see you repaid." This head of the beggars, who called himself Jia Shiqi, was decisive enough; the moment he saw the signs turn bad he had shown himself to stop it.

Zhang Xiaojing had been a buliang chief for nine years, and knew that these city foxes and gutter rats had eyes and ears all over the town, well-informed, so that even the authorities sometimes sought them out for intelligence. That he should today be trailed by beggars for no cause at all meant that someone was surely pulling the strings behind them. Force out the head of these people, and the matter would be far easier to handle.

Zhang Xiaojing neither withdrew his blade nor spoke, but only fixed the man coldly with his single eye. Jia Shiqi's face changed a little: one look at this fellow's dress told you he was of the government offices, yet an ordinary runner, once he heard the word "repaid," would let the thing drop—so how was it that this one came on, from the first, with a mind to kill?

He had meant to say another word, when all at once he felt the newcomer's face somewhat familiar—above all that dried-out socket on the left, from which a grim killing air breathed out. Jia Shiqi turned it over in his mind, and of a sudden a certain name came to him.

"You're... Zhang the Yama, of Wannian County?"

Changming Ward lay in the southwest of Chang'an, under Chang'an County; but the eyes and ears of the beggars were not so bounded as that. The Five-Faced Yama of Wannian County—vicious, venomous, ruthless, stubborn, pitiless—named not five men but one. This one-eyed dragon was a hard case to be avoided if at all one could.

"Who set you to follow me?" said Zhang Xiaojing lightly.

Jia Shiqi's mind raced: rumor had it this man was already down in the death cells, so the rumor was plainly false. He cupped his hands: "Had we known it was Chief Zhang, how should we ever have had the nerve? We're out of this business; we won't wade into it further."

"Who was it?"

Jia Shiqi forced a smile: "You know how it is, sir—that I can't say. It's the rule of the rivers and lakes."

Zhang Xiaojing reversed the barrier-knife and drove it downward. With a scream the point went into one beggar's thigh and was pulled out again, and blood spurted. Jia Shiqi's mouth twitched and his face went dark: "These three rotten lives—if you can let them go, sir, every beggar in Chang'an will bless your name."

Turned the other way about, the words meant that if he did not let them go, every beggar in Chang'an would become his enemy.

With a soft thud the second stroke drove cleanly into flesh. Zhang Xiaojing was a condemned man, and of all things a threat like this frightened him least. He said not a word, but only stabbed away at the luckless beggars, stroke by stroke, their screams rising and falling without pause and building an unseen, enormous pressure.

And as it happened not one of the three wretches died; each of them was howling at the top of his voice. Zhang the Yama had held his hand on purpose, that the other beggars beyond the wood might hear.

This put Jia Shiqi in a hard place indeed. Among beggars, nothing was prized above sticking together: a man might die in a cell, or freeze, or be beaten to death by some rich house, but he must not be done to death by his own kind. If Jia Shiqi stood by and let them die, he would likely lose every heart to him thereafter. This Zhang the Yama, for all he seemed a mere brute, understood the beggars' inner ways to the bone.

It cost him little struggle to make his choice. A mere silver wine-flask was not worth having the beggars stake their lives to keep the secret. Besides, he had marked a black hand-crossbow hung at Zhang the Yama's waist—a weapon used only in the army, with, behind it, likely some still more formidable power.

"All right, all right, I'll tell you!"

Jia Shiqi hid it no longer, and came out of the wood with his hands raised. He told Zhang Xiaojing that a foreigner had given him a silver wine-flask and set them to watch the ward gate; should any suspicious person come into the ward, they were to go to the King of Rinan's mansion and bring him word.

"The King of Rinan's mansion?"

"Yes, in the southeast corner of this ward. In the Zhenguan years a King of Rinan came to court and raised a great mansion here; afterwards he went home to his own country and the mansion fell derelict, but the ground it covers is no small stretch."

The description answered well to what the Türks would want in a hiding-place: out of the way, spacious, and with rooms enough. Zhang Xiaojing asked a few more questions about the man's looks and dress, and Jia Shiqi, giving it up, told all he knew, which tallied closely with Cao Poyan. When he had heard him out, Zhang Xiaojing clapped him on the shoulder and signed to him to lead the way.

Knowing that protest would do no good, Jia Shiqi could only send the three luckless beggars back first to the dispensary, propping one another up, and himself led Zhang Xiaojing and the hound off toward the ruined garden of the King of Rinan.

Changming Ward was truly out of the way and forlorn; the houses to either side of the inner street were few and far between, most of it pitted earthen slopes and woodland, with, of all things, a few patches of cropland and pools of water besides. Just because the land was worth so little, the ward covered at least half again the ground of a northern ward. So though they walked within a single ward, it cost their legs no small journey.

Halfway along, Zhang Xiaojing asked suddenly: "Did you see a great many carts come into the ward today?"

"You're joking, sir—not even a bird would drop its dung here; there might not be a single one in a whole day." Then, seeing his face begin to darken again, Jia Shiqi hastily amended: "Today, for certain, we saw none; whatever stirs at the ward gate can't slip past us brothers' eyes."

Zhang Xiaojing knit his brows and said no more.

The two men and the dog walked the better part of a quarter-hour before they came before the King of Rinan's ruined garden. Broken walls and crumbling battlements stood here, rank with weeds. But the main shape of the inner courtyard's gate was still there: two door-leaves, their black lacquer peeling, shut fast, and on the lintel a peony carved in stone, its lines fine and delicate, in which one could dimly make out the lavish airs of former days.

Jia Shiqi said the foreigner's charge had been that, the moment any suspicious person from outside the ward was found coming in, they were to come here and report it as fast as they could. There was no need to knock; they were to push the door and go straight in.

Zhang Xiaojing slipped aside and hid by the gate, holding the slender hound and drawing the hand-crossbow. Screwing up his courage, Jia Shiqi stood before the courtyard gate and, by their agreed plan, put both hands to push the door. There was no lock; it opened at a light touch, and then came a click, as though something inside the door had dropped to the ground. Before Jia Shiqi could so much as look, a column of yellow smoke had already gone up.

Zhang Xiaojing was greatly alarmed; he yanked Jia Shiqi aside and rushed in first. Lowering his head, he saw a smoke pellet on the ground still pouring out thick smoke, with a short length of fine cord tied to it. He hurriedly threw the pellet into a rain-pool nearby, but the yellow smoke already risen was drifting and swaying up into the sky, conspicuous indeed beneath the clear heavens.

Zhang Xiaojing turned and asked sharply: "His going back to the King of Rinan's ruined garden—did you see it with your own eyes, or did he say so himself?" Jia Shiqi said the man had come to the dispensary in person to give the charge and had then left, and that he had not himself seen him return to the ruined garden.

Zhang Xiaojing gave a "Hah." These Wolf Guards were sly indeed! Cao Poyan had never trusted the beggars from the very first; he had deliberately given a false address, so that even should the Jing'an Bureau trace the trail this far, the beggars would only steer them off in the wrong direction.

That smoke pellet must be the one the Türks had taken off Zhang Xiaojing's own body. It had been fastened behind the door-leaf, so that once the door was pushed, it gave off its smoke of itself. Thus the Wolf Guards hidden in their true refuge would have warning at once, and win time to withdraw.

A small contrivance, and it killed two birds with one stone: it both misled the Jing'an Bureau and warned the Wolf Guards. Cao Poyan had used this one smoke pellet to the very utmost indeed.

Now that the yellow smoke was up, the Türks had likely begun making ready to run, while the Jing'an Bureau's troops were still slow to gather. Zhang Xiaojing gripped Jia Shiqi hard by both shoulders and said urgently: "Where else in this ward is there a large garden or a great mansion? The one furthest from the King of Rinan's ruined garden."

Jia Shiqi thought a moment: "This is the southeast corner; the furthest off would be a brick-and-tile kiln in the northwest corner, though it's been shut down a long while." A fierce light flashed in Zhang Xiaojing's single eye; he had the man sketch out the route in rough, took two steps away, then turned suddenly back: "Go straight back to the ward gate now, and if you see any government runner or Lüben Guardsman come, stop them and send them to the brick-and-tile kiln!"

Jia Shiqi tucked his hands and smiled: "Chief Zhang, the emperor doesn't work a starving—" Before he could finish, Zhang Xiaojing said coldly: "The ones who set you to keep watch are Türks; they mean to raise a rebellion in Chang'an."

At these words the color drained from Jia Shiqi's face, and only now did he grasp how great a calamity he had brought on himself. Let a charge of "colluding with foreign bandits" come down, and not one beggar of Changming Ward could hope to live. Neither the Ministry of Justice nor the Court of Judicial Review would trouble to inquire whether the thing were a wrong done; what they wanted was to seize a batch of culprits so as to "have something to show."

He caught Zhang Xiaojing by the arm and pleaded piteously: "My one life is no loss, but those brothers are innocent—benefactor, save us!" Zhang Xiaojing looked at him and sighed: "Say afterward that you grew suspicious at the sight of the bandits and came to inform on them to me; that may save your life." Then he murmured one more word to him, thrust him abruptly away, and, hound in hand, ran off at a great stride.

Jia Shiqi took off his flowered-gauze lined futou; his head was soaked through with sweat. Zhang Xiaojing's words meant that he was willing to help round out the lie for him; whether it would answer was all a matter of luck. He gazed blankly after the retreating figure, then, as if waking from a dream, clapped the flowered-gauze futou any-old-how onto his head and pelted off toward the ward gate.

Zhang Xiaojing ran a dozen-odd paces, then slipped the dog's leash. There was no need now to fear beating the grass and startling the snake; he had to be led by the hound's nose. The hound had long been fretful and restless, and the moment the cord was loosed it charged off as if broken free, running straight for the northwest.

A man might not yet catch it, but to a dog's nose the smell of rock-oil here was already strong, and strongest of all to the northwest—no less than a bright lamp in the dark of night.

They ran on a slant the whole way, crossing the better part of the inner ward, until far off they could see a brick chimney standing up—the sure mark of a kiln furnace. Coming a little closer, they saw a high, curving wall barring the road, its bricks faintly blackened, the sign of long years close by a furnace's fierce heat.

This must be the brick-and-tile kiln Jia Shiqi had spoken of. A smooth little path of yellow earth wound off toward a wooden gate, with trees run wild to either side, all out of any order.

Zhang Xiaojing slowed his step and called the hound back, taking a moment to catch his breath. It would likely be some while yet before the Jing'an Bureau's men gathered here.

If rock-oil were stockpiled here, the guard would certainly be no small one, and he must go carefully.

He edged forward a few more paces, feeling his way, until the greater part of his body stood on the yellow-earth road. By rights there should be an outer lookout here, which ought long since to have spotted his movements. But there was not a stir from beyond the wall; all was silent yet.

No—the guard must be few, Zhang Xiaojing changed his mind.

If hands were plentiful, the Wolf Guards would never have hired beggars to keep watch, still less rigged some contrivance at the King of Rinan's ruined garden. That they had schemed so hard laid bare the very straits the Wolf Guards were in, pulled every way at once.

Zhang Xiaojing reckoned it over in his head. In the Lüben Guards' raid on the West Market that morning, fifteen men had been killed; he had slain one before the Zoroastrian temple, and in Xiuzheng Ward five had been done away with in all—which, added together, made twenty-one. That number was at the least half of the Türk Wolf Guards who had slipped into Chang'an. The Türks were too poor; they had no means to throw in any more.

To hold so large a kiln-yard with the men that were left, and see to the unloading of the rock-oil besides, was strained past all reason.

Zhang Xiaojing drew a deep breath and resolved to go in alone before the reinforcements came. It would at the least throw the enemy's dispositions into disorder and win time enough. The weightier reason was that he had to find Wen Ran before the Jing'an Bureau's relief arrived.

He carefully tethered the hound to one side, rubbed the fur of its neck affectionately, and rose again to his feet. The fierce killing air forged in the Western Regions broke violently out of him. Zhang Xiaojing rolled up his sleeves and checked the hand-crossbow one last time. On his left forearm showed a length of tattoo—a broken blade, its spine snapped through at the middle, the strokes rude and plain and hard.

"Wen Wuji, old friend, our Eighth Company is to fight the Türks again. If your spirit is watching from on high, you must guard your daughter well."

Zhang Xiaojing's voice was something between a sigh and a prayer. The light in that single eye grew fiercer still. He drew two smoke pellets from the pouch at his waist, swung both arms, and flung them out.

Two columns of yellow smoke spiraled straight up.

On the inner side of the curving wall, a bare thirty-odd paces from Zhang Xiaojing, Cao Poyan stood with a hand shading his eyes, gazing off to the southeast. There several wisps of yellow smoke had not yet been scattered by the north wind.

So the Jing'an Bureau's men had come into Changming Ward. For this Cao Poyan had long been prepared in his mind; he even felt they had come a little slower than he had thought they would. He had already sent word of it into the warehouse, and Long Bo had said the work here was all but done.

The timing was just right indeed.

What came next, then—let it be carried out to plan.

Cao Poyan locked fast the great door of the warehouse from this side, then took out the captured hand-crossbow and ran his forefinger along the edge of its bolt-groove. In truth he did not care for this weapon, at once sly and stingy; a horseman's bow of the steppe was far more to his taste. But his arm was hurt, and now, even with a bow in hand, he could no longer draw it.

How he longed to loose one more shot at a gazelle out on the steppe... Cao Poyan narrowed his eyes and studied the old calluses at the web of his thumb. These hands, it seemed, would never again have the chance to grip a bow.

With two swift rushes, two columns of yellow smoke rose up on the far side of the curving wall; which told him the enemy was close at hand.

He put away his musings, and his eyes turned cold—like a wolf in winter.

He was a man whose crown-lock had been shorn away; he had no right to flee, and was doomed only to hold this place to the death, and buy the warehouse time with his life. Cao Poyan touched the necklace with his hand, as though he would draw strength from it to meet the last battle of his life.

The great gate stayed quiet, and then all at once a human head popped up over the wall-top. Cao Poyan raised his arm and loosed a bolt, ducking sidewise in the same motion. The bolt struck the head square, but gave off the sound of driving into a bundle of straw. At that same moment, a crossbow bolt came flying from the other side and struck the earth just beside Cao Poyan's foot. Zhang Xiaojing's figure vaulted into the courtyard, and with one swift roll came to rest on the open ground some thirty paces from Cao Poyan.

The two men shifted their stance and, eye meeting eye, realized they had made the same mistake. Each had thought himself the few against the many; but at the first exchange each found the other was, of all things, only a single man.

"Cao Poyan?" Zhang Xiaojing called out his false name. This villain who had set the whole of Chang'an on edge had been run down again at last by the Jing'an Bureau. "Put down your weapon, and there's still a chance to live!"

Cao Poyan made no answer; he threw aside the spent crossbow and drew the dagger at his waist. Chang'an's control of weapons was too strict; but for a few shoddy short crossbows, the only thing the Wolf Guards had ever fought with was the dagger. Zhang Xiaojing too swiftly cast away his spent crossbow; before a strong foe there could be no leisure to re-cock, and it was better to go straight into a fight of naked blades.

The barrier-knife in his hand, though light and short, was still a good deal longer than a dagger; the advantage lay on his side.

Zhang Xiaojing used the blade-art of the Great Tang army: straight in and straight out, plain and hard and fierce. By rights, in such a case, Cao Poyan ought to have sprung in close and pressed the attack; but instead he fought a slow, roving fight, in no hurry at all. Such a strategy would, to be sure, keep him from harm for the moment, but neither could he hope to harm his foe by it.

After several passes, Zhang Xiaojing suddenly saw that the other did not fear death—he was playing for time! His single eye flicked to what lay behind Cao Poyan, and he saw it was a great wooden warehouse, its doors shut fast and an iron lock hung outside.

"This is bad—he's buying his fellows time to withdraw!"

The thought no sooner struck Zhang Xiaojing than his barrier-knife pressed the attack more fiercely still. Cao Poyan gripped his dagger and parried with all his might, the clang and clash of their blows filling the whole courtyard. Zhang Xiaojing, after all, had hacked his way out of mountains of corpses and seas of blood, and was rich in experience; he soon saw that his opponent's left elbow seemed to be hurt, for however he moved it he kept it at an odd angle.

So he deliberately laid on harder against the left, and it struck Cao Poyan full in his weak spot. The other, parrying on this side and falling short on that, soon took several cuts—not mortal wounds, but as one side waxed and the other waned, in a duel of masters he swiftly began to show the marks of defeat.

Just then a confusion of footsteps came from outside the courtyard, and the great gate was slammed open with a crash. Standing outside it was Cui Qi, bearing a battering-log on his own shoulder, glaring like a wrathful guardian-god, and dozens of Lüben Guards soldiers came swarming in past him on either side.

So Jia Shiqi had got the word through in time.

This sudden turn made Cao Poyan's movements stall for one slight instant. Zhang Xiaojing swept his barrier-knife toward his throat. Cao Poyan's reaction was very quick; he shot backward and barely dodged it. But the necklace of colored stones at his neck flew up sharply and met the blade's edge head-on.

Where the blade passed, the cord parted, and the little stones strung on it scattered and fell. And now Cao Poyan did a thing beyond all expectation: he thrust off backward with his foot and pitched his whole body forward again, reaching to snatch at the colored stones. There came a soft thut, and Zhang Xiaojing's blade-point ran his belly clean through.

Yet Cao Poyan's movement did not falter. Still he swung his arm with all his strength, straining to catch even a single one. But the colored stones had already fallen to the ground and rolled every which way. He threw up his head and gave out from his mouth a Türk syllable of no clear meaning, seemingly someone's name, though none there could make it out.

And so, with the barrier-knife driven through him, Cao Poyan slowly let his head sink down.

Zhang Xiaojing was startled: Cao Poyan must not die—too many things waited on him for their answers. He did not dare pull the blade free, and could only grip the hilt with one hand and hold Cao Poyan's shoulder with the other, and, leaning close to his ear, demanded urgently: "The woman you seized—where is she?" But there was no response at all. Zhang Xiaojing noticed of a sudden that a patch of hair had been shorn from the crown of this Wolf Guard's head, baring the scalp.

By Türk custom, a man whose crown-lock had been shorn was as one whose soul had been taken from him beforehand. No wonder Cao Poyan had set his heart on death; he had long since been a dead man.

Zhang Xiaojing shook him by the shoulder in a rage, trying to rouse him, but the Wolf Guard's body slumped limply down.

Beside the two of them, a great press of Lüben Guards soldiers rushed past, making straight for the warehouse.

"Break down the door!"

A voice full of vigor rang out across the courtyard. Cui Qi had got his spirits back by now; to his mind Cao Poyan was only a small fry, whose life or death mattered nothing, and the real feast lay in the warehouse before them.

This warehouse had been made over from the kiln's storehouse, its doors all of brittle pear-wood, which could not be held as any stronghold. A dozen-odd Lüben Guards flung themselves at it; the soldier in the lead gave the door a shove and found it barred from within and locked without. Not waiting at all for the battering-log to be brought up, they raised their blades and hacked hard down along the seam of the door. A great blade came down with savage force; it first sheared through the lock, then cut better than halfway through the wooden bar set crosswise within—but the hundred-forged steel blade was itself snapped clean off.

Another soldier drove his foot in hard, and with a clang forced the great door bodily open. In pairs, shoulder to shoulder, crossbows leveled, more than a dozen of them filed into the warehouse.

The moment they were inside, the air was so choking it could knock a man off his feet. The soldiers steadied their nerves before they took stock of the place. It was a bare, spacious storehouse; in the center stood two great earthen jars, stone lids weighting their mouths, and beneath them a rough stove had been raised of stones and firewood, its fire burning strong. Over the jars and the stove alike lay drops of black stain, and on the floor were many fine bamboo shavings.

At the far end of the storehouse were two more great doors, standing open, and at their threshold a loading platform four feet above the ground, empty and without a soul on it. The soldiers glanced at one another, doubt on every face, and edging their steps forward warily, kept their crossbows leveled.

The hound tethered outside the courtyard suddenly began to bark for no reason; Zhang Xiaojing worked his nose, hastily let go of Cao Poyan's corpse, and roared at Cui Qi: "Call your men out, quick! Quick!" Cui Qi was baffled: "Commander Zhang, don't fret; I think this time—"

Before the words were out, a violent explosion burst all at once from within the warehouse, deafening. The building swelled outward in an instant, and searing flame roared out of doors and windows; on the moment a wave of heat spread on every side, as though from the alchemy-furnace of Lord Lao. The soldiers standing near outside the warehouse were caught unprepared and knocked sprawling to the ground, and even those further off felt a faint scorching on their faces, and suffered greatly.

Everyone in the courtyard was stunned senseless by this sudden calamity; for a full ten snaps of the fingers not a soul reacted, all of them frozen where they stood like wooden puppets, their ears ringing. Only when Cui Qi's near-despairing roar rose over the courtyard did the crowd wake as from a dream and set to, all hands, at rescuing the wounded.

Cui Qi looked in alarm to Zhang Xiaojing; before the blast he had shouted for them to pull back, and so must surely know what this was. Zhang Xiaojing's face was as though the north wind of the desert had swept over it, and his trembling lips forced out three words:

"Fierce-fire thunder."

As far back as Emperor Gaozong's reign, the alchemist Daoists of Great Tang had found that saltpeter, sulfur, and the black charcoal made by burning soap-bean pods, mixed together, would raise a bright flame, which they called "fierce-fire." In the hard campaigns of the Western Regions, some craftsman in the Tang army had hit on an ingenious notion: he treated rock-oil by a special secret art, stirred it together with sawdust and white phosphorus, heated it, and poured it into a sealed earthen jar; at the sealed mouth he worked in a lump of fierce-fire, and led a length of dry vine, soaked in castor oil, out along the jar's mouth to the outside.

In use, one first lit the dry vine, and as it burned to the jar's mouth it would draw out the fierce-fire. The fierce-fire was searingly hot, and where it touched the treated rock-oil laced with combustibles, the two closed like dragon and tiger locked in strife, with a power that was astonishing. Because it burst with a sound like a thunderclap, it took the name "fierce-fire thunder."

Ordinary rock-oil could not be detonated at all; only rock-oil treated by this secret art had such power. Craftsmen who knew the treatment were very few, the process too intricate, and the fierce-fire thunder liable besides to go off by mistake, so that even the Tang army of the Western Regions used it but little. Who could ever have thought that steppe barbarians, who knew only the bow and the horse, would find—from where, none could say—a craftsman who could make fierce-fire, and would raise a thing so dangerous in the very heart of Chang'an.

It was as well that Zhang Xiaojing was so seasoned from the Western Regions that at one whiff of that familiar smell of sulfur he had reacted at once; otherwise the toll of dead and hurt would have been ghastlier still.

By the force of the blast, the store of fierce-fire thunder in the warehouse was no small one. They must have reckoned all along on being raided by the Jing'an Bureau, and made ready this killing stroke. Cao Poyan, holding the front, had from the first been a sacrifice, a screen for the fierce-fire thunder.

Before the very eyes of the Jing'an Bureau, the whole warehouse blazed up wildly, like a bright torch pouring out thick smoke. Its frame had not yet collapsed, and looking in through the windows and the open doors, one could see that the inside of the warehouse had become a hell of karmic fire. Of the dozen-odd Lüben Guards soldiers who had rushed into the building first, the wretched end needs no telling.

The sight was too terrible to bear; even these brave and hardy soldiers could only turn their heads away, misery on every face. His own face iron-grey, Cui Qi asked in a shaking voice: "Could it be... that this was a trap to lure us into an ambush?!"

Zhang Xiaojing shook his head: "No. Killing us serves no purpose. They did this to stop our pursuit, so as to move the finished fierce-fire thunder away the faster."

Cui Qi drew a sharp cold breath; two of the fierce-fire thunder had power so vast already—if there were dozens of such things... He said urgently: "But once we came into the ward, we made straight for this place, and never saw a trace of them!"

Zhang Xiaojing raised his hand and pointed. At the far end of the blazing warehouse, thick smoke rolled, but one could dimly make out another exit on the opposite side, and even the outline of a loading platform.

This had been a brick-and-tile kiln, with a great output and carts coming and going constantly. To use the Changming Ward gate would have been most inconvenient, so the kiln's owner must have petitioned the Forestry and Crafts Bureau and, by special leave, opened a gate straight through the ward-wall directly facing the kiln-yard, so that the cargo-carts could go out onto the street with ease—the Türks' carts had come and gone by that gate, and the beggars of Changming Ward had of course seen nothing of it.

Earlier Zhang Xiaojing had asked Jia Shiqi, and the man had said he had seen no great number of carts come into the ward that day; and at that Zhang Xiaojing had already suspected there was another way out. Now his guess was borne out indeed.

This could be laid at no one's door. The kiln had been shut down long ago, and who was still to remember such stale old particulars?

The Türk Wolf Guards had set Cao Poyan to hold the front, and had then slipped quietly out this way. But now the exit was barred by the great fire, and before it burned wholly out no one could hope to come near. The Jing'an Bureau had been one step short, and, never reckoning on it, had let the Türks get away once more.

Cui Qi's face was the grey of dead ashes; let this thing once go off in Chang'an, and his life was, in the main, at its end.

"No—there's still a chance!" A keen light flashed in Zhang Xiaojing's single eye. "A thing like fierce-fire thunder can't be made up in advance; it must be heated and used on the spot—they can't have gone far! The carts hauling the rock-oil won't be fast; give chase now, and we should still catch them."

At these words a little life came back into Cui Qi's eyes; he stood up and said heavily: "I'll go and tell the watchtowers to sound the Nine-Gate Drum!"

"Good—I leave this to you!"

Zhang Xiaojing turned and ran for the foot of the ward-wall nearby. Cui Qi was baffled, not knowing what he was about. Reaching the foot of the wall, Zhang Xiaojing stretched out his long apelike arms, stepped in turn on a few earthen footholds, and vaulted cleanly to the top of the ward-wall, then turned his head and shouted:

"Tell the Deputy Director to have every company round about act on my smoke-signals!"

His charge given, Zhang Xiaojing gave a whistle. Before long a fine chestnut horse came galloping up the street beyond the wall; Zhang Xiaojing flipped down and settled firmly into the saddle. Without a pause he shook the reins and sped forward at speed. Yao Runeng, on another horse, followed hard behind.

It turned out that Zhang Xiaojing had just now sent word by Jia Shiqi to Yao Runeng, bidding him lead two horses along the outer foot of the wall toward the northwest corner. Time now was more precious than gold or jade; there was none to spare for going round by the ward gate, and to vault the wall and out was the quickest way of all.

By now the street was in some disorder. More and more people were coming into the city, and seeing the black smoke suddenly rising from Changming Ward, they stopped one and all to look. In a moment mules, carts, camels, and folk were all jammed together in one place, buzzing with talk. Zhang Xiaojing drove his horse hard on, and several times came near to running down a merchant. One camel-drover cursed and swore and would not give way, and Zhang Xiaojing, without ceremony, laid a whip across his spine, so that the man leapt up on the spot with the pain. Only then did the people round about take fright and dodge to either side.

They chased as far as Dunyi-Guiyi—that is, the crossroads of East Dunyi Ward and West Guiyi Ward—and there had to halt. Zhang Xiaojing gazed round in all four directions and could see no suspicious trace anywhere. He tugged fretfully at the reins, and the horse, kept standing so long, snorted in impatience.

Time slipped by, snap upon snap of the fingers, and the fleeing Türks might as well have vanished into the sea. With their vastly dangerous fierce-fire thunder, and Wang Yunxiu perhaps their hostage, these fellows would be great trouble wherever they went.

Just then Yao Runeng pointed at the ground: "Commander Zhang! Look here!" Zhang Xiaojing looked down and saw on the yellow earth a few black drops like ink. Yao Runeng had already swung down from his horse, and crouching to study them closely, lifted his head and said: "These ink-drops are not the round of a straight-falling drip; the rounded head points west, the whisked tail east, so they must have fallen into the wind as a cart sped westward—hence this shape."

The Türks had withdrawn in some haste, with no time to reseal the casks, and this dripping rock-oil had become the best of pointers.

Zhang Xiaojing made him a sign of approval; young men who would observe so closely were few enough these days. At the praise a faint flush rose in Yao Runeng's cheeks; but the thought of the friction between them a while ago at once dimmed his elation a little.

"Ride!"

Zhang Xiaojing cared nothing for Yao Runeng's small qualms of mind; he wheeled his horse's head about and galloped off. Yao Runeng hurriedly mounted and followed; the pressing task was to catch the Türks, and all else could wait.

They rode past one crossing, and Yao Runeng checked the spilled rock-oil again, to find that the Türks had turned at the crossroads of Yong'an-Tonggui and run north all the way. Once the direction was made out, Zhang Xiaojing and Yao Runeng drew a sharp cold breath at the same moment.

The road the Türks had taken was the third street west of the Vermilion Bird Gate Avenue, running north and south. From here straight north, the rows of wards on either side were all rich and thriving places; north as far as Yanshou Ward lay the most opulent quarter of the western capital, first among all. And across the street on the west side of Yanshou Ward was the West Market, "where the treasures of all the world are gathered."

Here the crush of people was great even on ordinary days, and today was the first day of the Lantern Festival fair besides. The hour of the Monkey had come, the sun sliding west; who could say how many lantern-wheels, lantern-trees, and lantern-frames were being hoisted up, how many of the common folk and the peddlers were gathering.

A mere two jars of rock-oil had already cost the Lüben Guards dear. Were the Wolf Guards let break into this quarter with more fierce-fire thunder, the whole flower of Chang'an's western city might well be destroyed in a single day.

The situation had come to its most perilous pass, and brooked not a moment's hesitation.

Zhang Xiaojing reined in and, turning his head, said to Yao Runeng: "Listen. What I want from here on is absolute obedience—even if it's women and children I kill, there's to be not the least hesitation. If you can do it, come with me; if you can't, then be off!" With that he clamped his legs and galloped north. Yao Runeng knew how bad things had grown; he gritted his teeth, flung a smoke pellet from his breast, and followed hard after.

The watchtowers round about, seeing the smoke pellet go up, drummed on without a break and sent the news back to the Jing'an Bureau. At the same time Cui Qi's report came through as well. Within the great hall documents crossed and recrossed, and the air grew on the instant so taut it stifled a man.

"What are Cui Qi and Zhang Xiaojing about! To let them get away even at this!"

Li Bi flung his fly-whisk of stillness aside and strode quickly to the sand-table. The recorders of the several sections of the Jing'an Bureau gathered round as well, a dozen-odd pairs of eyes fixed hard upon it together. Tanqi set the black piece that stood for the Wolf Guards down at Yong'an-Tonggui, its head pointing north, so that the state of things was plain at a glance.

Li Bi snatched the moon-rod from Tanqi's hand and drew a deep line across the finely-wrought clay of the sand-table, his tone hard as iron cleaving through a nail: "They must be cut off south of Guangde-Huaiyuan; that is a death-line that must on no account be crossed!"

North of that crossroads all was vital ground of the capital. To the north lay Guangde Ward, the seat of the Jing'an Bureau's headquarters and the yamen of the Jingzhao Prefecture besides; further north still were bustling places like the West Market and Yanshou Ward, and the imperial city too. Were the trouble let past this point, then Li Bi need no longer be Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau at all.

One recorder said: "From Yong'an-Tonggui to Guangde-Huaiyuan is only some four li; we must set up a barricade to stop them as fast as we can." Another retorted: "Hereabouts is the very heart of the lantern-viewing; to set a barricade now will only breed more confusion—have you forgotten how Director He charged us?" The first recorder said: "And once the fierce-fire thunder goes off and lays waste dozens of wards, will there be no confusion then?" A third recorder put in: "Don't forget—the military commissioner Wang's daughter is still in their hands!"

Li Bi listened to them wrangle on without end and felt his mind grow vexed and troubled. He silently recited a Daoist formula of stillness to settle his heart first, then waved his hand: "Move the guard companies to all the crossings nearby for now, but set no barricade in the open."

The order was vague and unclear, for Li Bi himself did not know how to meet the thing, and could only take one step and see. The herald copied the order down and started out; then, without warning, Li Bi barked at his back: "At a run!"—so startling him that he near fell, and went stumbling out.

Under such crushing pressure, Li Bi could no longer mind keeping his nature detached and quelling it with stillness. Just then Xu Bin came up, still in that shrinking, timid way of his: "Deputy Director... ai, ai..."

"Speak!" And then, seeing that it was Xu Bin, Li Bi's manner grew a little kinder. This recorder had just won a great merit, seeing through the Türks' trick of bringing in the rock-oil.

Xu Bin seemed to have steeled himself to a great resolve; he drew a deep breath before he spoke: "Things now are perilous as a stack of eggs; why should the Deputy Director not consider commissioning the watchtowers to Commander Zhang?" At those four words two keen beams shot from Li Bi's eyes; Xu Bin's shoulders gave a shudder, but in the end he held firm and did not let his head sink.

"To commission" here meant to lend, and the tally it lent was authority. "Bearing the tally" had first been, in Han and Jin times, the phrase for the Son of Heaven delegating authority to a subject; the Jing'an Bureau used the old term, but with a different sense. To "commission the watchtowers" meant that all the watchtowers would no longer report to the Jing'an Bureau's headquarters, but would answer instead to the arrangements of the one who bore the tally.

Xu Bin's proposal amounted to letting Zhang Xiaojing take over the whole Jing'an Bureau and become a second center of command.

"Do you know what you are saying?" said Li Bi coldly. The man had won one small merit, and grown arrogant to this pitch already.

Xu Bin plucked up his courage: "For the watchtowers to relay to headquarters, and headquarters to relay again to Commander Zhang—the turnaround takes too long. We can wait; the Türks cannot. In an emergency, one must bend the rule to the need!"

"You have great faith in Zhang Xiaojing indeed."

Xu Bin said eagerly: "This fellow is the most dogged and the most trustworthy man I have ever known; give the tally to him, and it will surely be like a tiger... ai, ai, given wings." The words had begun grand enough to move mountains and rivers, but his stammer broke their force. For all the cares that filled him, Li Bi could not help a small laugh:

"Had I no faith in what he can do, I should not have used him. Only the bearing of the tally is no child's play; he is, after all, still a man under sentence of death."

"That is not what you said before Director He!" No sooner were the words out than Xu Bin saw he had been too rash, sweat starting on his brow, and he hastily softened his tone: "Ai, ai, what your servant means is that Commander Zhang is on the spot, and his reading of the situation is bound to be surer than ours, poring over papers hidden away in the hall."

Li Bi thought to himself: no wonder the man could never win a transfer in office all his life; he was hopeless at speaking. He waved Xu Bin back and turned to stare at the sand-table: "Where are Zhang Xiaojing and Cui Qi?"

Tanqi hastily took up the moon-rod, set the red piece for Cui Qi at Changming Ward to the south, and pushed Zhang Xiaojing's grey piece to the place of Yong'an-Tonggui. It could be seen that the Jing'an Bureau's main strength was scattered at the two ends, north and south, and that following close on the heels of the Türk Wolf Guards there was only one man—Zhang Xiaojing. That grey piece, standing there in the sand-table, looked at once of the utmost importance and of the utmost solitude.

Li Bi mused only three breaths before he issued an order: "Every watchtower on the third street, keep your eyes on the carts and horses nearby, and report to me once every thirty breaths!" He hesitated, then added: "Report to Zhang Xiaojing first; from now on, be sure he is the first to know all news."

The recorders round about were dumbstruck, and all looked at Li Bi; but Li Bi had no mind at all to explain.

Xu Bin was poor of tongue, but one thing he had said was not wrong: we can wait; the Türks cannot.

Yao Runeng, galloping north the whole way after Zhang Xiaojing, suddenly heard a drumbeat rise from a watchtower not far off—a fixed-code dispatch! He gripped the reins tight and cocked his ear on horseback to listen. This fixed code was so rare that he had to strain to recall the answering cipher in the little book.

"Commissioning the watchtowers?!" Yao Runeng could scarcely believe his ears; it would make this condemned man, in an instant, one of the most powerful men in all Chang'an.

But he dared not delay; he hurriedly urged his mount up alongside Zhang Xiaojing and told him of the new appointment. There was no excitement at all in Zhang Xiaojing's face; he only remarked simply: "The Deputy Director is a man of understanding after all—now tell the watchtowers to keep close watch on carts with broad sterns!"

The carts these Türks had seized were Su's Cart-and-Horse Company's, made for long-haul freight, their rear axle-bars broad, thick, and hard-wearing; whereas the carts that plied within the city of Chang'an had, as a rule, stern-bars pointed and narrow as a swallow's tail, the better to work through the lanes and streets. The difference between the two, folk outside the haulage trade truly did not, as a rule, know.

To have the constables up in the watchtowers tell so fine a difference was asking rather much of them, but it was for the moment the only way to pick out the Wolf Guards' carts quickly.

Yao Runeng drew himself up on horseback and, a small red flag and a yellow one in his hands, began to signal, a little comically. By the time he had passed the order on, the two of them were already past the crossing of Yanfu-Yongping.

The further north this street ran, the more people thronged it, and the thicker grew the festival air. On both sides of the wards, many workmen in black were up on bamboo scaffolds, busy hoisting colored lanterns aloft on bamboo poles, and lengths of Lantern-Festival spring-silk hung streaming down. Below, the common folk clustered here and there beneath the trees, gazing up and pointing as they talked. Western Regions performers spinning ropes sang and danced; peddlers of steamed cakes and pomegranate-water moved among them; and the eating-houses everywhere set out their stalls too and sold pickled fish, sheep's-cheese, and roast camel-hoof. There was even a band of youths with crescent mallets in hand playing at ball right there on the street corner, the dust flying; and each time a ball went in, several onlooking jie-drummers would beat up their drums, prouder than the Son of Heaven himself at his polo.

This whole scene of peace and merriment, in the eyes of Zhang Xiaojing and Yao Runeng, weighed heavier than it might. Unless the Türk Wolf Guards were caught, and soon, all of it would fall into hell.

The one piece of good news was that the great street was so crushed with people that only a narrow way was left down the middle; even to ride through was no easy thing, let alone to drive a cart. Only let the Türk Wolf Guards keep going north, and it would grow more and more choked; they need not think to build up any speed.

Just then a low lizard-skin drumbeat sounded, passing clearly through the din into the ears of the two men. Both were roused at once; Yao Runeng quickly made out the direction and looked toward a watchtower on the east side.

"Ahead, south of Chongxian Ward, two carts! Going north!"

Now the good of the commission showed itself. Had they waited for the watchtower to relay to the Jing'an Bureau and be relayed back again, the target would long since have moved off who knew where.

Yao Runeng cried out loudly, "Jing'an Bureau business—make way, make way!" and the two of them shook their reins, shouldered aside a few actors performing a canjun farce, and, heedless of the curses and shrieks all along the way, rushed swiftly through. They soon saw the two carts, going along at an unhurried pace. Yao Runeng, keen to make a show, spurred ahead and blocked their way, ordered the driver to stop, and displayed the Jing'an Bureau's waist-tally. But he was soon left gaping: it was a small band of musicians from Luoyang, the carts piled high with instruments and dancing-costumes, come to perform for some noble house's birthday.

And at that another dispatch came in: "Changshou-Daixian, three broad-sterned carts, going west."

Changshou Ward and Daixian Ward lay on the fourth street west of the Vermilion Bird Gate Avenue, which by rights was not on the third-street route they had reckoned on. This time Yao Runeng dared not decide for himself, and looked to Zhang Xiaojing.

Zhang Xiaojing waved his hand: "Ride over and see!"

The third street was badly choked now. It was very possible the Türk Wolf Guards had swung a little west first, meaning to double back by way of Huaiyuan Ward. Leaving the frightened troupe of players behind, the two men bolted west across the way.

The east–west streets were somewhat clearer than those running north and south. Hooves flew, leaving a long string of hurried prints down the great road. They soon reached the crossing of Changshou-Daixian, and a watchtower nearby reported the latest in good time: the three carts had just turned north.

This tallied exactly with Zhang Xiaojing's reckoning. His face grew stern; he drew the hand-crossbow and had Yao Runeng grip a smoke pellet in his hand. They rode a hundred paces or so further north, and Yao Runeng cried out suddenly: "That's them!"

At a crossing not far off, three carts stood halted, their horses' heads slanted eastward. All were of the same make, with long, large wheel-spokes and broad, thick stern-bars, and in the beds were several great casks, screened over with straw mats. They were not moving forward, for a train of enclosed carriages coming from the north was turning east, awkwardly.

The crossing was too small; if two trains of carts came head-on and turned into the same direction, they had to pass one after the other. This train of enclosed carriages had six-sided harness-bells hung at their four corners, painted panels and gauze curtains, and beside them rode a few guards on tall, big horses—no doubt the womenfolk of several noble houses, who had gone together to shop in the West Market and were returning to the eastern city.

By the traffic rule of the Statutes of Ceremonial, the low gave way to the high, and the departing to the coming. Those three carts flew no banner at all, and being of low station, could only give way meekly.

Zhang Xiaojing whipped his horse's flank for speed and closed swiftly. The three carts stood at a slant, so that from behind one could clearly see the drivers in profile, and into his single eye there soon swam a familiar face.

It was this very man who, in Xiuzheng Ward, had pared away his flesh with a blade, and then made off with Wen Ran as his hostage!

As though he felt it in his blood, no sooner had Zhang Xiaojing come near than the man, by some prompting of ghosts and spirits, turned his head, and their three eyes met square. Ma Ge'er froze an instant in astonishment, then gave a great shout. Five or six Wolf Guards scrambled out of the three carts and, with water-dippers and wooden basins, flung out a great spread of pitch-black rock-oil; then one of them threw down a pine-branch torch, and the ground burst at once into flame, forming a wall of fire not overly high.

So they had made ready, it seemed, against the Jing'an Bureau's likely pursuit.

Zhang Xiaojing was not afraid, but his horse gave a terrified cry, threw up its forehooves, and would by no means leap the flames. Seizing the chance, the three carts started forward violently and, heedless of the enclosed carriages still turning ahead, smashed savagely into them.

Striking the fragile flank head-on, an enclosed carriage was overturned with a crash on the instant. In a moment the shrieks of the women within and the whinnying of the shaft-horses were all mixed together. The guards round about were utterly at a loss; when had the city of Chang'an ever seen drivers so desperate and vicious?

One guard would still seize the reins and have it out with them; but Ma Ge'er's blood-lust broke loose, and he drew his dagger and savagely stabbed to death three guards and one of the women, then had the cart back up a few steps and ram forward again.

Seeing his mount was no more use, Zhang Xiaojing swung down, shielded his face with both hands, and charged through the wall of fire. Behind him Yao Runeng, seeing the enemy's trail confirmed, flung out a smoke pellet without hesitation, then drew his blade and rushed in. Black smoke and yellow twined together and rose straight into the sky.

Once through the wall of fire, Zhang Xiaojing found his brows and hair singed and his skin raw with pain. With no time to beat out the flames, he forced his single eye open and saw that Ma Ge'er's cart had already shoved the overturned carriage aside and was moving east. The two carts behind picked up speed in turn, making ready to flee.

He ran two quick steps and sprang up onto the top of the overturned carriage. A woman inside was just crawling out when Zhang Xiaojing set his foot on her head, and with a wretched cry she shrank back in. The guards roared in anger, but with the warning of what had gone before, none dared come near. Standing on the carriage-body, Zhang Xiaojing used the height to leap forward high and far, and landed exactly at the stern of the third cart. The broad stern-bar gave a superb foothold.

A Wolf Guard on the cart stuck out his head and thrust at him with a short wooden spear. Zhang Xiaojing clamped the spear-shaft under his armpit, pressed the crossbow in his left hand to the man's temple, and loosed, so that his brains spattered out on the instant. Now another Wolf Guard rushed him; Zhang Xiaojing threw the crossbow aside, bent and heaved up the triangular chock-stone used to fix the cart at rest, and wedged it savagely into the man's eye-socket. With a wretched cry the Wolf Guard was kicked off the speeding cart.

Zhang Xiaojing did not pause; treading the cart-bed's narrow edge and steadying himself against the great casks, he worked his way toward the front of the cart. The driver ahead felt that things had gone badly wrong, and had just turned to resist when a keen barrier-knife swept across from behind and near cut halfway through his neck.

This whole run of movements was quick as a lightning-flash struck from flint, with not a hair's breadth to spare. Zhang Xiaojing swept a glance round and, finding no one else on the cart, cut through all the traces of the shaft-horse ahead with one stroke, then leapt onto its back to go after the second cart.

The cart, its power gone, slowly came to a stop. Yao Runeng came up behind, but did not dare leave. The cart was loaded with several casks of fierce-fire thunder, which might go off at any moment. He could only first set off a smoke pellet, calling for Cui Qi's troops to follow in good time, and then looked ahead, to see that Zhang Xiaojing had drawn level with the second cart and raised his arm high, thumb up.

This was no word of praise, but a signal agreed on beforehand. Zhang Xiaojing wanted the Jing'an Bureau told at once to throw up a cordon at the Guangde-Huaiyuan crossing ahead and clear the people away. As things now stood, Zhang Xiaojing could not guarantee to stop every cart, and had to make ready for the worst.

A horse, after all, is a good deal faster than a cart, and Zhang Xiaojing soon drew close to the flank of the second cart. This time the Wolf Guards used no spears, but tore off the covering-cloths and took to flinging rock-oil instead. The black, viscous liquid flew scattering down from the cart, and one had only to throw a torch at the stuff for disaster to follow. Zhang Xiaojing dared not come too near, and could only keep hard after them.

It could be seen that the cart carried five casks of fierce-fire thunder, filling half the bed. Should those five casks burst, this whole street, one feared, would be no more.

The two maddened carts showed no least sign of slowing; from ahead came a run of frightened cries as stall-keepers and passers-by were knocked sprawling to the ground. They were near now the most flourishing part of the western city, and not far from the death-line Li Bi had drawn.

Zhang Xiaojing gritted his teeth and jabbed his barrier-knife hard into the horse's back; with a piteous cry the shaft-horse sprang forward.

The Wolf Guards on the second cart at once flung rock-oil desperately again, only to find the horse had suddenly turned side-on, and the man on its back was gone. Zhang Xiaojing had wrenched the horse's head about with all his strength and, by his fine horsemanship, swung swiftly to the far side, using the great bulk of the horse as a shield against the rock-oil. Taking advantage of the enemy's instant of distraction, Zhang Xiaojing nimbly vaulted over the horse's back and leapt for the cart.

But this time he was not as lucky as before; a Wolf Guard was standing just there on the stern-bar, and the two collided heavily and toppled together toward the middle of the cart-bed, knocking the great wooden casks askew every way for a moment. The driver seemed a seasoned hand, and at once threw the shaft-horse into a sharp turn to the left. Zhang Xiaojing lost his balance in a flash, his body pitching aslant and falling outward. The other two Wolf Guards rushed up and shoved him hard in the chest.

In the very instant of falling from the cart, Zhang Xiaojing, quick-witted in the crisis, gave a flick of his hand, and a long shadow like a snake flew out.

This was a binding-cord made of oxsinew, the gear the capital's buliang men used to catch thieves. A veteran buliang man cast the cord as though it were his own arm, so that even the tumblers of Kucha owned themselves outdone. Zhang Xiaojing, being a buliang chief, was of course more skilled at it still.

The cord was ordinarily wound about the right wrist, and at need one had only to flick the arm for it to fly out. In the instant Zhang Xiaojing struck the ground, the far end of the cord was already wound fast around a hanging-post on the cart's flank. The cart still raced on, and he gripped the haft at his end and would not let go for anything, his whole back flat to the earth, dragged bodily forward by the cart and leaving a long, harrowing scrape-mark behind.

The Wolf Guard on the cart drew his dagger and strove desperately to cut through the cord, but the rope was too tough and supple, and for the moment could not be severed at all.

Those on the cart could not shake him off, but neither could he climb back onto it. Dragged some thirty or forty paces, the back of Zhang Xiaojing's clothes was worn through, his spine a mess of blood and torn flesh. Of a sudden he scooped his other hand along the ground, caught up half a green brick, and, with the momentum, hooked his arm and hurled it. The brick cut a beautiful arc through the air and struck square the eye of the right-hand shaft-horse ahead.

The horse, startled all at once, veered desperately to the right, and set the other beside it plunging too. However the driver hauled and shouted, he could not master them, and the whole cart swung, all unwilling, to the right.

They were now on the cross-street between Huaiyuan Ward and the south wall of the West Market, and ahead, on the right side of the street, stood a huge lantern-wheel. The lantern-wheel rose six zhang high; at its base was built a wooden platform anchored with stones, and above was a great spoked frame of bamboo, pasted over with embroidered paper and spring-token designs. Several servant-boys in black were up on it, carefully raising great lanterns one by one on bamboo poles.

The cart could not check its rush, and at tremendous speed drove headlong into the base of the lantern-wheel. The force was savage; the two shaft-horses crashed into it and their brains burst out. How was a mere wooden lantern-wheel to bear such force? With a rending crash the whole frame toppled thundering down, and the boys on it, along with ten-odd great fish-and-dragon lanterns, blessing-and-longevity lanterns, and seven-jewel lanterns, came clattering and crashing down, all of them falling onto the cart.

And so the several Wolf Guards on the cart were pinned fast under the frame of the lantern-wheel and could not stir. In the violent collision the great wooden casks at the cart's rear went rumbling and rolling, and all tumbled out.

Before the cart struck, Zhang Xiaojing had let go in good time, and so was not dragged by it into the crash. He lay on the ground, his palms a mess of blood and torn flesh, his back aching to the bone. Before he could get up, a familiar smell drifted into his nose.

This was bad! Zhang Xiaojing's face changed utterly; he bent and hauled up an unconscious servant-boy in black and dragged him clear, meanwhile shouting with all his might at the common folk gathering round: "Get back! Get back! Get back!"

Fierce-fire was no reliable igniter; the least knock or friction might set it alight. Those wooden casks, after this whole run of chasing and colliding, had been perilous as a stack of eggs already; and now, struck so hard, the fierce-fire at their mouths had woken, and might touch off the rock-oil at any moment. And mark: these great casks held better than five times the quantity of the warehouse just now...

The common folk, knowing nothing of the danger, still crowded round to watch the excitement. Seeing his warnings do no good, Zhang Xiaojing in his urgency unfastened a smoke pellet from his belt and hurled it savagely into the crowd. The pellet burst, and it set the people scattering like a kicked hive; not knowing what evil sprite was at work, they cried out in alarm and dodged away to the rear.

Zhang Xiaojing heard what seemed a stir behind him and at once threw himself flat to the ground. At the same moment a roar came from behind, and a great gust of heat rose. But this roar was not the bursting of the warehouse; it was closer to the whoosh of a flame leaping up when oil is poured on fire.

Propping himself on his elbows, Zhang Xiaojing carefully turned his head, and saw the five great casks before him become five dazzling balls of fire; five raging flames licked at the huge lantern-wheel, and the paper lanterns and paper skins were the first to turn to flying ash, and then the whole great bamboo frame, the cart, and a few elms nearby began to burn as well, with now and again the crackle and pop of bursting bamboo, like New Year's firecrackers driving off evil. The black-smoking flame shot straight up to the sky, higher than the ward-wall, and one side of the wall's outer face was already stained a shocking black.

As for those pinned beneath the lantern-wheel, but for the one servant-boy he had hauled out with all his might, the rest were surely past saving.

But this was already the best of a bad business.

One great trouble with fierce-fire thunder was that, even with fierce-fire to touch it off, the rate of a true explosion was still not high. More often than not, it did not detonate the rock-oil but simply set it burning. The Wolf Guards had put five casks of rock-oil on the cart, and likely because the sealing was not good enough—which was why it had dripped and spilled all along the way—not one, of all things, had burst; every one had come to burn of itself.

Thus, though the fire was fierce as ever, what it showed was a creeping, spreading force, its power greatly lessened; otherwise Zhang Xiaojing and everyone on this half of the street would have been done for.

He stretched out his aching arms and lay on the ground, gasping in great breaths. That pursuit, brief as it was, had drained the last of his strength. Ma Ge'er's cart, the last of them, was running further and further off, and there was surely no catching it now; he could only pin his hopes on the Jing'an Bureau throwing up its cordon ahead in good time.

So great a fire soon roused the constables' post of Huaiyuan Ward. A score or more of constables in fire-proof cloth came hurrying up, bearing squirt-pumps and hemp fire-mops, and some shouldering water-bladders. For today's Lantern Festival, the constables' posts of every ward had been ordered to stand ready against a fire-alarm at any moment, prepared to the full.

But one look at so great a fire told these men there was no putting it out; they could only first mark off a fire-break to stop it spreading, and then wait for it to burn out of itself.

A few of them, seeing Zhang Xiaojing and the servant-boy lying at the fire's edge, hauled them up all-hands, cursing and swearing, plainly taking them for the arsonists. Zhang Xiaojing had lost his waist-tally and had not yet found the time to replace it, and so could not prove who he was. It was as well that Yao Runeng came up from behind just then, produced his own waist-tally, drove the men back, and helped Zhang Xiaojing over to sit down in the corner of a wall.

Zhang Xiaojing begged a ladle of sweet-plum water from a water-seller nearby, gulped it down at a draught, and went on panting and wheezing.

Yao Runeng noticed that Zhang Xiaojing, even as he fled the blast-zone, had not forgotten to drag out a servant-boy in black whom he did not know at all.

A base wretch who sold out his own comrade for intelligence; a seasoned, savage, cold-blooded former buliang chief; a saint who declared aloud that he would protect the people who counted for nothing; a diligent officer discontented with the court yet toiling for it with all his might. All these images, each at war with the last, threw Yao Runeng into a confusion of understanding.

He thought of the words Zhang Xiaojing had spoken before, and felt all at once a strong urge to ask him: what, in the end, was the capital charge for which you were condemned? But the moment was a little abrupt for it; Yao Runeng hesitated, and in the end held his tongue.

Reality left him no room to regret it. In the next instant the watchtower drums beat out once more, urgent, dong dong dong, and at the same time at least ten columns of yellow smoke rose up into the sky in the distance. This meant that some matter of the utmost gravity had befallen, and that every member of the Jing'an Bureau must drop all he held in hand and hasten to muster.

At the first beat of the drums Zhang Xiaojing opened his eyes. Seeing the yellow smoke rise, he murmured: "Guangde-Huaiyuan..."

Guangde-Huaiyuan was the death-line Li Bi had drawn with his own hand, on no account to be crossed to the north. What manner of turn could set ten columns of yellow smoke rising, one after another, from that sensitive place? What in the end had become of the escaped cart, laden with its fierce-fire thunder?

Yao Runeng said, a little anxiously: "You're wounded, Commander Zhang; hadn't I better go ahead and see how things stand?" But Zhang Xiaojing pressed a hand down on his shoulder and, bearing down on it, hauled himself to his feet, grimacing with the pain.

"We go together," he said hoarsely, and Yao Runeng could only obey.

Where they stood was the great road between the West Market and Huaiyuan Ward, no more than two li and a bit from the crossing. Zhang Xiaojing and Yao Runeng rose at once and hurried east. A few steps on, Zhang Xiaojing suddenly stopped, dragged over a constable who was fighting the fire, and stripped the fire-proof cloth cloak off his body.

Fire-proof cloth took no harm from flame, and was a prime tool for fighting fires. That Zhang Xiaojing should do this meant he had already made up his mind that some very great danger lay ahead. Yao Runeng hesitated a moment, then also stopped a constable and, with the Jing'an Bureau's waist-tally, half by force requisitioned another cloak and put it on.

They ran the whole way to the crossing, and saw from afar the Lüben Guards soldiers dragging up several thorn-brush hurdles and setting them crosswise in the middle of the road. Many of the common folk and the great and noble alike were penned off to one side, a great hubbub of voices.

To cordon off a road—above all a road so important—was the last action the Jing'an Bureau wished to take. That Li Bi had given the order at all showed the situation had come to a pass all but past retrieving.

Yao Runeng had the Lüben Guards soldiers open a way and let the two of them through. They soon saw that the four sides of the crossing had been barred tight with cheval-de-frise and thorn-brush hurdles; on three sides—south, east, and west—stood Cui Qi's Lüben Guards, and the north was thronged with soldiers bearing great shields. These were not under the Jing'an Bureau's direct command, but the elite Leopard Cavalry of the Right Xiao Guard.

North of Guangde Ward lay Yanshou Ward, and Yanshou Ward, slanting to the northeast, was but a single street from the imperial city and the palace city. With the Wolf Guards driven so near, the Sixteen Guards of the Southern Command, however slow, must needs bestir themselves at last, and the Leopard Cavalry had been the first to muster and come.

But with the military once involved, the days ahead would likely go hard with the Jing'an Bureau.

The Guangde-Huaiyuan crossing was empty now, only two half-pasted lantern-frames standing up at the street-side and a two-shaft cart halted in the middle of the road. The covering-cloth had been torn off, baring the five dark casks within. Ma Ge'er stood among the casks, holding a burning torch high in his hand. Not far from the cart, three corpses lay face-down on the ground, each with several dozen feathered arrows sunk in its back.

Plainly Ma Ge'er had driven the cart to the crossing, only to be stopped there by the Jing'an Bureau drawn up in full array. After a fight, the other Wolf Guards had all fallen, but they had won time enough for Ma Ge'er to light the torch and bring it to the mouths of the casks.

This stroke cowed everyone; no one dared let these five casks of fierce-fire thunder go off in so sensitive a place. His face fierce and twisted, Ma Ge'er held the torch a bare few inches from the mouths of the casks and let the shaft-horse walk slowly forward. The archers nearby were at their wits' end: who could guarantee to fell this brute with a single arrow? And who could guarantee that, once he was dead, the torch would not drop just onto the mouth of a cask?

Yao Runeng looked ahead and saw, at the southwest corner of Guangde Ward, Li Bi and the others standing on a high pavilion, staring hard at the crossing. With the great fire burning up to his own door, he could not sit quiet in the hall either.

Ma Ge'er was the last of the Wolf Guards, and knew himself doomed to certain death, yet showed not a trace of fear. So many Tang people to be buried with him—what a rare chance was this! He roared with laughter, gripping the torch tight in one hand and lightly shaking the reins with the other. The shaft-horse, knowing nothing of the strained air, only lowered its head and walked forward. Their course was still toward the north, toward the most flourishing and bustling quarter of all.

Yao Runeng said: "This won't do! I must go and tell the Deputy Director—the fierce-fire thunder is lit, but it need not explode!" But Zhang Xiaojing stopped him: "Nor need it fail to. This is Chang'an; without a full ten parts of certainty, the Deputy Director dares take no risk either."

Yao Runeng said urgently: "Then what's to be done? Are we just to stand and glare while he goes north?" Zhang Xiaojing did not answer; he narrowed his single eye and drew the fire-proof cloak a little tighter about him.

The situation at the crossing had grown taut to the utmost, tense enough to blow at any instant without need of fierce-fire thunder at all. Ma Ge'er's cart moved slowly forward as though no one else were there, and reached at last the edge of the northern cordon. The shaft-horse shoved through the thorn-wall, its two forehooves kicking against the face of a row of shields.

The soldiers round about could plainly have cut down this Türk Wolf Guard with a single stroke, yet not one could touch a hair of him. Those five brown casks were five silent death-fetching demons. In this strange deadlock the elite Leopard Cavalry kept giving ground and scattering, and the cart forced a way clean open among them. The general at their head, his face grim and dark, dared make no rash move.

Li Bi, standing on the high platform at the ward's corner, closed his eyes. Once the death-line was crossed, the whole nature of the affair would change, and there must be a decision. He said heavily: "Ready fire-arrows!"

At once twenty picked archers mounted the high platform, and beside them twenty auxiliaries dipped the round cotton arrowheads made ready beforehand in pine-resin oil, lit them, and handed them to the archers. At a word of command from the squad-captain, the archers swiftly nocked, drew to the full, and leveled on the cart outside the ward.

To sit and watch the Wolf Guard draw nearer the imperial city and the palace city would be for the Jing'an Bureau to hold the safety of the Son of Heaven and the whole court of no account. Weighing the two evils, Li Bi would sooner let it blow half of Guangde Ward and his own face into the sky than suffer it to go north one step more.

In his ears was the creaking of the bowstrings wound to their tightest; he knew that let but one word pass his lips, and the whole affair would be over. Twenty fire-arrows, at this range, could not miss their mark; but what would happen next, he could only leave to heaven.

"Young master, it's too dangerous here; you had best first—what is that?" Tanqi had meant to urge Li Bi to go down first, out of reach of the blast, but she suddenly saw a stir at the crossing, and could not help crying out.

Everyone looked along where her jade-white hand pointed, toward the crossing.

A figure was rushing at the cart at a speed never seen before, without a backward glance. He was wrapped in a cloak of an odd color, and his face could not be made out. Ma Ge'er's whole attention was fixed on the cordon ahead, and for the moment he did not notice. The figure seized the chance to leap onto the cart-bed, and with a flick of the long cord in his hand wound it fast about Ma Ge'er's wrist.

"It's Xiaojing!" Of all people it was Xu Bin, the near-sighted one, who first knew the figure.

At the name the men of the Jing'an Bureau were all roused. In these past hours this condemned man had wrought one marvel after another. However hopeless the situation, he could always dourly find a way to break it open. From the recorders above to the petty clerks below, there was not one who did not admire him with a whole heart.

That Zhang Xiaojing should strike so boldly now brought the hero's image in their minds nearer still to perfection. Had they not been bound by propriety, they would fairly have cheered aloud. Only Li Bi showed nothing; he watched with his hands behind his back, the twenty fire-arrows still leveled on the cart.

Zhang Xiaojing had no thought to spare for how the Jing'an Bureau might feel; his whole mind was on this fierce Türk bandit before him. Let there be the least slip, and the whole cart might be blown into the sky.

Wrapped in the cloak just now, screened by the crowd of onlookers, he had drawn quietly near the north mouth of the crossroads. A soldier within the cordon, unable to bear the enormous strain, had raised his spear a fraction, and this had drawn Ma Ge'er's attention for the moment. Seizing this chance, gone almost as soon as it showed, Zhang Xiaojing sprinted twenty paces, gathered his feet in a keen rush, and sprang onto the cart from behind.

Ma Ge'er knew him at once, this man who had made trouble for them time and again, and roared in Turkic: "I should have killed you long ago!" Zhang Xiaojing smiled coldly and said nothing, but that fierce single eye, like a lone wolf's, sent a shudder through Ma Ge'er's heart.

The two men fought on the cart as though careless of their lives. Zhang Xiaojing had only to pull Ma Ge'er half a foot aside, and it would be enough for the other soldiers to come up and lend a hand; Ma Ge'er had only to win half a snap of the fingers, and he could plunge the torch deep into a cask. The two were like men standing on a rope strung across a deep chasm, where the least carelessness meant being ground to powder.

This clash lasted but a few short instants. First Zhang Xiaojing's fist smashed savagely into Ma Ge'er's right eye, the broken iron shard clenched between his fingers blinding the Wolf Guard's eye outright; then Ma Ge'er drove his forehead into the bridge of Zhang Xiaojing's nose and set the blood spurting. The two fought with no method at all, yet with a matchless ferocity, like two blood-craving, wounded wolves.

Ma Ge'er's wrist was bound by the cord and his movement checked, and Zhang Xiaojing took the chance to strike fiercely at his head. But Ma Ge'er neither flinched nor dodged; bearing the fierce pain of the blows to his head, he reached out his fingers and gouged into the wound under Zhang Xiaojing's arm—the very wound Ma Ge'er had left on him in Xiuzheng Ward. At this Zhang Xiaojing's sight went black with the pain, and his movements stiffened.

Ma Ge'er did not press his advantage; that would have been pointless. He swiftly caught up the torch, cast a glance at the soldiers climbing up from every side, murmured a phrase of Turkic, and then threw the torch into a cask. Zhang Xiaojing gave a great shout and lunged to kick Ma Ge'er off the cart, but all of it was already too late.

From the cask's mouth the smell of sulfur swiftly rose, with a thin curl of smoke.

The soldiers who had been swarming up like ants shrank back again in fright, like an ebbing tide. On the high platform Li Bi closed his eyes in dismay: was it, after all, to fail?

"Young master, look, quick!" Tanqi cried in alarm. Li Bi's eyes flew open again, and what he saw made him step forward two paces, forgetting himself, and near fall from the high platform.

For Zhang Xiaojing had leapt into the driver's seat, was whipping the shaft-horse, and was frantically gesturing to the soldiers ahead to make way, driving north.

"What does Commander Zhang mean by this?" cried one of the Jing'an Bureau's recorders.

"Can he mean to drive the cart to some safe place? How is there time for that?"

"Even were there time, the direction's wrong—this is still going north!"

"Then how is it any different from what the Türks meant to do?"

If Zhang Xiaojing chose now to draw back, no one would blame him. Yet at the risk of being swallowed by the flames he drove the cart northward—where all was bustling ground, with no open space at all in which these five casks of fierce-fire thunder might safely go off.

Amid the clamor of tongues a strange conjecture rose in every mind. This man had once declared his discontent with the court openly; might he not be minded to seize the chance offered and drive the cart to the palace city to take his revenge?

The squad-captain of the archers could not help calling out: "Deputy Director, the cart will soon be out of range!" Li Bi's eyes flickered, and at last he gave an order: "Stand down the arrows." The captain's eyes went round; he thought he had misheard. Li Bi said it once more: "Stand down the arrows." His tone brooked no doubt.

The twenty archers could only lower their bows, baffled. The recorders looked as one to Li Bi; the Deputy Director was known always for his bold decisions, but this time it was bold past all reason.

Within, Li Bi too was fiercely at war with himself. He thought of the words Zhang Xiaojing had said to him: "You chose the man, I chose the road; we must both answer for our own choices." Having staked so vast a wager on this condemned man, he might as well gamble it out to the end.

He believed there must be a reason for Zhang Xiaojing to do as he did. Yet for all his cleverness, Li Bi could not think how this hand was to be broken open.

Zhang Xiaojing drove the cart in a wild gallop down the broad street between the West Market and Guangde Ward. Behind him the casks were pouring out black smoke. The fierce-fire thunder had not gone off at the first instant, which was the mercy in the mishap; but the fire was up, the rock-oil had caught, and it might burst forth at any moment.

Zhang Xiaojing suddenly bent low and flicked the cord across the shaft-horse's left ear, and the whole cart began to swerve and turn to the left.

"The wheel-gauge!" Li Bi grasped it all at once, and then Xu Bin cried out too: "The wheel-gauge!" Seeing the other recorders blank and uncomprehending, he added two words more: "The West Market—the wheel-gauge!"

The West Market had two entrances in all, one east and one west, each fitted with a dragon-sill. The dragon-sill was a stone threshold set crosswise beneath the gate, with two notches cut in it, the two notches five chi and three cun apart. In other words, only a cart of a wheel-gauge of five chi three cun could enter the West Market. Too broad or too narrow, and it could not get in. The dragon-sills of the other wards of Chang'an, meanwhile, had their two notches only four chi apart, admitting narrow carts alone.

Thus the broad-gauge carts that hauled bulk goods could enter only the East and West Markets and go to no other ward or market; while the narrow-gauge little carts of everyday use within Chang'an could pass freely among the wards, yet these alone could not enter the two markets. Great cart and small, freight and passenger, thus went their separate ways, which at once avoided congestion and made things easier for the market offices and the Jingzhao Prefecture to manage.

Su's Cart-and-Horse Company had always hauled bulk goods alone, and so of course made up its carts to the five-chi-three standard. If Zhang Xiaojing wanted to get the cart off the main street as fast as he could, entering the West Market was his only choice.

The east gate of the West Market lay just now some sixty paces off the cart's left front, to be reached at the cart's speed in an instant—but! The West Market too was a great center of Chang'an, its merchants beyond number, its goods heaped like mountains, and rich traders and envoys gathered there from every land. Were it to blow up in there, the loss would be as dire.

What medicine Zhang Xiaojing was truly selling in that gourd of his, Li Bi had not the least idea. There was nothing he could do now but follow the condemned man with his eyes, down one road to its dark end.

Before the eyes of all, Zhang Xiaojing showed a driving skill of the highest order. Using the cord for a whip, he turned the shaft-horses westward little by little, the wheels pressing two near-perfect arcs into the yellow-earth road. As the cart's body came fully round to the west, the hooves of the two shaft-horses passed just over the dragon-sill of the West Market's east gate.

The two spinning wooden wheels cut exactly into the two notches of the dragon-sill, snug to a hair. The speed of the whole cart was not in the least checked by the turn, and it went shrieking into the West Market.

Once inside the West Market, he did not go on down the main road to the crossroads, but plunged headlong into a residential courtyard at the side. First, with a crash, he smashed through a dozen or so great liquor-jars stacked together, then trampled down several fences and half a wooden house, and drove straight down along a sloping earthen bank.

In what state the five casks were, Zhang Xiaojing knew without looking back. After so many collisions the smell of sulfur had grown richer and richer, and was already as near its limit as made no difference. In truth, that the fierce-fire thunder had held out this long without bursting was a miracle owed to the blessing of all the gods and buddhas in heaven.

Death was near, yet in his single eye showed neither panic nor despair, only a stillness—that stone-like stillness.

At the foot of the earthen bank was a canal about six zhang wide, its surface locked under a thick sheet of ice. This was the Guangtong Canal, which entered the city by the Jinguang Gate and flowed into the West Market along the two wards of Jude and Qunxian. To ease the water-haulage of Qinling timber, the Guangtong Canal had just been widened once in the second year of Tianbao, deep and broad enough to bear great boats of five hundred shi.

Three double-hours before, it was here that Cao Poyan had jumped into the river and thrown off pursuit. On the ice there was still a cracked-open hole, the very mark Cui Qi had made when he fell in.

His face expressionless, Zhang Xiaojing drew the cloak tight and, one last time, whipped the shaft-horses hard. The momentum of the slope, and the shaft-horses running wild with pain, brought the cart to a tremendous speed. It swept over the stepped canal-embankment tamped of yellow earth and, without a backward glance, plunged down toward the broad face of the ice.

The heavy cart flew through the air and smashed down hard onto the thin ice. With a great report the ice broke through, as could only be expected, and the icy spray turned to a thousand hands that dragged the cart down to the deep of the water. And at the same moment the fierce-fire thunder in the cart-bed burst apart at last, a run of fire-clouds half above the water and half below, giving off a muffled boom, ring upon ring of ripples spreading swiftly outward.

The Guangtong Canal, like a startled giant serpent, all at once thrashed into a mad churning. Spray and sparks bloomed together, countless small shards of ice flew high, and with them thick smoke shot straight up to the sky. Had the poets who roamed the capital been standing on the bank just then to see this marvel, they would surely have chanted many a famous line.

Not long after the blast, a great press of the Jing'an Bureau's and the Right Xiao Guard's elite rushed to both banks of the canal. By now the ice along this stretch had all broken up, and on the water floated only half a broken cartwheel, charred black through and through.

The whole affair had begun on the ice here, and here beneath the water it ended, as though it were the Buddhist wheel of rebirth made flesh.

By a first reckoning, the canal-embankment hereabouts had been shaken open in a great fissure, a water-gate stood askew, half of a little city-god shrine beside the canal had been shaken down, some trees and small boats along the near bank were destroyed, and a few porters had broken their legs—and this was the whole of the loss.

How many of the five casks of fierce-fire thunder had in the end exploded could no longer be verified. But one thing was clear: had Zhang Xiaojing not sent the cart into the Guangtong Canal to quell fire with water, then wherever they had gone off, the loss would have been dozens of times what it now was.

The crisis was safely past at last, and everyone let out a long breath. Only now did they grasp Zhang Xiaojing's intent—that in such a desperate strait, the West Market's Guangtong Canal was the one way out; a wonder he could think of the plan at all, and a greater wonder that he had dared carry it out with his own body.

The men of the Jing'an Bureau arrived one after another and made ready to set about clearing the ground. Xu Bin ran faster than any of them; he dashed foremost to the canal-side and gazed anxiously over the water, straining to find some trace of his friend. He searched back and forth several times and saw no figure, and his lips began to tremble despite himself. It was he who had recommended Zhang Xiaojing to the Jing'an Bureau; if by this he had instead cost him his life, he would carry the guilt of it his whole life long.

In his distress Xu Bin seized Yao Runeng by the arm: "My eyes aren't good; you see clearly—have you found him or not? Ah, yes—the West Market office keeps six grasshopper-boats on the Guangtong Canal; call them up at once and search the middle of the river!"

Yao Runeng was overcome now with a rush of feelings; this condemned man had wholly won him over. Zhang Xiaojing had not been boasting after all; he had truly gone through fire and death for this city. Looking back now, save for the killing of Xiao Yi, all Zhang Xiaojing had done in these hours was beyond reproach. Yao Runeng was the more ashamed: to think he had doubted, all this while, so great a hero.

Still, he judged that under so violent an explosion a survivor was hardly to be looked for. Yao Runeng had not the heart to tell Xu Bin this judgment, and so kept standing at the river's edge in silence, his eyes fixed, solemn and still.

If Zhang Xiaojing had died so, then he and all he had lived through would become a riddle forever unsolved.

A sound of footsteps came, and turning to look, he found the Deputy Director too had come in person, standing far off on the earthen bank to watch, his face beyond reading. That lovely maidservant stood beside him, her goose-yellow brocade jacket conspicuous indeed. Yao Runeng thought: at the outset the Deputy Director had overruled all objection to employ Zhang Xiaojing, and had even fallen out with Director He over it; what would his feelings be, he wondered, facing this end now?

And just then, on the bank across the canal, some buliang men were waving their hands and crying out in excitement. Yao Runeng hastily put away his musings, and with Xu Bin looked over that way at the same moment.

They saw several buliang men helping a figure up from the river toward the bank. The figure was wrapped in a cloak and looked very weak, but at least it could move. Behind them stood a tall, nine-tiered stone sutra-pillar wrought with lotus petals.

The faith in Buddhism had grown a mighty fashion in Great Tang, and a vital spot like the Guangtong Canal naturally needed a sutra-pillar raised there too, to invoke the blessing of the bodhisattvas and temple-guardians, and serving besides to gauge the depth of the canal-water. The figure just now must have come to rest right at the foot of the stone sutra-pillar, and so had not been found by the first party of searchers.

Xu Bin leapt up in his excitement, half minded to swim straight across. He pressed Yao Runeng, asking over and over whether it was Zhang Xiaojing. Yao Runeng forced down his wildly pounding heart and gazed out as far as he could. His sight was very keen, and at one glance he saw that grey-brown cloak, with several great black holes burned in it.

Yes—that was the fire-proof cloak.

Which meant that Zhang Xiaojing was still alive?!

Most likely he had jumped from the cart of his own will in the last instant before the blast, and had been flung by the shock-wave over to this side of the stone sutra-pillar. The cloak had spared him the first wave of the flames' searing, and the eight-edged shape of the sutra-pillar had been easy to grip, so that he had not sunk to the bottom of the water. This was the gods' and buddhas' blessing indeed!

Xu Bin and Yao Runeng cheered like children, joy brimming past all hiding. Yao Runeng let out a great breath; an end like this could not be more perfect. He began to shape in his mind what he would say when they met in a while: whether it were better first to congratulate him on the pardon of his death sentence, or better to apologize once again.

Zhang Xiaojing did not know that two men on the far bank were cheering his survival. His head was still swimming and his body very weak, and after a few supported steps he had to sit down where he was. Fortunate as he had been just now to escape the blast, the taste of being first burned by fire and then soaked in ice was hard indeed to bear. The wounds at his severed finger, under his arm, and on his back began to ooze blood again.

A few buliang men attentively took off his sopping, tattered cloak and outer robe and put a dry, thick jacket about him. "Commander Zhang, thanks to you, all is safe and sound now." So said one of the buliang men ingratiatingly, holding out a cloth.

Zhang Xiaojing took the cloth, wiped the water from his eye-socket, and handed it back to the buliang man, but there was on his face not the least ease of a great matter settled.

The Wolf Guards were indeed all dead, yet he could not shake the feeling that the whole thing was not over. The fierce-fire thunder had been not too many but too few—a mere fifteen casks, enough at most to blow up a few wards, nowhere near enough to burn the whole of Chang'an to ash. Could the "Que-le Huo-duo" on which the Türks had pinned such high hopes really be so simple a thing?

Were it so simple, one need only drive the carts and ram; what need of a ward-map to guide them?

Besides which, Wen Ran's whereabouts were still unknown; neither in the warehouse nor in those three carts just now had any trace of a woman been seen.

There were too many questions in the matter. Zhang Xiaojing was just turning over how to put it to Li Bi when he heard a clanging tread drawing near from afar; looking up, he saw it was Cui Qi. Cui Qi had charge of the search on this side of the canal, and so had come first.

"Commander Cui, the thing isn't over; take me to the Deputy Director at once," Zhang Xiaojing said loudly.

But Cui Qi's face was stiff, with no hint of a smile. He walked up before Zhang Xiaojing and raised a hand, and two Lüben Guards soldiers rushed over like wolves and tigers and pinned Zhang Xiaojing's arms fast.

"Take him away." Cui Qi would not so much as meet his eyes.
