## Chapter 6. The Great Spies of the East: The Transnational Contest for Strategic Intelligence

The year 1941, in the Second World War, held two great international events: Germany's surprise attack on the Soviet Union, and Japan's surprise attack on the American base at Pearl Harbor.

A surprise attack the attacker naturally guards as an absolute secret, and the intelligence world just as naturally rates it the most important strategic intelligence of all. Every intelligence service on earth was chasing these two great international prizes, and the Soviet Union and the United States prized them beyond all others. And yet, on this very intelligence, on which the survival of their nations turned, the Soviet supreme commander Stalin and the American president Roosevelt both got it wrong.

Even before the world had finished reckoning up the intelligence failures of the past, America produced fresh ones. The surprise attack of September 11 was an intelligence failure. The invasion of Iraq that turned up no weapons of mass destruction was an intelligence failure. Historians have judged it harshly: it was intelligence failure that left America unable to head off September 11, and September 11 was the very hinge on which America turned from ascendancy toward decline.

Was there, then, any country in the Second World War whose intelligence failed less often?

There was: China.

Weak as China was, relatively, in 1941, the door to the intelligence that the superpowers could not force, China had already pried open.

### 1. A Japanese "State-Policy School" on the Shanghai Bund

Early to the mark, long in its sights: Japan's national policy toward China deserves the name "the long policy."

From the Meiji Restoration onward, Japan had China in its sights. In 1896 an army lieutenant, Arao Sei, went to ground in China and opened the Hankou branch of a Japanese pharmacy, the Rakuzendō; this was the first intelligence organ Japan planted in China. In 1898 an army captain, Nezu Hajime, was ordered to join the East Asia Common Culture Society as its managing director, and he set out a grand vision for intelligence: "The ideal is for experts burning with the sense of mission of the Emperor's loyal soldiers to make the treaty ports their bases and to soak into China as water soaks into cloth. To train up these picked men is the task of Nezu's East Asia Common Culture Academy."

Soak into China! To have men skilled at making war on China, Japan meant to found a spy school in China, and to raise on Chinese soil high-grade spies steeped in Chinese culture.

In 1901 a Japanese school called the Shanghai East Asia Common Culture Academy opened its doors in Shanghai. In name it was founded by the incorporated East Asia Common Culture Society, a private school; in fact it was run by the Foreign Ministry and the army, and it trained "China hands" and nothing else.

The East Asia Common Culture Society took "East Asian co-prosperity" as its creed; its members ran all through Japan's military, political, and academic worlds, and it guided Japan's China policy. This nominally private body carried a semi-official standing, was reckoned an outer arm of the Japanese Foreign Ministry, and went by the name "the state-policy society." The founder of the East Asia Society, Inukai Tsuyoshi, later became prime minister of Japan; the Common Culture Society was founded by the nobleman Prince Konoe Atsumaro, whose son Konoe Fumimaro also later became prime minister.

The academy the "state-policy society" founded was likewise a "state-policy school": its principal was appointed by the government, and its curriculum, its selection and posting of teachers and students, all fell under the government's education ministry, so that it was recognized as a "talent nursery" for the empire's diplomacy. This private school won official recognition from both Japan and China, was folded into the education systems of both, took government-funded students from both, and its graduates could enter the civil service of either country without examination. Most of its Japanese graduates went to the Foreign Ministry and to the state enterprise the South Manchuria Railway, and spread through every Japanese trading house that dealt with China. Its Chinese graduates went into politics and scholarship too; among them was Wu Kaixian, chief of Shanghai's Bureau of Social Affairs.

The men the Shanghai academy turned out were more Chinese than the Chinese: they could move among China's upper crust and travel into the Chinese interior to survey it. Every survey report was copied out in five, and sent up to the Foreign Ministry, the Ministry of Agriculture and Commerce, the Army General Staff, the East Asia Common Culture Society, and the academy itself, as raw material for the study of China policy, and so to help frame the state policy of the Empire of Great Japan.

"State policy," the policy of a nation: the mission was one of the highest honor, and what other school would dare, like the Shanghai academy, to call itself a "state-policy school"?

The Communists' work against Japan, too, was early to the mark and long in its sights.

In 1901 Japan set up the Shanghai East Asia Common Culture Academy in China to train bilingual spies. In 1923 the Communists were recruiting Party members and building a Party organization inside that same academy.

Mei Gongbin, a native of Huangmei in Hubei, knew Japanese culture from childhood. His uncle Mei Baoji was a member of parliament under the Beiyang government, and the family lived in the Japanese concession in Hankou, Japanese in everything from language to daily habits. Mei Gongbin sat the academy's entrance examination in 1921, and before he had even graduated he was given the chance to go and see Tokyo, and was encouraged in person by the president, Konoe Fumimaro. The school was sure that, after such extraordinary treatment, this Chinese star pupil would surely become a pro-Japanese stalwart.

Who could have guessed. Back in Shanghai, Mei Gongbin threw himself straight into the strike at the cotton mills of western Shanghai, against Japanese owners bullying Chinese workers. His Hubei townsman Wan Xiyan and his academy classmate Gao Ersong had both joined the Communists; in 1924 Mei Gongbin joined the Communist Youth League and built, inside the academy, the first Youth League branch in Shanghai's Xujiahui district, its members all academy students. In 1925 the League members went over to the Party, this branch became the first Communist Party branch in Xujiahui, and Mei Gongbin was its secretary.

A Japanese spy school had become a base of Communist organization, and a base of Shanghai's anti-Japanese movement besides.

The academy, breeding its cross-border intelligence men, began hiring more Chinese teachers. And so Wang Xuewen walked in through its gates.

Wang Xuewen had gone to Japan at fifteen to study at the Tokyo Common Culture Academy, and in 1921 was admitted to Tokyo Imperial University, where he studied under the socialist scholar Kawakami Hajime. In this way Wang Xuewen spent seventeen years studying in Japan, and brought home the true scripture of Communism.

This Chinese scholar, trained at a Japanese university, was in fact a secret Communist, and inside the Japanese spy school he raised up a set of progressive young men. Nishizato Tatsuo, Ishida Shichirō, Ishida Takeo, Iwahashi Takeji, and Ozaki Shōtarō of the 26th class; Anzai Kuraji of the 27th; Shirai Yukiyuki of the 28th; and Nakanishi Kō and Mizuno Shigeru of the 29th—these classmates together formed a secret society for Japanese-Chinese friendship, the "Japan-China Struggle League."

Zhou Enlai's instruction to Wang Xuewen was this: to have Japanese men of conscience working alongside us is a help in understanding Japanese militarism. Look further into the situation of these Japanese youths, and with those genuinely willing to stand with the Chinese people against Japanese militarism, take care to strengthen the ties.

The Japan-China Struggle League formed of its own accord a Youth League academy branch, electing Anzai Kuraji as branch secretary and Nakanishi Kō as organization officer. That branch secretary, Anzai Kuraji, would later become a secretary of the Central Committee of the Japanese Communist Party, a famous friend of China. That organization officer, Nakanishi Kō, would become the great spy of the East who obtained the intelligence of Pearl Harbor.

On September 18, 1931, Japan's Kwantung Army attacked the Chinese garrison at Shenyang in the Northeast. No warning beforehand, no plan for the event: the Chinese army abandoned resistance, withdrew from the Northeast, and vast stretches of Chinese territory fell into other hands.

The Chinese were enraged, and the anti-Japanese tide surged through Shanghai. Both sides poured in troops and readied for war, and Shanghai became overnight the new focus of the fighting.

War always frightens off the civilians, and always draws in the spies. A woman spy in men's clothing came to Shanghai.

Kawashima Yoshiko, born Aisin Gioro Xianyu, Chinese name Jin Bihui, was the fourteenth daughter of the Qing dynasty's Prince Su; she was later adopted by the Japanese Kawashima Naniwa and took a Japanese name. Kawashima Yoshiko never forgot that she was born a Qing princess, and hoped to use Japan's power to restore the Qing. When the Japanese agent Doihara smuggled the last Qing emperor, Puyi, out of Tianjin to Changchun, Kawashima Yoshiko played her part with a will, slipping alone into Tianjin to spirit out the Empress Wanrong.

To back up the events in the Northeast, Kawashima Yoshiko hurried to Shanghai, where she helped the assistant military attaché at the Japanese consulate-general, Tanaka Ryūkichi, set Japanese ruffians to making trouble and touch off the "January 28 Incident."

A German, too, was busy on the Shanghai Bund.

The German journalist Johnson came to China in 1929. A veteran of the First World War, he was soon a welcome guest at the German embassy and among the German military advisers.

In truth "Johnson" was really named Sorge, and his mission in China was to serve Moscow's international strategy. Japan's Kwantung Army was building up its forces along China's northeastern border, a grave threat to Soviet security in the Far East. So Soviet military intelligence borrowed from the Comintern a German Communist, Sorge, and sent him to Shanghai, to watch international movements from this Far Eastern intelligence hub and to defend the Soviet Union.

On good terms with the Germans in China, Sorge easily got hold of Chiang Kai-shek's dispositions for the encirclement of the Chinese Red Army. This intelligence Sorge not only sent to Moscow but passed on to the Communist organization. Communists of all countries were one family, and Sorge's work in China had Communist help. At the same time the Central Special Branch, under Zhou Enlai, was also getting Chiang Kai-shek's operational plans and sending them in good time to the Soviet area in Jiangxi. With intelligence this accurate, Mao Zedong and Zhu De won three successive campaigns against encirclement in Jiangxi.

Busy as he was in Shanghai, Sorge naturally needed the help of Chinese comrades. Agnes Smedley, who knew China's progressive literary men, introduced him to some contacts. The Liu Jinzhong couple, the Chen Hansheng couple, the Xiao Bingshi couple began working for Sorge. The Communist Special Branch also handed over to him such secret contacts as Cai Shuhou. In this way Sorge wove himself an intelligence network in China. In that network there were several Japanese as well: Smedley introduced Ozaki Hotsumi, who in turn introduced Kawai Teikichi and Mizuno Shigeru of the Common Culture Academy.

Japan was the source of the coming war in the Far East, and the Japanese naturally became prizes over which the world's spies fought.

### 2. The Agency Within the "Agency"

The January 28 Incident opened the fighting between the foreign concessions and the Chinese city of Shanghai.

The Japanese authorities, too, had to grow wary. Why had Shanghai not repeated the easy victory of the Manchurian Incident? Only now did they discover that their intelligence work against China lacked coordination, and that the consulate that oversaw the whole had no dedicated intelligence organ.

The newly arrived deputy consul-general, Iwai Eiichi, proposed founding an intelligence organ in China—which meant drawing on the reserve of Chinese-speaking talent at the Common Culture Academy.

Unluckily, the academy itself was in turmoil. With the two great powers locked in fierce struggle, this cross-border school split, as it was bound to: the right-wing teachers and students busied themselves supporting the Japanese army, while left-wing students like Nakanishi Kō went to the Nanshi stadium to mourn those who had fallen resisting Japan. The two factions went their own ways, and even came to blows now and then. When Iwai Eiichi came to the school for men, the school could not so much as keep hold of its own people; the students had all gone off to make trouble.

The school had no choice but to send these troublesome students home on leave, and on the voyage back Nakanishi Kō and Ozaki Hotsumi crossed the sea together.

Ozaki Hotsumi had grown up in Taiwan with his father and knew Chinese well; in 1922 he was admitted to Tokyo Imperial University, where he met socialist ideas. In 1928 he became the Shanghai correspondent of the Asahi Shimbun, wrote a great deal introducing China, and became a center for Shanghai's progressive Japanese. Ozaki Hotsumi took an active part in the work of the League of Left-Wing Writers, kept close company with Lu Xun, Xia Yan, Wang Xuewen, Tian Han, and others, and translated the American woman writer Smedley's autobiographical novel *Daughter of Earth*. Through Smedley he came to know the Soviet secret agent Sorge.

The Ozaki family was one of Japan's noted aristocratic houses; its ancestor General Kusunoki had died bravely defending the Emperor, and a statue of Kusunoki stands before the imperial palace, so that people call him "the Yue Fei of Japan." A descendant of such a line would have found everything in Japan smooth going. And yet Ozaki Hotsumi gave up his rich inheritance and set himself against the empire's mistaken state policy.

Two descendants of noble houses, both children of privilege, both denounced as traitors. But one gave himself for the progress and peace of mankind, and the other sank into the mire of private greed.

After Ozaki Hotsumi and Nakanishi Kō returned to Japan, they took no part in progressive left-wing work; instead they changed their coats and appeared as China experts, active in academic circles, and Nakanishi Kō even presented papers to international scholarly bodies.

This Institute of Pacific Relations had been founded by American scholars, and met by turns in the countries around the Pacific rim. The United States and the Soviet Union though opposed, Japan and China though at war, all had scholars meeting face to face on this platform. The topics always bore closely on the Asia-Pacific situation, and the views this authoritative body reached could often sway the decisions of the relevant governments. The first Chinese delegates their government sent were all scholars of the first rank: Yan Huiqing, Jiang Menglin, Hu Shi.

The scholars of both countries crossed swords on this platform now and then, and the historical ownership of China's Northeast was one fierce topic. In July 1929, at the preparatory meeting the Chinese branch convened in Shenyang, the chairman of the arrangements, Yan Baohang, obtained the memorial that the Japanese prime minister Tanaka Giichi had submitted to the Emperor, which said: "To conquer the world, one must first conquer China; to conquer China, one must first conquer Manchuria and Mongolia." The Chinese side held that this secret document laid bare Japan's policy of aggression, and above all its designs on China. When the Kyoto conference met, the Chinese asked that the Tanaka Memorial be put on the agenda; the Japanese flatly refused, and so Yan Baohang had the memorial translated into English and handed it around off the floor. That struggle exposed Japan's state policy by a side door, and put the countries of the Asia-Pacific on their guard.

Nakanishi Kō also discovered that Ozaki had a secret mission besides: one of the foreign friends Ozaki associated with was the very German journalist, Johnson, whom Nakanishi Kō had seen in Shanghai.

Sorge had come to Japan. Japan's ambition against the Soviet Union was plain for all to see, and its strategic plans had to be known. Japan and Germany were both under fascist rule, so a German like Sorge could move about Japan with relative ease.

In Tokyo Sorge built an intelligence cell, named for the initials of his own name, code-named the "Ramsay group." Its task was not to count how many aircraft or ships the Japanese army had, but to read Japan's strategic direction.

Bearing a great charge, Sorge was not without friends when he first set foot in this foreign country. He had friends in Japan too: Ozaki Hotsumi, Kawai Teikichi, Mizuno Shigeru, and the others he had met in Shanghai were now returning one after another to Japan. On the surface these high Japanese intellectuals had turned to the government; in secret they worked with Sorge.

Nakanishi Kō, though he never formally joined the group, kept up a scholarly collaboration with Ozaki Hotsumi. And Ozaki Hotsumi gave him a new task: liaison with China.

Ozaki Hotsumi recommended Nakanishi Kō for a post at the head office of the South Manchuria Railway, and the head office of the SMR was at Dalian, in China. Nakanishi Kō was more than glad of the chance to return to China, the second home of which he dreamed day and night.

That "SMR" was, on its face, in charge of running the railways of China's Northeast; in fact it was Japan's "state-policy company." After the Russo-Japanese War of 1905, Japan wrested from Russia its interests in the Northeast, and the southern stretch, below Changchun, of the Chinese Eastern Railway that Russia had built there passed to Japan. With the state putting up half the capital and the imperial house and nobility the other half, Japan founded the South Manchuria Railway Company, the "SMR" for short.

The SMR was an independent kingdom squatting on the soil of Manchuria, beyond the reach of the local Chinese authorities. Its president was appointed by the Emperor of Japan; its operating policy was set by the Japanese cabinet; its operating budget was the largest in the world; and it had become the custom that a retired SMR president would go on to serve as foreign minister in the cabinet. This top-flight Japanese enterprise was private in name, but in fact a "state-policy company," charged with a national economic mission. The SMR not only controlled the economy of China's Northeast but sought to grasp the economic lifelines of all China. Whenever the Japanese army moved, the SMR swiftly marshaled its trains in support; from the Northeast to North China to Central China, the SMR was always the logistical backbone of the army's campaigns.

This "state within a state" even had an army of its own. The SMR's head office was at Dalian, a city the Qing had ceded to Japan and which was called the "Kwantung Leased Territory," and the SMR's railway guards were called the "Kwantung Army." On the strength of the SMR's economic power the Kwantung Army swelled swiftly, servant turning master, growing step by step into the master of the SMR and then into the overlord of "Manchukuo." In the fighting in North China and in the war against the Soviet Union, the Kwantung Army was always the mainstay of Japanese forces.

The SMR was also an intelligence organ on an enormous scale, weighted toward economic intelligence. Its investigators, over a thousand of them, reached their feelers into the Chinese interior and mapped every important factory and mineral deposit in China, down to military terrain-intelligence maps. Every annual meeting of the Institute of Pacific Relations was sure to feature the SMR's papers on China, and army spies on cross-border secret work borrowed the cover of SMR employees.

Ozaki Hotsumi took a post as a commissioned adviser to the SMR—another advance for the secret intelligence work. Nakanishi Kō was not yet qualified to be any kind of consultant, but to get into the SMR's Research Department was still to burrow into Japan's treasure-house of intelligence.

After reaching Dalian, Nakanishi Kō laid plans to be transferred to Shanghai; most of Japan's secret-service organs in China were in Shanghai.

An "agency"? In Chinese the word carries a double weight: it can mean an office that handles affairs, and it can mean a close, cunning scheme. The Japanese joined the two senses in one, and called an office whose whole business was intrigue an "agency."

The "Matsu Kikan," the Pine Agency, worked against Song Zheyuan's Northwestern Army in North China. The "Ran Kikan," the Orchid Agency, worked against Li Zongren and Bai Chongxi of the two Guangs, Guangdong and Guangxi. The "Take Kikan," the Bamboo Agency, worked against Chiang Kai-shek's central army in Chongqing. The "Ume Kikan," the Plum Agency, worked against Wang Jingwei's Reform Government in Nanjing. Pine, bamboo, plum, and orchid are the elegant emblems of Chinese culture, but in the hands of Japan's spies they masked evil work.

The "Ume Kikan," the "Iwai Kōkan," and "No. 76" were the three great secret-service organs by which Japan controlled Shanghai and eastern China.

An army colonel, Kagesa Sadaaki, had himself engineered Wang Jingwei's flight from Chongqing, and had gone in person to Hanoi in Vietnam to bring Wang Jingwei to Nanjing. In Nanjing Wang Jingwei set up a "central government," on the face of it a rival to Chiang Kai-shek in Chongqing, but with a Japanese overlord behind it, so that the Chinese called it "the puppet regime of traitors." Kagesa Sadaaki, promoted to major-general, became the supreme military adviser of the Wang Jingwei regime, and the Ume Kikan was the empire's supreme military advisory mission to the Nanjing government. Its thirty-odd members, drawn from the Japanese army, navy, and Foreign Ministry, formed a little cabinet of Japan in China. This supreme secret-service organ could work the Wang Jingwei regime directly, and at the same time coordinate the secret-service organs of Japan's several systems.

No. 76, on the books the Secret Service General Headquarters of the Wang Jingwei regime, was really the Chinese branch of the Japanese secret service. Japanese agents were not, after all, Chinese, and always felt a certain distance moving on Chinese ground; so they gathered up some Chinese agents and built this organization, and later reclassified it as the Wang Jingwei regime's Secret Service General Headquarters. Its director, Ding Mocun, was concurrently minister of social affairs of the Nanjing government; its deputy director, Li Shiqun, was concurrently minister of police; and this secret-service organ of collaborators had one job, to crush Chinese anti-Japanese organizations. No. 76's first chief, Ding Mocun, was a turncoat from Zhongtong, and a grave threat to Zhongtong's Shanghai district. Zhongtong sent a beautiful woman agent, Zheng Pingru, to lure Ding Mocun and try to assassinate him. But the seasoned Ding Mocun saw through his opponent, and Zhongtong lost both its beauty and its men. This tale of a beauty and a spy became the raw material for Eileen Chang's story *Lust, Caution*, and seventy years later entered Ang Lee's film. Li Shiqun had in his early days been in the Communist Special Branch, had trained in the Soviet Union, was arrested on a dog-beating-squad operation and turned, joined the Nationalist secret service, and later went over of his own accord to Japan. This veteran agent who had crossed two parties and two countries built No. 76 with his own hands, elbowed Ding Mocun aside, took the governorship of Jiangsu as well, and drenched Shanghai in blood and terror.

Facing so vicious an enemy, the deputy head of the Communist Central Social Affairs Department, Pan Hannian, showed no fear. Pan Hannian meant to break into these very "agencies."

Through Guan Lu, win over Li Shiqun. That would give control of the Wang Jingwei puppet secret service.

Through Yuan Shu, reach Iwai Eiichi. That would break into the secret-service system of the Japanese Foreign Ministry.

To enter the diplomatic system was still not enough; Pan Hannian hoped to reach deeper yet, into the Japanese decision-making core. There was a foundation for the idea: the Japanese connections Zhou Enlai had banked in earlier years were now handed on to Pan Hannian.

Nakanishi Kō drew together his fellow academy alumni in China to form a Japanese intelligence cell. He also kept up his tie with Ozaki Hotsumi in Tokyo—and Ozaki was then an adviser to Prime Minister Konoe, a man at the very core.

For reconnoitering intelligence on Japan, these Japanese were the most effective of all.

There was, all the same, one great doubt: the Japanese were famous for their willingness to die for the Emperor. Could such men truly work for China's war of resistance?

### 3. Yan'an Had a Japanese School Too

Pan Hannian asked Wang Xuewen about Nakanishi Kō. Wang Xuewen was at that time working in Yan'an, his public post vice-president of the Marx-Lenin Academy, his secret post head of the Enemy-Army Work Department of the Eighth Route Army's General Political Department.

Deeply versed in Japanese, "Department head Wang" ran a training class for enemy-army work; its 150 trainees, with a rough command of spoken Japanese, were parceled out to the frontline units. In this way every part of the Eighth Route Army had enemy-work cadres, and cadres were sent even to the far-off New Fourth Army.

Wang Xuewen, who had once taught at the Japanese school in Shanghai, now opened in Yan'an a Japanese Workers' and Peasants' School, its students all Japanese prisoners of war.

A school for prisoners of war? Such a way of handling prisoners was seen nowhere else in the world. Even the Geneva Convention, for all its rules on the humane treatment of prisoners, provided only for prison camps; a school had never occurred to it. In the Second World War Germany packed millions of captured Soviet soldiers into concentration camps, shot the political commissars, and forced the rest into puppet units; the Soviet Union set captured German soldiers to hard labor, and shot 30,000 Polish officers in the Katyn Forest; America penned Japanese residents into internment camps in Hawaii; Japan held British and American civilians in the camp at Weifang. Set against the camps, this Communist school was a strange thing indeed.

Even the school's own students were not used to their new station. Most of the Japanese students came in bowed under a heavy load of ideas, unable to lay down the notion that they were "captives." The eldest was the forty-two-year-old Sakai, a foreman before he was drafted; on the Emperor's birthday, Sakai led five students quietly up a hilltop, faced east, and knelt in worship toward the Emperor.

And yet life at this school gave these former Japanese officers and men a freshness they had never known. Here the students were treated as company-grade officers; whatever you had been before, officer or private, all were equal. The little soldiers who hated class oppression were delighted—the Eighth Route Army really did treat people well. The school even had recreation: you could read Japanese books and papers in the library, and the students got up performances of their own. These days of making their own fun were freer than life in the Japanese army.

Every teacher here had studied in Japan and was deeply learned: Wang Xuewen, Li Chuli, Zhao Anbo, He Sijing. Jiang Youshu even taught them to sing the Japanese song "Karatachi no Hana," far above the level of the Japanese officers. Step by step the students worked their way through questions of theory, came to understand what exploitation was, cast off the feudal notions of a "divine land" and the like, and saw the "New Order in East Asia" for the aggression it was.

On this day Yan'an held a grand opening ceremony, with Commander-in-Chief Zhu De and more than two thousand others attending.

Mao Zedong's inscription read: "The Chinese people and the Japanese people are as one; they have only one enemy, and that is Japanese imperialism and the scum of the Chinese nation."

Zhu De's inscription read: "We are internationalists. We favor the proletariat and the revolutionary peoples of the world uniting and joining together to wipe out the present imperialist war and to build a new world, truly friendly, peaceful, free, and equal."

At the meeting a Japanese student read out an oath: "The Eighth Route Army not only did not kill us; it has let us know the joy of becoming men again."

Through their studies the Japanese students came to feel deeply the "sincere international love" of their Chinese comrades, and resolved to take up antiwar work. Yan'an radio broadcast in Japanese twice a week, and most of the scripts came from the Japanese students.

On October 26, 1941, the Conference of the Anti-Fascist Peoples of the East opened in Yan'an, and the Japanese students of the Yan'an Workers' and Peasants' School took the stage together—Japanese brothers swearing to join the Eighth Route Army.

The graduates of the Yan'an Japanese Workers' and Peasants' School went off to every battlefield the Communists led, and set up branch schools in northwest Shanxi, in Shandong, and in Central China, training in all five or six hundred people. These graduates became the Eighth Route Army's enemy-army-work cadres, carrying out battlefield propaganda against the Japanese army and winning over no small number of Japanese soldiers to turn against the war and come across.

The Communists' work against Japan was deeply humane. The Eighth Route Army and the New Fourth Army did not put Japanese prisoners to death, but took them alive wherever they could. The first thing an Eighth Route or New Fourth soldier said to a prisoner was, "We won't kill you." The second was, "You are our brother."

Belden, a reporter for the English-language Shanghai paper the *Shanghai Evening Post & Mercury*, saw with a New Fourth Army unit a Japanese prisoner named Kagawa Masao, who ate and lodged with the New Fourth Army's enemy-work cadres and, though no one watched him, would not run off. Kagawa Masao explained: to flee was dangerous—caught by Chinese peasants, he would be killed; questioned by the Japanese gendarmerie, he would be killed—and it was safer to live with the New Fourth Army. This Japanese prisoner spent his days busily helping the New Fourth Army write letters, urging Japanese soldiers to lay down their arms.

Do not kill prisoners, do not abuse prisoners, let prisoners stay or go as they please: these lenient prisoner policies pried a crack in the solid discipline of the Japanese army. In 1937, at its first battle at Pingxingguan, the Eighth Route Army took not a single prisoner; by 1940 Japanese soldiers were coming across of their own accord. Some Japanese prisoners the Eighth Route Army released on purpose, and back in their units they became living advertisements for Eighth Route Army policy. It was Japan, on the contrary, that showed no mercy: of the men its gendarmes seized, questioned, and sent back, some were actually killed.

Comparing the prisoner policies of every side, Nakanishi Kō sighed from the heart: the Communists were the cleverer after all, and these prisoner policies, unheard of in the war histories of the world, were the most powerful of psychological bombs.

Nakanishi Kō studied Mao Zedong's *On Protracted War* with care; in these days he was given an intelligence task by the army—to write "A Survey of China's War-Fighting Strength."

All sorts of analyses were then in fashion in China. The "national-subjugation" school saw only China's weakness and not its vastness, and held that China could only wait for foreign aid, that to resist alone was to be conquered; and so the Chiang Kai-shek government never dared declare war on Japan. The "quick-victory" school saw only China's vastness and not its weakness, and held that China could swiftly beat Japan; after the first victory at Pingxingguan there were those in the Chinese army who thought mobile warfare could win it, and after the single victory at Tai'erzhuang the *Ta Kung Pao* called it an "all but decisive battle." Little did they know that the Japanese army still had the strength to press on.

Neither school saw China's progress and its wealth of support; both lacked any dynamic analysis, and neither set out the stages through which the war would pass.

Mao Zedong, comparing the two countries, first grasped the basic features on the plane of quality: "The enemy is strong and we are weak; the enemy is small and we are large; the enemy is retrogressive and we are progressive; the enemy is poorly supported and we are well supported." On this sure grasp of quality Mao Zedong then set out the change of quantity, summing up the course of the war in three stages: defensive, stalemate, counter-offensive.

Japan or America, the SMR or the RAND Corporation—whoever could produce an intelligence analysis like this at the very outset of the war would be the world's foremost master of intelligence.

Nakanishi Kō's "Survey of China's War-Fighting Strength" was born. Chiang Kai-shek stressed the strength of the land, "trading space for time," using China's vast territory to slow the Japanese advance, and pulling back in passive retreat where he could not stand and fight. Mao Zedong stressed manpower, "the united front," drowning the relatively few Japanese in a sea of population and vigorously waging a people's war. China's capacity for resistance had enormous latent power, which the Japanese side underrated—an important reason its offensive was checked; and the Chinese authorities, too, underrated the people's capacity for resistance—another important reason China's war effort was on the back foot. The overall judgment: the greatest resource of China's war-fighting strength was the guerrilla war of the masses. The people's guerrilla units of North and Central China would tie down the bulk of the Japanese China Expeditionary Army, and leave Japan unable to finish off the "China Incident."

An authoritative report from an authoritative scholar: Nakanishi Kō's standing in Japanese intelligence circles rose sharply, and Ozaki Hotsumi commended this rising talent to the Chief Cabinet Secretary, Kazami Akira. Now Nakanishi Kō could reach higher officials, and so reach deeper secrets.

Only they did not know that Nakanishi Kō's method of analysis was learned from Mao Zedong's *On Protracted War*.

Nakanishi Kō admired Mao Zedong; he trusted the Communist Party as a mature great party; and he was glad to work under that party's leadership, even though he himself was not Chinese.

The power of intelligence, too, is a power of resistance.

Nakanishi Kō wished with all his heart to add, to China's power of resistance, a measure of the power of intelligence.

### 4. A Deft Stratagem to Sow Discord

By the time the war of resistance reached its stalemate stage, the Communists' intelligence channels had spread out on every side.

The Pan Hannian system, in charge of work behind enemy lines, ran channels into the heights of Japanese politics and the depths of the Wang Jingwei regime. Most successful of all was the Japanese cell that Wu Chengfang handled. Nakanishi Kō, holding his ground in Shanghai, had broken into the thought section of the special-service class at the headquarters of the Central China Expeditionary Army, into the research section of the SMR's Shanghai office, and had even carved out and run a special investigation squad of his own. The Japanese comrades Nakanishi Kō handled were deployed across the country as well: at Nanjing, centered on Nishizato Tatsuo, was a set of Chinese comrades—Li Desen as group leader; Wang Jinyuan planted at Wang Jingwei's side as his secretary; Chen Yifeng posted at a news agency; and Qiu Linxiang, Cheng Weide, Zheng Baiqian, and others, with Zhang Mingyuan handling liaison between Nanjing and Shanghai. In the North China branch, Ozaki Shōtarō had penetrated a Japanese secret-service department, and the Chinese Qian Ming handled liaison with Shanghai. At Taiyuan in Shanxi there was Shirai Yukiyuki, and a branch was being set up at Dalian too. Nakanishi Kō's connections reached even into Japan itself, in a regular correspondence with the prime minister's adviser Ozaki Hotsumi.

Yan'an, presiding over the whole, had its own means of gathering intelligence. The Central Social Affairs Department set up a newspapers-and-publications section to sort out open intelligence in a systematic way; the Second Bureau of the Military Commission plucked intelligence from the sky over the wireless. The intelligence sent in by every system was cross-checked and analyzed together at Zaoyuan, where the Central Social Affairs Department was housed, and at once sent up by Department head Kang Sheng to the Central Secretariat—a short journey, for Mao Zedong lived in the same little village.

And so Mao Zedong, huddled in a loess cave, could yet command the great affairs of the world.

Pan Hannian, out on the front line, kept receiving telegrams from Yan'an demanding intelligence.

Nakanishi Kō learned from inside the Japanese agencies that, after its military reverses, the Japanese authorities had begun their "peace work" against China. This "peace work" was nothing but secret inducement to surrender—inducing both the Nationalist central government and its regional factions to give in.

Once such secret dealing succeeded, the united front of China's resistance would fall to pieces. Pan Hannian was on the sharpest guard, and at once made Japan's inducement-to-surrender work the focus of his intelligence effort.

Japanese agents had engineered Wang Jingwei's defection, meaning to have him form a regime and set himself up as a rival to Chiang Kai-shek in Chongqing. Kagesa Sadaaki, Imai Takeo, and Wang Jingwei were in urgent secret negotiation over a treaty, and on December 30, 1939, they produced the "Draft Outline for Adjusting the New Japan-China Relations." The very next day Nakanishi Kō copied it out and handed a copy to Zheng Wendao. Twenty days later Wang Jingwei's able lieutenant Gao Chongwu fled Shanghai for Hong Kong, denounced Wang Jingwei's treason, and made the treasonous secret pact public.

Hearing the news, Nakanishi Kō and Zheng Wendao exchanged a smiling glance. Mao Zedong had seen this secret pact long before. On January 28, 1940, the anniversary of the Shanghai Incident, the Communist Central Committee issued a directive to all Party members: "Overcome the Danger of Capitulation, Strive for a Better Turn in the Situation."

The art of war says, "master the enemy by striking first." To stop capitulation and betrayal, too, one must seize the initiative—and to seize the initiative meant getting the intelligence first, and so having a counter-move ready in advance.

Nakanishi Kō kept his eye fixed on the progress of the "peace work," and step by step laid the riddle bare.

In March 1939 the Japanese cabinet added a new body, the Kōa-in. Most of the China diplomacy that had been the Foreign Ministry's charge was handed to this office for the administration of Asian colonies: Japan no longer treated China as a whole country. The Kōa-in set up a special secret-service department, drawing Japan's several secret-service organs in China under one hand, to make the inducement of surrender more efficient.

Nakanishi Kō learned that certain mysterious Chinese, claiming to represent this or that high Nationalist official—from Yan Xishan in Shanxi to Yu Hanmou in Guangdong, from Long Yun in Yunnan to Xu Chongzhi in Chongqing—meant that the Nationalists' regional power-holders had eight lines running to Japan at once.

The identities of these representatives were doubtful, and the lines still only tentative; but the tentative could very well come to something. Had Wang Jingwei not defected? If the regional warlords broke off piece by piece, would the central power not be hollowed out? And more worrying still was the center itself: would Chiang Kai-shek follow in Wang Jingwei's footsteps?

Using his good relations with the military, Nakanishi Kō got as close as he could to the army's agents, and found that a new section chief had been posted to the intelligence section of the general army headquarters at Nanjing—that Colonel Imai Takeo was in Hong Kong running the most highly classified of all the peace operations, "Operation Kiri."

In February 1940 Imai Takeo went in person to Hong Kong for secret talks on behalf of the Japanese government; the Chinese representative called himself Song Ziliang and claimed to be a brother of Song Meiling. The two sides reached agreement on guarding against and opposing communism, but the points on which they could not agree could be settled fully only by the highest authorities coming forward. The two representatives envisioned a secret meeting somewhere in China among three men: Itagaki, Chiang, and Wang.

The Operation Kiri that Imai Takeo guarded so closely had, in fact, long been within Pan Hannian's sight.

On the reports of Nishizato Tatsuo and Nakanishi Kō, Pan Hannian went in person to Hong Kong to confirm it. Imai Takeo and Song Ziliang had chosen Hong Kong for their talks precisely to keep clear of Shanghai, that spy heaven, and clear of the eyes of the Communists and the Soviets.

But Pan Hannian had plenty of eyes in Hong Kong, and was even on visiting terms with the chief of Juntong's Hong Kong station, Wang Xinheng. That Wang Xinheng had himself been a progressive youth, had studied at Sun Yat-sen University in Moscow, and was a classmate of Chiang Ching-kuo. In the Juntong Hong Kong station that ran the talks Pan Hannian had his eyes; at Hong Kong University, where the go-between Zhang Zhiping worked, Pan Hannian had his connections; and in the Hongmen brotherhood that guarded the talks and the Hong Kong police who watched them, Pan Hannian had his men.

Pan Hannian reported the plot of Operation Kiri to Yan'an, and at the same time laid plans to stop it.

Pan Hannian quietly told Iwai Eiichi: that Nationalist representative at the Hong Kong talks, Song Ziliang, was a Red in disguise, deliberately arranging for the Japanese-Chinese summit to be held at Changsha, under Nationalist control.

Juntong luring Itagaki into a trap? Iwai Eiichi was startled, and then overjoyed. Startled that the Chinese agents were so sinister as to try to snare the supreme commander of the Japanese army; overjoyed that a hole had opened in Operation Kiri, so that the Foreign Ministry could hit back at the overbearing army.

And so the most highly classified Operation Kiri was called off by higher authority, with the very man running it, Imai Takeo, kept in the dark.

Imai Takeo had long reckoned on interference from within or sabotage from without; but never could he have imagined that the Communists on the outside could mount a pincer through the inside of Japan itself. Imai Takeo would have done well to study again the Chinese classic the Thirty-Six Stratagems, among which is the Stratagem of Sowing Discord.

Against the Nationalist factions' plots with Japan, Yan'an had its methods too. The Eighth Route Army's representative Wang Shiying convened a mass meeting in Shanxi, exposing Yan Xishan's secret talks with the Japanese army and even naming the representatives of both sides.

Treason fears nothing so much as public opinion, and a plot fears nothing so much as the light. With a single "open scheme," Mao Zedong overcame the danger of capitulation and won a better turn in the situation.

### 5. From "Barbarossa" to the "Kwantung Army Special Maneuvers"

As 1941 came on, the earth seemed to spin faster. The intelligence Wu Chengfang received from the Japanese cell quickened from once a month to once a fortnight.

In January, Chiang Kai-shek launched the New Fourth Army Incident, and Nationalist-Communist cooperation faced collapse. The Incident was a gesture of goodwill from Chiang Kai-shek toward Japan, and the Communists urgently needed to know how Japan would respond. The ordinary view would be that Japan would back the Incident and join with Chiang Kai-shek against the Communists. But Nakanishi Kō judged that Japan would not on this account ally with Chiang Kai-shek; rather it would exploit the Nationalist-Communist rift to press the Nationalists harder.

Sure enough, the Japanese government openly arranged for Wang Jingwei to visit Manchukuo, an affront to China's sovereignty. Nishizato Tatsuo and Wang Jinyuan, traveling with Wang Jingwei, further discovered that Yan Xishan of Shanxi had also sent men to make contact with the puppet regime. Chiang Kai-shek's New Fourth Army Incident, far from raising the price of a secret peace, had let Japan seize the chance to beat the price down.

For a Japanese to reconnoiter intelligence on Japan was a native advantage, and Nakanishi Kō had it. On March 8, the Japanese ambassador to the United States, Nomura, reached with the American Secretary of State, Hull, a "Draft Understanding between Japan and the United States." On May 19, from Nanjing came word that the British Foreign Secretary Eden, the Chinese ambassador to Britain Guo Taiqi, and the Japanese ambassador to Britain Shigemitsu Mamoru had worked out a peace scheme, which Guo was carrying to America for its views. None of this top-secret intelligence escaped Nakanishi Kō's eye.

Having intelligence channels made all the difference. From Shanghai, Hong Kong, Nanjing, Chongqing, channel after channel reported the international secret talks, and the German friend Anna Wang also detected Munich-like plotting in the Far East. The Communists could not only see through their opponents at home but see clear through the great powers abroad. On May 25, 1941, Mao Zedong issued an intra-Party directive, "Notice on Exposing the New Far Eastern Munich Plot."

Mao Zedong watched Japan; Stalin watched Germany.

Sorge's Ramsay group achieved a great deal: on April 9, 1939, it foresaw the Japanese-German alliance; on December 25, 1940, it reported that Germany was massing 80 divisions on the Soviet border to attack the Soviet Union—half a year ahead.

On March 5, April 26, May 15 and 21, June 1 and 15, 1941, Sorge sent secret telegram after secret telegram from Tokyo, urgently forecasting Germany's plan to invade the Soviet Union, "Operation Barbarossa."

In the same period the "Red Orchestra," lying low in Western Europe, and military attachés abroad also sent word back, and even Britain and America warned the Soviet Union more than once.

But the Soviet intelligence services kept doubting whether the reports were true, some even holding it a British trick to turn against the Soviet Union the German army poised to strike Britain. Even as he was building up Germany's armaments with all his might, Hitler played a deceiving game of goodwill toward the great European powers, signing the Munich Agreement with Britain and a Non-Aggression Pact with the Soviet Union.

Stalin had his own difficulties: getting the intelligence was hard, and once he had it, judging it was hard too. The Soviet Union valued intelligence work, but that very regard bred scattered, overlapping organs; and the internal purges had crippled the Cheka's foreign networks.

Soviet foreign intelligence was mainly the charge of the intelligence department of the Red Army General Staff, and Sorge belonged to that system. But Sorge's secret telegrams had to pass two sieves, the head of the General Staff's intelligence department and the chief of the General Staff, before they could go up to Stalin. And Sorge's superior in Moscow, the Red Army intelligence chief Berzin, had already been shot as a foreign spy. The new head of General Staff intelligence, Golikov, made a judgment of his own: "The rumors and documents about the inevitability of war against the Soviet Union this spring must be regarded as false intelligence spread by British, and possibly by German, intelligence."

Real intelligence taken for false—so how could the intelligence set before Stalin be anything but false?

The Comintern had Party organizations in every country, handy for gathering international intelligence; but the Soviets said, "Except for Pieck, no German can be trusted"—Pieck being the German Communist leader.

Foreigners could not be trusted, and Germans least of all, and the man bringing the German intelligence, Sorge, was precisely a German. On June 13, 1941, the Soviet news agency TASS was ordered to issue a statement: "The Soviet side holds the rumor of an alleged German intent to tear up the treaty and attack the Soviet Union to be wholly without foundation," and "the rumor that the Soviet Union is preparing to attack Germany is fabrication and provocation."

Just then China sent in intelligence.

Zhou Enlai, at Chongqing, was like Zhuge Liang pacifying the Five Routes from the calm of his own home: his intelligence feelers had long since reached out on every side, not only through the Japanese-occupied parts of China—Shanghai, Hong Kong, and the like—but out into the wider world, with Zhou Enlai's connections running from Tokyo to Southeast Asia to America.

Yan Baohang, planted in the upper reaches of the Nationalists, obtained the intelligence of Germany's coming attack on the Soviet Union; Zhou Enlai judged it accurate and reliable, and at once telegraphed a report to the Center at Yan'an. To get it to the Soviet side in time, Zhou Enlai also drafted a telegram in Russian for Stalin, asking the Yan'an headquarters to relay it to Moscow that same day. In the same period Pan Hannian, too, reported to Yan'an on June 13 that "a German-Soviet war is about to break out." Yan'an, for its part, had several means of reconnaissance, and had caught the very same intelligence as Yan Baohang.

Stalin, receiving the intelligence from China, believed it at last.

China too was a foreign country; but China's interests ran with the Soviet Union's, and the Chinese had no reason to sow discord between the Soviet Union and Germany. What was more, this was intelligence sent by the leaders of a brother party, a telegram signed by the head of the Communists' own intelligence work in person. And Yan Baohang, who had obtained it, was himself an agent of the Soviet embassy in China.

In the great international intelligence contest of the Second World War, the Communists were ahead by a round.

Germany and Japan were bound by a military alliance. Germany had struck the Soviet Union—would Japan strike in concert?

Barely two days after the German attack on the Soviet Union, on June 24, Tokyo held a grand banquet to welcome "China's head of state," Wang Jingwei, and the two sides signed a "Japan-China Treaty." In July the Japanese Army General Staff and the Kwantung Army headquarters drew up the plan for the "Kwantung Army Special Maneuvers," and great numbers of Japanese troops were moved from the home islands to China's Northeast.

Maneuvers are often the cover for a surprise attack—the German "Barbarossa" against the Soviet Union proved it. And these "Kwantung Army Special Maneuvers" were very likely the cover for a Japanese drive north.

Watching Japan about to move against the Soviet Union, certain Chinese, too, were excited. In the secret compromises reached in that "Operation Kiri" there had been no lack of tacit anti-Communist understandings.

Mao Zedong named the "Three Norths policy": Japan advancing north to attack the Soviet Union; Chiang Kai-shek pressing the New Fourth Army to cross north over the Yangtze; and the demand that the Eighth Route Army cross north over the Yellow River.

The Chinese Communists had suffered much in the past, struck without warning by Chiang Kai-shek in 1927. Now the Chinese Communists, stepping into a world war, were no longer naïve, and the old business of being clubbed from behind by their partners must not happen again. The whole Party and army were on the sharpest guard, and the intelligence system, above all, was mobilized to the last man to reconnoiter international strategic intelligence.

Intelligence work is a systematic undertaking; a few excellent agents cannot by themselves keep the whole system running—much depends on the design at the top.

Struck by the great "six-two-two" failure, the Soviet Union had to overhaul its intelligence organs. The intelligence department of the Red Army General Staff was split in two, reorganized into the intelligence department of the Supreme Command and the intelligence department of the General Staff. The Supreme Command's intelligence department answered directly to the supreme commander, Stalin, and held the foreign networks; the General Staff's department handled battlefield reconnaissance for the army. The People's Commissariat for State Security was folded into the People's Commissariat for Internal Affairs, becoming the Main Directorate for State Security, whose First Directorate worked against the United States, Britain, and Latin America, and whose Fourth Directorate worked against Germany and Japan. Two years later this directorate again broke away from the internal-affairs ministry, was raised to an independent commissariat, and its foreign stations swelled swiftly to nearly forty.

By comparison, the Communists' overhaul was the more forward-looking.

Out went the "Decision on Strengthening Party Spirit" and the "Decision on Investigation and Study." These two decisions not only guided ordinary work but raised the Party's intelligence work to the level of theory, making it clear that intelligence work was a special form of investigation and study.

There was a reshaping of the organization too: the Central Investigation and Study Bureau was founded, with Mao Zedong himself as its head, and the Center added a new department—the Central Intelligence Department. Across the whole Party and army, in every place, a concentrated and unified intelligence power took shape.

The Soviet Supreme Command's intelligence department had its eye on Japan; the Communist Central Intelligence Department had its eye on Japan; and in the second half of 1941 every super-spy in the world had his eye on Tokyo. The German army drove east—which way would the Japanese army turn?

Sorge, holding his ground in Tokyo, had the surest intelligence of all. On September 14 he reported the decision of Japan's Imperial Conference, top-secret intelligence Ozaki Hotsumi had drawn out of the Emperor's confidential bureau. The Emperor had convened the meeting on September 6; for secrecy's sake there was not even a secretary's record, only a set of notes that Prime Minister Konoe Fumimaro compiled. Those notes showed that Japan had decided to open its attack on Britain, America, and the Netherlands in the middle to latter part of October. From this Sorge judged that the Japanese navy would strike south into the Pacific, and that the army would not drive north into Siberia. On October 18 Sorge sent an urgent message: "Japan will attack the United States and Britain; the threat to the Soviet Union has passed."

But only a few hours after this telegram went out, Sorge was arrested.

An "international spy ring"! The case shook Japanese politics, and Prime Minister Konoe Fumimaro "resigned on grounds of illness." The unpublicized reason behind it lay in his own adviser—Ozaki Hotsumi was an international spy sleeping at the prime minister's very side.

The horses were changed, and the army minister, Tōjō Hideki, took the premiership. Japan's tradition was that soldiers commanded the army and civilians governed; now an army general held the premiership, the mark of a whole nation passing onto a war footing.

Japan was about to go mad.

The whole world held its breath and watched, hearts in their throats, every eye on which way Japan would move.

### 6. Top-Secret Intelligence Hidden Deep in the Tiger's Den

Which way would Japan move?

Nakanishi Kō and Wu Chengfang fell to quarreling: Nakanishi Kō judged it would be south, Wu Chengfang judged it would be north, and neither could talk the other round. This was because what decided the direction was not the men who analyzed the intelligence, but Tokyo.

Just then something happened in Tokyo. On October 18 Tōjō Hideki formed his cabinet, a move that shook Tokyo, shook Shanghai, shook the world. With the horses changed at the top, would the strategy change too?

Nakanishi Kō analyzed it coolly: whoever came to power, Japan's war policy would not greatly change.

By now Nakanishi Kō had the confidence for his analysis of international strategy. The fixing of a strategy turned on two things: the basic situation and the shifting situation. How to read the basic situation? Nakanishi Kō followed the method of Mao Zedong's *On Protracted War*—fix the quality first, then attend to the quantity. On the plane of quality, Japan's state policy was to occupy China first, and on this it would not give an inch, however much it compromised with America; while China's will to resist was firm and thorough, and however strong Japan grew, the Chinese people would not submit. So what decided whether Japan went south or north was, in the last analysis, the China front. North? That would require, as its premise, a great German victory and the fall of Moscow. South? If Japan judged it could finish the China Incident within two years, it would not shrink from war with America.

The analysis of the basic situation pointed to a Japanese drive south. But having reached it, Nakanishi Kō was not easy. It had not fully weighed the shifting situation—the international scene was changing, and the Japanese ruling group was changing. Japan was split into rival factions with clashing views. On the empire's strategic direction there had always been two schools: the navy for the southern advance, the army for the northern. And the new prime minister, Tōjō Hideki, had commanded the Kwantung Army, had himself drawn up the plan for war against the Soviet Union, and had always been a firm advocate of the northern advance.

At Yan'an, too, the judgment was made under strain.

Up to now the analyses the Pan Hannian system had sent from Shanghai had consistently judged Japan would go south. Nakanishi Kō's Japanese cell, with a grasp of the overall situation inside Japan, found that Japan's stocks of strategic materials were desperately tight—the navy had only 30 days' fuel left. This meant Japan had to find oil fast, and rubber too, and oil and rubber could be found only in Southeast Asia.

Nishizato Tatsuo learned at the headquarters of the Central China Expeditionary Army that the Emperor had spoken at the Imperial Conference: the northern advance must not be rash. Yuan Shu learned at the Shanghai consulate that, unless America changed its embargo against Japan, a Japanese-American war would be unavoidable. Ozaki Hotsumi sent word from Japan that Japanese forces in China were being drawn off south toward Thailand. In September the Imperial Conference passed the "Outline for Carrying Out Imperial National Policy," setting early October as the deadline for completing preparations for war against America.

After Tōjō Hideki took power on October 18, the intelligence the Pan Hannian system sent still judged the same: Japan was pressing on with its preparations for war against America, while going on with the talks to lull America.

Intelligence on movements calls for dynamic reconnaissance—to confirm whether Japan would go south or north, and to keep an unbroken grasp on the latest movements at the Japanese core.

Mao Zedong was so anxious he lay three days and nights without sleep, forever pressing the Central Intelligence Department for intelligence on Japan's movements: verify it exactly, that the Japanese army is about to go south, and the date it will open the war, and report it early.

The telegrams the Central Intelligence Department sent out demanding intelligence carried four characters at their head: "most extremely urgent."

Top-secret strategic intelligence is never left lying out for the taking; the secret is sure to be buried deep in the enemy's camp.

Nakanishi Kō was just about to contact Ozaki Hotsumi when a telegram came from Tokyo: "Go west. Shirakawa Jirō."

Only Nakanishi Kō understood: the telegram meant that Ozaki Hotsumi was in danger in Tokyo, and was warning him to flee to Yan'an.

But Nakanishi Kō did not go west; he went the opposite way—east. To get strategic intelligence, an agent must be willing to run the risk.

Heedless of the danger, Nakanishi Kō slipped back to Japan to reconnoiter on the spot.

The old watchman at the army's uniform factory said the works had switched from making padded winter coats to making shorts. Shorts for uniforms? Such clothing could only be for the tropical Southeast. Padded coats giving way to shorts meant the northern units would be moved south.

In the eating-houses the merchants talked of following the army south to make money. Countless amateur spies were nosing after intelligence, hoping to profit from the shift in strategy.

He walked into army headquarters to look up an old friend in the press department, Satō—and so learned that the army's press men had all gone over to the navy. The news was on the navy side.

The front page of the *Asahi Shimbun* on October 26 ran: "The Prime Minister, with the Navy Minister, worships at the Ise Shrine." Japan's army and navy would give each other no ground, and had always gone their separate ways to worship; now the heads of the two services worshiped together, a sign that army and navy had come to stand as one.

This was the good of reconnoitering on the spot: no need to collect documents, no need to analyze figures—you had only to come to Japan and open your eyes, and the content of the strategic intelligence was before you. South.

Having settled that it would be south, Nakanishi Kō still would not end his reconnaissance; he meant also to unmask that "Kwantung Army Special Maneuvers."

He ran into Satō of the army press department again. The fellow was busy over on the navy side—the navy was gathering in the Seto Inland Sea, ready to set out. Satō said, too, that the units drawn from the home islands were not heading for Manchuria but massing in secret on Taiwan: the Special Maneuvers were a feint to the north and a blow to the south.

Nakanishi Kō, too, meant now to go west—not to take shelter in the base area, but to return to Shanghai, and go on reconnoitering strategic intelligence.

What Yan'an demanded was not only Japan's strategic direction but the dispositions and the date of the war's opening. Strategic direction was only an abstract judgment; how to make dispositions to meet it called for more concrete intelligence still.

Nakanishi Kō left Shanghai on October 26; barely three days later, on the night of October 29, Shanghai was swept in a great roundup. Chen Gongshu was seized in the French Concession, and Juntong's Shanghai district was all but wiped out. On November 4 came a great roundup at Dalian. The Japanese gendarmerie arrested more than fifty men in the SMR's Research Department, most of them Nakanishi Kō's progressive classmates.

With a great war at hand, the counter-espionage organs struck first to prevent any leak.

The Chinese liaison officer Zheng Wendao was deeply anxious for Nakanishi Kō's safety. Before setting out, Nakanishi Kō had arranged with Zheng Wendao to return in eight days. Zheng Wendao waited from the second day to the eighth, and at last spotted Nakanishi Kō among the passengers coming off the ship.

Nakanishi Kō leafed hurriedly through the SMR's internal documents—*Internal Reference*, *Intelligence Exchange*, *Investigation Bulletin*, *Military Bulletin*. As the largest state-policy company, the SMR held a high security clearance, and could see secret documents on a par with the embassy and the general army headquarters.

Here was the "Imperial Operational Outline" passed by the Imperial Conference: "First, hold the 16 divisions stationed in Manchuria and Korea on guard against the Soviet Union; second, carry on the war against China by the established policy; third, in the south, intensify preparations for war against America and Britain, with the end of November as the limit…"

Here was the "Imperial Army's Southern Deployment for the Greater East Asia War": "Lieutenant-General Sakata, 3 divisions, Thailand; Lieutenant-General Imamura, 3 divisions, Malaya; Lieutenant-General Homma, 4 divisions, the Philippines; General Terauchi, 2 divisions, Hong Kong."

The *Internal Reference* of November 6, "Main Points for the Negotiations with America": "Ambassador Kurusu flies today via Hong Kong to America to assist Special Envoy Nomura in the negotiations, setting out in detail Japan's final concessions on the terms; he is firmly to demand a swift agreement on Plan A, and, faced with the American side's empty and unrealistic talk, is to bring home to them the limit of what Japan can accept; the talks are set to a limit of November 30, with no further delay…"

That "limit of November 30" in the *Internal Reference* matched exactly the "end of November as the limit" in the "Imperial Operational Outline."

A secret letter came from Nishizato Tatsuo, its developed content this: at the banquet the general army headquarters at Nanjing gave to welcome a Kwantung Army tour party, the party's leader, drunk, boasted of the secret to Nishizato Tatsuo: "The Kwantung Army keeps 200,000 to guard against the Soviets; the rest all move south. The navy is assembling in its combat staging waters at Hitokappu Bay off Etorofu Island; in late November the warships weigh anchor, heading southeast." This proved the Japanese navy would open war against America after November 30.

Nakanishi Kō reasoned: from the tactical mark of a Japanese surprise attack, they would pick a Sunday, the Americans' day of rest; June 22, when Germany struck the Soviet Union, was a Sunday. After November 30 there were three Sundays: December 1, 7, and 14. Judging by the Japanese navy's course and speed—putting out from northern Japan and taking the roundabout southward passage would take some time—the 1st was too early and the 14th too late; the reckoning came out at the 7th.

"On December 7, 1941, Japan will launch surprise attacks at many points across the Pacific."

This was sound intelligence indeed, and Wu Chengfang no longer doubted it.

Pan Hannian heard out Wu Chengfang's report, brooded a long while, and said, half to himself: "If events bear this intelligence out, it will make an indelible historic contribution to the international war against fascism."

And the elated Wu Chengfang heard one more, ominous, worry: "If only the people of every country will take it seriously…"

### 7. The Supreme Commander's Supreme Responsibility

As 1941 drew toward its close the smell of gunpowder thickened in the air; everyone knew Japan was about to fight a great war, only no one knew whether north or south.

Moscow kept firing off urgent telegrams to Yan'an.

On June 23, 1941, the day after the German attack on the Soviet Union, the Executive Committee of the Comintern sent an appeal from Moscow, calling on Communists the world over to build an "international anti-fascist united front" and to make "defend the Soviet Union" their central task.

That same day the Communist Central Committee issued the "Decision on the International Anti-Fascist United Front," drafted by Mao Zedong. Mao Zedong set out the Chinese Communists' concrete way of aiding the Soviet Union: hold firm to the anti-Japanese national united front, hold firm to Nationalist-Communist cooperation, and drive the fascist Japanese bandits out of China.

Yan'an's reply did not yet satisfy Moscow. Stalin telegraphed Mao Zedong, and in the tone of a request asked whether the Communists could pull out several brigades or regiments and place them near the Great Wall to pin down the Japanese army. The Commander-in-Chief of the Eighth Route Army, Zhu De, held that for the Eighth Route Army to leave its base areas and fight in the Great Wall region, where the Japanese were heavily fortified, would be no better than exhausting the troops on a distant campaign—a cardinal taboo of the art of war. Wang Ming at once objected: the Communist Party was a branch of the Comintern and must carry out the Comintern's instructions to the letter. Mao Zedong's view was that the Eighth Route Army should aid the Soviet Union as best it could, and that this aid should begin with two things—intelligence and the wrecking of the railways.

If intelligence could prove the Japanese army would not go north, then Moscow's demand for a troop movement would fall away of itself. On July 6 Mao Zedong telegraphed Zhou Enlai in Chongqing, judging that Japan seemed set not to attack the Soviet Union but to pin down Britain and America. On July 9 the Comintern issued instructions to the Communist parties of all countries, calling for aid to the Soviet Union in the German-Soviet war. The Communist Politburo signaled its full agreement, and Mao Zedong resolved on "a policy of long-term struggle, wearing the Japanese bandits down over time, rather than a policy of staking all on a single throw." In this period Sorge's intelligence from Tokyo to Moscow also showed the Japanese army would not go north, but Moscow was still uneasy. The German-Soviet front was giving way step by step—what if the Japanese army came after all? On September 7 the Soviet defense commissariat telegraphed Yan'an, asking that the Eighth Route Army send troops into southern Manchuria if a Japanese-Soviet war broke out.

By October 6 the German army was already at the gates of Moscow. The Comintern was deeply displeased with the Communists, and on October 7 Dimitrov telegraphed Yan'an with a string of fifteen questions: what measures, in the end, did the Chinese Communist Party mean to take to strike Japan militarily and actively on the China front, so that Germany's ally in the East, Japan, could not open a second front and invade the Soviet Union? Wang Ming at once made trouble in Yan'an, accusing Mao Zedong to his face of a mistaken policy for the war of resistance. Wang Ming's view was that, with both the Soviet Union and China in extraordinary straits, relations should be mended with the big bourgeois Chiang Kai-shek. Wang Ming, who a while before had said he would no longer contend with Mao Zedong for power, now proposed to review the Center's political line.

On October 18 Tōjō Hideki took power, and Mao Zedong at once telegraphed Zhou Enlai for his views. On October 19 Moscow declared martial law as the German army mounted a general assault on the city. On October 20 Yan'an held a Politburo meeting, and Mao Zedong said the situation of late had the flavor of reaching a turning point: Moscow was in crisis now, but the German offensive had perhaps reached its high-water mark, and the decisive point lay in the coming week or two—a week on, the shape of things would be clearer. That same day Mao Zedong telegraphed Zhou Enlai in Chongqing: the Tōjō cabinet was a military cabinet preparing directly for war; it was preparing directly for war, but would not necessarily move at once; its warlike tendency held the danger of a northern advance, but the possibility of a southern advance had not been lost. The Nationalists were sure it would be north; we need not agree with them.

Yan'an again telegraphed Pan Hannian urgently, asking him to confirm the southern advance. On October 25 Nakanishi Kō went to Tokyo to reconnoiter on the spot.

On November 7, at the Red Square parade in Moscow, Stalin gave a public speech. That same day Mao Zedong gave a radio address in Yan'an: "Though Japanese fascism is preparing for both a southern advance and a northern advance at once, whichever of these adventurous roads they take, it is certain they will drive west to destroy China." While the whole world watched to see whether Japan would strike the Soviet Union or America, Mao Zedong reminded the world: the one Japan was striking was China.

On November 17 Stalin telegraphed Mao Zedong himself, hoping the Communists would send part of their strength to develop inside and outside the Great Wall. To have the Eighth Route Army fight its way out past the Great Wall to "take up arms in defense of the Soviet Union"—was that not staking all on a single throw?

International pressure, domestic pressure, pressure within the Party: how was Mao Zedong to answer it?

Wrecking the railways plainly could not satisfy Moscow's demand. So Yan'an would have to lean on intelligence.

The Communists gave Moscow accurate intelligence that Japan would not go north, and Stalin moved the 200,000-strong army defending the eastern front against Japan to the western front, and won at last the defense of Moscow.

At the same time the Communists passed to America the intelligence of Japan's southward advance. As the two sides had as yet no intelligence relationship, the credit was ceded to the Nationalists: Pan Hannian relayed the intelligence to the American side through Juntong's Shanghai district.

As for technical reconnaissance against Japan, there again the Nationalists were early to the mark. In the autumn of 1934, Wen Yuqing of the Ministry of Communications' cipher-telegram decoding group submitted a proposal to Chiang Kai-shek that a specialized organ be set up to break Japanese cipher telegrams. Chiang Kai-shek issued a personal order, selecting Japan-trained students fluent in Japanese. "To break the Japanese army's cipher telegrams would be like adding several hundred thousand troops at the front."

Zhongtong's Second Confidential Section had Li Zhifeng; Juntong had Qian Daming and Yang Si; the Ministry of Communications' cipher-decoding office had Wen Yuqing and Huo Shizi; the Military Commission's confidential office had Mao Qingxiang. Each unit ran its own patch, competing behind closed doors, and efficiency stayed low—and then up popped a lone hand, Chi Buzhou.

Chi Buzhou passed through two decoding units, working from Zhongtong to the Military Commission's cipher-research group, without any result to show, and then the Ministry of Military Administration's central radio station came knocking too. Sick of the buck-passing among the Nationalist departments, Chi Buzhou decided to work alone in his spare time—and in March 1939, of all things, he broke the medium-grade cipher of the Japanese Foreign Ministry, which held military secrets.

The results of breaking the Japanese ciphers drew notice at the highest level. On April 1, 1940, Chiang Kai-shek decided himself to gather the decoding talent of the several units at his own side, founding the Military Commission's Technical Research Office. Wen Yuqing was its director, but the man who really ran it was Chiang Kai-shek's brother-in-law, Mao Qingxiang.

The Technical Research Office had over four hundred technical staff, and the greatest achiever among them was still Chi Buzhou. Chi Buzhou found that, from May 1941, the telegraph traffic between Tokyo and Honolulu, in Hawaii, had suddenly swelled. The Japanese Foreign Ministry repeatedly asked its consulate-general in Honolulu to report in detail the number of American warships anchored at Pearl Harbor, and even their berths and their times of departure. From this Chi Buzhou guessed that Japan meant to raid Pearl Harbor from the air. But how the higher-ups viewed this intelligence, Chi Buzhou could not know.

On December 3 Chi Buzhou broke another special cipher telegram from the Tokyo Foreign Ministry to its ambassador in America. This telegram ordered the destruction of all codebooks and secret documents, and showed besides: "The Imperial Government will take resolute action in accordance with the decision of the Imperial Conference." From this Chi Buzhou judged: Japan would raid Pearl Harbor on a Sunday.

Reported up the chain, Chiang Kai-shek had Mao Qingxiang hurry the word to the military attaché in America, to notify the U.S. Office of Naval Intelligence…

When the Chinese military attaché in America, Xiao Bo, said Japan was going to attack America, the American officers roared with laughter: intelligence on Japan that even we Americans haven't got, and you Chinese have?

This important intelligence the Chinese handed over for nothing may never have reached the American president at all.

Roosevelt too, of course, ought to have worried about a Japanese drive south. But the American president seemed in no hurry to prepare for war. Of late America was busy negotiating with Japan.

Set apart in its ocean, America was screened from the European front by the Atlantic and from the China front by the Pacific, with all the geography's advantage of watching a fire from across the river. America had always leaned toward isolationism; the common people had no wish to join some world war for another country, and neutrality could, besides, turn a profit on both sides. Since the outbreak of the war between China and Japan, America had extended loans to China and supplied scrap steel and oil to Japan, and its purse had fattened not a little. It was the three-nation military alliance of Germany, Italy, and Japan that finally spurred America, which stood for free trade and could not let the fascists monopolize the world; and so it began to face Japan down.

In the Japanese-American talks America demanded that Japan quit the three-nation alliance, withdraw from China, and go back to the state of things before the Mukden Incident of 1931. But how could meat once in the tiger's mouth be spat out? Japan flatly refused. When the German army struck the Soviet Union, America was rather relieved: with the Germans split off eastward, the pressure on Britain eased. And if the Japanese army too went north in concert with Germany, America could go on at its ease. Keep talking, then—America did not mind the talks dragging on.

Just then Yan'an spoke out: America was still aiding the Japanese aggressor through trade. The name was hard to bear, and the American president seemed only now to grasp that scrap steel and oil were strategic materials.

On August 1 America halted its exports of aviation fuel to Japan. On August 9 the Army Department of Japan's Imperial General Headquarters gave up any design of settling the northern question within the year, and bent itself wholly to the southern advance. The "Imperial Army Operational Outline" decided: "In the south, with November as the limit, intensify preparations for war against America and Britain."

Strategic deception: readying to fight on one hand, talking hard on the other.

On August 18 Japan notified America that it hoped for a summit of the two nations' leaders. On the 26th Prime Minister Konoe again wrote to President Roosevelt in person. On September 6 the Imperial Conference framed the "Outline for Carrying Out Imperial National Policy," resolving that if the talks failed it would fight.

On October 18 Tōjō Hideki took power, which put America somewhat on edge. The new Japanese cabinet at once sent Special Envoy Kurusu to America to assist the talks—and this Kurusu was a personal friend of Roosevelt.

Strategic deception: talking cunningly on one hand, laying out the blow on the other.

Chiang Kai-shek, too, feared a Japanese-American accommodation; China's war of resistance needed more foreign allies.

On November 22 the Chinese ambassador to America, Hu Shi, saw the American Secretary of State, Hull, and after the meeting telegraphed Chongqing urgently: America would come to terms with Japan. This was news Chiang Kai-shek did not care to hear, and Chongqing hoped the accommodation would fail. On November 24 Hu Shi replied to Chongqing: it would be hard to check the American accommodation. Chongqing hurried about on every side, and Britain too raised objections with America. On November 26 America put the Hull Note to Japan, holding to its original hard demands.

That same day the Japanese navy's Combined Fleet set out from the port off Etorofu Island.

The Japanese army had begun to move, and America was still hoping there would be no fight. On November 27 President Roosevelt received the Japanese ambassador Nomura and Special Envoy Kurusu himself, trying to salvage the talks. That same day Secretary of State Hull telephoned the Secretary of War: Japanese-American relations now passed into the hands of the army and navy.

With diplomacy passing to the military, it seemed America too must ready itself to fight. And yet the American forces did not go onto a war footing.

On December 1 all the ministers of the Japanese government attended the Imperial Conference, and the Emperor there and then approved the order for war against America and Britain. On December 3 Roosevelt was still hunting for a Japanese negotiating partner.

The drama in Washington was reported without cease to Chongqing, and Chiang Kai-shek laughed in spite of himself. On December 5 Chiang Kai-shek took up his pen and wrote in his diary: "The Japanese special envoys, answering reporters in America, are base in their words—utterly pitiable."

Base words, a ninety-degree bow: Japan was masking a savage surprise attack with peace talks. This device neither Roosevelt nor Chiang Kai-shek saw through. On the very eve of war Roosevelt, still unwilling to give up, telegraphed the Japanese Emperor in person on December 6, hoping to keep the talks alive. This telegram the Tokyo military held back, and it was never delivered to the Emperor—the arrow had already left the bow.

That night America intercepted a telegram from the Japanese Foreign Ministry to its special envoys, naming the final deadline for breaking off the talks. Roosevelt was aghast: this meant war.

Eighty minutes before the attack, Roosevelt telegraphed orders for the American forces in Hawaii, the Philippines, the West Coast, and Panama to go onto a war footing. By the time the telegram reached Hawaii, Japanese bombs were already striking the decks of the American warships.

Between Washington and Chongqing the telegrams ran without cease. Roosevelt did not want Japan to attack America; Chiang Kai-shek was glad to see it—two supreme commanders of opposite wishes, yet reaching the same intelligence judgment: Japan would not dare attack America.

A supreme commander should have the highest capacity for intelligence decisions; yet the supreme commanders of great world powers made the wrong intelligence decisions, a thing to astonish one in men of their rank.

Germany's surprise attack on the Soviet Union—Stalin misjudged it. Japan's surprise attack on America—Roosevelt misjudged it.

That the supreme commanders of the world's intelligence powers should err on intelligence bearing on the very survival of their nations is beyond understanding, and hard to forgive.

Half a century after the Second World War, historians in America and Russia are still pursuing this "intelligence-gate." There are three main views: the theory of contempt, the conspiracy theory, and the theory of misjudgment.

The theory of contempt holds that the Americans, scorning China's intelligence capacity, would not believe it. But the Americans, scorning China, ought not to have scorned themselves: America had broken the Japanese ciphers before the war, and its own ambassador in Japan had reported that Japan was going to open war.

The conspiracy theory is the sensational one: that Roosevelt deliberately let Japan attack Pearl Harbor to rouse the nation to war. But even to let the water through, one need not loose a flood; a single battle would have roused the nation just as well.

In truth the likeliest is misjudgment. One report said Japan would strike by surprise, another that Japan wanted to talk terms—whom was Roosevelt to believe?

America at that time had no organ governing intelligence for the whole nation. Its intelligence departments sat under the navy and the army, with no central body over them, and before the Second World War it had even closed the "American Black Chamber" that broke ciphers. Only after the hard lesson of Pearl Harbor did America, in 1942, set up the Office of Strategic Services under the Joint Chiefs of Staff; as for that famous Central Intelligence Agency, the CIA, it was not born until 1947, late and slow to grow.

The supreme commander bears, of course, the supreme responsibility—not for the war alone, but for intelligence too. The many intelligence organs set their many reports on the supreme commander's desk, some for and some against, some clear and some vague, and it falls to the supreme commander himself to analyze and to judge. And so the supreme commander's attitude toward intelligence, the supreme commander's capacity for intelligence, became the key premise of strategic decision.

Roosevelt, Stalin, Chiang Kai-shek—all were outstanding statesmen, and the latter two might be called noted soldiers besides. But none of the three could be called an intelligence expert.

Mao Zedong was an intelligence master worthy of the name.

On great matters Mao Zedong always took a hand himself. The strategic movements of the Japanese army were the very matter that concerned Mao Zedong just then. As head of the Central Investigation and Study Bureau, Mao Zedong went over the reports of the Central Intelligence Department himself, went over the reports of the Second Bureau of the Military Commission, and drafted telegrams to discuss with Zhou Enlai.

Mao Zedong judged the Japanese army most likely to go south, and won the strategic initiative.

Having won the initiative, Mao Zedong very much wanted to help Roosevelt, did in fact help Stalin, and, at the same time, helped himself most of all. On December 8, the very day of the Pearl Harbor attack, Mao Zedong issued a public statement pressing for an international anti-fascist united front. Mao Zedong's earlier foresight had come true: the Japanese drive south changed the shape of the world. Once-neutral America and Britain now threw themselves into the war and into support for China; China's Chiang Kai-shek turned from his divisive "Three Norths policy" to leaning on Nationalist-Communist cooperation; and the Communists' position at home and abroad greatly improved.

On December 8 Chiang Kai-shek, too, made a move: he declared war on Japan. Reckoning from the Mukden Incident of 1931, the great Sino-Japanese war had already been fought for ten years, and this declaration came very late indeed. It could not but set people guessing that Generalissimo Chiang was still waiting for foreigners to make the first move. And it reminded people, too, that the chairman of the Chinese Soviet Republic, Mao Zedong, had issued a "Declaration of War Against Japan" as early as April 15, 1932.

Pearl Harbor: in the second round of the great international contest of strategic intelligence, China was again ahead.

Achievement proves ability, and ability wins respect. After Pearl Harbor the U.S. Army Observation Group took up quarters in Chongqing, and in Yan'an; the great powers of the world too would work with the Chinese now.

### 8. Brothers from Foreign Lands, Aiding Unto Death

Having obtained the intelligence of Pearl Harbor, Nakanishi Kō could withdraw with an easy conscience. But still he was slow to pull out. Once he left, this intelligence network driven into Japan's highest organs would all have to withdraw, and that meant years of lying low thrown away.

Was my intelligence task done? Japan's going south now, but it could still turn its head and go north. I must keep my eye on Japan yet.

Holding fast to the intelligence work, Nakanishi Kō's Japanese cell carried on, right up to May 1942, when at last the whole force was wiped out. The Japanese Tokkō, tracing Ozaki Hotsumi's career, discovered that Ozaki Hotsumi had a set of progressive friends in Shanghai.

Even as the Japanese were being arrested one after another, the Chinese comrades who supported them were still at liberty. Li Desen in Nanjing, Zheng Wendao in Shanghai—neither had come to grief. Pan Hannian reckoned that these Japanese comrades would not confess, and that the Tokkō, without hard proof, would not dare finally close the case. In that event, if the Chinese around them suddenly vanished, it would furnish the very proof that they had been serving China. So the surest way to save these Japanese comrades was for the Chinese, one and all, not to withdraw, but to hold their ground.

And so Zheng Wendao held his post, going on to the SMR's special investigation squad; Li Desen held his post, going on with his Chinese-medicine clinic in Nanjing; Wang Jinyuan held his post, going on as Wang Jingwei's secretary.

The courier Zhang Mingda and Zheng Wendao heartened each other: "This is carrying our heads to strike the golden bell."

To carry one's head and strike the golden bell—for the sake of faith, to go willingly to one's death: this Buddhist saying meant to trade death for something clear and resounding.

Each waited, holding off from contact, the Communist agents standing fast in place. A month passed, and the Japanese gendarmerie struck without warning. At the Nanjing station Li Desen and his wife Zhang Min, Chen Yifeng, Wang Jinyuan, and others were arrested one after another; only the courier Zhang Mingda got away by luck.

Zheng Wendao too was taken. As the prison van tore through the streets of Shanghai, Zheng Wendao knew that where he was going he would face Nakanishi Kō, and would be made to furnish the proof of Nakanishi Kō's treason.

The moment to die had come—Zheng Wendao flung himself up and burst the van door open.

Shanghai was in an uproar: a prisoner had jumped from a van to kill himself. And the brave man who took his own life was said to be a Communist? As word of the Chinese arrests spread through Shanghai, Pan Hannian at once took countermeasures, ordering the relevant people to withdraw immediately, cutting the links of the Wu Chengfang system, and keeping the larger system running.

Zheng Wendao, who had sought death, did not die; he was gravely hurt in the fall. The Japanese nursed him with care—this was the man closest to Nakanishi Kō, who could furnish the effective proof to bring Nakanishi Kō down. Half a month later, when Zheng Wendao's injuries had mended a little and he could just get out of bed, he was hauled again to the interrogation room.

Zheng Wendao had long foreseen this pass: he knew far too many secrets.

The Nakanishi Kō whom Zheng Wendao handled was the core of the whole Japanese cell, and being close to the core, Zheng Wendao knew the situation of the whole cell. Zheng Wendao also handled liaison with Zhang Mingda, and Zhang Mingda ran on to the Nanjing intelligence station—another great network. On the Shanghai side, Pan Hannian's liaison man Liu Zhao had been Zheng Wendao's Party sponsor; in Beiping, the Chinese Qian Ming who supported Shirai Yukiyuki was Zheng Wendao's schoolmate; and Zheng Wendao held one more top secret—the link to the secretly kept Central Archive. Take Zheng Wendao, one might say, and the enemy took the master rope of the whole Communist secret intelligence net in Shanghai.

There was nothing for it but to die. Zheng Wendao flung himself up, burst out through the window, fell from the second floor, and was dashed to his death on the streets of Shanghai.

The Japanese gendarmes could never have foreseen that Zheng Wendao would choose this way to fight back.

The Japanese, for whom suicide was a commonplace, often mocked the Chinese as having no courage to take their own lives; and so Japan had no trouble finding traitors in China. Zheng Wendao's first leap from the van had shaken the Japanese gendarmes—China too had its brave men. The gendarmes nursed Zheng Wendao with care, out of respect for a brave man, and still more in hope of a confession. The Japanese, rich in the experience of suicide, knew that the ordeal of dying is beyond terror, and that a man who has died once will not dare to die a second time.

But the thing that even the Japanese dared not imagine, a Chinese did: Zheng Wendao took his own life a second time, and this time it took.

Nakanishi Kō, learning in prison of Zheng Wendao's death, was moved to the depths.

When Nakanishi Kō had gone back to Tokyo to reconnoiter, Zheng Wendao saw him off at the dock, and at parting Nakanishi Kō charged him: "As Communists, we should all sacrifice for the cause of the revolution; knowing there is danger of sacrifice, we should still work and hold fast for the revolution's sake. That is just where we stand now. Perhaps some morning or some night I shall be seized by the Japanese gendarmes; even so, I'll keep my vow, and betray no secret of the organization. You too must keep the vow of a Communist."

Looking at the comrade of that vow, Zheng Wendao spoke at once the words long in his heart: "If you're taken, maybe I'll be taken with you, and I can cover for you to the end. And if that can't be done, then, to protect the organization, I'll just die and be done with it."

Nakanishi Kō remembered that Zheng Wendao, speaking words so resolute, had eyes full of laughter.

Nakanishi Kō would remember his brother Zheng Wendao's laughing eyes forever. Through their long collaboration, Nakanishi Kō and Zheng Wendao had become as brothers.

Zheng Wendao was a native of Zhongshan in Guangdong; he lost his father young, went with his merchant elder brother to Qingdao, studied at the advanced industrial school attached to Tongji University in Shanghai, and after graduating labored in a Shanghai factory. In 1937 he joined the "Jiangkang"—the Communist-led Jiangnan Anti-Japanese Guerrillas—and under He Kexi fought across Jiangsu, Shandong, and elsewhere; in March 1938 he was brought into the Party by Liu Zhao, and in September was transferred to Shanghai as Nakanishi Kō's secret liaison. To guard the organization's secrets, Zheng Wendao broke off all contact with his sweetheart and his elder brother.

Mourning his Chinese brother Zheng Wendao, Nakanishi Kō poured himself in prison into writing "A History of the Chinese Communist Party." "I will live on to the very last, and to the last instant I will not believe that I can 'die'; I will live seven lives, live forever."

On August 15, 1945, the Tokyo High Court tried Nakanishi Kō and Nishizato Tatsuo in open court, a defeated state machine going on persecuting the antiwar men at home. But no sooner had the judges and the judged stepped out of the courtroom than they heard the Emperor proclaim the unconditional surrender. On October 10 the court declared Nakanishi Kō acquitted and released. Others who came through that purgatory were Nishizato Tatsuo, Ozaki Shōtarō, and Hamazu Yoshikatsu.

The prison correspondence between Nakanishi Kō and his wife was published and made a stir in literary circles. Ozaki Hotsumi's younger brother, Ozaki Hotsuki, wrote a book in memory of his elder brother and became head of the Japan PEN Club. Nakanishi Kō and Nishizato Tatsuo joined the Japanese Communist Party and were elected to the House of Councillors.

In 1982 Pan Hannian was posthumously cleared and his good name restored, and others in the same case—Liu Renshou, Wu Chengfang, Li Desen—regained their freedom. Once rehabilitated, Fang Zhida went at once to Shanghai, found his old comrades Qian Ming and Ni Zhixiang, and raised a memorial stone for Zheng Wendao.

In 1983 Nishizato Tatsuo visited China and was reunited with his Chinese comrade Xu Qiang. Nakanishi Kō's widow, Nakanishi Masako, revisited Shanghai, and, in the company of Wang Jinyuan, Chen Yifeng, and Qian Ming, came before Zheng Wendao's grave and laid there a poem of her husband Nakanishi Kō's:

{p} You shed your warm blood gladly to cover another,

{p} You gave your life for the peace of the world;

{p} For a shared faith you made good your own pledge—

{p} You are, in my heart, the Chinese Communist most worthy of my respect.

Nakanishi Kō and Zheng Wendao, two brothers of Japan and China, reunited after death.

Such is the fate of the nameless hero: bearing shame and heavy burdens to a career of legendary merit, suffering wrong and injustice to a vindication at the last.

The German-born Soviet agent Sorge kept his true identity hidden for years. It was American scholars who found the secret in the Japanese archives and made public the intelligence contribution of the Sorge group. The world was astonished, and the intelligence world agreed: Sorge was one of the greatest spies of the twentieth century.

A great spy should not be buried in silence. The legend of the East, too, came gradually to light: a band of high Japanese spies who secretly supported the Chinese people's resistance and supplied the strategic intelligence of Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor. Brothers from foreign lands, aiding unto death: the Japanese Nakanishi Kō willingly bore the name "traitor," and the Chinese Zheng Wendao killed himself to shield his friend.

Nakanishi Kō and Zheng Wendao: heroes of East Asia, born of the mingling of Chinese and Japanese culture.

The greatest sound is silent; the greatest form has no shape.

The great spies of the East muted their sound and hid their shape, until they had no name.

Germany's surprise attack on the Soviet Union and Japan's surprise attack on America—these two great pieces of international strategic intelligence the Chinese obtained in advance, and used, and by them won the strategic initiative.

The Chinese agents no one noticed had slipped quietly into the front rank of the world's intelligence race.

The posture of strong and weak, the momentum of victory and defeat, in this hour and on this count, turned silently over.

The weak overcoming the strong, ahead in intelligence.

That first victory of the Chinese nation in a war against aggression, that birth of a new and prosperous China—did they too, perhaps, begin with this lead in intelligence?

### Principal Sources

Ozaki Hotsuki, *Shanghai 1930* (Iwanami Shinsho). "Shanghai the demon-city; a sweep of the stage; a group portrait of youth." The author's elder brother, Ozaki Hotsumi, was long a correspondent in China; the book records faithfully the state of Shanghai's foreign concessions in those days, with many precious photographs besides.

Mei Changming, comp., *The Memoirs of Mei Gongbin* (Tuanjie Press). Mei Gongbin took part in Shanghai's revolutionary movement in the Great Revolution years, and founded the Communist Party's independent Xujiahui branch inside the Japanese school, the Shanghai East Asia Common Culture Academy.

Ozaki Hotsuki, *Remembering Ozaki Hotsumi* (Keisō Shobō). Ozaki Hotsuki was chairman of the Japan Writers' Association and head of the Japan PEN Club; as Ozaki Hotsumi's younger brother he gave his life to recording the deeds of the anti-fascist Ozaki Hotsumi. The book gathers essays by Kazami Akira, Nakanishi Kō, Kawai Teikichi, Saionji Kinkazu, Ozaki Hotsuma, and other friends and kin.

Nakanishi Kō, *In the Storms of the Chinese Revolution* (Aoki Shoten). In 1968 Nakanishi Kō was invited to lecture at Tokyo University of Education on "China's Revolution and I," to a warm response. He planned to make a book of the transcripts of these dozen-odd lectures, but died of illness before he could finish, and the lecture drafts were later edited and published by specialists.

Nishizato Tatsuo, *Revolutionary Shanghai: A Record of a Japanese Member of the Chinese Communist Party* (Nitchū Shuppan). Nishizato Tatsuo was active in China for twenty years, from 1926 to 1946, witnessed the great events of Japanese-Chinese relations, met high political figures, joined the Communist intelligence organization in secret, and supplied much important intelligence.

Fang Zhida, Liang Yan, and Chen Sanbai, *The Alarm of the Pacific War* (Oriental Press). The lead author, Fang Zhida, once ran the secret courier work of the Shanghai intelligence organization and in the 1980s served as deputy head of the Central United Front Work Department; the author Chen Sanbai had taken part in the secret work, and the writer Liang Yan had also taken part in the war of resistance.

Yin Qi, *Pan Hannian's Intelligence Career* (People's Publishing House). Xia Yan, *Lazy to Seek Old Dreams* (SDX Joint Publishing). *Fifteen Years in the Enemy Camp: The Memoirs of Li Shiyu* (Nanhai Publishing). Sent by the Jin-Cha-Ji Social Affairs Department, the author slipped from Beiping into Nanjing, held the post of head of the judge-advocate's office in the Wang Jingwei regime, obtained top-secret intelligence, and rescued comrades in the resistance.

Zeng Long, *My Father Yuan Shu* (Jieli Press). The author is intensely curious how his father, Yuan Shu, worked in practice the great leaps among "left-wing cultural figure—Nationalist agent—collaborator—revolutionary soldier."

Hu Ping, *Intelligence Japan* (Orient Publishing Center). A full account of Japan's foreign intelligence work, and above all its work against China, including the main organs, figures, and events.

Wu Tong, comp., *Storms on the Sea of Espionage* (CCP Party History Press). The espionage and intelligence activities of Japanese spies in China since modern times.

Iwai Eiichi, *Shanghai Remembered* (the *Shanghai Remembered* Publication Committee and Izumi Printing Co.). The author was the head of the Japanese Foreign Ministry's intelligence work in China, and ran the secret-service organ the "Iwai Kōkan" in Shanghai. He witnessed the great events of Japanese-Chinese relations, and recorded his dealings with high intelligence figures, among them the Japanese master spies Doihara and Kagesa Sadaaki and the Communist agents Pan Hannian and Yuan Shu.

*The Memoirs of Imai Takeo* (China Literature and History Press). The intelligence general of the Japanese army, in charge of the inducement-to-surrender against China, "Operation Kiri."

Xu Zehao, *From Prisoners to Comrades: The Enemy-Army Work of the Eighth Route and New Fourth Armies* (Anhui People's Press). A systematic account of the Chinese Communist Party's enemy-army work during the war of resistance, and above all its work with Japanese prisoners of war.

Yang Kuisong, *The Grievances and Grudges Between Mao Zedong and Moscow* (Jiangxi People's Press). A study of the relations between the Comintern and the Communists; after Germany's surprise attack on the Soviet Union, the Comintern secretary-general Dimitrov telegraphed several times to demand insistently that the Communists defend the Soviet Union.

Yuan Nansheng, *Stalin, Mao Zedong, and Chiang Kai-shek* (Hunan People's Press). A detailed account of the relations among two countries and three parties, of the historical tangles of the Soviet Union with China's Nationalists and Communists, and a deep look at the effect of Pearl Harbor on Sino-Soviet relations and the world's strategic balance.

Liu Zonghe and Gao Jinhu, eds., *An Intelligence History of the Second World War* (PLA Press). Wen Min, *Seventy Years of Soviet Espionage* (Gold Wall Press). A full exposition of Soviet espionage, with a thorough explanation of the intelligence failure by which the Soviet Union was caught in Germany's surprise attack.

Yu Liren, comp., *Fifty Years of the CIA* (Shishi Press). A systematic account of America's largest intelligence organ, including its work against China.

Wu Yue, *Chiang Kai-shek's Top-Secret Trump Card: The Legend of Chi Buzhou* (Qingdao Press). The subject was the Nationalist Juntong's cipher-breaking expert, who detected the intelligence of Japan's launching of the Pacific War. The book returns again and again to why America did not accept the Chinese warning, and reveals besides the Nationalist-Communist cooperation on intelligence against Japan.

Kolesnikova and Kolesnikov, *Duel with the Third Reich* (Shanghai Translation Publishing House). A biography of Sorge by Soviet authors, drawn from the Soviet archives.

Shirai Hisaya, comp., *A Documentary Collection on the Sorge Affair* (Shakai Hyōronsha). The materials come from "American official documents," including the records of the U.S. House Un-American Activities Committee and the "Sorge Affair" report of the Civil Intelligence Section of the General Headquarters of the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers.

Fang Zhida, *A Heart of Loyalty: The Turbulent Story of the "CCP Spy-Ring Case"* (*Dajiang Nanbei* magazine). Among the Japanese cell handled by the Pan Hannian intelligence system, Fang Zhida (Zhang Mingda) was the Chinese liaison officer, and one of the few not arrested.

*The Interrogation Records of Nakanishi Kō: Intelligence Work Dedicated to the Chinese Revolution* (Aki Shobō). Mitsunaga Genzuchi, an assistant inspector in the First Section of the Tokkō of the Tokyo Metropolitan Police, copied out and kept at his own home the records of the interrogation of Nakanishi Kō, and they were published after the war. They include the record of thirty-three interrogations of Nakanishi Kō, a schedule of the intelligence Nakanishi Kō supplied to the Chinese side, and commentary by Fukumoto Katsukiyo, a short study of Nakanishi Kō, and a chronology of his connections. The book is rich in content, but Nakanishi Kō himself cautioned that its contents are not reliable, since he sometimes lied to meet the interrogation.

Nakanishi Kō, *From Within the Ramparts of Death: Letters to My Wife* (Iwanami Shinsho). An anti-fascist fighter, in a fascist prison, holding fast to life and holding fast to the struggle.

Liu Zhao, *Recollections of Anti-Japanese Activities in Jiangyin in the Early War Years* (Selected Historical Materials on the Revolutionary Struggle in Suzhou). This essay gives a rare account of the martyr Zheng Wendao's early revolutionary career.

He Xiaolu, daughter of He Kexi; interviewed July 9, 2010. Zheng Wendao served in He Kexi's anti-Japanese guerrillas; when the Shanghai intelligence organization asked the unit for men, he was transferred to be Nakanishi Kō's liaison. It was He Kexi who recommended to the organization that Zheng Wendao be brought into the Party.

Jing Yue and Jing Hong, son and daughter of Qian Ming; interviewed May 1, 2011. Qian Ming (Jing Ruonan) and Zheng Wendao were dormitory-mates at Tongji University and both liaison officers of the Nakanishi Kō cell. In the 1980s Jing Yue accompanied his father to Japan and found Nakanishi Kō's family.

Liren and Liu Zhenhong, daughters of Liu Zhao; interviewed May 1, 2011. During the war of resistance Liu Zhao served as Pan Hannian's secret courier, and was also Zheng Wendao's Party sponsor.
