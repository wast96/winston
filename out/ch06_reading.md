## Chapter 5. A Song of Sorrow

At the Ten Thousand Plum Manor, no plum was in flower.

It was April now; peach and azalea had their blossoms out, all up the slopes. Facing that hillside of flowers, Hua Manlou seemed almost unwilling ever to leave the place again. Over his calm, quiet face came a radiance past describing, like a girl in first love at the sight of her lover.

Lu Xiaofeng could not hold it in. "I hate to spoil the scenery. But once dark falls, Ximen Chuixue receives no one."

"Not even you?" said Hua Manlou.

"Not the King of Heaven himself," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"And if he's away?" said Hua Manlou.

"He'll be there. He goes out four times a year at the very most," said Lu Xiaofeng, "and only to kill."

"So he kills at most four men a year," said Hua Manlou.

"And every one of them a man who deserved killing," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Who is a man who deserves killing? And who decides whether they deserve it?" Then Hua Manlou sighed. "Go to him yourself. I would rather wait for you here."

Lu Xiaofeng said nothing more; he understood the man too well. No one had ever seen Hua Manlou lose his temper, but once he had decided a thing, no one had ever changed his mind either.

Facing the hillside of flowers, he added slowly, "When you see him, best try my way first. Then yours."

No flower was to be seen in the house, yet the house was full of their fragrance: light, faint, like Ximen Chuixue himself. Lu Xiaofeng lay back in an easy chair woven of evergreen vines and watched him. The wine in the cups was a pale jade-green; the snow-white robe he wore was light and soft. A flute-song gentler than the spring wind drifted somewhere, now near, now far, and no player to be seen.

Lu Xiaofeng sighed. "In all your life, have you ever once truly worried?"

"Never," said Ximen Chuixue.

"You're truly content? Entirely?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Because I ask very little," said Ximen Chuixue coolly.

"And so you've never asked a favour of any man?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Never once," said Ximen Chuixue.

"And so when a man comes asking one of you, you refuse him?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"I refuse," said Ximen Chuixue.

"Whoever asks, whatever the thing, you refuse?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"What I mean to do, no one need ask of me," said Ximen Chuixue. "For the rest, all men are alike."

"And if someone meant to burn your house down?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Who would burn my house?" said Ximen Chuixue.

"I would," said Lu Xiaofeng.

Ximen Chuixue smiled. He smiled so seldom that his smile always seemed to carry some mockery in it past naming.

"I came this time to ask your hand in a certain business. I promised someone," said Lu Xiaofeng, "that if you wouldn't come out, I'd burn your house to clean ash."

Ximen Chuixue gazed at him a long while. "My friends are few," he said at last, slowly. "At the most there have been two or three. But you have always been one of them."

"Which is why I've come to beg this of you," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"And why, whenever you care to burn my house, you may begin," said Ximen Chuixue evenly. "From whichever end you please."

Lu Xiaofeng was stopped cold. He understood this man too well also: his word, once out, was an arrow loosed. It did not turn back.

"In the storeroom at the back there is pine resin and lamp-oil," said Ximen Chuixue. "I'd suggest you start there. And burn at night, if you can. Flame like that must be very beautiful at night."

"Have you ever heard of two men called Datong and Dazhi?" said Lu Xiaofeng suddenly.

"I hear there's no question in the world they cannot answer," said Ximen Chuixue coldly. "Do they truly know everything under heaven?"

"You don't believe it?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Do you?" said Ximen Chuixue.

"I asked them by what means you could be moved. They said there was no way," said Lu Xiaofeng. "I didn't believe it either, then. Now I begin to think they understand you very well."

Ximen Chuixue looked at him, and smiled again, briefly. "This once, they were wrong."

"Oh?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"You are not entirely without a way to move me," said Ximen Chuixue.

"What way have I?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

Ximen Chuixue was actually smiling. "Shave that moustache clean off, and whatever it is you mean to do, I'll go with you."

***

His friends, meeting Lu Xiaofeng after this, might well not know him. The man who had four eyebrows now had two; where the moustache had grown, the skin was smooth as a newborn child's. The pity was that Hua Manlou could not see it.

Nor, of course, could he see Ximen Chuixue, who had come back at Lu Xiaofeng's side; yet he smiled and said, "Lord Ximen, I believe?"

"Hua Manlou," said Ximen Chuixue.

Hua Manlou inclined his head. "My one regret is that this infirmity of mine keeps me from looking on the foremost swordsman of the age."

Ximen Chuixue studied him. "You truly cannot see?" he said abruptly.

"My lord will surely have heard," said Hua Manlou, "that Hua Manlou has eyes, and is blind as a bat."

"And yet you heard my footsteps?" said Ximen Chuixue.

Like Dugu Fang before him, he could not keep from asking it. He was as proud of his lightness-skill as of his sword, and his lightness-skill had earned the pride.

"So far as I know," said Hua Manlou, "there are at most four or five men alive today who can move without the least whisper of sound. My lord is one of them."

"Yet you knew I had come," said Ximen Chuixue.

Hua Manlou smiled. "Only because of the killing-air my lord carries about him."

"Killing-air?" said Ximen Chuixue.

"A keen sword out of its sheath has its sword-air," said Hua Manlou mildly. "And my lord has killed how many men in his life? How should he carry no air of killing?"

"No wonder, then, that you stopped at my gate and would not enter," said Ximen Chuixue coldly. "My killing-air was more than you could bear."

"The flowers here are lovely past anything commonly seen," said Hua Manlou, smiling. "If my lord gave himself to them a little more, that air would fade away of itself."

"Flowers are lovely," said Ximen Chuixue coldly. "How should they compare with the flower of blood at a killing?"

"Oh?" said Hua Manlou.

A strange light had come into Ximen Chuixue's eyes. "The world never runs short of faithless, treacherous men to kill. When you drive your sword into such a throat and watch the blood-flower bloom along the blade... if you could see the glory of that one instant, you would know there is no beauty on this earth to match it." And he turned on his heel and walked away without a backward glance.

The dusk-haze lay vast and grey, a gauze thrown down across the banks of flowers, and in an instant he had vanished into it.

Hua Manlou let out a soft sigh. "Now, at last, I understand how he forged that swordsmanship of his."

"Oh?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Because he truly holds killing to be a thing sacred and beautiful. He has given his whole life over to it," said Hua Manlou. "Only when he kills is he truly alive. All the rest is waiting."

Lu Xiaofeng thought about it, and sighed softly in his turn. "It's as well, then, that the men he kills deserve it."

Hua Manlou smiled, and said nothing more.

Then boundless night had covered the earth. The first thin stars were up, and a waning moon, curved as a moth's eyebrow, hung at the far tree-line; the wind still carried the scent of flowers, and the dark was mysterious and beautiful.

Hua Manlou walked slowly along the hillside, as though he too had passed into some mysterious and beautiful dream.

Lu Xiaofeng could not contain himself. "Why don't you ask me whether the visit bore fruit?"

Hua Manlou smiled. "I know you've won him over."

"You know? How could you know?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"He neither pressed you to stay nor saw you to the gate, and you left in no ill humour," said Hua Manlou. "So of course you had already fixed a meeting-place."

"And do you know what way I used on him?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Mine, of course," said Hua Manlou.

"Why yours?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Because he may be without feeling, but you are not. He knew you would never burn his house," said Hua Manlou. "And had you truly burned it, he'd not have cared a straw."

Lu Xiaofeng laughed, and sighed through the smile. "Sharp as you are, there's one thing you'll never guess."

"What thing?" said Hua Manlou.

Lu Xiaofeng felt the place where his moustache had been. "Take your time guessing. When you've hit it, I'll tell you."

Hua Manlou laughed. "And once I've hit it, what need will I have of your telling?"

Lu Xiaofeng laughed too. But before he could speak, he saw Hua Manlou's calm, easy smile freeze, in that one instant, into something strange and stiff past telling.

"What is it now?" he could not help asking.

Hua Manlou did not answer, did not even hear him. He seemed to be listening to some secret sound far away, a sound only he could hear.

Suddenly he changed direction and set off round the back of the slope.

Lu Xiaofeng could only follow. The night was darker now; stars and moon alike had sunk behind the peaks.

Then he heard it too: a thread of song, faint and far, touched with a light melancholy and beautiful enough to break the heart. The words were as desolate as they were lovely: a tender girl, at the edge of death, telling over to her lover the driftings and sorrows of her life.

Lu Xiaofeng was not attending closely to the words; it was Hua Manlou's face that troubled him. "Have you heard this song before?"

At last Hua Manlou nodded. "I've heard it sung."

"Sung by whom?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Shangguan Feiyan," said Hua Manlou.

Lu Xiaofeng often said there were exactly twelve things in this world he trusted absolutely. One of the twelve was Hua Manlou's ears.

Other men mistake even what they see with their own eyes; Hua Manlou had never once misheard. He did not say it, but his face told Lu Xiaofeng as plainly as words: the singer, now, was Shangguan Feiyan.

The girl who had vanished without a trace: how should she suddenly be here? Why hide alone on a moonlit waste of mountain, singing so desolate and grieving a song?

For whom was she singing?

Was she too, like the drifting orphan girl of the song, telling over to her lover, at the edge of death, the bitterness of her fate?

Lu Xiaofeng asked nothing more, for a point of lamplight had appeared in the dark. The song was coming from where the light glimmered.

Hua Manlou was already in motion, skimming towards it; he could not see the lone lamp's light, yet his flight was aimed without the least error.

The lamplight drew nearer and nearer, and Lu Xiaofeng could make out a tiny temple, sacred to the mountain god, perhaps, or to the local earth god; who could say?

And then the song stopped short, and heaven and earth went empty and silent past all saying.

Lu Xiaofeng glanced at Hua Manlou. "If she were truly singing for you," he said, in spite of himself, "she wouldn't leave."

But she had left. The lamp still burned; in the grim little temple of the mountain god there was no human shape to be seen.

The black-faced mountain god, steel whip in hand, bestrode his tiger, and in the dim lamplight seemed on the point of laying on the lash, scourging the wicked of the world for the sake of the good. On the altar-table, its lacquer flaking, stood a battered copper basin full of clear water; and on the water floated a single strand of black hair.

"What are you looking at?" said Hua Manlou.

"There's a basin of water on the table," said Lu Xiaofeng, "and some strands of hair in the water."

"Hair?" said Hua Manlou.

The hair was very soft, and the scent of a girl's hair still clung to it.

"A woman's hair," said Lu Xiaofeng. "It seems a girl was here just now, singing while she combed her hair, with this basin for her mirror. And now she's gone."

Hua Manlou nodded slowly, as though he had known from the first that she would never wait for him here.

"A girl with the heart to comb her hair in a place like this, at an hour like this," said Lu Xiaofeng, "plainly cares a great deal for her looks."

"What girl of seventeen or eighteen doesn't?" said Hua Manlou quietly.

"And isn't Shangguan Feiyan just such a girl of seventeen or eighteen?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"She always did care for her looks," said Hua Manlou.

Lu Xiaofeng watched him, and ventured, "You'll have touched her hair, of course. Before."

Hua Manlou smiled. There are many kinds of smile; this kind meant yes.

"Is this her hair?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

He trusted Hua Manlou's fingertips as he trusted his ears. He had seen him tell a true antique from a false one at a single touch.

Hua Manlou had taken the strand and was drawing it softly through his fingertips; and over his face came another strange expression, of which none could have said: was it joy? or sorrow?

"It is her hair, then?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

Hua Manlou nodded.

"She was here only now, combing her hair and singing," said Lu Xiaofeng. "So she's alive and well."

Hua Manlou smiled again. There are many kinds of smile; but of this one, too, none could have said: was it joy? or sorrow?

If she had been here, why had she not waited for him? And if she hadn't known he would come, for whom had she been singing?

Lu Xiaofeng sighed inwardly, and could not decide whether to offer comfort or pretend he understood nothing.

A wind came through the door. And the black-faced mountain god astride his black tiger split suddenly down the middle, his steel whip, four chi long, snapping into eight or nine pieces; then the whole great image crumbled, block after block, onto the floor.

Through the swirling dust Lu Xiaofeng saw that on the wall behind the shattered god, a man hung in mid-air.

A dead man, the blood on him not yet dry. A pair of judge's pens had been driven through his chest, pinning him alive to the wall; and from the pens fluttered two strips of yellow sackcloth, like soul-banners.

"Blood for blood!"

"Here is the example for those who mind other men's business!"

The same two lines, written in the same fresh blood; only here the blood had long since dried.

Lu Xiaofeng did not need to see the dead man's face to know who he was.

Dugu Fang!

Not Liu Yuhen. Dugu Fang. The man who longed for death had not died; the man who did not wish to die was dead.

"The image was shattered long ago, by inner force," said Lu Xiaofeng bitterly. "And the dead man was set out here for us. To be seen."

Hua Manlou's face had gone white. At last he could not keep from asking, "Is the dead one Shangguan Feiyan?"

"The dead one is Dugu Fang," said Lu Xiaofeng. "I never dreamed he'd be the second to die."

Hua Manlou pondered. "Why should he have come here? Why should Shangguan Feiyan have come here? Can she have been harmed too? Can she have fallen into the hands of the Blue-Robe Tower?"

Lu Xiaofeng frowned. "You, who take everything else in the world so lightly... why is it that where she's concerned you must always think the worst?"

Hua Manlou was silent a long time, then sighed from his depths. "Is it because I care for her too much?"

It is. Care too much, and you cannot help thinking; think too much, and you cannot help thinking yourself into corners. Which is why the more two people love, the easier they fall into misunderstanding, and the more parting hurts them.

Lu Xiaofeng managed a smile. "However it stands, she's alive. With a blade at her neck, how could anyone sing so sweet a song?"

The song was not sweet at all, because Lu Xiaofeng was the one singing it.

"When life goes well, drink joy to the last drop; never let the golden cup stand empty to the moon..."

He beat time on his cup with a chopstick and sang it over and over; sing as he would, there were only ever the two lines.

At each round Hua Manlou drank a cup, until at last he could bear it no longer. "I don't say you sing badly. But could you possibly sing two different lines?"

"I could not," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Why not?" said Hua Manlou.

"Because these two lines are all I know," said Lu Xiaofeng.

Hua Manlou laughed. "They call Lu Xiaofeng a prodigy, brilliant past measure: any art under heaven, one look and it's his. But when you sing, you are truly stupider than a donkey."

"If my singing offends you," said Lu Xiaofeng, "why don't you sing yourself?"

He was pressing him on purpose, forcing Hua Manlou to sing; for he had never seen Hua Manlou so knotted up inside, and never seen him drink like this.

The wine was nothing much; what good wine does a mountain-village tavern keep? But any wine is better than no wine. Hua Manlou drained his cup at a draught, and sang out loud and clear:

"A coil of cloud her hair, a shuttle of jade her pin; a thin plain dress, a gauze of palest wash, and her two moth-brows lightly knit. Autumn winds rising, rain keeping them company; beyond the curtain, two or three clumps of plantain. Night without end... and what is a heart to do?"

This "Endless Longing" was written by Li Yu, last ruler of the Southern Tang, in memory of his dead wife, the elder Empress Zhou; and in its grieving, clinging lines is a longing past the telling.

It came to Lu Xiaofeng that Hua Manlou truly had fallen in love with that strange and lovely girl. He never spoke of it, because he loved deeply; and he loved deeply because he had never loved before.

But Shangguan Feiyan?

Her movements were too secret, her doings too strange; even Lu Xiaofeng could not fathom her mind, let alone Hua Manlou, netted fast in love.

Suddenly Lu Xiaofeng laughed. "I sing badly. You sing worse. Mine at least made you laugh; yours has taken the laughing clean out of me."

"Then better we simply drink," said Hua Manlou. "There's wine this morning; let this morning be drunk."

They had just raised their cups when a voice said, "Which of you gentlemen is the young lord Lu Xiaofeng?"

The night was late, the drinkers long gone; no one else should have been coming to a village tavern in the hills, and no one at all should have been coming for Lu Xiaofeng. Yet here this man was, and it was Lu Xiaofeng he wanted.

By his dress he was a hunter out of the hills, and in his hand was a bamboo basket with a roasted pheasant in it.

"And what do you want with Lu Xiaofeng?" Lu Xiaofeng could not help asking.

The hunter set the basket on the table. "The young lord Lu's aunt bought this specially, and sent me to bring it to the young lord to go with his wine."

Lu Xiaofeng stared. "My aunt?"

The hunter seemed to stare in his turn. "You are the young lord Lu? Lu Xiaofeng?"

Lu Xiaofeng nodded. "Except that I'm no young lord, and I have no aunt."

"Oh, you have one, no mistake about it," said the hunter.

"How so?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"If that young miss isn't your aunt, why would she pay five taels of silver for these pheasants and another five to have me carry them here? Only..." The hunter hesitated.

"Only what?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

The hunter eyed him sidelong, holding down a grin. "She said the young lord Lu was a man with four eyebrows, that I'd know him at a glance. But you seem to have only two."

Lu Xiaofeng tried to keep a straight face, and couldn't. "And when did you ever see a man with four eyebrows?"

The hunter laughed too. "Just because I never had. That's why I wanted a look. It wasn't all for the five taels."

"And what sort of person is this aunt of mine?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"A little girl," said the hunter.

"A little girl?" cried Lu Xiaofeng. "A man grown, with an aunt who's a little girl? Does that seem likely to you?"

The hunter smiled helplessly. "I didn't believe it either. But she said that young as she is, she stands very high in the family line. She said she has a grand-nephew called Hua Manlou who's past fifty already."

Lu Xiaofeng looked at Hua Manlou, wanting to laugh and not quite daring.

But Hua Manlou smiled. "Quite right. I do indeed have just such a great-aunt."

The hunter stared again. "You're Hua Manlou? You're past fifty?"

"I take good care of myself," said Hua Manlou. "It keeps me young-looking."

"And how is it done, this taking care?" the hunter could not help asking. "Could... could I learn it?"

"Nothing easier," said Hua Manlou blandly. "I merely eat, every day, fifty earthworms and twenty wall-lizards, with three catties of human flesh on top."

The hunter goggled at him until his eyes seemed ready to drop from his head; then he spun round and bolted out the door and away into the night without one backward look.

Lu Xiaofeng finally let the laughter out.

Hua Manlou was laughing too. "You had it right. When that little goblin starts lying, she could talk the dead back to life."

As he said it, his chopsticks tilted, as if idly, towards the window on the left.

Lu Xiaofeng was already airborne. One somersault, and the window was open: outside it crouched a small girl with two braids, hand over her mouth, giggling.

Shangguan Xue'er's eyes were as big as ever, her look as good as ever. The giggling, though, was over.

Lu Xiaofeng hauled her in by the braids. "Here's the little goblin. Not content to be my aunt, she'll be your great-aunt too."

Xue'er pouted. "A body only said it in fun. If you can't take a joke, you needn't take it out on a body's braids."

"And after all, she did spend ten taels of silver on you," said Hua Manlou, smiling. "The pheasant is very good. If you won't be grateful, you might at least be civil."

Xue'er beamed. "My grand-nephew has a conscience, at least. That's one fair word spoken."

Lu Xiaofeng roared. "So the ones with consciences rank a generation below the ones without."

Laughing, he let go, and quick as a fox-cub Xue'er slipped under his arm.

Not quite quick enough. Lu Xiaofeng caught her by the braids again, plucked her back like a chicken and set her down in a chair. "I've a question for you," he said, stern-faced, "and you'll answer it honestly. No lies."

Xue'er blinked up at him, the picture of injury. "I have never told a single lie in my life."

"That sentence was one," said Lu Xiaofeng.

Xue'er flared up. "If you don't believe one word I say, why talk to me at all?"

Lu Xiaofeng knew well enough how foolish it was to trade words with the little goblin. He kept his face stern. "Answer me: why have you been following us all this way?"

"I haven't been following you. If I'd tried, I couldn't have kept up," said Xue'er. Which happened to be true.

"Then how did you find us?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"I knew you were coming to find Ximen Chuixue," said Xue'er. "So I came first!"

"And you've been waiting here since?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"A body's been waiting a whole day. No change of clothes, no bath; I smell. Come and sniff if you don't believe me," said Xue'er.

Hua Manlou laughed again, and Lu Xiaofeng had to cover it with a dry cough or two. "And what were you waiting for?"

"Because I have a secret," said Xue'er, "and you have to hear it."

"What secret?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

Xue'er's mouth turned down, and once more she seemed on the point of tears. From inside her clothes she brought out a little golden swallow, exquisitely made. "Look. This is what I found in the garden that night!"

Lu Xiaofeng looked at it, and could not see what kind of secret it was supposed to be.

"My father gave it to my sister before he died," said Xue'er. "She treasured it like nothing else; she wore it on a golden chain, and when I begged to borrow it, just to wear for two days, she'd have died sooner than lend it. And now... now I pick it up off the ground."

"Perhaps she dropped it," said Lu Xiaofeng.

Xue'er shook her head hard. "Never. It fell, I know it, while somebody was carrying her body away."

There were tears standing in her eyes now; she looked grief-stricken in earnest, and her voice had gone hoarse.

"You truly believe your sister is dead?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

Xue'er bit her lip and nodded hard, choking. "I don't just know she's dead. I know who killed her."

"Who?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"That wretched cousin of mine," said Xue'er with hatred.

"Shangguan Danfeng?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Her. She didn't only kill my sister," said Xue'er. "She did for Xiao Qiuyu, and Dugu Fang, and Liu Yuhen too."

"All three of them, at her hands?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

Xue'er nodded. "I saw it with my own eyes. She was with Liu Yuhen in a room at an inn, talking away, and all at once she lifted her hand and killed him with her Flying Phoenix Needles. Then she hid the body under the bed."

Lu Xiaofeng sighed. "So Liu Yuhen, who begged for death and couldn't get it, died as quick as that."

"The Flying Phoenix Needles are her own secret weapon, the thing she's best at. One drop of blood and the throat closes; deadly poison," said Xue'er. "That must be how she killed my sister too. Only I don't know where she's hidden my sister's body..." Before the sentence was done, her tears were falling.

Lu Xiaofeng sighed again. "All very reasonable, all very likely. It sounds exactly like the truth. The pity is, I don't believe one word of it."

For once Xue'er did not flare up. She only wept. "I knew you wouldn't believe me. You... you... she has you bewitched already."

Lu Xiaofeng looked at her, and felt his certainty shake a little after all. "She and your sister were cousins too. Why should she kill her?"

"Who knows why she does anything?" said Xue'er through her teeth. "Perhaps she always hated my sister, for being cleverer than her, and prettier."

"And Liu Yuhen? He served her faithfully, heart and soul," said Lu Xiaofeng. "Why kill Liu Yuhen?"

"A woman with more venom in her than a viper, who could bring the stroke down on my own sister," said Xue'er bitterly. "Who in the world couldn't she kill?"

Lu Xiaofeng sighed. "I know you hate her. But..."

Xue'er cut him short with a cold little laugh. "You think I hate her over you. You think I'm jealous. She's all sweetness to my face, but she's bullied me behind backs since we were small..."

This time Lu Xiaofeng cut her short. "She's only nineteen this year, and you're already twenty. If you're the elder, how could she bully you?"

Xue'er had nothing to say.

And then Lu Xiaofeng's heart failed him again, and he said gently, "If you're truly frightened for your sister, you can rest easy. I know she isn't dead."

Xue'er bit her lip. "But I did see her kill Liu Yuhen. With my own eyes, through the window. Because I..." Her voice stopped dead. Her whole body had gone rigid.

For Liu Yuhen, whom Shangguan Danfeng had hidden under the bed, had just walked in.

***

The night mist drifted chill, the moonlight blurred. Liu Yuhen came walking slowly in out of that hazy moonlight, into the little tavern; and under the moon his ruined, hideous face was more dreadful than words could make it.

But his manner was mild and his voice was gentle. He looked at Xue'er. "If you've played enough out here, come home with me. His Highness sent me specially to fetch you back."

Xue'er's eyes went wide. "You... you're not dead?" she stammered.

A shade of old grief crossed Liu Yuhen's eyes. "Dying," he said darkly, "is not always such an easy thing."

"And my cousin?" said Xue'er.

"She hopes you'll come home soon too. You're still small; when you're grown, there'll be time enough to go about," said Liu Yuhen. "Look at your cousin: she goes where she pleases now, and no one says her nay."

Xue'er looked at him as though in terror, and suddenly she seized Lu Xiaofeng's hand and cried, "Please, please don't let this man take me back! I'd rather stay with you!"

"That too must wait till you're older," said Liu Yuhen. "You're a child still, and the grown-ups have real work in hand. How could you go along?"

From outside came the rumble of wheels and a horse's whinny. A carriage had drawn up at the door: the very one Lu Xiaofeng himself had ridden in.

"Up you get now," said Liu Yuhen. "Sleep sound in the carriage, and you'll be home before you know it."

In the end Xue'er went. She did not look back once.

Lu Xiaofeng watched her climb into the carriage, so small and forlorn that he sighed despite himself. "She's a lovable child at bottom, plainly. Why must she always lie?" he murmured.

Hua Manlou had been sitting quietly all this while. "Every lie has its reason," he said suddenly. "Some people lie to deceive others. Some lie to deceive themselves."

He sighed, and went on, "And there are sadder ones still, who lie only to win a little sympathy. To make somebody notice them."

"Because from a child she's had too little love and too little sympathy?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Yes," said Hua Manlou.

Lu Xiaofeng sighed, with a rueful smile. "You were right. Some people deserve forgiving even when they do wrong. Perhaps I should have spared them more thought long ago..."

He had not finished when he saw Liu Yuhen in the doorway again, watching him. "Xue'er has a message," said Liu Yuhen slowly, "that she asked me to bring you."

Lu Xiaofeng waited. And in the terrible man's eyes there seemed, of all things, a warm flicker of a smile. "She says she forgot to tell you just now: without your moustache you look much younger than with it. And much handsomer too."

***

Lu Xiaofeng kept fingering the new stubble on his upper lip. He had fingered it the whole road, from Yanbei all the way into Shanxi, as though he grudged his moustache every hour it took growing back.

Hua Manlou smiled. "You know I've never once minded being unable to see. But just now I would truly like a look at you with that moustache shaved away."

"The look is young and handsome," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Then why grow the moustache in the first place?" said Hua Manlou.

"Because I was too handsome already," said Lu Xiaofeng. "I feared every woman alive would be smitten to death, one after the next."

Hua Manlou laughed. "Your temper's been short these two days. Are you angry at yourself, by any chance?"

"Why should I be angry at myself?" said Lu Xiaofeng coldly.

"Because you feel you did rather poorly by that poor, lovable, lying little girl," said Hua Manlou. "And you're uneasy besides, not knowing whether she's being bullied and put upon, now she's home."

Lu Xiaofeng came abruptly to his feet, and was on the point of walking out when two invitation-cards were brought in: "A humble table is laid to wash the dust of the road from your honoured persons. Your presence is begged." The name beneath was Huo Tianqing.

A few plain phrases, the characters written fair and square, the ink laid on thick, so that every stroke stood faintly proud of the paper: a man without eyes could read it with a fingertip.

Hua Manlou smiled. "A most considerate man, it seems, this Steward Huo."

"Considerate, and a good deal more," said Lu Xiaofeng dryly.

The cards had come by a quick-tongued young fellow, who bowed at the door. "Steward Huo's orders: if the two gentlemen will honour him, I'm to have a carriage waiting here to bring them to the Yan mansion of the Pavilion of Pearls and Splendour. The Steward is awaiting their arrival now."

"How did he know I'd come?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

The young fellow smiled. "For eight hundred li around, there's little that happens, great or small, that Steward Huo doesn't know."
