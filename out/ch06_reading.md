## The Moon and the Mud

On and on ran the footpaths between the paddies.

Hard walking, it was.

Hijikata Toshizō and Okita Sōji crept, all but crawling, toward the enemy at Bubai Bridge.

The sky was clear as the sea, a night bright with stars and moon; yet there were two or three shreds of cloud that now and then hid the moon.

Each time, the Bushū plain below fell into darkness.

And each time the dark came down, Toshizō and Okita, as if by agreement, rolled off into the paddy. Legs, hips, and chest were caked with mud.

"This is filthy,"

{j} Okita whimpered, near to tears.

"I'm nothing but a mud-turtle. If I popped up like this, it's the other side that would get the fright. Eh, Hijikata?"

"Hold your tongue."

"It's madness, this strategy of yours, Hijikata. I wasted my praise on you earlier. There was no war-tale like this in the storyteller's repertory. Is it the Kusunoki school, that traces itself to Kusunoki Masashige? Or the Kōshū school, the kind Takeda Shingen was partial to?"

"It's the Hijikata school."

"There's nothing good about it—it's the mud-turtle school."

Okita Sōji had become a rōnin of the Shirakawa domain in Ōshū, but his late father had been a shogunal foot-guardsman posted in Edo, so that Okita was an Edo native born and bred. Unlike Toshizō, raised in a farmhouse of Bushū, he had a tongue that ran freely.

Where Toshizō and Okita were now crawling forward would be, in today's terms, about the middle of the third block of Bunbai-chō.

There were still three or four chō to Bubai Bridge.

All at once the feel of the earth underfoot changed.

(...?)

It had become, of a sudden, a mulberry field. Good walking. Presently, when the great zelkova at the foot of Bubai Bridge came into view under the moonlight, Toshizō,

{j} "Sōji—that's the riverbed there. Let's part here,"

{j} he said.

Okita would circle round from this point and come at the upstream side; Toshizō would close from downstream. They would take the enemy band between two fires. When the two had crawled along the riverbed and met, with luck, beneath the bridge, they would level their bared blades, dash up the embankment at a stroke, and cut in. That was the scheme.

"Understood?"

"Mm."

Okita seemed to be in a daze.

It was no wonder. For all that he was a prodigy of the training-hall, this was the first time in his life that Okita had passed beneath a bared blade.

"Are you afraid?"

"Well, yes. I'm not a man who's cut someone down once already, the way you are, Hijikata. But it never once entered my head that I'd come to a pass like this—to kill a man in my life. What is it a person's supposed to do?"

"Try it, and you'll know. This one thing can't be told in words. In short—rather than see to it you're not cut, cut. First, second, and third, take the initiative."

"Hijikata,"

{j} Okita said, in an odd voice.

"Something's queer with me. The place around my rump's gone all fidgety. Just there, it's trembling and itching, somehow..."

"What a troublesome boy you are."

"If you'll excuse me, I'll just finish up in that mulberry field there, so wait for me."

"Be quick about it."

{j} he said; but Toshizō's own lower belly had begun to feel unreliable.

(Vexing—but Sōji seems to have set me off.)

Best get it over with, he thought, and squatted down beside an old mulberry tree; and astonishingly close by, Okita too was squatting.

"You, too, Hijikata?"

"Mm."

"They say a green thief, before he breaks in, will get a trembling in his gut and foul himself. It's true, then."

"Hold your tongue."

As they crouched sniffing each other's raw smell, somehow the trembling passed off, and their nerve settled.

(Now, then. ...)

He set himself to rights and, to be sure, checked the peg that held his blade in its hilt.

"Sōji, we may as well go."

"We may indeed."

The voice had come back to its bottomless brightness.

Toshizō parted from Okita and went down to the riverbed.

The bed was a whitish stretch of sand, with a single ditch of a stream running down the middle of it. To beneath the bridge was about one chō.

Okita, for his part, went dashing at a crouch through the mulberry field. He was making a wide circle, to come round to the upstream side.

A wind was getting up.

***

Toshizō ran each time the moon slid behind cloud, and at last dashed into the darkness under the bridge.

Overhead were the planks of the bridge.

The creak of footsteps up there would be Hiruma Hanzō, or Shichiri Kennosuke.

The moon did not reach in this far.

Toshizō sat, all but embracing one of the bridge-piers.

There seemed to be men on the embankment and on the road above, for low voices came from here and there.

(A careless lot,)

{j} he thought; but the enemy, for their part, were likely talking among themselves to keep their fear at bay.

(This will be the biggest brawl since Ikaho.)

There had been such an affair, in Jōshū.

In his years of wandering the provinces, Chiba Shūsaku had stopped a while in Jōshū and taken on pupils. It was in the fourth month of the third year of Bunsei, when Shūsaku was twenty-seven.

Jōshū, like Bushū, was a country fond of the sword, so that from village after village swordsmen famed and unknown vied to enter his school, and in ten days' stay they came to a hundred-odd.

Shūsaku was still young.

The seasoned Shūsaku of later years would not have done such a thing, but in those days he had a streak of showing off, no doubt. To display the prestige of the Hokushin Ittō-ryū he had founded, he had a great votive tablet made, carved with the names of a hundred-odd Jōshū swordsmen who had entered under him, and made to hang it at the shrine of the Ikaho Myōjin nearby.

The one this dismayed was a swordsman native to Maniwa in Jōshū, Maniwa Jūrōzaemon. He was the head of the Nen-ryū house, the eighteenth of his line.

The fencing world of Jōshū had long been held down by this Maniwa school; and for Maniwa, to have nearly all his pupils taken by Shūsaku, and then their names carved and published at a shrine, was not to be borne.

To stop the hanging of that tablet, Maniwa Jūrōzaemon gathered more than three hundred pupils from across the province, took over all eleven inns of Ikaho, and stood facing a hundred-odd of the Chiba side; and further, as a rear guard, he massed a thousand-odd of the local gamblers on the Jizō riverbed.

It was for all the world a battle.

They seemed on the point, any moment, of rushing the Chiba side's inn; but Shūsaku, being an Edo man, considered that there was no profit, later, in quarreling with country swordsmen, and slipped out of Jōshū alone.

But Toshizō was no Edo man.

Nor was the other side.

Being a quarrel of country swordsmen—Kōgen Ittō-ryū against Tennen Rishin-ryū—they meant to go at it until they fell vomiting blood.

(Ah. ...)

When Toshizō noticed, Okita had crawled up to his very feet.

—It's me.

{j} Okita clung to him and, at his ear,

{j} —there are two of them there,

{j} pointed to the shadow of the embankment.

"Very well. Once we've made a blood-offering of those two, I'll leap the stream and crawl up from the far embankment there. Clear?"

"Right you are."

Okita and Toshizō left the darkness under the bridge and came round to either side of the two men.

"Here,"

{j} he called. When each of the two had turned round, Okita said,

{j} "Okita Sōji—I come,"

{j} and swept the man's trunk cleanly, felling him. It was work at the very brink.

"Hijikata Toshizō—I come,"

{j} Toshizō drove in and cut with a leftward diagonal; then, springing lightly back, he cleared the stream at a bound, seized the grass of the far embankment, and hauled himself up with a great stride. In the way he carried himself, he was a man who might have been born onto this earth for the very purpose of fighting.

Up on the road there was an uproar.

The surprise had succeeded. The enemy were not only thrown into confusion by the pair coming up from where they least looked for them; being split in two, they seemed to imagine that some great number had come.

Toshizō hauled himself up onto the road.

Before his eyes stood the great zelkova. That was the north end of the bridge, where, by old Gon's scouting, the men were thickest. Some of them, hearing the screams below the embankment, had run down to the riverbed.

Toshizō darted swiftly in beneath the zelkova and cut one black shadow down, straight through from above.

The man fell to the ground with a fearful sound. A single stroke seemed to have killed him outright.

At once Toshizō ran to the corpse and took the man's sword.

(Will this one cut, I wonder.)

His own sword he had put back in its sheath. Given only a rough, hasty grinding, he had no confidence in its edge.

Toshizō did not leave the shadow of the zelkova.

"The dark beneath a tree"—it is a good saying: from the enemy's side he could not be seen, while he himself could see the shadows running to and fro on the moonlit road and the bridge as clearly as in daylight.

(Running about as if at a weaving.)

Toshizō sprang out.

He swept hard at the hip of the nearest man, but the fellow's peasant bones must have been very hard, for the blade rang and turned and would not cut. The man he had struck arched his back and ran five or six staggering steps; and only when he had got that far did the terror seem to take him, for with a shriek—

"There! Under the zelkova!"

(Missed it, did I.)

Toshizō ducked swiftly back beneath the tree.

Four or five men came running at the scream, but the tree's dark was too deep for them to close in. Under the moon, a tree serves the office of a castle.

—Surround him.

{j} A composed voice was heard.

It was Shichiri Kennosuke.

Before long the men grew in number, until they were fourteen or fifteen.

"Fukatsu,"

{j} Shichiri called, by name, a man who seemed a pupil.

"Get a fire ready."

He meant, it seemed, to light up the foot of the tree.

The man called Fukatsu went round behind the others, crouched to the ground, and began striking a flint.

A straw bundle had been packed with gunpowder. Touched off, it flared up at once.

Toshizō slipped swiftly round to the back of the tree, but underfoot was a bluff.

(No good.)

As he made to turn back, Fukatsu, the fire-man, had already raised the straw torch and was about to hurl it at the tree's roots.

That upraised right arm dropped away with a cry. Okita had come round behind him.

With a quickness that astonished even Toshizō—that boy!—Okita cut down the man beside him, who held a hand-spear, and in the same motion kicked the straw torch in a great arc down to the riverbed.

The place fell back into its former darkness.

The instant it did, Toshizō rushed out from under the tree and cut at the big shadow he took for Shichiri.

He had meant to strike home with all his force, but Shichiri's stroke was the fiercer: it beat Toshizō's blade aside for the moment and, as he faltered, came at his head. As he barely caught it—

*Thak—*

{j} sparks flew, and Toshizō's sword was struck off short at the guard.

(No good.)

He sprang back.

(A wretched peasant sword.)

By the time he had drawn his own with a flash, he had already hurled himself upon the man to his right.

That quarter gave way all at once; and in five or six wild swings through the dark, Toshizō had wounded the hands, arms, and shoulders of several men.

Okita had come round to Toshizō's back. Guarding each other, the two kept the enemy off.

"Sōji—how many have you cut?"

"Three,"

{j} he said, composed.

"But, Hijikata, something's wrong,"

{j} and as he spoke he cut a man who came at him from the front with a rightward diagonal,

{j} "there—you see? Something's wrong,"

{j} he said.

"Wrong how?"

{j} Toshizō, too, was at last out of breath.

"My sword's gone like a club. And that fellow—he's not dead."

"The fat's clogged it, I expect. Time we—"

"Time we what?"

Another man came driving in at Okita. Okita struck the outthrust wrist cleanly down, sprang smartly back, and

{j} "Time we what, Hijikata?"

"—we ran."

"That's the thing. I've had my fill of this. It's begun to frighten me."

For all that, the line of Okita's blade was as coolly settled as could be.

"Run for it,"

{j} and with the word Toshizō sprang in, split the face of the man before him from the right temple, trod down over the corpse as it toppled back, and ran along the top of the embankment.

He leapt down into the mulberry field just below.

Okita came after.

Twenty or thirty steps off, and the enemy could no longer make out their shapes.

When they had run back to the graveyard of the Shōkōin, they undid the cloth-wrapped bundle hidden among the gravestones. Toshizō's clothes, with mud and the blood of his kills, had gone stiff as leather.

"Sōji—change your things."

"Me?"

Okita glanced down at his own clothes.

"They'll do. There's mud stuck to them, but that sort of thing brushes off once it's dried a little."

"..."

Toshizō turned and looked Okita over from collar to hem, as though licking him with his eyes; and by degrees his mouth fell open and would not close. What manner of cutting did this fellow do? He had not so much as a splash of another man's blood on him.

"You..."

He felt a spiteful little envy.

(Is the fellow a child of demons?)

Toshizō changed into a plain cotton crested robe borrowed from the Satō house, put on field hakama, and, tying the cords of his hand-guards and gaiters one by one, said,

{j} "What hour is that?"

He was listening hard to the sound of a far-off bell.

"The hour of the Boar, I should think—ten at night."

"Sōji,"

{j} Toshizō was already walking. The moon fell on his two shoulders, shining with grease.

"Go back to Edo."

"And you, Hijikata?"

"I'm going back."

Toshizō walked fast. Okita, all but running to keep up,

{j} "Let's go back together."

"You fool. After a thing like this? Can the two of us walk the high road side by side, heads in a row?"

"Hijikata,"

{j} Okita gave a low chuckle.

He said no more. Say it, and Toshizō, being what he was, would grow earnest and fob him off.

(It's a woman, then.)

Okita did not yet know the taste of a woman. For some reason he seemed born cool to such things, and thought it strange that the other men of the hall should lose their heads over the women of the unlicensed quarters.

But Toshizō's mood just now he felt he could somehow understand, so

{j} "Here, then, we'll say goodbye,"

{j} he said.

Okita, leaving behind the smile of a biddable boy, slipped into the pitch-black mulberry field. To be safe he would keep off the high road, follow the Tama River to Yanokuchi, and rejoin the highway at Kokuryō. By then, no doubt, day would be breaking.

Toshizō went straight out onto the high road and into the Fuchū post-town.

The town was without lights already.

The moon had hidden itself now.

Feeling his way along the eaves of the post-town houses, he passed into the wood of the Rokusha Myōjin.

Lights had been set, here and there, in the stone lanterns.

Presently, groping his way to the shrine-maidens' tenement, he tapped, softly, at the door of the house of the bell-shaking shrine maiden Kozakura.

There is a way of tapping. It had been agreed on.

Kozakura seemed to guess at once that it was Toshizō, and slid the bar and let him in.

"What's happened?"

She made to take Toshizō's hand, but—

"Ugh, the stink,"

{j} she let it go. The smell of blood, perhaps, had soaked into him.

"Have you any salve?"

"You're hurt?"

{j} The shrine maiden cocked her head a little.

"And shōchū, too."

He bared himself to the waist. Out of some odd vanity he had said nothing to Okita, but he had taken a wound at the root of his right shoulder, in one place, and another on his left upper arm, deep enough to show the white fat.

"A dog bit me."

"Do dogs have teeth like this, I wonder?"

Kozakura rose to make ready what was needed for the dressing. Watching her go off into the back with a little sway of her hips, Toshizō said,

{j} "Never mind—come here,"

{j} sharply. He could bear it no longer. The riot of blood at Bubai Bridge had not yet died down in him.

(A fight and a woman—the two are one thing.)

The smell of blood was on both. So he thought.

Toshizō drew the woman to him and pulled her down across his knees.

By that time Okita was walking east along the south bank of the Tama River, singing every child's song he could remember.
