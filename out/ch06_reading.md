## Chapter Six

Fan Bowen sat toying with Lin Peishan's little cosmetics bag, his whole face wearing the free-and-easy air proper to "the poets," his head tilted to one side as he listened, quietly, to the low, halting, half-swallowed murmur of Lin Peishan's voice. They were sitting behind a clump of arbor-vitae, screened alike from the eyes of the other strollers and from the sun that was beginning to slant to the west; yet whether it was because there was no breeze, or for some other reason, tiny beads of sweat kept starting, again and again, from Fan Bowen's temples.

They were in a quiet, cool corner of Jessfield Park; they had been sitting on that red-painted long wooden bench for half an hour now.

Lin Peishan wore that day a foreign frock of pale-blue gauze that left half her bosom and both white arms bare; her figure, in the full bloom of its sixteen-year-old girlhood, showed uncommonly round and even, and a pair of small breasts like little steamed buns lay hidden within a petticoat of white Indian silk, save that a good part of the upper swell showed above its edge, holding the inch-and-a-half of net lace taut, as though it were stretched to bursting. As she talked she teased the fine grass at her feet with the tip of her shoe, her manner lively and yet composed, as if she were recounting some irrelevant story about another family altogether.

Her voice sank lower and lower, and at last died away; with a charming smile she lifted her face and gazed at a patch of cloud in the eastern sky that had suddenly turned crimson.

"Go on, Sister Shan!— I've been waiting for you ever so long."

Fan Bowen too followed Lin Peishan's gaze up into the sky and looked at it a while, then suddenly turned his face to her as he spoke. Once more he wiped the sweat from his temples, and with a somewhat anxious air fixed his eyes, unwavering, on Lin Peishan's pretty face.

Lin Peishan looked back at him, but with neither anxiousness nor excitement — only a languid softness filling her eyes. All at once she gave a spurt of laughter, spread both hands, and made a gesture of "that's the end of it," answering in a voice clear as ringing crystal:

"There's no more! I've told it all! Surely you don't feel it isn't enough?"

"It isn't that I've not heard enough — it's that I've not understood enough!"

Fan Bowen's genius for witticisms was stirring again. Lin Peishan laughed once more, stretched herself lazily, and one arm swung past before Fan Bowen's face, wafting a breath of sweet fragrance. It was as though a few ants were crawling across Fan Bowen's heart; his body gave a slight shudder, and he clean forgot the very words he had been about to say. He gazed, spellbound, at Lin Peishan's long eyebrows, her small round eyes, the two scarlet lips slightly parted, the white teeth half showing, the gleaming throat, the swelling bosom — he gazed and gazed, and across his brain flitted a host of ideas none too distinct. But when his eyes at last came back and settled on Lin Peishan's face, he suddenly perceived that her expression was as coolly calm as ever, the look of an outsider; for though she smiled tenderly, that smile plainly admitted of no special reading. And so another sort of ant-crawling flavour spread through Fan Bowen's heart, and all at once he remembered the words he ought to say:

"The one thing I don't understand is why Sunfu won't approve of you and me—"

"That's Sunfu's affair — there's no need to talk of it any more!"

Lin Peishan cut in quickly, breaking off Fan Bowen's unfinished words. And yet neither her face nor her tone showed any unusual displeasure, or any unusual strain.

Fan Bowen's heart gave a jump; he thought it strange. He waited a while, and seeing that Lin Peishan again said nothing, he asked once more:

"And I understand still less what it means that now even Sister Yao won't hear of it either?"

"I don't understand it either! Whatever Sister said, I've just repeated to you word for word. Who has the patience to puzzle over it!"

This plainly displayed air of an outsider was what stung Fan Bowen to anger. It cost him a great effort, but he left off his "poetic" witticisms at last and spoke to Lin Peishan quite plainly and directly:

"What kind of talk is this! How is it that whatever Sister Yao says, you just recite it over again, and then say you haven't the patience to give it more thought? As though it were something that had nothing to do with you! As though you weren't yourself, but had turned into somebody else!— Sister Shan, you ought to have a self of your own! What is your own mind on the matter? You simply must have a self of your own!"

"My own self is right here, sitting beside you. The one who's been talking with you all this long while — that is my own self!— But as for there being some other self besides, that I have never known, and never cared to know either. Sister said a great many things to me, and charged me too to keep them secret; but since you ask me, and since what Sister said had you mixed up in it, in the end I did recite it over just as she said it. You ask what my opinion is?— Why, I've never had any fixed opinion about anything. I feel that everything is fine, and everything is also a little bit not-fine. I've never cared to trouble myself over anyone else's opinions.— Well? You're still not satisfied, you still feel it isn't enough?— Then it's really too hard for me!"

Lin Peishan smiled as she delivered this whole long speech, her tone at once tender and mellow, so that Fan Bowen, vexed as he had been, felt his heart much soothed on hearing it. But one thing still did not escape his notice — that in all these words Lin Peishan had still not uttered her own attitude toward the matter — and, above all, her own attitude toward Fan Bowen himself.

Fan Bowen heaved a sigh, propped his head on his hand, and looked at the grass on the ground and at the exquisite, rounded curve of Lin Peishan's calves. All at once — moved by he knew not what impulse — he opened the cosmetics bag he held in his hand and looked into the little mirror set inside it. Neither too round nor too sharp, but with something of a triangle to it, a face of graceful elegance was mirrored in that little oval glass. The face showed somewhat pale, but in that very pallor there was a touch of melancholy, of the sort that rouses the tenderness of nervous young women. In a moment the mirror shifted, and the reflection was no longer the whole face, but the cross-section of the brows and eyes. The eyebrows were dark and long, matched with eyes equally long and brimming with cleverness; yet brows and eyes together held, amid their keen alertness, a look of pent-up grievance as well. In a word, it was a face best fitted to draw the tender pity of sentimental young women of around twenty! And if it could win, too, the liking of lively, innocent girls of fifteen or sixteen who did not know the world held any sorrow, that was because the face had also two thin lips very apt at witticisms, forever parted in a smile that was not quite a smile.— Fan Bowen looked into the mirror a while, let out a breath, shut the cosmetics bag, and raised his head to gaze at Lin Peishan once more. The tender smile still lingered at the tips of her brows and the corners of her mouth. And in the pupils of her eyes, clear as autumn waters, Fan Bowen seemed to see all the old intimacy and trust between the two of them. Could all this really be cancelled because of Wu Sunfu's "disapproval"? Cancelled because of the Wu young mistress's "disapproving too"? It could not! Fan Bowen felt of a sudden an excitement he had never known, roused to a courage he had never known. He seized Lin Peishan's hand abruptly and cried:

"Peishan! Peishan!— Shan!"

Taking it, seemingly, for the same fond teasing as always, Lin Peishan did not stir and did not speak, but answered Fan Bowen's rather ardent cry with her eyes alone. And in that look there was plainly some other ingredient, plainly the thought of some other thing, something very far removed from the present scene. Fan Bowen, however, did not notice. He only felt that Lin Peishan's palm was warm and soft in a way it had not been before, and charged, as it were, with a prickling, tingling electric force. Hand in hand though they were as a matter of daily habit, at this moment there was a strange allurement in it; Fan Bowen turned his head aside, longing to steal a kiss on the sudden. But no sooner had he brought his head close to Lin Peishan's ear than his courage all at once deserted him. Her pretty displeasure was a thing to be reckoned with. And so he turned the movement into a question:

"Sister Yao won't hear of it just now? But why?"

"Oh, come!— I've told you I don't understand it myself!"

Lin Peishan gave this hurried answer as if in alarm, and laughed again. Yet the coaxing sweetness in the words was very plain too, and Fan Bowen, savouring the flavour of it, took it suddenly into his head that the meaning behind the answer was as good as, "It's all up to you; for my part I'm as willing as not, either way," and he could not keep his heart from pounding, and his face grew somewhat hot. He gazed greedily at Lin Peishan, from her face to her breast and from her breast to her face, all of it brimming with the alluring lustre and softness of youth. This notion, too, went flashing across his bewildered nerves: that if he used a little force, this "Shan" of his would very likely put up no resistance? He all but ventured a movement — but, as ill luck would have it, from the clump of arbor-vitae behind them there rose of a sudden a rustling, scrabbling sound, and Fan Bowen's whole body started, and that wild ambition fled away again.

Just then a gust of cool wind blew up. In the trees opposite some bird or other was calling. A flock of pigeons came whirring down before Fan Bowen and Lin Peishan and walked slowly about on the grass as though taking a stroll, then stood still and, cocking their heads, looked at the pair. Fan Bowen's attention shifted to the pigeons; and finding that these pigeons had quite the bearing of "poets," he fell to thinking of a short poem again.

Lin Peishan, who had been lost in thought all this while, suddenly gave a queer little laugh to herself, gently freed the hand Fan Bowen was holding, and stood up, saying:

"I'm going back! Sit too long on this wooden bench and one's bones ache."

Fan Bowen's poetic mood was broken off at once; he too got up in a flurry and looked at Lin Peishan, not quite understanding why she should want to go back so suddenly. True, sitting here had been of no great help toward the settling of his "problem" — both his bold resolves had come at last to nothing — yet the two of them sitting quietly here together, was that not entirely to the taste of a "poet" like himself? He truly had no wish to go. But since he had never once opposed any proposal of Lin Peishan's, he could not oppose this one now either, and could only heave a sigh at her.

By long habit, this silent, gentle protest of his would draw a few words from Lin Peishan, and so there was commonly the chance of turning the thing about. But today Lin Peishan was different; she took her cosmetics bag back from Fan Bowen's hand and said, without the least warmth:

"I am going back! The sight and the sound of everything, it all makes me cross!"

Without so much as waiting for Fan Bowen to answer, and without calling him to come along, Lin Peishan spun round and ran off quickly toward the main path of the park. A few seconds later the trees had hidden her from sight. Fan Bowen instinctively shifted a few steps forward and looked all about him, but Lin Peishan had run clean out of sight and left no trace.

A strange desolation pinned Fan Bowen to the spot for a long while. His first thought was to run straight out to the park gate and wait there for his "Shan"; but a man's pride — and above all a man's fit of pique when he has fallen out with a woman he has long been used to petting and teasing — held Fan Bowen's feet fast.

As though he had lost something, he wandered about the park. The sun slanted westward, and the strollers grew more and more numerous, every one of them paired off two by two. They shot glances at Fan Bowen at once questioning and mocking, and brushed past him chattering and murmuring, filling the air with waves of merry laughter. All of it made Fan Bowen both envious and full of hate — above all because the men were every one of them like the paunchy bourgeois profiteers he so detested. Before this crowd of smug and self-satisfied people he felt he had, in very truth, no hole to hide his head in.

Go back to the Wu mansion and take up dallying with Lin Peishan again? Fan Bowen felt that would be to think far too little of himself as a man! Go back to the room he kept at the Dalai Hotel? To that he was more unwilling than to anything on earth. This poet, so used to his free-and-easy ways, felt all at once that a home — a home with father and mother, brothers and sisters — was, after all, of some use. But he had none. He had become the loneliest man in the world! And so the thought that so often comes to poets in their anguish — "death" — swelled little by little in his mind. He paced with drooping head, and his rich imagination fastened tight upon the "death" within this problem. On this heavenly May afternoon, in this Jessfield Park where women drifted thick as clouds, that he — a young poet, of dashing appearance, endowed with the sort of air that stirs the feelings of every woman who sees him — that he should suddenly die, would that not be a wonder to startle the world outright? Would it not be sure to move every kind of woman in the park — the aloof, the melancholy, the tender and sentimental young girls — to shed a handful of pitying tears over his beautiful corpse, and, at the very least, to set their sweet hearts throbbing? Would that not be the fittest, most poetic death for a poet?— Fan Bowen could think of no better way than this to turn his anguish into contentment, and his defeat into victory!

And there, right before him, was that large pond, so conveniently placed. It was a fine spot; the young men and women who came to the park all sat a while on the benches here. "Let me play Grand Master Qu for once!"— so Fan Bowen thought, and ran to the edge of the pond. What rather dashed his spirits was that on the benches round the pond there were, after all, not many modern young women worth a look. A few Western children, though, were sailing toy wooden boats there. A little girl in white and a little boy in grey lifted up a handsome sailing-ship, some two feet long, and set it on the pond; the three red silk sails on the boat filled full with wind, and off it went, driving majestically forward. The thick oil-green water of the pond parted before it in a fine white ripple. The children who had launched the boat ran along the edge of the pond after the little sailing-ship, shouting and laughing at the top of their voices.

The poetic afflatus gave another leap in Fan Bowen's soul, and on the instant he had two good lines; the notion of "death" withdrew far into the distance, and he longed to finish this poem drafted in his head. Just now, before he had hit upon the third line, the wind of a sudden shifted its quarter, and freshened besides, and the little sailing-ship on the pond heeled to the left and capsized.

At this unlooked-for turn for the worse, Fan Bowen's shock and disappointment were in truth far keener than those of the few Western children who had launched the boat! On the journey of life, too, one meets again and again with just such a spiteful, veering wind, that capsizes the little sailing-ship of one's life! Man is but pitifully ruled by an unknowable "wind"! Fan Bowen steeled his heart, stepped back a pace with a flourish, and crouched down, meaning in good earnest to leap into the pond! But at that very moment a repentance came rushing headlong over his whole soul, and this "repentance" showed itself, moreover, in the shape of a human voice calling out behind him.

Fan Bowen took the chance to straighten up and turned his head to look; and it was, after all, no one else but Wu Zhisheng, standing there some three feet off with a smile on his face.

Without knowing why himself, Fan Bowen felt his face redden. He stole a look to gauge Wu Zhisheng's expression, and, seeing plainly that there was nothing amiss in it, breathed easy at last, walked slowly up to Wu Zhisheng, and forced a smile by way of greeting.

"Are you all on your own, then?— Eh, watching other people sail their little boats by yourself?"

Wu Zhisheng asked it coldly, with an expression that seemed half deliberate and half careless, but was certainly a little out of the common.

Fan Bowen said nothing, only forcing himself to give a nod. But Wu Zhisheng, of all things, pressed him with another question:

"Truly all alone?"

Fan Bowen forced another nod, and forced out a trace of a smile besides. He would have liked to run off, but reflecting that Wu Zhisheng's company was after all "less dreary" than blundering to and fro on his own, he could not bring himself to go. His one hope was that Wu Zhisheng would turn to some other subject. And "as Heaven would have it," Wu Zhisheng did change tack, and with a sigh asked:

"Do you know about Zhang Susu's affair? Zhang Susu? A few days ago didn't you say she was forever letting slip a 'poetic temperament'—"

"What? Her affair! Don't tell me she's caught some deadly epidemic?"

"No. A person like her doesn't fall ill! It's that things have gone wrong between her and Li Yuting! This Professor Li has left her 'disappointed,' and she's moping over it!"

Fan Bowen burst out laughing. In his heart he was truly grateful to Wu Zhisheng for bringing him such a welcome piece of news. And his witticisms came rushing to his lips again:

"Just like one and one making two, it's the natural result! A 'grey' professor is bound to disappoint a Miss Zhang who craves 'strong stimulation'; but there's no call to mope over it! That ill becomes the poetic temperament she lets slip now and then!"

"But you still don't know that Professor Li feels disappointed in Susu too!"

"What! A grey professor, presuming to—"

"There are things he's quite equal to — reckoning in coppers and silver, for one."

"Oh—so it's got to do with money again?"

"How should it not! It's because Professor Li has found out that Susu's father has all but run through the family fortune, and so he's disappointed too."

At these words Fan Bowen opened his eyes wide, and for a good while said nothing; then all at once he broke into loud laughter, shrugged his shoulders, and said:

"I—I for one despise the gold of the propertied class!"

"Because the gold of the propertied class despises your new poetry too!"

Wu Zhisheng answered coldly, but put on, of set purpose, an air of perfect gravity. The colour changed at once in Fan Bowen's face — first a flush, then straightway a livid white; with a hateful glare at Wu Zhisheng he turned to go. Plainly his temper was really roused. But before he had gone a few steps he came running back, clapped Wu Zhisheng on the shoulder, put on a "no-more-joking" face, and said, dropping his voice:

"I hear that somebody's been at work trying to make a match between you and little Shan!"

But Wu Zhisheng never turned a hair; he only gave Fan Bowen a careless glance and answered in a slow voice:

"I've heard some talk to the contrary myself."

"Talk to the contrary — how? Tell me! Tell me!"

"In this present age, not only does the man choose the woman — the woman chooses the man as well; not only does the man toy with the woman — the woman toys with the man as well!"

The colour changed in Fan Bowen's face again on the instant, and he came within an ace of turning on his heel and leaving. He had made up his mind that the day was against him, that he would meet a rebuff at every turn and be vexed at every turn; and his genius for drollery, moreover, seemed to have quitted his body, while he himself was all too apt to take offence. But Wu Zhisheng, feigning to take no notice of anything, looked hard at Fan Bowen's face and said again, gravely:

"I'll tell you honestly! Lin Peishan is waiting for you!"

Fan Bowen could not help starting all over, thinking that Lin Peishan had not gone home after all, but was still waiting in the park. He asked hurriedly:

"Where is she waiting for me?"

"In her heart, of course.— Waiting for you to win the Nobel Prize for Literature!"

So saying, Wu Zhisheng himself broke into a great "ha-ha" of laughter. Fan Bowen, without a sound, turned and went; this time he went in good earnest, running to a clump of trees, and with one turn was gone. Wu Zhisheng gazed after him with a smile for a while, and could not but marvel a little that this "poet" should be able to stalk off in a huff and never once look back. He waited a minute or two more, and, concluding that Fan Bowen was indeed gone for good, ran up at last onto a mound behind the pond, ringed round with trees like a little pavilion, and called out:

"Fourth Sister, it's getting late; if you want to see the zoo, we must be quick about it."

The Fourth Young Lady, Huifang, was just then leaning against a willow tree, rubbing her eyes with her handkerchief. Without a word, she gave Wu Zhisheng a single glance and followed him. The rims of her eyes were a little red. When they had walked some way, the Fourth Young Lady took a quick step to draw level with Wu Zhisheng's shoulder, and suddenly asked in a low voice:

"Ninth Brother!— Was he meaning to jump into the water? He looked very like it."

"I didn't ask him."

"Why didn't you ask him! You ought to have asked him.— Just now we followed him a good long way, and didn't you see how wild and distracted he was the whole way, how strange his manner? When we came in we met the second Miss Lin, and she too seemed to have something on her mind. . . ."

Wu Zhisheng suddenly laughed aloud. He looked at his cousin for a good while, then said:

"Fan Bowen would never kill himself. He has talked of killing himself times without number. Just now, when you saw him as if about to jump into the water, he was really standing there making poetry!— a new poem, 'Chanting on the Riverbank.' A poet of his sort will never truly kill himself. Set your mind at rest!"

"Pah! What business is it of mine! Telling me to set my mind at rest! Only—"

The Fourth Young Lady blushed, checked her words, and walked on with her head down. Yet somehow in her heart the tender, pitiable image of Fan Bowen had stamped itself deep. She had fallen behind Wu Zhisheng's shoulder again. When they had walked on a further stretch, the Fourth Young Lady sighed low, and all at once let fall a single tear.

This nameless melancholy of the Fourth Young Lady's was itself a thing of only the last three or four days. Her heart had turned to a thin membrane, so that even the slightest, most trifling stir — the ripple of anyone's joy or sorrow — could set her own heart trembling in answer. Sitting alone in a quiet room, she felt a chill desolation as of an ice-cellar; but mixed into a crowd of people, she felt an unbearable menace, as though every single person had set a smug, complacent face and was menacing her. In all the world she alone was forlorn and solitary — so she often thought. She hungered for someone of her own to hold fast and weep her fill upon, to pour out her heart to till she was eased; these three or four days since coming to Shanghai had been like three or four years, and her heart was heaped with words beyond counting, and tears beyond counting!

Perhaps it was on this simple ground — that she was herself feeling the sorrow of loneliness — that the Fourth Young Lady had conceived so deep an impression of the crestfallen, forlorn Fan Bowen? But as she walked along behind Wu Zhisheng, what with her own low spirits, and still more with the unbroken stream of strollers and scenery along the way, she gradually forgot that touching, pitiable look of grief on Fan Bowen's face. By the time they had entered the zoo and were standing before the bear-pit, watching the huge black bear pace back and forth in measured steps like a philosopher, and now and then rear up like a great blockhead and wag its clumsy head, the Fourth Young Lady forgot even her own low spirits for the moment, and smiled.

Wu Zhisheng ran into a schoolmate, and the two of them fell to talking. This schoolmate had a shock of hair unkempt as thatch-grass, but a face uncommonly handsome; while he talked with Wu Zhisheng he kept turning his eyes to look at the Fourth Young Lady; little by little their voices dropped lower, and yet the Fourth Young Lady, half by chance and half not, caught the two remarks of a question and its answer:

"Is that your 'feiyangsan,' then?"

"No,— it's my cousin!" The Fourth Young Lady's face flushed suddenly again. Though she did not know what a "feiyangsan" was, she guessed something of the meaning from Wu Zhisheng's answer; and, all bashful, she turned and walked a few steps away, to the monkey cage on the right. This was a cage of iron bars about half a room in size, in which a great many monkeys of every size were leaping and bounding. Back in her home town the Fourth Young Lady had seen the performing monkeys of the Shandong showmen; and what she still remembered quite clearly was a monkey she had seen five or six years before at the incense-fair by the Earth-God Temple, one that would laugh again and again — and how white its whole mouthful of teeth had been! But that too was the last keepsake of her happiness, for afterwards, since at fourteen she had grown to a woman's shape, old Mr. Wu would no longer let her go to such places as the incense-fair, where men and women mingled together. Now she saw monkeys again, and so many of them, and the bygone days of her childhood came flooding back against the current of memory.

She stood there before the monkey cage, lost in reverie, longing to pick out a monkey that too could laugh.

But among these monkeys there was not one that could laugh. Having, it seemed, a touch of the "city-dweller's" nerves, they only scampered and sprang about at random, shrieking hysterically, chee-chee. The Fourth Young Lady felt disappointed, and was on the point of turning to look for Wu Zhisheng, when all at once she caught sight of a curious scene. On a wooden box in the corner of the cage a monkey lay lazily sprawled, while another monkey, with a most solemn face, picked lice for the one lying down; from their fond and loving air anyone would fancy that between this pair of monkeys there was some special relation — that they were husband and wife! The Fourth Young Lady stood spellbound; something like gladness, and something like grief, and still more like an uncommonly bittersweet, tingling flavour, all went churning through her heart at once! She dared not look on, and yet could not bear not to look; she was fairly bewitched, until Wu Zhisheng's voice startled her out of it:

"Come along! They'll be closing here soon!"

The Fourth Young Lady gave a violent start, turned her head and gazed blankly at Wu Zhisheng, not taking in what he had said. Then a flush of red stole suddenly out from the middle of her fair, tender cheek — from the very spot where a dimple formed when she smiled — and spread quickly to her brows and the corners of her eyes. That mingled feeling of shame and dread, of having had one's secret spied out, drove the Fourth Young Lady fairly to the verge of tears. She struggled to keep the tears brimming in her eyes from falling, turned away, and walked on the tips of her toes without a word. The strollers in the zoo had nearly all gone by now, but she did not notice; she went a few steps, saw a chair, and sat down on it forlornly, bowed her head, and hid her face in her handkerchief.

"Fourth Sister, are you feeling unwell? The zoo-keepers will be coming to hurry us out. They close here at five o'clock."

Wu Zhisheng said it softly, standing beside the Fourth Young Lady; plainly he had not understood her mood. Nor was that to be wondered at: Wu Zhisheng, used to keeping company with modern city girls like Lin Peishan and Zhang Susu, could of course not begin to fathom the hidden griefs and stirrings of an old-fashioned "young lady of the inner chambers" such as the Fourth Young Lady. But the strange thing was that this very failure to understand seemed rather to ease her heart, and his gentle, solicitous tone gave the Fourth Young Lady some measure of comfort; she uncovered her face, looked at Wu Zhisheng through the sparkle of her tears, forced a smile, and at the same time stood up, answering with a touch of shyness:

"It's nothing,— let us go back."

By this time the sun had half sunk below the horizon, and a cool wind blew up, so that people felt uncommonly fresh and light of spirit. Men and women strollers came pouring into the park batch upon batch. Wu Zhisheng, for his part, would have liked to walk about a little more, or to go to the big reed-matting shed where ice-cream and soda-water were sold and drink something. But the Fourth Young Lady dreaded crowds above all, and dreaded still more the questioning sidelong glances shot at her by the young men and women paired off two by two; she insisted on going home — though, once at home, she was by no means sure of feeling any happier.

They passed by the pond again. There were many people here now, and all the benches were filled. But by a big tree not far from the pond a young man sat on the grass with his back against the trunk, his head hanging down, seemingly asleep. The Fourth Young Lady, quick of eye, knew him from afar for Fan Bowen. She threw Wu Zhisheng a questioning glance. Wu Zhisheng had already seen that it was Fan Bowen too; he nodded with a smile, then crept softly round behind Fan Bowen and, from the far side of the tree, thrust out his hands all at once and covered Fan Bowen's eyes.

"Let go! Who's this?— What a nasty trick!"

Fan Bowen said it lazily and most pitifully, his body not stirring in the least. The Fourth Young Lady, behind Wu Zhisheng, only stood staring dazedly. After a moment she stepped lightly over to Fan Bowen's side. Wu Zhisheng pressed his hands the tighter over the eyes, but could not keep from laughing aloud.

"Wu Zhisheng!— there couldn't be a second such. If I've guessed wrong, off with my head!"

"That's not your guessing right — I told you myself.— Guess again: who else is here?"

This time Fan Bowen would not guess; he struggled hard, his face flushing scarlet.

"Ninth Brother. Do let go!"

The Fourth Young Lady, much troubled at heart, pleaded on Fan Bowen's behalf. At the same moment Fan Bowen had already wrenched free of Wu Zhisheng's hands; he sprang up, rubbed his eyes, then suddenly turned, caught the Fourth Young Lady's hand, and, bowing most respectfully, said:

"My deliverer! Fourth Young Lady, I thank you!"

The Fourth Young Lady hastily shook her hand free of Fan Bowen's and turned her back, the red rushing at once from the corners of her eyes to the roots of her ears; but she could not help asking, in a small voice:

"You haven't gone back, Mr. Fan?— What are you doing, sitting here?"

"Ah—making poetry."

Fan Bowen answered. And then, as if he had forgotten everything again, he tilted his head, rolled his eyes up to the sky, and struck the attitude of one painfully chanting verses. Wu Zhisheng found it comical to watch, but did not laugh out, only giving the Fourth Young Lady a wink. Fan Bowen suddenly heaved a sigh, stamped his foot, came up close to the Fourth Young Lady, and said again:

"When I'm heartsore I make poetry. Poetry is my tears. And the more heartsore I am, the finer my poetry!— But Zhisheng is a real wretch; he broke off my train of verse. A good poem, and only one line wanting. Now the whole of it is clean forgotten!"

The Fourth Young Lady watched Fan Bowen bring out the words one by one, watched that pale yet winsome face of his, and then all at once her heart trembled again — a trembling of some strange flavour she had never known before.

"Well then, pray go on making poetry. Good-bye!"

Wu Zhisheng said it coldly, swung one arm, and turned to go. The Fourth Young Lady seemed to hesitate a moment, but after a glance at Fan Bowen she too followed listlessly behind Wu Zhisheng. Fan Bowen stared wide-eyed after the retreating figures of the Fourth Young Lady and her companion. Just as those figures were about to be lost in the crowd, Fan Bowen suddenly gave a loud laugh and came running after them, put out his hand and linked it through Wu Zhisheng's right arm, and said, with a touch of entreaty:

"No more poetry. Why shouldn't we take a walk together!"

"But we're going home."

The Fourth Young Lady, for once, spoke up first; she smiled at Fan Bowen, then quickly lowered her head again.

"Then I'll come to — the Wu mansion too!"

Fan Bowen paused a little, and then made up his mind.

They exchanged hardly a word along the way, and the three of them came to the front of the Wu mansion just as the great black-lacquered iron gate was on the point of closing; the gatekeeper, seeing that it was the Fourth Young Lady and the others, drew the gate open again and said with a broad grin:

"Fourth Young Lady, there are people come from the town; fled here, they say."

These two flat, commonplace sentences at once drove the drifting clouds from the Fourth Young Lady's mind. She could not help giving a little cry of "Oh!" and ran quickly in at the gate. Though she had heard Sunfu mention the ill news of their home town three days before, it had seemed too much out of the common, too hard to believe, and the Fourth Young Lady had never taken it to heart. Now it was as though her eyes had been suddenly opened and she truly saw the ghastly upheaval that was in any case so hard to credit, and the colour of her face turned an ashen grey.

The great drawing-room was crowded with people, all standing and talking in a noisy hubbub. The first to meet the Fourth Young Lady's eye was Fei the little-moustache. This old fellow, in a long gown of grey cloth, had to answer the Wu young mistress and to answer the Seventh Young Master, Ah Xuan, both at once, and was fairly run off his feet. The Fourth Young Lady went to the Wu young mistress's side, and heard Fei the little-moustache saying, panting and gesturing:

"It was eight o'clock — er, it must have got on to nine; young mistress, it was nine o'clock! The Hongchang pawnshop was set ablaze.— Without Battalion Commander He's two machine-guns, those rioters, those mutineer soldiers, would likely never have burned the Hongchang. Young mistress, isn't that so? The machine-guns were mounted right by the Hongchang's watch-tower — rat-tat-tat, it was truly frightful! And yet what good did it do!—"

"Here, here, little-moustache, what about my case of little books, after all? You've said nothing at all about my case of little books!"

Ah Xuan, catching hold of Fei the little-moustache's arm, broke in.

Fei the little-moustache rolled his eyes and stared blankly at Ah Xuan, not understanding what these "little books" were. But the Wu young mistress laughed, and the Fourth Young Lady seized the opening to ask:

"Was the whole town really plundered clean? I can't believe it — a town so big! Was it the Hongchang pawnshop they burned? And what of our own house?"

"Fourth Sister, the house wasn't burned.— Mr. Fei has had a hard journey too; let him rest a while, and we'll talk more when Sunfu comes back. Ah, a mutiny of soldiers!"

As the Wu young mistress spoke, her gaze all at once grew unfocused, as though some insoluble problem had suddenly seized upon her heart. She stood fixed and lost in thought for a good while, then with an effort gathered her wits, forced a bitter smile, made Fei the little-moustache a gesture of "pray be seated," and quietly walked away.

Here Ah Xuan still clung to Fei the little-moustache, pressing him about that case of little books. The Fourth Young Lady's attention, however, turned to a knot of young men gathered before the window: Fan Bowen, Wu Zhisheng, Du Xueshi, and one young man in foreign dress whom she did not know. They were all listening to someone tell how the rioters and mutineer soldiers had stormed the Hongchang pawnshop. The voice sounded very familiar to the Fourth Young Lady, but as she could see only the man's back she could not for the life of her think who it was. Presently he turned half to one side — a long face like a wild horse's, but with a pinched-in nose, big out-flung ears, and hair like a stiff brush. The Fourth Young Lady knew him at once for Zeng Jiaju. She all but cried out "Oh!" She detested this Zeng Jiaju above all others; and though just now, because he too had newly fled from Shuangqiao, she felt something like the warmth of meeting in a time of upheaval, still she had little wish to see him, and still less to fall into talk with him. After hesitating a while, the Fourth Young Lady went into the large dining-room, chose a chair near the door and sat down with her back to Zeng Jiaju and the rest, but pricked up her ears to listen to their talk.

"So then, you snatched a pistol from the mutineers' hands, and shot dead a few countryfolk into the bargain, and only so made your escape? Ha! You really are a marvel!"

It was Fan Bowen's voice, cold and edged with mockery.

"Just so. I'm handy enough with my fists and feet."

"But what of your honoured father? With that spirit of a man who could stand against ten thousand, as you told it just now, you ought surely to have brought your honoured father safe out of danger! How is it you saved your own single hide and nothing more? And your wife, and your son — did you take no thought for them either?"

These words of Du Xueshi's had a sharper sting still; the pair of round eyes in that cat-like face of his were raised in a way that struck fear into one.

Never dreaming that such a rebuke would come, Zeng Jiaju, who had been boasting away for half the day, could not for the life of him help looking abashed. But lying was his native gift, and he hit at once upon a most high-sounding answer:

"Oh—as for that, they're in no danger at all. Nobody knows who they are; they've only to slip off and hide with some friendly family, and there's an end of it. It's not like me, whose name is too well known in the town, where every way I turn I meet someone I know — who doesn't know the Second Young Master of the Zeng house?"

"Just so! I was about to ask the Second Young Master Zeng what high office he holds in Shuangqiao? Very likely it's 'town mayor'; anything smaller, I know you wouldn't stoop to it — am I right?"

It was Fan Bowen's caustic tone again. As he spoke he nudged Wu Zhisheng's shoulder and winked at Du Xueshi.

The other young man in foreign dress — he was Du Xueshi's nephew, Xintuo, the eldest son of Du Zhuzhai, just come back from France — stood off to one side, doing nothing but smile with a cold eye, his whole face wearing the look of one who found nothing to his liking.

This time Zeng Jiaju looked more abashed still. Hearing Fan Bowen say something about a "town mayor," he had been rather puzzled at first; blockhead though he was, without a glimmer of sense, he yet knew well enough that Shuangqiao had no such official as a "town mayor." But when he had looked Fan Bowen carefully up and down and seen a person of imposing, out-of-the-common bearing in foreign dress, he grasped at once that it must be his own narrowness of understanding, and that a man of this surname Fan would surely not be talking without some ground for it. And so he forced a smile, and, nothing daunted at the thought of blowing his own trumpet clean past all bounds, put on a grand air and hastened to answer with a solemn face:

"To be sure! It's town — town mayor. Small matters I really wouldn't touch — that goes without saying! But then, I'm this here too — No. 23!"

The last two words he brought out with special force. Nobody understood what "this" was. Luckily Zeng Jiaju had already fished two slips of paper from his pocket, one his visiting-card, the other his newly-got "party certificate." He spread these two things flat on his big black palm and passed them before the eyes of Fan Bowen and the rest, as though inviting their admiration. The "party certificate" was dirty and creased. The visiting-card, though, was brand-new, run up overnight after Zeng Jiaju had fled to the county town and spent three days there. Du Xueshi snatched it from him and was about to look it over closely when, on the other side, Fan Bowen burst into a great guffaw. Quick of eye, he had made out not only that the one slip was a party certificate, but also that the line of small print on the visiting-card ran: "Party Member No. 23, such-and-such sub-district branch, such-and-such county, such-and-such province."

Du Xueshi too had made it out, and, as if in great anger, flung the two slips of paper on the ground and cursed:

"The devil! It's just such a pack as you that have brought China to ruin!"

"Oh, come, Young Du! Don't be so wicked. To him this 'thing' is more precious than his own father, his wife, and his son!"

Wu Zhisheng, who had not said a word till now, put in his say too, shot Zeng Jiaju a scornful glance, then took Fan Bowen by the arm and went into the large dining-room. The Du uncle and nephew, left behind, followed in as well, and — bang — Young Du kicked the door shut with his foot.

These four crowded in a rush to the sofa-couch by the window at the front of the large dining-room and sat down, never seeing the Fourth Young Lady sitting alone by the door. No sooner were they seated than they laughed aloud; and through Du Xueshi's laughter ran curses still. Fan Bowen's seat happened to face the Fourth Young Lady, so he saw her first; he got up quickly, blocked the way before the other three, and said:

"Guess, all of you — who else is here?"

"There's one more, but not a person — it's that blend of poetic and love stamped on your heart and never for a moment forgotten!"

Wu Zhisheng answered without thinking. But Fan Bowen, for a wonder, did not fling back a retort; he only stepped aside, and the Fourth Young Lady, her face flushed crimson, was seen by them all. Wu Zhisheng was the first to feel awkward; he stood up and said, to cover it:

"Fourth Sister, let me introduce you — this is the son of brother-in-law Zhuzhai, Du Xintuo."

"A returned student from France, a Doctor of All Trades — he can reel silk, and keep bees too, and he's an artist, and a Bakuninist, and a—"

Fan Bowen cut in to recite off Du Xintuo's titles for him, but before he had done he burst out laughing himself.

Du Xintuo did not laugh, yet neither did he look put out; with a very easy air he bowed to the Fourth Young Lady and put out a hand. But seeing that the Fourth Young Lady's own hands stayed pressed motionless at her sides, and that her answering bow carried some little constraint, this Du Xintuo smiled gently and drew his hand back again quite naturally. He had been back in China only three days, but he had always understood what a complex society China was; and perhaps it was just this measure of understanding that led him, in his three or five years in France, to enter ten-odd schools and try his hand at every kind of study: horticulture, poultry-keeping, bee-keeping, mining, river-and-sea engineering, textiles, shipbuilding, even military chemistry, political economy, philosophy, literature, art, medicine, applied chemistry — everything, anything, he had thrown himself into with zeal for a few weeks or a few days, and that was how the elegant nickname "Doctor of All Trades" had come about; and if he had learned anything at all peculiar to himself in France, it was his own brand of Bakuninism — that view of life which "finds nothing to its liking, yet cares about nothing either" — and this, of course, was one of his "all-round" accomplishments too.

Had he ideals? His ideals were many, many. To put it more exactly, when he was lying in bed he had ideals in uncommon plenty, but the moment he was out of bed he had nothing left but that temper of his which "finds nothing to its liking, yet cares about nothing either."

He did not care to talk much, but at the same time he was truly a gentle and likeable person.

For the moment, because the Fourth Young Lady had been "discovered," those three talkative young men fell silent a while. Du Xintuo, though he did not care to snatch at talk in a crowd, had no objection, when everyone else was quiet, to saying a few words himself and warming the air a little. Smiling, he said in an offhand way:

"A man just come to Shanghai always feels this place of Shanghai to be past all imagining. Ideas of every kind and sort are all to be found in Shanghai. Take, for instance, the Monsieur Zeng out there — ah, you all find him odious, but in truth such a man is the most pitiable of all.— Fourth Aunt, you know him, of course; am I right in what I say?" It had truly never crossed the Fourth Young Lady's mind that a gentlemanly young man like this, several years her senior, should call her "Aunt," and she could not help smiling. Seeing the Fourth Young Lady smile, Fan Bowen smiled too, and gave Du Xintuo a clap on the shoulder, saying:

"My dear young Xintuo! I can hardly presume to number myself among the uncles-by-marriage."

"Joking again, Bowen!— It's just because there are too many jokers like you that China has been brought to ruin! The mere sight of that fellow Zeng puts me out of humour; the very thought of him makes me angry! Didn't I tell you all, the moment he came in, that the man was sure to be a scoundrel? And so he is! I've a good mind to thrash him. Anywhere else, I'd have thrashed him just now for certain."

Du Xueshi said it with his eyes raised and his cheeks puffed out. It was just that air of his when angry that made people laugh to see him. Fan Bowen came out at once with another witticism:

"Just so! Thrash him! You're the very man to thrash that Zeng wild-horse. For though you have an 'iron hand,' it's lucky that his is the thickest face under heaven!"

"But, Young Master Du, the second Zeng boy is odious past all bearing. Those shifty, thievish eyes.— Ah, and still there's no knowing how things stand in the town!"

The Fourth Young Lady, as if in dread that Fan Bowen and Du Xueshi would fall to quarrelling, grew anxious, and so, casting off her shy reserve, hit upon these words to turn the talk aside. And so the conversation shifted for the moment to Shuangqiao. Du Xintuo, as usual, said little, only smiling with a cold eye, though he nodded his approval too at several of Fan Bowen's shrewd sallies. On a certain point the two of them did in fact get on together. Du Xueshi was as displeased with his nephew as he was with Fan Bowen, and he cried:

"I won't have you open your mouth again, Bowen! A jumble of wrangling opinions is the great bane of China; only by putting China into a strong and mighty iron hand, and forbidding all empty talk, will there be any remedy. As for these bandit troubles — they all come of the commanders' negligence; there may even be, who knows, a mind to 'nurse the brigands to keep oneself indispensable'—"

"But everyone has to eat, and there's no help for that. The spread of banditry is no simple matter in its causes."

Wu Zhisheng was quick to gainsay him again. The opinion he had held to throughout was that unless the problem of the distribution of what was produced were solved, China, or the world, could not escape disorder.

"Just so, everyone has to eat.— Ah, it's all the sin of money. For money's sake Shuangqiao has been thrown into bandit turmoil; for money's sake the capitalists raise up factories among the fields and gardens, and black smoke blots out the sky and ruins fair Nature; and, more than all, for money's sake the peasants forsake their lovely countryside and crowd into the cities to live in filthy pigeon-coops, and the very soul of their humanity is drowned away!"

Fan Bowen was giving rein once more to his "poet's" adoration of Nature. As he spoke he glanced at the Fourth Young Lady. She did not well understand the meaning of these words of his, but that melancholy, sentimental cast of colour that flickered across Fan Bowen's face made the Fourth Young Lady take a deeper interest still, and she smiled from her heart.

Du Xueshi pursed his lips and was about to forbid Fan Bowen to speak again, when all at once someone came bursting in — it was Lin Peishan, a cosmetics bag in her hand, seemingly just back from outside. Her first words were:

"Did you all see there's a wild horse in the great drawing-room? And an Earth-God idol besides. I began to fear I'd come to the wrong house!"

They all burst out laughing. Lin Peishan swung her waist round in a half-circle, and, seeing that Fan Bowen was here, and Du Xueshi too, her liveliness all at once vanished; biting her lip in a faint smile, she swept like a breath of fresh wind across the large dining-room and went out by the door at the back.

She ran upstairs again and burst straight into her sister's room. The second, inner curtain of pale-blue satin had been drawn too, and the room was pitch-dark. Lin Peishan pressed the electric button on the wall, and a flood of light startled the Wu young mistress out of the reverie in which she lay reclined upon the sofa.

The two sisters looked at each other a moment and said nothing. Then suddenly Lin Peishan skipped forward, half-knelt before the sofa-couch, put her arms about the Wu young mistress's powdered neck, and cried in a hurried, low voice:

"Sister, Sister! He — he — spoke to me today! What am I to do?"

The Wu young mistress did not understand her sister's meaning, and turned to look hard at that face of hers, which seemed at once flurried and troubled.

"Why, Bowen!— he says he loves me!"

"Then do you love him, or don't you, after all?"

"I?— I don't know!"

The Wu young mistress could not help laughing. She gave her head a shake, shaking off one of Lin Peishan's hands, and was about to say something when Peishan added:

"I feel that every single person is lovable, and none of them lovable."

"Don't talk nonsense!"

"Isn't it so?"

"It may be so, but you can't think that way. For you must marry in the end — you must fix on one man — one man, to be the companion of your whole life."

Lin Peishan fell silent. She tilted her head and thought a moment, then stood up and said languidly:

"Always to be with one and the same person — how dull! Look at you, you and brother-in-law!"

The Wu young mistress gave a start of alarm, and the colour changed in her face. Two things rolled from her side onto the carpet before the sofa: a tattered copy of The Sorrows of Young Werther, and a withered white rose. The Wu young mistress's eyes followed and fixed on these two things, and she gazed at them spellbound, while the gnawing anguish that Lin Peishan had broken off for the moment now worked at her and gripped her twice as stubbornly as before.

"You said brother-in-law doesn't approve of Bowen, didn't you?"

Lin Peishan asked at last, but in a tone as though she were discussing someone else's affair.

The Wu young mistress forced down the vexation churning in her heart and lifted her face to look at her sister; after a while she answered at last:

"It's because he has already found someone better than Bowen."

"The Du Xueshi you spoke of before?"

"And your own mind on it?"

"I don't know."

Hearing yet another "I don't know," and seeing too a strange flicker in her sister's eyes, the Wu young mistress supposed the girl was only shy, and could not help laughing, and pressed her softly:

"Won't you tell even your sister the truth? One word will do."

"I fancy that if I married Young Du, I should certainly still be pining in my heart for someone else—"

Here Lin Peishan paused, and there was a touch of excitement in her face. The Wu young mistress, hearing such words, could not keep her heart from pounding. But Lin Peishan of a sudden broke into a fit of giggling, turned away, and said, as if to herself:

"Married to this one, and pining in one's heart for another,— oh, oh, what a hateful thing that is! Sister! Sister!"

So crying, Lin Peishan sprang over again, laid both hands on her sister's shoulders, and, like a little girl, pressed her own face to her sister's. The Wu young mistress's face was hot as fire! Lin Peishan started back a step in astonishment, and saw that her sister's face was not only flushed with an under-tinge of green, but that glistening teardrops hung at her lashes besides. Lin Peishan looked on in alarm, unable to bring out half a word. Little by little the Wu young mistress's face turned again to a dreadful pallor. Through the sheen of her tears she saw that this sister standing before her was plainly the very image of her own self before she married: the same face and figure, the same innocence and liveliness with its touch of fancy, and standing, likewise, on the very edge of the pit of "the life of contradiction." Could it be that the two sisters must share the selfsame fate as well?— so the Wu young mistress thought in her grief. In a trembling voice she burst out with a question:

"Shan! Who is it you're pining for in your heart? Bowen, is it?"

"Not he either. I don't know! Sister, I want to cry!— I only want to cry!"

Lin Peishan suddenly threw her arms about the Wu young mistress and spoke hurriedly, her voice a little atremble too; but she did not cry, only gave one strange cry, then all at once let go, laughed once, and went skipping and bounding out again.

The Wu young mistress stared wide-eyed at the blue door-curtain swaying on the doorway, her mouth wide open as if she would cry out, yet no sound came; two great teardrops broke at last from her eyes and fell upon her hand. Then she bowed her head again to look at the tattered book and the withered rose on the carpet, and a wave of grief past all resisting wrung her tender heart in two; she flung herself down on the sofa-couch, and amid her bewildered moaning asked herself in despair:

"Shan? Can Shan take my place?— She cannot? She has someone in her heart, then? Ah, my foolish, doting heart!— They say the gunfire on the Longhai line is fierce; if he's killed, then it's all over! All over!— And yet, and yet, didn't he say he was coming back to Shanghai soon? Oh! I dread to see him! Oh, oh, forgive me, let me go! Let me hide away somewhere!"
