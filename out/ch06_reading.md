## Identity

In the dead of night, beams of light crossed and recrossed the sky above the Songhu Garrison Command, sweeping past one another like the blades of a pair of scissors. After the Japanese invaded Shanghai the year before and launched the Shanghai war, the Command had hurriedly fitted itself out with anti-aircraft searchlights. The lockup's watchtower had one too, and every so often it grazed the high wall of the cellblock. The harsh light came through the narrow window, and in an instant the inside of the cell was lit, and in an instant went dark again.

Liang Shichao had picked up a habit in the army: on reaching an unfamiliar place he would always look about him, taking the measure of his surroundings from every side first of all. The men's cells were ranged side by side in three rows; one side of the third row faced straight onto the boundary wall, and just now it was very quiet. From the cells across the corridor came an occasional snore, and now and then a man would start awake from a dream and cry out a few times.

He looked at the several comrades in the cell, and his heart was heavy with worry. That morning he had left the clinic together with Dr. Qin, walking some way behind. Dr. Qin was a refined, composed man, who went neither fast nor slow. As they fell back from the market he had worried whether Dr. Qin would get clear; in the end it was he himself who had failed to get away, and he had no idea now how the doctor fared.

A year and more before, Liang Shichao had been wounded in the fight against the encirclement and suppression, and had come from the Soviet area to Shanghai to be treated; Qin Chuan'an was the doctor who had tended his wound. Once healed, he had been assigned by the organization, for the time being, to work with the local Party body, and so had stayed on to help at the clinic.

Under questioning in the daytime he had lied to the enemy, saying that he had once been a soldier in the Nineteenth Route Army, that he had served for years under Brigade Commander Weng, that he had been gravely wounded holding off the Japanese at Zhabei in the ‘January 28,’ and that, being under treatment in a Shanghai hospital, he had not moved out with his unit when it was posted elsewhere. That Captain You had half believed him and half not; he had gone off and made a circuit, and come back with a cigarette between his fingers, and then had the warder march him back to his cell. Would this Captain You credit his story as easily as all that?

For two days the enemy had questioned them by turns, pressing them on who had called the meeting, who the several men were who had fled, and why they had gathered in that place. But this afternoon the interrogation had changed its tune: Captain You had shifted his interest from the dice to the pai gow. Had the enemy got hold of some new circumstance, and was he muddling them on purpose?

They had all said they had come to play cards—but where was the money? True, Old Fang had told each of them to bring a little extra cash, and they had; yet empty every pocket and put it all together, and it came to no more than a hundred-odd silver dollars. For so paltry a sum, why go to a hidden room in a library to play cards? The International Settlement might make a show of cracking down on gambling, but everyone knew that even the police themselves were fond of a wager. Liang Shichao saw clearly that they would never believe this story. Most baffling of all was that Old Fang—who had arranged the meeting and told everyone to come—had, after all, not appeared at the appointed hour.

Lin Shi was badly hurt. He had taken a bullet in the right leg when he was seized, and for most of these two days had been half unconscious—which, for the moment, kept him relatively safe: in the interrogation room he was liable to pass out at any time, so that no sooner had the enemy dragged him out than the warder would haul him back to the cell.

Even as he turned over in his mind every detail of that day—from before the meeting to the moment the agents burst in to make the arrests—Lin Shi was watching the other three men in the cell.

Chen Qianyuan had come back from his first questioning covered in injuries. Lin Shi guessed that the enemy, seeing how young he was and reckoning that he had not been long in underground work and might know no important secret, had simply made an example of him, beating him and beating him again, in the belief that dragging him back to the cell would frighten the others.

Back in the cell though he was, Chen Qianyuan could not calm himself; the moment the warder stepped away he would stand at the cell door and peer out, plainly in great anxiety. He must be worrying, Lin Shi thought, about that young woman comrade—most likely his sweetheart; the two of them had gone into the market and up the stairs together. All the way, escorted from Baiyunguan to Longhua, the pair had kept close by each other.

The women's cells lay near the first row of the men's, over against the boundary wall on the other side; their little window faced the men's cells, but three rows of buildings stood between it and the third row where the men were.

“What can you see, standing like that?” Liang Shichao went to the cell door and helped Chen Qianyuan back to sit down on the edge of the bed.

Yi Junnian had likely been given the electric torture; he had come back without a word, but there were plain burns on his wrists and ankles. In the first interrogation Captain You had asked Lin Shi whether Yi Junnian had thrown the dice from his pocket onto the table, and Lin Shi had said he had not seen it. Then Captain You had asked: well, did you see Yi Junnian afterward put the dice into his pocket? Lin Shi answered him that he had never seen any second pair of dice at all—that in that room he had seen dice only the once, and that was the pair Captain You himself had fished out of his own pocket.

Back from the questioning, Yi Junnian sat like this against the wall on a few reed mats, and Lin Shi kept studying the man. When the enemy had rushed in, he had seen Yi Junnian snatch the dice from the table and slip them into his pocket—so Yi Junnian certainly knew about the dice. Just how many people knew? Captain You knew about the dice too, and Lin Shi had understood then and there: the organization had been penetrated from within.

At first only Old Fang had known about the dice—yet he had not come to the meeting. Liang Shichao had said, once: of all this, it is Old Fang who knows the most. No one took up his words. It was a long while before Yi Junnian said that Old Fang could not possibly be the trouble. Yi Junnian seldom spoke, which was no wonder: a seasoned comrade, once inside the enemy's prison, was generally rather silent.

Why had Old Fang not come to the meeting? Lin Shi turned the question over for a long time, but, like Yi Junnian, he was unwilling to suspect any comrade lightly.

Lin Shi ran through in his mind the people who had come to the meeting. There was one man Yi Junnian had greeted, calling him Old Wei. Before the agents burst into the meeting place, this Old Wei had been very much on edge, urging everyone to get the meeting started. And later, when they fell back, it was he again who was first out of the room and made good his escape. It was as though he had second sight.

“Tell me—why in the end did Old Fang not come to the meeting?” Liang Shichao asked Chen Qianyuan.

“Maybe he got word that the agents had learned where the meeting was?” Chen Qianyuan ventured.

“Then oughtn't he to have warned the rest of us?” Liang Shichao had a notion of his own. “What do you say—could Old Fang have been arrested?”

The cell fell silent.

Lin Shi stirred, and Yi Junnian rose and went over to look at him, and to check his wound. “How are you? Feeling any better?”

“I feel cold all over; the wound's gone septic.” Yi Junnian had been solicitous about his injury all along, but Lin Shi did not want anyone to know how badly he was hurt.

Yi Junnian felt his forehead. “You're too weak; get some more sleep.” Then he took off his padded gown and laid it over Lin Shi, and turning to the other two said: “Mind how you talk in here—the walls have ears.”

Lin Shi did find it strange: the Judge Advocate's office had so many cells, and the people held in them were always a mixed lot—why put them all in together? Was it to make a setting in which they would talk among themselves?

“What day did Old Fang tell you about the meeting?” Liang Shichao asked Chen Qianyuan again.

“The afternoon of the day before. He came hurrying over to make contact, and the moment he'd said his piece he was off again, saying he had others still to notify. He told us one by one; he knew perfectly well about Dong Huiwen and me, the two of us, yet even so he notified us separately. It was only when the two of us met on the eve of the meeting that we learned we were bound for the same place the next day.”

“Now that I think of it, why did Old Fang tell me about the dice at all?” Liang Shichao said, as if to himself.

Seeing the two of them turn to look at him, Yi Junnian said: “The very first day I was transferred to Shanghai I made contact with Old Fang, and for these three years I've worked alongside him the whole time. Even if the rest of you doubt him, I trust him still. That he did not reach the meeting place that day—there must be a reason for it. The situation is extremely tangled; we must trust that the organization will get to the truth of it sooner or later. When he came to notify me of the meeting, he came straight to my painting-and-scroll shop; he knows the place well. Were there really something wrong with him, I'd have been arrested long ago—there'd have been no need to wait till today.

“Still, now that you bring up the dice, I find it a little odd myself. By rights, since he was to come to the meeting himself, there was no need to tell everyone about it; yet that day he told me too. So I think perhaps he already foresaw that something unexpected might happen the next day, and told us all in advance how to make contact with the man sent down from above, in case he could not reach the meeting place in time.”

A fresh thought struck him: “It's as well he didn't come, didn't hold the meeting on time. Otherwise, the moment the comrade sent from above declared who he was and announced the secret task—if it's as you say, and there really is an enemy's spy within—then great harm would truly have been done.”

“And there's no knowing what task the higher-ups meant to give us, after all.”

Once more Yi Junnian stopped them from carrying the discussion further. In a cell they ought never to have touched on secret work at all. He changed the subject, and asked Chen Qianyuan what his trade was.

“The International News Agency—I translate and edit wire dispatches for them.”

“You know a foreign tongue, you can translate—that's no small thing.” Yi Junnian said admiringly. “You're sure to do important work for the Party one day.”

“I'm too young.”

“What does youth matter? Many young comrades have long held important posts of leadership. And you, then?” Yi Junnian looked toward Lin Shi.

“I work at a bank.”

“I've been a soldier.” Liang Shichao put in.

“Wei Dafu is a street-runner for a house-letting agency, and I keep a painting-and-scroll shop. To throw us all together—this task is out of the ordinary.”

Lin Shi thought to himself: this Yi Junnian—on the one hand telling everyone not to discuss secret work, on the other raising the very subject himself. His curiosity ran deep, and that in itself made Lin Shi curious.

“My guess is that the comrade sent down from above either never reached the meeting, or is among those who fled from it.” Chen Qianyuan spoke his mind even as the thought came to him.

The warder came to the cell door and rapped with his baton on the little window in it. “No talking!”

In fact Liang Shichao had yet another question in his mind. He himself had taken gunshot wounds, and more than once. When the Judge Advocate's office had sent for the Command's medical officer to change Lin Shi's dressing, he had leaned in to have a look at the wound. The bullet had passed clean through the side of the calf and out the other side, tearing away a great patch of muscle. The raw wound was large, but it had been dealt with in fair time—a doctor had been found while they were still at the Municipal Police station. Liang Shichao judged that the gunshot wound was not really so grave, and when he felt the man's body it was not especially hot to the touch. Why should Lin Shi feign so severe an injury?

And this owner of the painting-and-scroll shop—why did he keep stopping them from discussing the matter of Old Fang? The man was in truth much interested himself; it was he who had raised the topic in the first place, yet he had quickly shut his mouth, and a little while later, on the contrary, was urging everyone to be careful and not to talk loosely. Underground work was a sore trial to the mind; this part of the revolutionary work really did not suit him, Liang Shichao felt.

“Of the two women comrades who were arrested, one is called Ling Wen, a well-known woman writer. Her husband died a martyr in Guangzhou. The other woman comrade I don't know.” Yi Junnian turned his head and glanced at Chen Qianyuan.

“Huiwen teaches at a primary school.”

“After the Guangzhou Uprising, far too many comrades died.” Liang Shichao asked Yi Junnian suddenly: “You worked in Guangzhou too?”

“How did you know that?”

“That fellow surnamed You said, when he questioned me, that of the whole netful hauled in, three had all been to Guangzhou—there was bound to be something in it.”

“So Lin Shi worked in Guangzhou as well? And to think none of us ever met.” Yi Junnian gave a faint smile.

Outside the window the searchlight beams swept back and forth, and weariness came over him in waves along with the pain of his wounds. Chen Qianyuan strained to recall whether, when he had gone out that morning, he had hidden away the translation manuscript spread on his desk. If he could come out of the Longhua lockup alive, he hoped he might finish rendering the book. Drifting in and out, he called to mind those still-unproofed lines:

…There are no miracles in nature or history, but every abrupt turn in history, and this applies to every revolution, presents such a wealth of content, unfolds such unexpected and specific combinations of forms of struggle and alignment of forces of the contestants, that to the lay mind there is much that must appear miraculous…
