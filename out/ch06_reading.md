Chen Geng understood deeply that pushing into the enemy's detective organs to wrest away intelligence was the underground Party's most effective means of countering the enemy's plots and sabotage, a move of the greatest importance in the struggle on the hidden front. Without such a struggle the Party could not survive in the territory the enemy ruled, and would surely be broken by him. To get inside the enemy's detective organs in Shanghai there were two sides to work: one was the Kuomintang's police and gendarmerie and its municipal party office; the other was the Concession police stations that the imperialists had set up. And so, after investigation and study, Chen Geng established the first counter-espionage asset, Yang Dengying, inside the enemy's professional anti-Communist command organ, and thereby opened the way to reach deep into the enemy's vital departments and gain control over Shanghai's enemy detective organs.

### Yang Dengying, the "Japan Hand"

The building of the Yang Dengying relationship went fairly smoothly.

When Chen Geng was transferred into the Central Special Section as intelligence chief in April 1928, Chen Lifu, within the Central Organization Department of the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters, was just then preparing to set up a Party Affairs Investigation Section. This was the forerunner of the notorious "CC Clique" secret-service organization, which used party investigation as its cover to carry on counter-revolutionary secret-service work, oppose the Soviet Union and the Communists, suppress the people's resistance, and slaughter members of the Chinese Communist Party and other revolutionaries.

When this Kuomintang secret-service body began to take shape, it set about building a detective apparatus in Shanghai and casting about for suitable men. The Kuomintang had as yet no experience of secret-service work and no seasoned cadres for it, and it took great pains to find someone to head the Shanghai secret-service organ.

To this end Chen Lifu and Yang Jianhong came to Shanghai, and on the pretext of "handling cases" made wide contact with the Kuomintang's party, government, military, and police organs there while also visiting relatives and friends, actively looking for agents. After canvassing many quarters, Yang Jianhong picked his fellow townsman Yang Dengying to build the Shanghai detective organ.

Yang Dengying, whose original name was Bao Junfu and who also went by Liu Junshan, was born in 1901 in Zhongshan county, Guangdong. From boyhood he studied in Japan, finishing middle school in Tokyo and then graduating from Waseda University, and returned to China in 1919. He was fluent in Japanese and moved in wide circles; he had many Japanese friends, dealings with a number of Japanese businessmen, had worked as a clerk in a Japanese trading house, and was on close terms with several Japanese news agencies, so that in ordinary eyes he was a "Japan hand." In Japan he had come across Marxist writings, and during the May Thirtieth Movement he went often to the Shanghai General Labor Union; later he made friends among some of our comrades and took part in the activities of the Kuomintang left. It was then that he met at the Shanghai General Labor Union the head of the foreign-affairs union, Yang Jianhong; both being Guangdong men, they hit it off at once and grew very close. Yang Jianhong soon left Shanghai for Guangzhou, where he attached himself to the Kuomintang right and became a trusted man of Chen Lifu's. In October 1925 Yang Dengying went to Guangzhou to look up Yang Jianhong and came to know a good many Kuomintang right-wingers. In 1927 Yang Jianhong returned to Shanghai and would often drop in at Yang Dengying's home. By the spring of 1928 Yang Dengying's dealings with the Kuomintang had grown closer still. Early that year, while Zhang Daofan (later the second head of the Investigation Section after Chen Lifu) and Yang Jianhong were setting up this Party Affairs Investigation Section, they often came to Shanghai to stir up anti-Communist work and were on close terms with Yang Dengying, keeping nothing from him. Whenever Zhang Daofan came to Shanghai he too would eat, drink, and carouse with Yang Dengying, and they threw in together. Once he knew them, Yang Dengying courted them the more eagerly, doing all he could for them to win their favor. Being clever, with a knack for detective work, outwardly biddable and inwardly sharp, he soon won their trust.

Between Yang Dengying and Zhang Daofan there was also a murky special tie. In the factional struggles within the Kuomintang there had been a rival Zhang Daofan could not get past: so long as the man lived, Zhang Daofan could not rise, and at Zhang Daofan's request Yang Dengying had done the rival away, so that Zhang Daofan was able to climb to a high post in the Kuomintang. With this handle on him, Yang Dengying knew that if Zhang Daofan ever crossed him, he could expose Zhang's whole secret. In his private life, too, Yang Dengying had in 1928 helped Zhang Daofan bring a Frenchwoman (Chinese name Guo Shuyuan) to Shanghai and had managed the affair of Zhang's marriage to her on his behalf, winning his goodwill. Yang Dengying kept a hold on this woman, and through her had a grasp of Zhang Daofan's movements and many of his secrets.

For all these reasons Zhang Daofan trusted Yang Dengying completely and granted his every request, and became his protector within the Kuomintang.

Yang Dengying also knew some Communists and showed sympathy for the Communist Party; when we had something to ask of him, he would attend to it with all his heart. After the Spring Festival of 1928 Chen Yangshan moved into Yang Dengying's home on North Sichuan Road. That March Yang Jianhong told Yang Dengying that the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters in Nanjing had set up an Investigation Section, that it now meant to build a detective apparatus in Shanghai in the name of the Central Party Headquarters, and that it wanted Yang Dengying to take part in handling cases; Yang Dengying agreed. The next day Yang Jianhong invited Yang Dengying to his home, introduced him to Chen Lifu, and told Chen Lifu that Yang Dengying was a Japan hand. They all went off together to a Japanese restaurant to eat. There Chen Lifu and Yang Jianhong set out their aim more fully. Yang Jianhong said: "The main thing is to find a way to stamp out the activity of the various 'reactionary party elements' inside the Shanghai Concessions, and above all the Communists." He went on: "Every time we send an official letter to the Concessions to handle a case, the paperwork usually drags on so long that secrets leak. What we must work out now is a way to open this channel, simplify the procedure, and guard the secrets strictly." Chen Lifu said: "First we must make contact with all kinds of social bodies, organs, and schools and gather every sort of intelligence material." At the time Yang Dengying's mind was in some conflict. Anything else he could do, but to be a detective—if he did not tell the Communists, he would be failing his friends, and moreover, without the Communists' help he would get nothing done and would even risk his life. If he did a little something for the Communists and picked up a bit of Communist news to fob off the secret service, he could hold a Kuomintang post and yet run no risk.

After turning it over and over, he told Chen Yangshan the whole of it—how Yang Jianhong had twice pressed him to become a detective, to spy in Shanghai on the Communists and other anti-Chiang parties, to set up a case-handling apparatus, and to build ties with the Concession authorities—and said he was willing to supply us with whatever intelligence he came to know. He also handed Chen Yangshan a wanted-list he carried on him, and said that from then on he would supply the intelligence he learned to the Communists promptly.

Chen Yangshan felt that Yang Dengying's attitude was sincere and that the news he offered was of real value, and he wrote a report to the Center asking for instructions (sent by way of the Zhabei district committee), proposing that a working relationship be built with Yang Dengying. Zhou Enlai studied Chen Yangshan's report to the Center and judged that an asset like Yang Dengying, though politically not very reliable, was greatly needed in the hidden struggle, that the Central Special Section had precisely to open up work of this kind, and that Yang could be given more political education and more Party education so as to be made to work for us. He decided there and then to use Yang Dengying, and sent Chen Geng to meet Chen Yangshan and settle that henceforth Chen Yangshan would cut off his ties with all other quarters and be in sole-line contact with Chen Geng, responsible for the liaison work with Yang Dengying.

On receiving Zhou Enlai's instruction, Chen Geng first arranged a talk with Chen Yangshan to learn Yang Dengying's circumstances in detail, then sought out Yang Dengying for a talk to learn directly of his ties with the Kuomintang. In working out how the work should go forward, Chen Geng pointed out to Yang Dengying that, besides keeping up his contact with the Kuomintang Investigation Section, he should also build ties with the Kuomintang's Shanghai municipal party office, the Shanghai municipal government, and the Songhu Garrison Command. Finally Chen Geng asked Yang Dengying what he needed. Yang put it thus: mixing with these Kuomintang agents and party bullies took money; and what was he to do if the Kuomintang ever demanded Communist intelligence from him? Chen Geng answered him point by point and largely met his requests. Two weeks later Chen Geng met Yang Dengying again to confer and deploy the work; at the same time it was settled that Chen Yangshan would transfer to the Central Special Section's intelligence branch, and Lian Desheng was assigned instead to keep contact with Yang Dengying.

From then on Yang Dengying was led directly by Chen Geng, and became the first counter-espionage asset our Party built inside an enemy detective organ.

And so the enemy's professional anti-Communist detective organ in Shanghai, from its very start, worked under Chen Geng's control. In line with our Party's requirements, and to suit the Kuomintang's aims as well, Yang Dengying drew up an "anti-Communist" plan.

### Lampson: "The Police Wish to Deal with No One but Yang Dengying"

The Central Special Section's guidance of Yang Dengying's work, and his winning of the enemy's trust as he pushed deep into the enemy detective organs, passed through roughly three stages.

The first stage, from May to October 1928, was the early period of Yang Dengying's activity after he built ties with the intelligence branch.

In May 1928, on Chen Geng's instruction, Yang Dengying courted Chen Lifu and Zhang Daofan hard, built up personal friendships, and won their goodwill, biddably carrying out the assignments Yang Jianhong set him. There was reason in Chen Geng's having Yang Dengying do this: he knew the enemy was still testing Yang Dengying. Just at this time Yang Jianhong took Yang Dengying to see the Kuomintang Songhu garrison commander Qian Dajun, to arrange the paperwork for a request that the British police send men to seize Communists. Yang Jianhong told Yang Dengying nothing beforehand; not until the two of them, together with detectives of the British police, went to Marsh's New Village to make the arrests did Yang Dengying learn the address of the raid. This time the people staying there had already moved, and the raid came up empty. Uneasy, Chen Lifu even went to the spot to inspect it himself. That summer Yang Jianhong again drew Yang Dengying into a raid on our Party's Pingliang Road organ, again keeping it strictly secret from him beforehand, so that he knew nothing.

To strengthen his leadership of Yang Dengying, Chen Geng met him often, worked hard to win him politically, and had him use his ties with Chen Lifu, Zhang Daofan, and Yang Jianhong to make bold contact with the relevant enemy figures and carry on his work. Chen Geng reckoned that to deal with the Kuomintang secret service Yang Dengying had to be given some Communist documents to hand over. With approval obtained, Chen Geng sent Yang Dengying, issue by issue and ahead of anyone else, our Party's declarations, handbills, and open publications such as Red Flag and Bolshevik, as capital for him to approach the enemy and win the enemy's trust; at times he also gave him a little intelligence about the Party's activity, true and false mixed together, to throw the enemy off. Besides this he gave Yang Dengying a sum of activity funds for entertaining. By now Yang Dengying, with Yang Jianhong's consent, had set up a secret contact point on Range Road. To get the intelligence in Yang Dengying's hands in time and to protect him, Chen Geng further assigned Lian Desheng to stay at Yang Dengying's side in the guise of his bodyguard.

Lian Desheng was a native of Shangyu county, Zhejiang. His family was poor from his childhood, his father died early, and in his teens he fled with his mother to Shanghai to make a living. Before he was twenty he had spent several years as an apprentice in a small shop, and then went to work as a conductor for the British Tramway Company in Shanghai. Poor as his family was, he loved to help others; when a friend was in trouble he would give generously and warmly, and he was much respected by the workers at the British Tramway Company, where the mention of "Conductor No. 224" drew nothing but praise. He joined the Chinese Communist Party before the May Thirtieth Movement, and during that movement was one of the leaders of the tramway workers' strike, taking part with Gu Shunzhang and others in putting down the scabs who broke the strike. Once, when all Shanghai's tram workers struck and the whole city's trams stood still, a single trolley-bus whose driver and conductor had been bought by the capitalists refused to strike and kept running; one night Lian Desheng and Gu Shunzhang, four men in all, sprang armed onto that car and forced it to stop at once. After "April 12" Lian Desheng could not go back to his old unit, and the Party sent him to study at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow; he had just returned from the Soviet Union and been assigned to the Central Special Section. He went with Yang Dengying to the various detective organs, often calling at the Kuomintang Songhu Garrison Command, the public security bureau, the municipal party office, and the British and French Concession police stations and other secret-service organs. Whenever Yang Dengying obtained urgent intelligence, he handed it to Lian Desheng to carry to the Central Special Section. To outward appearance Lian Desheng looked very much a "bodyguard," burly and uncommonly strong; but what fitted him still better for the work was that he was alert and quick, bold yet careful, and good at coping with every sort of situation. Besides passing on Yang Dengying's intelligence, he used his special standing to gather a good deal of intelligence of his own. When a Party secret organ came under sudden attack, he would take up a post at the door and pretend to keep watch; if a comrade who did not know came up to make contact, he would signal our man to walk away, and so he rescued many comrades. Only Yang Dengying, fond of putting on official airs and quick-tempered, would treat Lian Desheng—sent by the Party to help his work—as a subordinate to order about as he pleased, flying into a rage over nothing. Lian Desheng did his utmost to restrain himself and avoid a clash. In private he said to the comrades: "For the Party's work I will bear even the greatest anger. If I fell out with him, how would I answer to the Party?" For all he had to put up with, he did the work superbly.

At this point the Kuomintang wanted Yang Dengying to build close ties with the police stations, so as to have their help in seizing people at any time; and Chen Geng too asked him to gather every kind of intelligence at the police stations. Through his case work Yang Dengying built a working relationship with the British police political branch—its Western chief detective, Lampson, and the assistant Tan Shaoliang (a Guangdong man he had known all along). The British police, aware that Yang Dengying was on close terms with the Songhu Garrison Command, were themselves keen to use him. On this footing Chen Geng, besides working carefully on Yang Dengying's thinking, took the initiative in helping him with ideas and schemes, building up his nerve for approaching the enemy and his capacity for the work. In August, Tan Shaoliang asked Yang Dengying to find out for him whether two foremen from the docks were being held at the Songhu Garrison Command. Yang Dengying went straight to Qian Dajun to inquire. Qian Dajun confirmed there were indeed two such men and asked what the police wished to do about them; Yang Dengying gave no answer. To his surprise, when Yang Dengying returned to the police station, Tan Shaoliang told him the two men had already been released. On the Englishmen's orders Tan Shaoliang produced two hundred yuan for him, which Yang Dengying declined to take; with a faint smile he said, "You have the wrong man!" The affair made a deep impression on the Englishmen. Later Lampson and Tan Shaoliang asked Yang Dengying to get them a set of the Songhu Garrison Command's seal impressions. Yang Dengying reported it to Chen Geng, who told him to find some way to serve their wishes and win their trust. So through the garrison command's deputy adjutant-general Mao Naigong, Yang Dengying obtained the full set of seal impressions of all the command's departments and its detective squad. Lampson, seeing these, prized them like treasure and was deeply grateful; his verdict was: "Yang Dengying is not one of the garrison command's men, but he can make the command's men do as he says." He also let it be known: "The police wish to deal with no one but Yang Dengying." Of course, what made Lampson prize Yang Dengying so particularly was that he was not only a high Kuomintang official with the run of every door but also a rare "Japan hand." From the leaking of the Tanaka Memorial in 1927, Japan had been preparing to launch its war of aggression against China. To that end, Japanese intelligence organs of every system were swarming through Shanghai. At the time Japan had many troops stationed on North Sichuan Road, and the Englishmen felt keenly threatened; the friction between Britain and Japan in Shanghai was sharp. When Lampson sought Yang Dengying's judgment on the inner workings of the Japanese intelligence organs and on the outlook for Sino-Japanese and Anglo-Japanese relations, Yang Dengying would tell it off as if reciting his own household stores, laying out the intelligence systems of the Japanese government and its financial combines one by one; his broad knowledge and sure judgment left Lampson won over. To match himself against the aggressive Japanese, Lampson badly needed the help of a Japan expert like Yang Dengying, fluent in Japanese.

Through this period Chen Geng, by way of his tie with Yang Dengying, could learn promptly each time the enemy detected the location of one of our Party's organs, warn the Party organization at once so that the comrades concerned could move in time, and so keep the Party's organizations safe from the enemy.

In October 1928 Yang Dengying was formally appointed by Chen Lifu as the Kuomintang Central Government's special commissioner resident in Shanghai. The appointment was signed by Chiang Kai-shek: "Yang Dengying is specially deputed to assist in handling and disposing of important cases in Shanghai. By this order."

### Chiang Kai-shek's Appointed "Central Special Commissioner in Shanghai"

The second stage, from October 1928 to the end of 1929, was the period of Yang Dengying's activity under Chen Geng's guidance.

In this period Yang Jianhong, who had once embezzled a large sum at Hankou, was informed on by Sun Ke, and after being transferred out of the Kuomintang Investigation Section he took poison and killed himself. At the same time Zhang Daofan and Ye Xiufeng resigned in turn as heads of the Kuomintang Investigation Section, and Xu Enzeng took over as director. Being busy with internal affairs and with the Kuomintang's factional struggles, they left Yang Dengying's work unquestioned. Yang Dengying, meanwhile, under the signboard of the Kuomintang "Central Special Commissioner," in fact operated under the direction of the Central Special Section, moving on his own among Shanghai's Kuomintang military and police organs and the imperialists' detective organs, all but free of anyone's control. The one man he kept close ties with was Chen Lifu. Though he handled no cases himself for the time being, Yang Dengying represented the Kuomintang in sitting as an "assessor" at the Concession courts. Following the spirit of Chen Geng's instructions, he often went to Chen Lifu to settle matters, and Chen Lifu always granted his requests. When Yang Dengying, at loose ends, would drift over to Nanjing, Chen Lifu and Zhang Daofan always received him warmly. Each time Yang Dengying came back to Shanghai from Nanjing, he would boast to the various police and detective organs, as though he had brought some new assignment from Nanjing, so the police and detective organs all took him for a "man trusted by the Center," which added considerable convenience to his work.

Yang Dengying gradually built up fairly deep ties within the British police station. He became a welcome guest of Lampson's and grew very familiar with Lampson's household. His tie with Tan Shaoliang was closer still—both being Guangdong men, in their dealings they became bosom friends. Every day after making his rounds of the Kuomintang military and police organs, Yang Dengying would go to Tan Shaoliang's to chat and eat together, and in the evenings they would go to the Yipinxiang Hotel to smoke opium, lying side by side and talking of everything. So the imperialists too made frequent use of him, and Lampson often asked him along to help make arrests. Lampson took him for an expert and told him everything. Yang Dengying could see all the intelligence and material Lampson had; when Lampson met a serious, knotty case he would consult Yang Dengying; he even asked him to serve as adviser to the British and French police stations. In this way, through Yang Dengying's ties, we could keep a basic grasp of the British and French police stations.

Although at this time Yang Dengying had not yet had dealings with Xu Enzeng, the new head of the Nanjing Investigation Section, he learned a great deal of the enemy's affairs from the Kuomintang military and police organs, and especially from the British police. Several times the enemy's raiding plans came up empty.

Chen Geng conferred with the comrades concerned: to cover Yang Dengying's work, we would sometimes deliberately leave an organ in place, or set up an organ for the enemy to smash, so that the enemy would not grow suspicious of him.

To raise Yang Dengying's standing, Chen Geng obtained the Central Special Committee's approval to buy him a motorcar. In October 1929 an office was set up for him in an over-street building opposite Dade Li on North Sichuan Road, to serve as a clearing-house for intelligence, and formally hung out the sign "Shanghai Office of the Kuomintang Central Investigation Section." Chen Geng assigned An E (Zhang Honghui, also called Zhang Yuying) to act as its secretary; Yang Dengying brought all the intelligence he gathered to the office, and whatever was of use to us An E copied out and sent to Chen Geng promptly. Yang Dengying now had a formal organ for outside liaison, convenient for making contact with detectives and informers. Besides sending over the Party's various open publications promptly, the Special Section went on giving him some not-very-secret internal Party documents, so that he had capital for boasting of his exploits to the enemy.

### Xu Enzeng Puts Yang Dengying in Full Charge of Major Cases

The third stage, from the end of 1929 until April 1931, when Yang Dengying was arrested because the traitor Gu Shunzhang gave him up, was the period when Yang Dengying, active under Chen Geng's direct leadership, came into the confidence of Xu Enzeng, director of the Kuomintang Central Investigation Section. By this time Yang Dengying had changed to a new French-made motorcar, had bought tax-free cars for the Shanghai Kuomintang public security chief Chen Xizeng and for Chen Guofu and Chen Lifu as well, and had shipped the two cars for "the two Chens" to Nanjing.

At the end of 1929, Xu Enzeng, Chen Lifu's trusted man and the new director of the Kuomintang Central Investigation Section, following Chen Lifu's wishes, came in person to Shanghai to call at Yang Dengying's home, and from then on Yang Dengying enjoyed Xu Enzeng's confidence too. Xu Enzeng handed the major Shanghai cases that Chen Lifu passed down to him directly to Yang Dengying to handle. Yang Dengying's ties with the Kuomintang military and police organs, and especially with the British detective organs, grew closer still, and so Shanghai's enemy detective organs were, through Yang Dengying, brought further under the Special Section's control. From then on Xu Enzeng, following Chen Lifu's intent, would often come to Yang Dengying's home to call, and not only told him the address of the Party Affairs Investigation Section's secret office—"No. 5 Zhongshan East Road, Nanjing"—but also sent men to stay regularly at his North Sichuan Road office. Though Xu Enzeng often wrote or wired Yang Dengying with instructions, he never once inspected his work. Xu Enzeng even told Yang Dengying: "In matters of money the Center [meaning Chen Lifu] always defers to you." And he sent him two copies of the Family Letters of Zeng Guofan, by way of drawing him in.

The British imperialists too made much of Yang Dengying. Lampson told him: "If there is a truly urgent case, you need not go through arrest procedure; treat the man as caught in the act and seize him whenever you like." This was one of the reasons Xu Enzeng had to hand him some of the important cases. In this way we too could get intelligence before the enemy acted.

At this time, besides Chen Geng, the Central Special Section man in contact with Yang Dengying was the deputy chief of the intelligence branch, Liu Ding. They found that Yang Dengying's standing in Shanghai's enemy detective organs had risen markedly and that he was playing a bigger part. But they also sensed that he now served us less readily than before, at times shrinking back, and often putting on airs of self-satisfied conceit. In view of this, the Central Special Section grew more wary of him, guarding against his informing on us to the enemy to do us harm. It began to take care not to let him come into contact with so many of our people, and pulled An E and Lian Desheng, who had worked at his office, back out. At the same time it kept up his political and ideological education, to keep him from getting above himself or from changing in ways unfavorable to us. Chen Geng also sent Chen Yangshan to do political and ideological work on Yang Dengying's wife, so as to steady his loyalty to and faith in the revolution (she was a sensible woman, with some grasp of the revolution, and played a good part in helping Yang Dengying stay with it). It was hinted to him, too, that there were many others doing this work for us, that his was not the only such house; and besides helping him politically, Chen Geng also gave him fitting encouragement, in the Party's name, when it was needed. On the one hand Yang Dengying was boldly used, and on the other he was carefully protected, so that he should not give himself away. To meet Xu Enzeng's demands, Chen Geng also had people write up false intelligence for Yang Dengying to hand over and hoodwink the enemy, thereby raising his standing in the enemy's eyes.

The intelligence in Yang Dengying's hands grew steadily. His chief contribution to us in this period was to supply intelligence about traitors within, sparing our Party the harm of turncoats and informers. In the autumn of 1930, for instance, a responsible official of the Shandong provincial committee came to Shanghai for a meeting, was arrested in his hotel and turned traitor, and was shut up in the detention house of the Songhu Garrison Command's Baiyunguan detective squad. After some days he called out that he had something to tell and demanded to see the head man. The garrison command let Yang Dengying see him; apart from giving up his own identity and history, the traitor knew nothing of other matters. Torture pressed nothing more out of him, and Yang Dengying hurried back to report the situation to Chen Geng. Following the Center's instruction, and to verify the arrested man's true identity and whether he had turned, Chen Geng sent Liu Ding, in Yang Dengying's "expert" guise, to go and take two photographs of the traitor. As it happened the traitor had studied at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow and recognized Liu Ding, the man come to photograph him, crying out: "He is a Communist, I know him!" Liu Ding calmly and quick-wittedly bluffed his way past and brought the intelligence back to report, confirming that the man was indeed a responsible official of the Shandong provincial committee who had studied in the military class of the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow. Yang Dengying then went again and pressed the traitor for his confession, but still got nothing more out of him. Seeing that the man was of high standing and could give up nothing further, the Kuomintang shot him.

In the first half of 1930 Chen Geng notified Yang Dengying that two of our Party's organizational organs had been exposed and that the police and the Kuomintang authorities were preparing to go and search them. If the enemy detective organs struck, it would cost us heavily. On accepting the task, Yang Dengying at once moved the documents and other things from the two places, and the enemy's search came up empty. Beside the Chinese quarter next to Shanghai's North Railway Station stood a two-bay shopfront house, a propaganda organ of our Party, betrayed by a traitor. Chen Geng moved out all the documents, flags, and other things beforehand, and then had Yang Dengying telephone the enemy to "report" it. The Shanghai municipal public security bureau and the police station, "helping" under Yang Dengying's lead, searched the place, and turned up only some propaganda matter of little use. After these things were handed to the public security bureau, its chief Yuan Liang was uncommonly pleased, commended Yang Dengying on the spot, and even issued a written order: "Hereafter, when Inspector Yang comes to report news, act quickly on it."

Seeing all this, some may ask: at the time Shanghai was a nest where the reactionary forces of China and the West gathered thickest, an anti-Communist base where the enemy was especially strong and the White Terror severe; how could the Party's secret workers move about so freely? How could an English secret-service chief like Lampson be made to serve us? In truth it is no wonder. In every form of society ruled by an exploiting class, "with money you can make the devil push the millstone" may be called a universal rule. Especially in the Shanghai of the old days, called "the adventurers' paradise," the trait of "money is almighty" was on show at every moment. The enemy's fatal weakness lay precisely in this, Chinese and Western alike: each man out for profit, each with an eye for money. These adventurers of every stripe, come thousands of li to Shanghai, meant to make a killing. As the book Shanghai: Paradise of the Adventurers reveals: "These twentieth-century adventurers do not go into the wastelands to seek their living, nor look for their material in the ways of truth and right; they ply their arts solely in the human sea. They stand aside and secretly gauge the weight of another man's purse, then set up their clever traps and let the choice fruit fall one by one into their laps. 'When others sicken, we grow fat' is their creed, their religion, their whole philosophy of life." The Central Special Section made use of just this insatiable greed of these "adventurers," luring in these man-eating beasts. Even Lampson, this political detective chief of the British police, took a sizable bribe from us (including a motorcar), and what he handed over in return was a great mass of intelligence of the utmost importance.

### A Model of Someone "Pulled Out" of the Enemy Camp

In the struggle on the hidden front the situation is intricate and tangled. It requires, on the one hand, that one bore like the Monkey King into the belly of Princess Iron Fan (the ogress Raksasi) to work one's mischief, "breaking in" to fight in the enemy's heart; and on the other, that one "pull out" certain men from the enemy's detective organs and build them into counter-espionage assets for our use. Mao Zedong once said, in 1935:

The Japanese imperialists and Chiang Kai-shek can use the tactics of playing off one force against another to deal with the revolutionary ranks, and the Communist Party can use the same tactics to deal with the counter-revolutionary ranks. They can pull the bad elements out of our ranks and off they go; we of course can just as well pull the "bad elements" out of theirs (good elements, so far as we are concerned) and bring them over. If we can pull a good many men out of their ranks, then the enemy's ranks are the smaller and ours the larger.

Yang Dengying's case is a model of Zhou Enlai's Central Special Section "pulling out" a man from the enemy's camp.

The course of using Yang Dengying proves entirely correct the Central Special Section's policy of boldly using every kind of usable person among the enemy. Only by attacking the enemy can one reach the aim of defending oneself. Through this counter-espionage asset that the Special Section built inside the Kuomintang's professional anti-Communist organ, it helped us reach deep into the enemy's vital departments, control Shanghai's enemy detective organs, obtain a great deal of intelligence of the utmost importance, grasp the pattern of the enemy's movements, control many of the enemy's important concrete activities, and break through the enemy's plots—all of which gave favorable conditions for safeguarding the Party Center's organs.

"Pulling out" means using every sort of means to draw over, so far as one can, certain personnel of the Kuomintang secret-service organs, the detectives of the imperialist police stations in the British and French Concessions, and any others who could be made use of. The people pulled out in those days were, in general: sympathizers of the Party; friends and other social contacts; and anyone usable, including those bought with money—drawn out by various means. Toward all these usable objects one had both to be bold in using them and cautious in handling them, testing them in the course of use. Different objects called for different means. With those who sympathized with us, we could make contact in the open guise of Party members; with some it could be the coming-and-going of ordinary friends, keeping up the tie without exposing our political colors. Some, though they did much for our Party, did not know they were working for the Communists. Through the Party's political influence, through making friends by personal ties, and even by the method of buying with money, we widely won over and used a good many people to help the Party. In those days we had ties with some of Shanghai's ruffians too; asked to lend a hand, they would help. For the safety of the Party organization this work was very necessary. Inside the enemy secret-service organs, at that time, we had sympathizers and usable ties. The enemy's plots and plans we could learn of; when the enemy meant to arrest our people, we knew beforehand; several times the enemy meant to smash our central organs and failed. Before 1931 there was no great damage, largely because of the intelligence these ties supplied. When Comrades Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and others were arrested, we did get intelligence of it beforehand, but a little too late to give warning. Had the intelligence come a little earlier, it could have been averted.
