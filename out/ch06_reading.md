## Chapter Six. It Was Not Me, It Was the Wind

### 1. Codename "Carpenter"

Sicheng Li, in the French Concession of Shanghai—Lane 391 off Rue Ratard, today's Julu Road—was a small lane of just twelve shikumen houses, facing north, tucked between Route Cardinal Mercier (today Maoming South Road) and Avenue du Roi Albert (today Shaanxi South Road), and quite unremarkable. If it had anything odd about it, it was only the strange way the house numbers ran. They did not go from the street inward, nor split odd from even; they ran 1 through 8 east to west along the street, then doubled back west to east, inner houses first and outer ones after, so that No. 12, last in the sequence, was pushed out ahead of 9 and 10 to stand right at the lane mouth—which was why the boss of the "Welfare Electric Company" had taken a fancy to it, rented it, and set up his odd-job trade there.

The boss of the "Welfare Electric Company," whom none of the lane's residents had ever met or so much as spoken to, had his hired man convert the whole ground floor of No. 12—parlor and side room together—into the standard shop-in-front, workshop-behind layout: a big glass display case out front, stocked item by item with wireless components of every type, along with household bulbs, switches, and sockets, large and small; and the dim workshop behind crammed with repair tools of every description and a lathe driven by a little motor.

It was a workshop of the most ordinary kind.

In the Shanghai of those days such little workshops were as common as dirt.

And yet this one workshop, saying its prayers in a snail shell, was raided and shut down by the concession police.

It happened on the afternoon of December 17, 1930.

Five or six patrolmen came out of nowhere and poured in. Without a word they first used their batons and pistols to drive everyone in No. 12 into a corner, wrenched their arms behind their backs one by one, clapped on handcuffs, and then turned the place upside down, searching high and low.

Just as the patrolmen searching upstairs were passing down telegraph keys, headsets, and buzzers, one after another, a fitful knocking came from beyond the back door of the scullery.

In an instant every eye turned to that little wooden door, which someone was pounding hard enough to shake it.

That little wooden door had been shut by the patrolmen when they burst in.

Shutting the door on entering was to keep up appearances.

Keeping up appearances was the standing practice of the concession police.

The standing practice of the concession police was this: to lower the flags and still the drums, to slip out of sight, not to beat the grass and startle the snake—the better to wait by the stump for the hare, to take the turtle in the jar, to bag more of the accomplices who came walking into the net.

The French patrolman posted at the foot of the stairs turned, crossed the scullery, and moved toward that little wooden door with its none-too-thick panel.

As he went, he drew his pistol out again from the holster he had only just put it away in.

And a sly smile came over his face at the very moment he drew it.

The more urgent and impatient the knocking on the shut back door, the more that smug, sly smile broadened on his face—and the more it took on an edge of tension, a shade of wariness.

That uncanny look was the look of a hunter who has spotted his quarry and fixed on it.

He softened his steps as he drew nearer and nearer the little door, softened and steadied them again and again.

Only when he was sure his right hand, tight on the trigger, was at its most supple, most ready to fire on the instant, did he stretch his left hand toward the door's latch. As he turned it, he even held his breath.

He yanked the thin-paneled little door open all at once. He was so quick that the filthy great hand outside—the grimy hand that had been stubbornly, heavily slapping the flimsy panel—very nearly caught him across the face.

The two men, one inside the door and one out, both froze.

The man outside can never have imagined that the person opening the door for him would be a blue-eyed, fair-haired, high-nosed French patrolman.

Still less did the man inside imagine that the criminal he was so bent on running to ground was a house painter—a man whose face was dirtier than his hands, and whose clothes were more streaked with paint and grease than his face.

A sense of having been made a fool of took hold of the French patrolman. He roared despite himself, threw up his arms, brandished his pistol, and bawled at the painter.

The French patrolman grabbed the painter by the collar and demanded to know what exactly he was up to, and why he was pounding on this particular door.

The painter looked blank and, in a baffling pidgin English, stammered that he had come to collect a debt—to press for wages he was owed.

What debt? What wages? The French patrolman half caught it, half did not, and was thoroughly at sea.

The painter clung to him, wanting him to arbitrate, saying a debt was a debt and repayment only right and just. The boss of the Welfare Electric Company had hired him to do the fitting-out, labor and materials both; he had painted the walls, done the work, and the boss had welshed on the bill and dodged him several times over—today he would have his wages, and no mistake.

The more he talked the hotter he got, the more he shouted the more worked up he grew. The more the patrolman tried to hold him back, the more righteous he became, clamoring to push his way inside and have it out with the boss to his face. He said: You "three-stripers," you put on your tiger skins and you're supposed to keep things fair, to protect the toiling masses, to stand up for the common folk and speak for the poor.

Seeing him wail to heaven and carry on without end, the French patrolman moved to shut the back door and be done with him. To his surprise the man seized the panel and would not let it close for the life of him, cursing the patrolman for a watchdog in league with the boss of the Welfare Electric Company, hired by the boss on purpose to deal with him.

At this the French patrolman truly lost his temper. In a fury he gave a hard shove, then a kick to the chest that sent the painter flat on his back, slammed the door, and paid him no more mind.

He thought he had chased off a troublesome creditor. Never in his dreams did he guess that this painter was one of the very "wanted men" he and his fellows had come that day most hoping to lay hands on.

He was Tu Zuochao, back from the Soviet Union where he had studied wireless technology, a member of the Central Special Branch, codename "Carpenter."

### 2. Vanished Without a Trace

"Carpenter" Tu Zuochao had come to know Zhou Enlai during the Party's Sixth Congress.

Tu Zuochao was a designated delegate to the Sixth Congress—that is, an observer, also called an alternate delegate—as were Zhou Enlai's wife Deng Yingchao and Luo Yinong's widow Li Zheshi. Tu Zuochao's delegate's badge was No. 80.

The Sixth Congress was held outside Moscow. In the countryside beyond the city was the village of Zvenigorod, and in it a "Silver Villa," a landlord's estate before the October Revolution, ringed all about with pine woods. Through the pines ran several broad roads, wide enough for motorcars. In front of the main house was a very broad flight of dozens of steps. The main house was a great hall that could hold several hundred people in session, and its four walls were hung all over with red banners celebrating the opening of the Sixth Congress.

In the hall of the Sixth Congress, Tu Zuochao happened to sit right beside Deng Yingchao, and so came to know Zhou Enlai. Tu Zuochao was a Hunanese with a heavy Hunan accent, and both Zhou Enlai and Deng Yingchao had to strain to follow him; but they did make out his story. Hearing that he had trained as a carpenter from boyhood, working at the trade first with his brother-in-law and then with his eldest brother, they said: We'll just call you "Carpenter," then.

It was only after the Sixth Congress closed that Tu Zuochao formally switched to the study of wireless communications. On the eve of the congress's end, Zhang Guotao sought him out and told him: "What the Red Army back home urgently needs is trained wireless men. In battle, again and again, the fighting goes wrong because communications cannot keep up, and we take losses we should never have taken. The organization would like you to change over to wireless." Not long after, Zhou Enlai too had a talk with him, telling him to go and study wireless, and to study it hard. Zhou Enlai added: "The units are waiting for men. We have no men, we have not mastered the technology, and the units have no way to keep in touch."

On October 26, 1928, the Far Eastern Bureau of the Comintern met to consider the Chinese delegation's proposal to train ten wireless operators in the Soviet Union; on both the recommended list and the final list appended to the resolution, Tu Zuochao stood first choice. Under this resolution, on November 6 of the same year, Moscow's University of the East struck Tu Zuochao—who had already been transferred to the Tolmachev Military-Political Academy—from all its rolls, and cut off his every allowance.

And yet, though the transfer order from the Tolmachev Military-Political Academy reached him, Tu Zuochao never went to report.

Tu Zuochao had vanished without a trace.

The vanished Tu Zuochao, with Song Lian, Tan Xianyou, and Liu Xiwu—four in all—were secretly sent to Leningrad and put through rigorous training at the Frunze Military Communications School. They learned to send and receive, studied the fundamentals of wireless and the care and repair of transmitters and receivers, then went on to work placements in small experimental workshops, learning carpentry and forging while building wireless components with their own hands. At the same time they trained systematically in handling watchdogs and carrier pigeons, in wire telegraphy, in drawing military maps, and in repairing motorcars.

The other six on the list of ten were Fang Tingzhen (Fang Zhongru), Mao Qihua, Shen Kanfu (Chen Baoli), Li Yuanjie, Cheng Zuyi, and Chen Changhao. All had earlier been students at Moscow's University of the East, taking "wireless communications" in the "International Wireless Training Class," along with English and "horsemanship, driving, weapons, and demolition."

Tu Zuochao had once had meningitis, and his mind did not work quite as it should; sending and receiving came hard to him, his speed was too slow to keep up with the course, so on his instructor's advice he made the mechanical side his specialty and studied the repair of sets.

In October 1929, Tu Zuochao and Song Lian were ordered to Khabarovsk to serve as mechanics with the Chinese-laborers' guerrilla unit of the "Comintern Far Eastern Detachment" under Liu Bocheng's command. Afterward they also worked for a time at the "Cheka" station in Vladivostok (Haishenwai).

Meanwhile, on a lawn at Stanford University in California, a wireless set of American design had made contact with every station on the continent, to great success. Before long the Nationalist government's own signals began crisscrossing the skies of China.

What the Nationalist government mostly used was Morse code, the simplest of telegraphic methods: letters and characters represented by a handful of long and short signals, decoded against a codebook back into words. Yet even this near-primitive means of sending and receiving was beyond our Party. Fighting its hard fight in blood and fire, the Chinese Communist Party and its armed forces could often only rely on messages carried hand to hand, moving along the underground routes. Such human relay was not merely slow; it was unsafe. Living under the rule of the reactionary authorities, a message had to pass through blockade line after blockade line, and so the intelligence could only be written on an undergarment—in invisible ink, in rice water—packed close with tiny characters and worn against the skin. If the enemy found it out, it meant a head offered up and hot blood spilled: a brave death, a martyr's end.

To keep pace with the times and break out of this passive, backward state for good, the Party Center resolved to found a station of its own and build a professional corps of its own. The thousand-catty load fell squarely on Li Qiang.

### 3. Ring the Police Station, Quick

Li Qiang—original name Zeng Peihong, courtesy name Youfan, aliases Zeng Peihong (written otherwise) and Zeng Zongda—was born in 1905 in Changshu, Jiangsu.

Li Qiang came to the revolution through New Youth. In 1922, in Hangzhou, he came upon New Youth under Chen Duxiu's editorship, and drew deep instruction and profit from it. He especially admired the earnest counsel of that great champion of China's cultural revolution: "Young gentlemen, you must study with the spirit of Marx's own practical inquiry, and not study merely Marx's doctrines. If you study only the doctrines, then the spirit of Marx's actual inquiry is wholly lost, and you are no more than a scholar of Marxism."

In August 1925, Li Qiang crossed from the Communist Youth League into Party membership. In the summer of 1926 he was ordered to try his hand at making explosives and grenades for armed uprisings. Li Qiang was an engineering man—he had read civil engineering at Shanghai's Nanyang Road and Mining College—and knew little chemistry. So he bought a few original English books on the making of munitions at a bookshop, and some reference works on ordnance from the secondhand stalls, studied them on his own until he had it clear, and then made the things by the methods the books laid down.

Li Qiang's gifts had left a deep impression on Zhou Enlai. And so, after the Sixth Congress, when Zhou Enlai set about building wireless communications, he had a special talk with Li Qiang and told him: "From now on the Fourth Section of the Central Special Branch will be charged solely with establishing a station, and you will be its section chief. You'll take charge of designing or assembling the transmitters and receivers. I'm sure you have what it takes—the makings and the ability. You're the best man for it; there's no one to touch you."

Li Qiang looked troubled. "You know I studied civil engineering. Ask me to build railways and roads, bridges and houses, and I'd take it on gladly—but I've never had anything to do with telegraphy. I don't even know what a transmitter looks like!"

Zhou Enlai smiled. "The way I see it, you're a jack of all trades. Explosives and grenades weren't your field either, and haven't you already turned those out? In wartime, where are the ready-made experts? Anything you don't know, you can learn. You've a quick mind and good hands—just get on with it, and come to me with any trouble; I'll help you."

Li Qiang set his teeth, nodded, and said: "All right, then. We'll get started and see." (Yang Xicai, 2011)

Li Qiang was as good as his word. Fishing for a needle in the sea, he pieced together the basic ideas from a great pile of foreign wireless magazines, then spent day after day out and about, making friends with the wireless trade and comparing notes on technique. He turned himself into an amateur wireless buff—modest, eager to learn, never ashamed to ask, worrying at every question until he had it to the bottom.

He also deliberately cultivated some friends in commerce, calling often at the Yamei Radio Company and the Dahua Radio Company to buy tools he needed and such wireless gear as fell outside the authorities' restricted list.

One day Li Qiang came again to the Shanghai sales office of the American Radio Company and handed a purchase list, bearing the names of certain electronic components, to the smiling foreign clerk. To his surprise the smile vanished clean off the man's face, and a pair of eyes full of suspicion, even hostility, began darting about, sizing Li Qiang up and down.

Li Qiang's parts list had been drawn up from the contents of a book called An Illustrated Guide to Telegraph Apparatus. This was an internal manual printed by the Telegraph Bureau of the Nationalist government's Ministry of Communications: issued in limited numbers, strictly confidential, not in general circulation. And the very components Li Qiang wanted were all goods the authorities had expressly forbidden to be sold; the moment anyone came in to buy them, every dealer—Chinese firm or foreign hong alike—was duty-bound to report it.

So the foreign clerk fetched his supervisor. And the supervisor conceived the notion of turning Li Qiang in, of currying favor and reward with the concession police. He asked Li Qiang to be patient a moment, while with a wink and a jerk of his brows he signaled the clerk to go and ring at once. He said he must first check with the warehouse: if their stock was already exhausted, he would have to order from the factory.

### 4. The Manner of Lord Mengchang

His guilty scheme Li Qiang saw through at a glance.

But Li Qiang could not simply turn and walk out, and fall out with the foreigners. For those key components on the restricted list could be had only from foreign hands. Lacking these indispensable parts, he might have six arms and three heads and the powers of heaven and still not turn out a single transmitter. So, facing such a supervisor and such a clerk, Li Qiang made attack his defense and struck first, throwing their pretext back at them in fluent English: "In that case, never mind, I won't trouble you. I can look elsewhere. Shanghai's a big place, there are any number of firms run by foreigners, and with money in hand, need I fear I won't find the goods in stock?"

At these words the charm worked. Whether it was his ease or the merchant's own instinct that told, the supervisor changed his mind on the spot and called back the clerk he had already sent off to telephone.

As an obliging smile, faintly fawning, returned to the foreign clerk's thin face, Li Qiang not only got what he had come for and went off laden, but gladly took up the supervisor's suggestion and asked for a few extra low-cost consumable spares besides.

Li Qiang's work was also greatly helped by Cai Shuhou.

Cai Shuhou—original name Cai Suhou, also called Cai Shaodun—was born in 1898 in Zhuji, Zhejiang.

Cai Shuhou was a government-funded student in Japan from 1921, reading electrical engineering first at the Tokyo Technical College for Electrical Machinery and then in the electrical department of the Tokyo Institute of Technology, and giving himself over to the study of high-voltage equipment, its design and manufacture.

In 1924 Cai Shuhou finished his studies and came home, opening the Shaodun Electric Company at No. 1 Youheng Road in Shanghai (today Yuhang Road, or by one account East Youheng Road, that is, today East Yuhang Road). Xia Yan was Cai Shuhou's schoolmate. Shaodun Electric, Xia Yan said, "was a small electrical-goods shop of two bays' frontage, dealing in household appliances, with Cai Shuhou both proprietor and engineer. It was called a 'company,' but in fact there was only an accountant named Zhang, one fitter, and one apprentice, and every piece of business large and small he carried himself." Xia Yan said too that in the electrical field Cai Shuhou "could be reckoned a true expert. He could not only repair all kinds of machines but had inventions of his own; the earliest 'electric-light news' set up on the roof of the Great World in Shanghai was of his design and making." (Xia Yan, 2006)

Xia Yan greatly admired Cai Shuhou as a man, and wrote in Lazily Seeking Old Dreams (2006): "There truly are strange doings and strange men in this world—by which I mean that at a time when 'public morals were sinking by the day,' there could be a figure like Cai Shuhou, with something of the manner of Lord Mengchang about him, ready to run political risks and spend his money and effort for us exiles. From the '4-12' onward, right up to the end of 1929, the Shaodun company was a gathering-and-scattering point for fugitives, and later became a liaison post of the Party's Zhabei District Committee."

In 1929, Cai Shuhou moved the Shaodun Electric Company from Hongkou to No. 403 Avenue Foch (today No. 369 Yan'an Middle Road), on the boundary of the International Settlement and the French Concession, to make neon-sign transformers—and there, too, gave Li Qiang's transmitter work a place of shelter.

Li Qiang got hold of some lathes, milling machines, and planers, and in the second-floor bridge-room of the Shaodun Electric Company set to work with his own hands, machining trial parts.

After countless trials, in the late spring of 1929 Li Qiang at last assembled the first wireless set that could both send and receive. Its power was only 50 watts and its sensitivity not high, yet Zhang Shenchuan, well versed in signals work, was still able to use it, first to copy down the Nationalist stations' international news and weather forecasts, then to make contact and exchange signals with amateur stations around the world. It was proof in practice that Chinese Communists could indeed use it to chase the wind, to listen for the wind, to catch the wind—and become, in the fullest sense, windtalkers.

### 5. Who Was on Duty Last Night?

Zhang Shenchuan too was a talent Zhou Enlai had picked out himself.

Zhang Shenchuan had been a student at Qingdao University; he joined the Party in November 1926 and soon became secretary of the local Party branch in Shanghai's French Concession. In October 1928, Zhang Shenchuan was summoned to the Huizhong Hotel on Sanma Road (today Hankou Road), where he met Zhou Enlai, codename "Wuhao," and Gu Shunzhang, going by the alias "Liming."

Zhang Sengbao, Zhang Shenchuan's daughter, was glad to talk with me about her father's early years. In March 2007, in Beijing, I asked her to tell me in detail how the two of them, Zhou Enlai and Gu Shunzhang, had first met her father. She said Zhou Enlai saw her father in a ground-floor room of the Huizhong Hotel: "Zhou Enlai first asked my father about his past—where he had studied, what political activities he had taken part in, when he had joined the Party and on whose recommendation, and so on. Then he said to him: We have a plan, to build a station, and by it to develop a wireless communications work of our own. We cannot rely on human relay alone; that is too slow, and too dangerous. So we have decided to send you inside the Kuomintang to learn wireless sending and receiving, and come back to train operators of our own."

Zhang Shenchuan said not a word of demur, and at once declared himself ready to follow the organization's arrangement. Zhou Enlai pointed to Gu Shunzhang beside him and told him: "Good. From tomorrow, you're under his command."

In the winter of 1928, Zhang Shenchuan, under the alias "Zhang Yanming," passed into the "Shanghai Radio School" that was housed inside the Sixth Army Radio Station at the General Headquarters of the National Revolutionary Army. The organization bought him a telegraph key, a buzzer, dry cells, and other tools for self-study, and for 12 silver dollars a crystal set and headphones besides; Li Qiang rigged an aerial and an earth wire at his lodgings so he could copy the French-language weather forecasts broadcast on long wave from the Zikawei Observatory.

In May 1929, Zhang Shenchuan graduated from the Shanghai Radio School with outstanding marks, so winning the regard of the head of the Sixth Army Radio Station that the man kept him on to intern at the station, often setting him to work the set alone and to stand the night watch in others' place. He seized this fine chance to copy out, in secret, two of the station's military codebooks, handing them promptly to the organization. At the same time he learned from the fitter who shared his dormitory the trade of changing out transmitting aerials and topping up wet cells, and privately assembled a valve radio of his own.

Just as Zhang Shenchuan's work was going smoothly and bearing rich fruit, three Kuomintang agents arrived at the Sixth Army Radio Station, graduates of a radio-training class at the Whampoa Military Academy. All bore special missions and had been specially trained. The moment they came they were up and down and into everything, working up an atmosphere of alarm within the station—as if Communist spies were rife—until the whole place, top to bottom, was jumping at shadows.

Though Zhang Shenchuan met each of the enemy's moves with a move of his own, redoubling his caution and his guard, the hawk-and-hound agents still fixed hard on this well-liked "intern" whose professional standing led the field. They had their nose for it, their sixth sense. They laid snares all around him and began making trouble of every kind: first "chatting about home," probing sidelong, feeling out his "background," then bluster and bluff—striking the mountain to shake the tiger, reconnaissance by fire—posting on his transmitter a slip of paper that read in bold: "Who stood the night watch last night? Errors in reception! This must be looked into! It must!"

This was plainly picking a quarrel, stirring up trouble, deliberately finding fault with Zhang Shenchuan.

For it was Zhang Shenchuan who had covered the watch the night before; the duty book bore his name plainly.

For Zhang Shenchuan, on the watch the night before, had received without the slightest error, not one thing amiss, nothing to look into at all.

### 6. The Lamp Is Haunted

At this Zhang Shenchuan tendered his resignation to the head of the Sixth Army Radio Station, saying tactfully: "There are more and more interns at the station now, and fewer and fewer chances to practice; I think I had better look for another way out. I hope you'll bear with me."

The head was a man of sense. He understood Zhang Shenchuan's difficulty perfectly, and had no wish to see a promising young man's fine prospects ruined at the hands of such ill-intentioned vermin. So he neither pressed him nor tried to keep him, but with a few words of comfort and encouragement saw Zhang Shenchuan out the gate.

After leaving the Sixth Army Radio Station, Zhang Shenchuan moved in with He Guo, lately back from work-study in France, and under Li Qiang's guidance tried using the assembled valve radio to copy the political and economic news from foreign-language stations—San Francisco in the United States, Khabarovsk in the Soviet Union—for the Party leaders' reference.

In the late autumn and early winter of 1929, our Party's first secret station was at last born, in Shanghai's western district.

Li Qiang, Mao Qihua, and Zhang Shenchuan wrote, in A General Account of the Development of Our Party's Underground Wireless Communications, 1929–1949 (1988): "In the autumn of 1929, the organization rented a three-story shikumen house at No. 9 Fukang Li on Jessfield Road in western Shanghai for the site of the station." This account comes from Zhang Shenchuan's An Unforgettable Memory (1982). Following Zhang Shenchuan, the books Zhou Enlai, Commander of the Hidden Front (2013), A Biography of Li Qiang (2004), Soul of the Huangpu: Zhou Enlai Under the White Terror (1999), The Central Special Branch of the CCP (1996), and The Iron Fist of the Red Terror: A Record of the CCP's Central Special Branch (1993) all give the site of our Party's first secret station as No. 9 Fukang Li on Jessfield Road (today Wanhangdu Road).

In fact the exact address of our Party's first secret station should be No. 9 Fukang Li on Great Western Road (later Zhongzheng West Road, today Yan'an West Road)—Fukang Li being later Lane 433 of Zhongzheng West Road, today Lane 420 of Yan'an West Road (the original building is demolished, and the Meiliyuan Mansions now stand there). There was no such place as "No. 9 Fukang Li, Jessfield Road." For Shanghai had only five Fukang Li in all, on Xinzha Road, Connaught Road (today Kangding Road), Great Western Road (today Yan'an West Road), Haining Road, and North Zhejiang Road (today North Zhejiang Road).

The Fukang Li on Great Western Road was small and quiet, just ten shikumen houses in the lane.

The neighbors knew only that a young couple lived at No. 9 at the lane's end—a well-off household, the husband of a commanding air, the wife dignified and gracious—but had no idea where they came from or what work they did, for they kept much to themselves. In truth, even had they mixed freely, the neighbors could never have known that they were a false-married couple. Their appearing everywhere as a pair was entirely Zhou Enlai's arrangement.

Zhang Sengbao said: "One day Zhou Enlai asked my father to a talk, and introduced Pu Qiuchao, just back from study in the Soviet Union, to 'live at the station' with him, to serve as his cover and to learn sending and receiving too. To suit their surroundings they dressed with some care, and the house was fitted out to look the part. Once the station was arranged, Gu Shunzhang even came to inspect it. The transmitter was Li Qiang's doing. From the circuit diagrams Li Qiang drew, they bought parts bit by bit and built a trial set. When it was done, at night my father would call out on the amateur station's call sign, other amateur stations would answer, and after a few nights' trials they could exchange traffic. And so the Party's first underground wireless station came into being."

The first to use shortwave stations as a tool for passing intelligence—and from that to make and break codes—was the Party Affairs Investigation Section of the Kuomintang's Central Organization Department, the very thing Chen Lifu so loved to dwell on as one of his "two proudest achievements." In telling how the Investigation Section "first created a pair of shortwave stations linking Shanghai and Nanjing," Chen Lifu mentioned a man. That man was Li Fanyi. Li Fanyi was an electrical engineer back from study in the United States, where he had learned shortwave. Returning travel-worn, he found that Nanjing had only longwave stations and no shortwave, and pressed hard for the building of two shortwave sets, one in Shanghai and one in Nanjing, to ease communication between the two cities. And yet, "no one took any interest, and no one understood these new things, so no one believed intelligence could be sent by wireless," as Chen Lifu tells it.

At this Chen Lifu sent for Xu Enzeng. Chen Lifu prided himself as the founding father of the Kuomintang's investigation-and-statistics apparatus. He never made any secret of it: "The Kuomintang's 'special-service organization' was founded single-handedly by him" (Zhao Jiaming, 1992). He gathered up a set of men "with higher education and scientific training." Xu Enzeng had a background of study abroad, and had gone on to found in Shanghai the Kuomintang's first broadcasting station, China's first commercial wireless station, and an international commercial wireless station, and had a real feel for wireless. So Chen Lifu especially valued him, and named him to succeed Ye Xiufeng as head of the Party Affairs Investigation Section of the Kuomintang's Central Organization Department. And Xu Enzeng did indeed do him credit, soon making shortwave the platform for building a special-service network, and setting up special-service stations in Beiping, Tianjin, Xi'an, Hankou, Jinan, Hong Kong, and elsewhere—but this was already 1931 and 1932. By that time our Party not only had long since had its first station; that station had long since moved from No. 9 Fukang Li on Great Western Road to No. 32, Lane 1, Fudefang on Hart Road, had exchanged signals successfully with the Hong Kong station Li Qiang founded, and had, through Hong Kong, established wireless contact with the Southern Bureau and with the soviet areas in Jiangxi, Guangxi, and beyond.

More striking still: the man who devised the code used by the Kuomintang's stations was Chen Lifu, and the man who devised the code used by our Party's was Zhou Enlai. Chen Lifu's was built on the Commercial Press's plain-code book—first altering the code numbers, then scrambling the order and stripping out great numbers of unusual and obscure characters, to open a wider gap from the Commercial Press version. Zhou Enlai's, by contrast, married three systems into one organic whole: inverting and substituting the Chinese plain code, swapping English letters for Arabic figures, and turning the result back into a character-code system—self-contained, never repeating, of the very highest security, and honored with the name "the Hao cipher." The name came from Zhou Enlai's Party codename "Wuhao," and caught exactly, too, the cipher's breadth and depth. As Luo Qingchang, former deputy director of Zhou Enlai's office, put it: "Our Party's first cipher was the 'Hao cipher,' and its first decipher clerk was Deng Yingchao. You could say Zhou Enlai was a cipher expert, and that this was one of the important factors in defeating the Kuomintang." (Luo Qingchang, 1998)

Put it that way, and if it really came to a comparison, the Kuomintang was ahead of us only a little, and only in transmitting power. The fatal weakness of our station was that its transmitting power was too low.

Li Yanming said his father and the others "had turned out that first transmitter, and it was somewhat cumbersome and not too sensitive, only 50 watts, so everyone had a go at a 100-watt domestic set. But the moment you pressed the key, the induced current it threw off was enough to light up the electric lamp in the house next door, and the neighbors would cry out: 'How odd tonight—why does the lamp keep flickering even after it's switched off?' Once they heard that, they knew it was no good, and had to give up and stop the trials."

### 7. The Welfare Electric Company

As stations went up one after another in Shanghai and Hong Kong, and the Red Armies in various places captured a few sets in the counter-"encirclement" campaigns, there was still no one who could operate them or work them; so the Party Center decided to open a class in Shanghai to train operators, drawing in cadres selected from the several great soviet areas, with Li Qiang and Zhang Shenchuan in charge—one to teach the mechanical side, the other signals. To avoid drawing notice, the trainees were taught in twos and threes, on single-line contact, house call by house call, in separate groups.

The first to take the sending-and-receiving course were ten young people selected by Party organizations around the country, among them Pu Qiuchao, who would later play false husband to Zhang Shenchuan, keep the station at No. 9 Fukang Li on Great Western Road, and look after our Party's first secret station. Their teaching gear was of the plainest—one key, one buzzer, two pencils, a few blank notebooks—but they all worked hard, and soon learned the operation.

In 1930, Tu Zuochao, Mao Qihua, Song Lian, Fang Tingzhen (Fang Zhongru), Shen Kanfu (Chen Baoli), Li Yuanjie, and others returned from the Soviet Union to join our Party's wireless communications work, at the same time strengthening the class's teaching ranks. And so, in October of that year, the second class of the CCP's secret wireless training school began its concentrated study, under the name of the Welfare Electric Company, at No. 12 Sicheng Li on Rue Ratard in Shanghai.

Tu Zuochao said: with so many people gathered together, it looked neither like running a school nor like doing business—neither one thing nor the other, too big a mark, too dangerous. Zhang Shenchuan said too that gathering was worse than dispersing. If they must gather, the commercial districts of the International Settlement were better than the residential streets of the French Concession: the settlement's business quarters had heavy traffic and mixed company, and gave better cover. They could open two electrical-goods shops, register with the authorities, take out trading licenses, and have boss, clerks, and apprentices deal quite legally in bulbs and other electrical goods, and fit and repair lamps, water pipes, and radios; the trainees would live split between the two shops, trade as usual by day, and study wireless deep in the night. If one shop were wrecked by the enemy, the other still stood. But Gu Shunzhang would not agree. He said he had a reliable informer inside the French Concession police, and that if any Party organ ran into trouble within the French Concession, he would be the first to know of it, and could respond to the emergency at once.

Because Gu Shunzhang insisted, the "Welfare Electric Company" ended up, after all, at No. 12 Sicheng Li on Rue Ratard.

The two characters of the name—fuli, "welfare"—are said to have come from Tu Zuochao himself. His English was poor, and he could never manage "frequency" but always came out with "flequency," which to a Chinese ear sounded like fuli; so Li Qiang, as a joke, dubbed the shop the "Welfare Electric Company."

Sixteen trainees in all gathered at the Welfare Electric Company. From Guangdong there were Mai Jianping, Chen Tan, Wen Ming, Wu Shifen, He Shida, Li Jingmei, Yang Zhishui, and Feng Yiping; from Jiangsu, Li Guoxi, Su Gangda, Shi Guang, Zhang Qingfu, and Zhu Chenguang; from Hunan, Gao Zhensong and Xie Xiaokang; and from Fujian, Wang Xixiong. Most of them had taken part, one place or another, in the Canton–Hong Kong Strike, the three armed workers' risings in Shanghai, the Autumn Harvest Uprising, and the Guangzhou Uprising, and were Party members and Youth League members sent up by local Party organizations.

The head of the training school was Gu Shunzhang. Organization and funds were in the charge of his assistant, Wu Kejian, secretary of the Central Special Branch. The actual work still rested with Li Qiang and Zhang Shenchuan, one handling the mechanical side, the other signals.

The Welfare Electric Company soon caught the eye of the reactionary authorities' informers. For the trainees were all young, some of them still students, and though they had been given work clothes and got up as workmen, when they went out on errands or to the street to buy grain or vegetables, their hair was still combed to a shine, their leather shoes polished bright, their jerseys a gaudy mix of colors, new in style and vivid in hue—an eyeful at a glance. And with the company keeping up no outside business dealings and no contact with society at large, it was all too easy to arouse suspicion.

The first to trouble the Welfare Electric Company was a repairman in a waterworks uniform. He said he had come to see to the water supply, yet he never looked at the pipes, only at the writing on the blackboard and the various bits of electronic apparatus heaped in a jumble on the tables. The less he understood, the more he had to ask; the less it was any concern of his, the more he probed to the root and questioned in fine detail.

Then came four uninvited guests with the look of neighborhood toughs, pushing in at the door of the Welfare Electric Company through a drizzling winter rain. Li Qiang asked their business. They said they had come to "offer congratulations" and to "cadge drink-money," and produced an image of Guanyin. Li Qiang gave a "ha-ha" laugh, spoke a few phrases of the underworld's cant, pressed four silver dollars on them, and only so saw them off.

Zhang Sengbao said: "Every sign showed that this training base was far from safe, so my father warned Gu Shunzhang again and again that we had better guard against trouble before it came and move out early. But Gu Shunzhang was headstrong and would go his own way, insisting that my father was crying wolf and making a fuss over nothing. My father told him to hear the others out. He was a man who could not take in a word of any opinion but his own. The more the others said the place could not be trusted, the more he said: I don't see much of a problem—just study in peace. He even beat his chest and said: 'If there's trouble here, I'll surely be the first to know, and I'll get word to you to move at once—so what have you to fear?' He was the leader, and once he had put it that way, even Li Qiang had to defer to him. My father and the rest could hardly say more, and could only grit their teeth and hold on—hold on right up until disaster struck."

### 8. A Hand to the Curtain Behind Him

The day it happened was cold, with a wind blowing and a fine rain falling—this Zhang Shenchuan remembered most clearly of all.

Zhang Shenchuan remembered clearly that it was December 17, 1930.

That morning Zhang Shenchuan came, as usual, to the Welfare Electric Company and went upstairs to give the trainees their signals lesson, while Li Qiang and several others downstairs discussed the building of transformers.

Around midday Li Qiang went with Zeng San and Song Lian to eat at Mao Qihua's home, while Zhang Shenchuan and most of the trainees took their meal at the school.

In the early afternoon Zhang Shenchuan went on lecturing the trainees and coaching them in reception drill.

Later in the afternoon, Li Qiang, Wu Kejian, Tu Zuochao, Song Lian, Mao Qihua, Wu Yunfu, and the others had not come to the premises. Only Zeng Hualun, Zhang Shenchuan, and eighteen trainees were on hand, studying sending and receiving in the front upstairs room. Against the wall of that room stood a lectern, with a blackboard on the wall; the circuit diagrams Fang Tingzhen had drawn the day before, teaching electrics, had not yet been wiped off. Zhang Shenchuan sat at the lectern keying out signals, two code sheets improvised on the spot for the lesson laid out before him. Tables and chairs stood about the room; on the tables were cells and buzzers, and each trainee wore a headset, practicing reception. As they studied, the door was suddenly pushed open and six patrolmen burst in, one of them a foreigner, walking in front. Two patrolmen rushed up to Zhang Shenchuan, drew their pistols across the lectern, jammed them against his chest, and shouted: "Don't move! What's your name? Where are you from? Write it down yourself!"

Zhang Shenchuan, seeing how ill things stood, seized the moment while the patrolmen were busy laying hold of the roomful of trainees and, with a stroke of the hand, drew back the curtain behind him. This was the danger signal they had agreed on beforehand. It was this signal, sent in time, that let Li Qiang, Zeng San, Song Lian, Mao Qihua, and the others come through unhurt and escape a calamity.

Li Qiang, Zeng San, Mao Qihua, and the others, seeing Song Lian—who after lunch had gone back first to the Welfare Electric Company for writing paper—come hurrying back in a fluster to raise the alarm, rushed off to Sicheng Li to see what had happened. Hearts afire, their whole thought was on Zhang Shenchuan, Zeng Hualun, and the trainees, and they took no heed at all of Song Lian, who had lost his wits and lagged dithering behind, half following and half not.

Li Qiang, Zeng San, Mao Qihua, and the others had only just reached the corner of Rue Ratard and Route Cardinal Mercier (today Maoming South Road) when they saw the gatekeeper of Sicheng Li already waiting on the south side of the street. He was a kindly soul, past sixty, quite alone, who lived out his years in the little sentry box at the lane mouth; and Li Qiang, Zhang Shenchuan, and the rest, far from looking down on him, would often greet him with a smile and slip him a little help. So the moment trouble came to the Welfare Electric Company, far from gloating or kicking a man when he was down, he passed word to Li Qiang and the others, saying: "Don't go in again! Your people have all been taken! Taken off in a police van!"

Just as Li Qiang, Zeng San, Mao Qihua, and the others were asking the kindly gatekeeper how it had all come about, Song Lian, trailing far behind, quietly slipped away and ran off alone to hide at his father-in-law's, never sending word to Tu Zuochao.

Tu Zuochao, knowing nothing of it, came as before with his paint pot in hand, a round skullcap on his head, dressed in a very dirty padded gown, to varnish silicon-steel laminations at the Welfare Electric Company as Li Qiang had told him to.

Now old-style transformers were built up of silicon-steel laminations, and if they were not clamped tight they would hum as they worked, giving off an alternating-current buzz. So they first had to be bound round with an iron hoop and then soaked in clear varnish.

It was Tu Zuochao's ill luck that, in a moment's carelessness, he never looked up at the signal above before he knocked at the back door.

Once out of danger, Tu Zuochao first walked slowly out of the lane, still keeping up an air of unconcern, watching a street conjuror and a plaster-medicine hawker outside the lane. Only when he was sure no one was tailing him did he change trams twice, one after another, and come to the International station on Seymour Road. There he found Li Qiang. Tu Zuochao told him the news that the Welfare Electric Company had been raided, and he said he knew.

### 9. Not One Exposed as a Communist

On December 18, 1930, the fourth sheet, fifteenth page of Shen Bao carried word that Zhang Shenchuan, Fang Tingzhen, Shen Kanfu, Chen Tan, Shi Guang, Su Gangda, Feng Jingsan, He Shifu, Zeng Hualun, Li Guoxi, Wen Ming, Zhu Chenguang, Mai Jianping, Xie Xiaokang, Zhang Qingfu, and others—"upward of twenty men and women"—had been "brought into the station" together by "Chinese and Western detectives." It ran: "Around noon yesterday, Yuan Liang, chief of the Municipal Public Security Bureau, received sudden secret intelligence that a large body of reactionary elements was in hiding at No. 12 Sicheng Li on Rue Ratard in the French Concession, where they had set up a wireless set in secret with treasonous intent, and requesting that officers be swiftly dispatched to make arrests. On this report Chief Yuan at once dispatched a number of detectives, who went first with a warrant to the special bureau at the French station to request assistance. The detective chief then sent Chinese and Western detectives along with them; and indeed, on the third floor of No. 12 they seized wireless earphones and sundry wiring, took upward of twenty men and women on the spot, and brought them all into the station, where, after the detective chief had briefly questioned them, they were handed over to the detectives to be taken away for prompt disposal."

The next day Gu Shunzhang, riding a motorcycle, took Li Qiang to Route Voisin (today Fumin Road), and got into Zhang Shenchuan's home ahead of the enemy to find and carry off all twenty-one Party documents Zhang had been keeping for the trainees. But on the way back, going too fast and running red lights, they were stopped in the street at a crossroads by an Annamese policeman, which brought Li Qiang out in a cold sweat. Luckily Gu Shunzhang was quick enough: he at once fished out a few silver dollars and pressed them into the man's hand, and the man, his eyes lighting at the money, gave the two of them no further trouble.

On the third day, the Central Special Branch rented a suite of rooms at a hotel on Fujian Road (today Fujian Middle Road) to meet and go over the Welfare Electric Company affair, naming and criticizing Gu Shunzhang and Song Lian. Song Lian, they said, had played it safe to save his own skin: why, being the first to learn the school had been raided, and knowing full well where Tu Zuochao lived, had he not warned him in time? Gu Shunzhang, they said, had underrated the enemy, set too high a store by his own "all-seeing intelligence," and gravely broken the rules of secret work by massing so many people for training in the very center of enemy rule. Gu Shunzhang for his part argued and quibbled, and even shifted the blame onto another, saying it was the trainee Yang Zhishui who had informed. Gu Shunzhang said, making a great mystery of it: "I've thought it over carefully, and Yang Zhishui is the most suspect of all. Early on December 17, after breakfast, Yang Zhishui claimed he was ill, asked Fang Tingzhen for leave to go out and see a doctor, and never came back—and what came instead were the patrolmen making arrests. Look at it: isn't this a louse on a bald man's head, plain to see?"

The testimony of the comrades in prison flatly gave the lie to Gu Shunzhang's slander. For in several sessions of savage interrogation the enemy never once brought up Yang Zhishui's name, and later, when the case came to trial, Yang Zhishui was never called to give evidence. And so, of the twenty, not one was exposed as a Communist.

Even as the Fourth Section was soberly taking stock of the Welfare Electric Company affair, the "Red Squad" of the Special Branch's Third Section turned out in full force, massing about Rue Ratard, Route Cardinal Mercier, and Avenue du Roi Albert (today Shaanxi South Road), on the one hand keeping Party comrades who did not know the truth from blundering into the trap, on the other secretly watching every move of the patrolmen left on guard. As soon as the guards sounded the retreat and pulled out to a man, they hired a motorcar, drove into Sicheng Li under cover of night, peeled off the seals, carried out the small generator and other important gear the police had impounded, and stuck the seals back exactly as before. In all this they had the wholehearted help of the kindly gatekeeper. They gave him five silver dollars, too, in thanks.

### 10. Comfort for the Living; Till Tomorrow

After Zhang Shenchuan and the other twenty were arrested, they were held and questioned once at the French Concession police station, then "extradited" to the Shanghai Municipal Public Security Bureau. After questioning, they were split among four holding cells, and used the chance to concert their false confessions. (Li Qiang, Mao Qihua, Zhang Shenchuan, 1988)

In truth the false confessions had been ready from the start. From the day they first reported to the school, there had been false confessions against any mishap—false names, false native places, false ages, false motives; in any case, they had come to the Welfare Electric Company only to learn a trade, the trade of repairing electrical goods. Several iron rules had also been laid down at the time, requiring every trainee to say nothing of the school to any outsider, not to use the Welfare Electric Company as a personal mailing address, to keep no Party documents or progressive books, and, once arrested, on no account to tell the truth, but to give only the false confession.

The iron rules came into their own after the "extradition" to the Municipal Public Security Bureau.

In the holding cells of the Shanghai Municipal Public Security Bureau, Zhang Shenchuan, Fang Tingzhen, Su Gangda, and the other twenty each suffered the tiger bench, the duckling's paddle, and other cruel tortures; but not one admitted to being a Communist, and none would tell the truth about the Welfare Electric Company.

At the end of that year, Zhang Shenchuan, Fang Tingzhen, Su Gangda, and the other twenty were escorted under guard to Nanjing and shut into the Nanjing First Army Prison—the Xiaoyingzi Army Prison—whose full name was the "First Army Prison of the Judge Advocate's Department, Army Directorate, Ministry of Military Administration, Executive Yuan of the National Government."

{v} "After we had been there a while, the enemy interrogated us again. One afternoon, with a few flakes of snow drifting down, a guard took me to the interrogation room. It was the same two interrogators as before; after the usual general questions, the round-faced judge advocate pointed at the electric-torture apparatus and asked: 'Do you know what this is?' I said: 'I've never seen it.' He said: 'It seems you don't yet know how terrible it is!' Then he asked: 'Are you a Communist? Speak the truth!' I answered: 'I don't know what a Communist is; I'm not one.' He had the guard bind wires to my two thumbs and at once pressed on: 'Are you a Communist or not?' I answered: 'No!' He signaled to the other man, who began to crank the current, and my whole body was at once in an agony as of ten thousand knives; my two legs buckled under me despite myself, and beads of sweat rolled down from my head in strings." (Chen Tan, 1992)

On April 29, 1931, the military tribunal of the Judge Advocate's Department of the Army Directorate, Ministry of Military Administration of the National Government, charged them under Article 6 of the Emergency Law for the Punishment of Crimes Endangering the Republic—"propagating doctrines incompatible with the Three Principles of the People"—and at the Central Military Prison sentenced seventeen of them—Zhang Shenchuan, Fang Tingzhen, Su Gangda, and Chen Tan among them—each to nine years and ten months' imprisonment, and three—Shen Kanfu, He Shifu, and Xie Xiaokang—each to six years and six months; no written judgment was issued, and no appeal allowed.

After sentencing, Su Gangda was shut into the Central Military Prison, and there he stayed for five years.

The Central Military Prison, first built in 1930, stood outside Jiangdongmen in the city of Nanjing, one of the "four great prisons" of Republican Nanjing; it was also called the "Ministry of Military Administration Prison" and the "Central Army, Navy, and Air Force Prison," and was set up and run by the Judge Advocate's Department of the Army Directorate, Ministry of Military Administration of the National Government.

Chen Tongsheng wrote, in Fighting in the Blackest of Years (1959): "The Central Military Prison was the largest prison in China, built in the shape of the Eight Trigrams. At its center was a tall pavilion in the Trigram form, ringed all about by high fortress walls. Beyond the walls was a deep moat, and coil upon coil of barbed wire. Every door and window inside was set off with iron bars. On the north side were nine wards—Heaven, Earth, Man, Sun, Moon, Star, Wisdom, Benevolence, Courage. At one end of each row of wards stood one or two workshops, ten in all. In the north wing, all prisoners but the old and gravely ill mostly took part in the labor."

Chen Tongsheng—that is, Chen Nongfei, original name Zhang Hanjun, alias Jiang Zhongyi—was born in 1906 in Lingling, Hunan. He joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1926, was arrested in October 1934, and in November 1935 was sentenced to eight years' imprisonment, held in the south wing of the Central Military Prison, Xin Ward, No. 14.

Chen Tongsheng said: "The south wing had four wards—Reform, Repent, Self, Renew—where life was harder than in the north wing and the discipline crueler; the fellow prisoners all called it 'the prison within the prison.' Here were kept the newly arrived and those who had erred at their labor, most of them beaten, shackled, and manacled."

When Su Gangda first came to the Central Military Prison, he was naturally kept in the south wing too, and was beaten savagely, shackled, and manacled, so that within a few days his flesh was split open.

Su Gangda—original name Ren Ji, also called Su Guangtai—was born in 1909 in Yixing, Jiangsu, and joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1930. Born into poverty, Su Gangda got through school entirely on funds from the family ancestral hall, first reading the normal course at Suzhou Senior Middle School, then passing into the National Labor University to study at Jiangwan in Shanghai.

The National Labor University, "Labor U" for short, founded in May 1927, was the first national university funded by the Nanjing government, with colleges of engineering, agriculture, and social science and its own attached factory and farm—one concentrated trial of the work-study ideal that had run since the May Fourth movement, a trial itself steeped in a strong anarchist coloring. It had the firm backing of Cai Yuanpei, Wu Zhihui, Li Shizeng, Zhang Jingjiang, and others, and yet was mired past extricating itself in the factional strife within the Kuomintang's upper ranks and among its senior intellectuals.

In November 1929, Su Gangda, Zhou Libo (original name Zhou Shaoyi, courtesy name Fengxiang, also called Fengwu, born 1908 in Yiyang, Hunan, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1935, author of The Hurricane and Great Changes in a Mountain Village), and other progressive students were struck from the rolls and expelled by the school for distributing leaflets on campus, asserting their own independent character and opposing the ruling class's spiritual enslavement.

Out of school, Su Gangda was sent back by the Party's Jiangsu Provincial Committee to Yixing, where under cover of a primary-school teacher's post he organized peasant risings at Boyang and other places, all of which came to nothing.

That was the lowest ebb of the Jiangyin peasant risings; almost all the well-known martyrs—Qian Zhenbiao, secretary of the Party's Jiangyin County Committee; Chen Shuxuan, member of the Party's Songpu Special Committee; Chen Weiwu, secretary of the Party's Jiangyin Town Committee; Wang Zengtao, secretary of the Party's Changjing District Committee; Mao Xueqin, military secretary of the Party's Songpu Special Committee—all fell in 1929 and 1930.

In September 1930, Su Gangda came to Shanghai again and, chosen by the Party's Jiangsu Provincial Committee, entered Sicheng Li on Rue Ratard in the French Concession as a trainee in the sending-and-receiving course.

Not long after entering the Central Military Prison, Su Gangda learned that in this, the largest prison in the China of those days, apart from Kuomintang navy, army, and air-force men serving sentences or held in detention, great numbers of political prisoners were also confined—political prisoners made up a third of the more than a thousand inmates. He learned too that on April 29, 1931—the very day he, Zhang Shenchuan, Fang Tingzhen, Chen Tan, and the rest were heavily sentenced by the reactionary authorities to nine years and ten months—Yun Daiying, member of the Party Central Committee and secretary of the acting committees for Shanghai's Central and Eastern districts, had died a hero's death within the Central Military Prison, sold out by Gu Shunzhang, at only thirty-six. He learned, too, that the martyr was gone, but the martyr's prison verses passed widely among the fellow prisoners. And so he would silently recite Yun Daiying's lines—"Wandering the rivers and lakes, I recall old journeys; of old friends, the living and the dead, each keeps his thousand autumns. I have long put by grief and care as the common lot, and hold my high spirit still, a captive of Chu"—gritting his teeth to bear an unbearable agony, keeping himself upright and unbending in the "iron-window university."

He also saw Shen Kanfu on his last road, on behalf of all the "co-defendants."

Shen Kanfu—that is, Chen Baoli—had "worked underground in Shanghai, in the Central Special Branch's 'dog-beating squad.' Once, executing a traitor, he had jumped from the second floor of the Sincere Company and injured his foot, and the organization sent him to the Soviet Union for treatment; when he was healed he stayed on to study wireless. Later he came home and taught at the training school in Sicheng Li." He suffered from severe tuberculosis; life in prison was "extremely harsh," he coughed blood without cease, his "condition grew worse and worse," and the prison "put him in an isolation sickroom" and forbade anyone to "visit."

{v} One day the guards came to say he was dying, and let us go to the mortuary to see him. Some of us co-defendants hurried to the mortuary, and there was Comrade Baoli, at his last breath. He opened his eyes and looked at us, plainly holding on by sheer will so as to see us. Only then did the prison authorities allow our co-defendant Comrade Su Gangda to tend him. Later Comrade Chen Baoli died at last; he was the first of our co-defendants to give his life. (Chen Tan, 1992)

Not long after Shen Kanfu perished, Mai Jianping, Xie Xiaokang, and Zhang Qingfu died of illness one after another. Mai Jianping died of "severe tuberculosis and stomach disease," Xie Xiaokang of "long-standing dysentery," Zhang Qingfu of "severe dysentery, together with coughing of blood and an unstaunched suppurating fistula of the neck."

In December 1935, Su Gangda was transferred under guard to the Suzhou Reformatory to serve his sentence, passing from political prisoner to "penitent."

The Reformatory was a "reforming" organ set up by the Kuomintang; the board and lodging at the Suzhou Reformatory were rather better than at the Central Military Prison, and there was no torture or corporal punishment, but it was a mixed multitude, with people of every kind. Here were Wu Jue (courtesy name Yanke, born 1912 in Huaiyin, Jiangsu, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1932); Peng Kang (courtesy name Zijie, born 1901 in Shangli, Jiangxi, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1928); Liu Renjing (courtesy name Yangchu, also called Yiyu and Jingyun, born 1902 in Yingcheng, Hubei, a delegate to the CCP's First Congress and first secretary of the Youth League Central Committee); Cao Diqiu (given name Cong, courtesy name Zhongbang, style-name Jianmin, alias Zhang Yunqing, born 1909 in Ziyang, Sichuan, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1929); Li Chuli (original name Li Zuoli, born 1900 in Jiangjin, Sichuan, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1928); Ke Zhongping (born 1902 in Baoning, Yunnan, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1930); Zhang Hanfu (original name Xie Qitai, born 1905 in Wujin, Jiangsu, joined the American Communist Party in 1927 and later transferred to the CCP); Guan Wenwei (born 1904 in Danyang, Jiangsu, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1926); Yu Shoukang (original name Liu Songshan, born 1909 in Haiyang, Shandong, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1932); Li Shaoshi (original name Guojun, also called Zhen, courtesy name Monong, born 1906, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1926, son-in-law of Liao Zhongkai); Zhou Huinian (born 1911 in Xinyang, Henan, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1927, joined the Central Special Branch in 1930); and the "revolutionary mother" Granny Xia (original name Huang Youmei, born 1870 in Wujin, Jiangsu, mother-in-law of Zhao Shiyan and mother of Xia Zhixu). There were also Zhang Ding and Ling Zifeng (both young painters at the time), and Gu Shunzhang, too, with his second wife Zhang Wenqin. It was from here that Gu Shunzhang walked, step by step, to the end of his life.

{v} The attitudes of the "penitents" fell broadly into three types. The first were willing to repent, meaning to find something to do under the Kuomintang's reactionary regime once they got out—a very few, in effect surrendering. The second were passive and dispirited, heavy with pessimism and despair, sighing and groaning all day, meaning to give up political activity once they got out, find some other line in society, and drift along; there were not a few of these. The third held fast to their revolutionary faith, firm of will, meaning to take up the revolution again the moment they got out—these too were few. The three types behaved differently toward the reality inside the Reformatory. The first fawned and flattered the Kuomintang reactionaries; the second took no interest in anything; the third opposed the enemy in their minds while betraying nothing on the surface, struggling against him by rather subtle means. (Guan Wenwei, 1985)

Su Gangda, of course, was of the third type. Faced with people of every stripe, faced with the "daily readings" (daily readings of Zhou Fohai's The Theoretical System of the Three Principles of the People and Chen Lifu's On Vitalism) and with mind-assaulting persuasion of the "communism does not suit China's national conditions" kind, Su Gangda kept to the dark and watched the light, kept to the still and watched the moving, and composed a bedside-whisper of four-character lines: "Good morning to you; eat well, take an extra helping; study the arts; keep up your training; hold fast to the maxims; put your head down and toil; give comfort to your kin—till we meet again tomorrow."

A maxim, after all, lays its weight on a man's own cultivation. For a Communist made of special stuff, a faithful and valiant soldier of the hidden front, the one maxim to hold fast could only be: keep your mouth shut as tight as a bottle, and guard your will as you would guard a city.

At the time the Reformatory used Transformation as its "textbook," having the "penitents" "study" word by word and line by line the "surrender papers," "repentance papers," and "declarations of withdrawal from the Communist Party" of CCP traitors, among them the Career Account and Confession of "the former sham CCP general secretary Xiang Zhongfa." Transformation was printed by the Investigation Section of the Organization Department of the Kuomintang's Central Party Headquarters. In its preface the editors declared: "We have edited this little book to serve as reference material for those who believe in communism, that they may understand China's inherent national conditions and answer the question whether communism is suited to China. But we hope that those who believe in communism, in reading this little book, will cast aside their past prejudices, stand in the position of a third party, and study it with care."

The reform officer pressed Su Gangda to "study with care" this Transformation, and then to write a "study reflection" for publication in the Reflection Fortnightly. Su Gangda agreed readily enough, but the piece was slow, very slow, in coming.

At his wits' end, the reform officer reported it up to the education director. The education director reported it to the thought-assessment chief, who called Su Gangda into his office for a dressing-down.

The thought-assessment chief blew out his whiskers and glared, banging the table till it rang: "I'm telling you, be honest with me—do you want to get out or not? If you do, then write your 'study reflection' properly! Otherwise, how are we to assess whether your transformation is good or not?"

Su Gangda, all innocence, answered helplessly: "It's just that I want to write something really good, write something fine, and leave you a good impression—that's why I've been weighing every word, and it's come slowly. Or is it that you want me to be slapdash, to scamp it and get it over with, to fob you off any old way?"

The thought-assessment chief, stumped by Su Gangda's counter-question, could not get a word out for a long while. And Su Gangda's "writing something really good," his "weighing of every word," went on right up to the day he regained his freedom—and still he had not produced half a sheet of paper.

In October 1936, through Zhou Enlai's efforts and active rescue on many fronts, Zhang Shenchuan, Fang Tingzhen, Su Gangda, and the others were freed from prison—to see their kin again, and to see tomorrow again. And by this time the Chinese Communist Party's wireless communications, too, had flowered everywhere across the beacon-fired reaches of the Jiangnan and the northern frontier, in a great and sweeping magnificence.
