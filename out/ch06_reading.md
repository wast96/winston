## The Ninja Cipher

Kazama Gohei came away from the thickets of the Chinnō-in well pleased with himself. A shiver ran down the back of his neck, as though he had caught a thread of cold wind, and he made some small private gesture of satisfaction, turning toward the streets of the capital with his feet light under him.

(A kunoichi better than I could have asked for.)

To Gohei, Kisaru was nothing but material for the art of the kunoichi. The word gives itself up if you take the character for "woman" and break it into its three strokes: ku, no, ichi. It is a ninja's cant.

To the ninja of Iga and Kōga, a woman was in the end no more than a kunoichi. Her misfortune is that she is quick to feel a man's love. Give her that love and there is no danger, no humiliation, no betrayal she cannot bear: it is a marvellous, terrible gift. So the trainers of Iga fed their women a counterfeit love and were careful never to love in truth. Not the kunoichi's art alone but all the arts of the rappa teach the same lesson — a man keeps himself whole only by keeping his spirit pitiless.

Gohei counted himself lucky. His own kunoichi was no common woman. She had a rare and exquisite skill that made her an extension of his own hands, and for the sake of the man who would most likely be her husband she would work until the blood ran dry in her.

(The old master turned out a fine girl.)

Gohei could not keep down the smile that itched at his cheeks.

He knew that Jūzō's work was the killing of Hideyoshi. What he did not know was who stood behind Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon and Matsukura Kurando; let that come to light, and an upheaval to shake the realm was certain. It set Gohei's heart pounding with the pure exhilaration of the ninja. He had resolved, of course, to do the thing alone. He had to. Turn it over to the machinery of the Kyoto magistracy and this brilliant piece of glory would only end up carved into shares.

Going north along Ōmiya avenue, the tiled roofs of the Jurakudai fill the eye, vast enough to pass for a fortress. The long girdle of earth and stone that rings it throws up a white glare against the blue vault of the sky. A few chō south-east of that outer wall lies the grassy waste of the old Kan'in palace grounds. A cluster of samurai houses stands there wall to wall, and in one corner of it was the residence Gohei had been granted by Maeda Gen'i, magistrate of Kyoto.

He came to the little gatehouse of his own residence, and still Gohei had not noticed.

It had begun the moment he stepped out of the Chinnō-in. A shadow had trailed Gohei through the crowds of the highway, showing and hiding, showing and hiding.

Watching Gohei pass through his gate, the shadow halted by a distant wall. Within the gate a page was washing Gohei's feet. On either side of the entrance two young samurai knelt with their knees squared. It was a station in life a former rappa could never have dreamt of reaching — a warrior of two hundred koku.

The one thing this house lacked was women. Among the tradesmen who came and went there were those who offered to find him a concubine, but Gohei never took them up on it. Let a woman in and she might learn what he truly was; he dreaded the trouble of it. In those days many a warrior household ran its affairs on men's hands alone, so his own drew no notice on that account.

Gohei went through to his sitting room and waved the page away. Before long the same page was at the paper screen, a finger to the floor.

"A moment ago someone left a letter-box with the man at the gate."

"The name?"

"None given. A woman, by the account."

"A woman . . . ?"

A kunoichi.

The letter-box was a piece of gold lacquer fine enough for a lord's furnishings. He undid the cord and lifted the lid, and Gohei's face floated up out of the black lacquered bottom as in a mirror. Sunk in it lay a single slip of paper, a sheet of fine writing-stock cut narrow. He worked in a finger and turned it over: a few strange lines had been written there in a woman's hand. It was when he saw them that Gohei's brows drew together in a hard knot. These were no ordinary characters, and no kana either.

This was the ninja cipher. It was a hidden writing used only by ninja, for the dispatches they sent among their own. There are forty-nine of its characters, one for each syllable of the kana; the left element is drawn from the seven of wood, fire, earth, metal, water, man, and body, the right from the colours — blue, yellow, red, white, black, purple — and the two are combined to make a letter.

That the message came from a ninja was plain from the writing alone, but this woman's hand he did not know. Hold it to the light as he would, the brushwork was not Kisaru's.

Among his own kind, no one but Kisaru should have known that Gohei was now in the service of Maeda Gen'i. If it was not Kisaru — and if, as the hand showed, it was a woman who used the cipher — then Gohei was at a complete loss. The very fact that every stroke was in the cipher seemed to carry a silent threat: I know you, though you hide your true self so carefully.

The blood drained from Gohei's face until it was whiter than the paper in his hand. He bent himself double, pressing down his belly, straining to kill the tremor that welled up and spread from inside him. The very daintiness of the brushwork made the air rising off it the more uncanny, the more charged with menace.

A rappa is not trained in the useless discipline of mastering his own cowardice. If anything, a rappa lets terror stand as it is inside him. Trembling, Gohei picked out the letters one by one and read. — Rendered into hiragana, it came out as follows.

{v} I will bring you to meet Lord Jūzō. Tomorrow, the hour of the cock. Beneath the camphor tree on the Nijō riverbank. — To Gohei of Iga.

"She says she'll bring me to Jūzō . . ."

He had spoken aloud without meaning to. A woman of strange contrivances. Bring the two together, and have Jūzō cut him down — was that it? If so, she must be an Iga woman come into the capital at Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon's direction, and Jūzō would surely be in on the whole of it.

And yet the thing puzzled him. If Jūzō knew where Gohei was, he would not go about it by such a roundabout little trick; he would come into the house and cut him down.

"The hour of the cock tomorrow — six in the evening — and the sun still in the west. She can hardly mean a duel by that light. What in the world is this woman scheming?"

Gohei had taken hold of himself again. But behind the fear there remained an unnameable disgust. As if to cut it off, he laid his hand to his short sword.

"Hah!"

A small light flashed. The gold-lacquer box, lid and all, split into four and rolled clattering, innocent, across the mats.

"You called, my lord?"

The paper screen slid open, and the young page stood staring at Gohei's face as though dumbfounded.

Not a quarter-hour had passed since then. A woman stood at the eaves of Iseya Kahei's shop, behind the Hōkō-ji. She had got herself up as a townsman's daughter, her big frame decked out plainly, the blood glowing clear and fresh in her full cheeks. In the sun that was just beginning to dye the Kyoto sky the colour of madder, the woman threw a long shadow.

From inside the shop the apprentice hailed her.

"Who is it you want?"

The woman narrowed her eyes in that smoky smile of hers that was her mark, and said,

"Would a gentleman named Tsuzura be lodging here, by any chance?"

She dipped a little bow.

"Tsuzura? You've got the wrong house, I think. This is a swordsmith's. If it's the Iseya inn you want, there's one down along the riverbank."

The apprentice answered puzzled, and by his face he plainly did not know that Jūzō was staying there. Jūzō would drift in on a whim and put up in the annex of the Iseya, but not even the live-in apprentice had ever once caught the presence of the man in the annex. And this woman knew where he was.

Kuroami, hunched at the shopfront over his grinding, stopped his hands. Idly he lifted the blade off the whetstone and made a show of sighting along it from guard to point, and let his white eyes slide toward the woman.

(So this is Kohagi.)

In that instant Kuroami's eyes were caught by the woman's. With her eyes alone she gave him a little smiling nod. Inwardly rattled, Kuroami dropped his face and set the blade to the stone.

(No mistaking it — she's a rappa.)

So Kuroami's sixth sense murmured to him. And a formidable rappa, by the seeming of it.

(I see. This one even Master Jūzō could not cut down.)

Kuroami thought of the eye he would have to turn on the woman standing there — the eye of a man who must run her through — and without meaning to he put his weight into the blade against the stone, and just then,

"Even if you do not know him,"

the woman's voice reached his ear.

"the master of the house within will surely know."

The woman gave a soft laugh.

Kuroami went on grinding his blade in silence.

"Master . . ."

The apprentice turned toward him, prompting an answer, and a warning.

Without looking up from his grinding, Kuroami said in a low voice,

"Show her to the annex."

Jūzō would not be in the annex today. Choosing his moment, Kuroami rose and ambled into the room.

The woman was sitting there, correct and composed.

Seeing Kuroami, she said,

"My name is Kohagi."

"Of what house?"

"Lord Jūzō should know me . . ."

"This Lord Jūzō you speak of — I've no acquaintance with him."

". . ."

The woman did not answer. She sat gazing at the shrubbery of the garden, and then, as she began to gather herself to leave,

"If Lord Jūzō should call me to mind afterward, be so good as to tell him this: that I will bring him to meet a man named Kazama Gohei, tomorrow at the hour of the cock, beneath the camphor tree on the Nijō riverbank."

The woman rose smoothly to her feet, and Kuroami's hand shot out as if to strike at her skirts.

"Rappa!"

"Kuroami. That would be rude."

Named, he swallowed a startled cry, and in that opening the woman swept her skirts aside and was out of the room. By the time Kuroami stood on the garden stone, her shadow was nowhere.

Out of the shrubbery, from the far side, a figure came as though to change places with her. It was Jūzō.

"Kuroami — by the look of that, you'll not cut her down so easily."

". . ."

Kuroami would not look at Jūzō; there was something in his manner of a man ashamed of his own fluster.

"No need to be in a hurry to kill her. For the time being she's Imai Sōkyū's adopted daughter, scion of some good house — play along with her quietly."

"That fine lady left word for you."

Jūzō listened to the message Kuroami relayed. "So this time she's hatched something odd," he said, quietly.

Midges hung in a column above the garden. The two of them gazed vaguely at the midges, and in the eyes of both was the same thought: rain, most likely, tomorrow. With that vivid green at his back, Jūzō gripped the handle of an umbrella and set out into the streets well before the hour. He was in his usual showy short-robe and hakama, and over them, for all that it was summer, a light sleeveless haori of thin stuff. His purpose in the streets was not to meet Kazama Gohei. He meant to meet Kohagi, and so Jūzō went from the south of the city to the north, picking his way on that hard, deliberate tread.

The eastern edge of Nijō gave onto a small quarter of court-noble mansions, and beyond that, further east, there were no more houses at all. In dread, no doubt, of the Kamo's occasional floods, the ground ran on in a wild waste of summer grass all the way to the riverbank. At the foot of the levee stood a single old camphor tree, and he could see it dark and wet with the rain.

Jūzō halted at a crossing among the mansions, drew himself against the wall of a noble's house, and furled his umbrella. The rain came down on him without mercy.

(About time she came out.)

His far-off gaze was, if anything, an amused one, and he kept a watch on all around him.

Sure enough, at the entrance of the one noble house that jutted out nearest the riverbank, he saw a woman's palanquin come to a stop. It was somewhat far from where he stood. Jūzō had reckoned in advance that the woman would arrange to watch the doings at the riverside from just such a spot. Only that this woman should have the run of a court noble's house too — the strangeness of what she really was struck him afresh with wonder. Perhaps the crossing lay in the path of the wind: before his eyes the shafts of rain had begun to blur and whiten.

To keep his footfalls quiet, Jūzō threw away the umbrella and drew near the house; in one motion he twisted through the rain and was inside the low wall.

The house stood in ruin.

To adorn his own power with the honours of the court, Hideyoshi had bestowed estates on the emperor and on great noble and small alike, buying their goodwill. But by the look of this house its master's stipend could not run to a hundred koku, Jūzō thought, and pressed himself into the shadow of the water-basin.

Time flowed on among the shrubs. The rich mass of green leaves covered Jūzō over and shielded the man's back from the driving rain. After a while there came a soft patter of feet along the wet veranda, and then a white bare foot appeared, treading the rotted boards.

The bare foot passed before Jūzō's eyes. It was Kohagi. Presently the foot that had come out through the privy door stopped just in front of him. She was going to wash her hands. But the woman's hand, reaching for the dipper on the basin, checked all at once.

"Is that you there, Lord Jūzō?"

"It is."

"You'll get wet."

"I'm wet already."

"You should change, then. The house stands empty; there's no one to mind."

"Never mind."

"But still —"

At the sign of the woman stepping down to take Jūzō's sleeve, the man waved a hand as if to stop her.

"Wait. Don't come down. I'm a rappa. Being rained on won't give me a cold. If I come up, it's you who'll be in trouble."

"In trouble?"

"Whose agent are you —"

The woman gazed silently into the shrubbery, then softened into a warm laugh.

"Would that not be well? Even were you to take my body. . . . You once said you would treat me as a courtesan, and Kohagi has not forgotten it."

"Fool. . . . Take word to Kazama Gohei for me."

"You'll not meet him yourself?"

"What is Gohei doing these days?"

"The magistrate's man, they say."

"Just about what I thought. Still, if Gohei's the magistrate's man, there's some interest in meeting him after all. Why did you set out to bring the two of us together?"

"Meet, and there should be rare things to talk of."

"Woman."

He stepped up to her, seized her by the collar and threw her down with all his strength into the rain.

"There's a limit to how far you play your games."

Jūzō looked down at the woman where she lay.

"Whose vixen you are I don't know, but you're no more Sōkyū's adopted daughter, nor the young lady of any fine house, than I am. You're one of Gohei's crowd, and the magistrate's — aren't you."

As she tried to rise, Jūzō's foot came down on the back of her right hand. The woman bore the pain in silence.

"Hearing he's the magistrate's man has changed my mind. I meant to leave word with you, thinking that face to face I'd have to cut down an old comrade. But if Gohei is the magistrate's dog, then there's some sport in meeting him. Let me name another day and place. Ten days from today, the hour of the ox. The place — atop the roof of the First Palace of the Jurakudai."

"Atop the roof of the Jurakudai . . . ?"

"That should suit a ninja better."

"Lord Jūzō."

"What is it?"

Jūzō, who had already set his hand to the top of the wall, turned back.

"I am not Maeda Gen'i's man. I have never even seen his face."

"Is that so. Then from which quarter is it that you were hired?"

". . ."

"You won't say. But whosever agent you are, no matter. You are only a woman."

"Wait —"

Into the eyes of the woman calling him back came a desperate look. But Jūzō's body was already gone from the place. The man had vaulted the wall, and was walking off through the pouring August rain toward Sanjō.

His body was soaked through with the white rain, but there was nothing to be done about the dark mood that stayed behind, far off, within the wall. As he walked in the rain, Jūzō turned the woman's look in that moment over and over in his mind. Something had lodged in the taut of her eyes then, the unmistakable air of one who has begun to want a man. And now that gaze spread through his chest, laying dark colour over dark.

Kōga or Iga, he did not know what the woman truly was. But it was because the sight of a fellow rappa calling him back had struck him as something pitiable, that she had done it — or so at least Jūzō murmured to his own heart, and with a stifling feeling he thought of the wretchedness of it, of Kohagi.

A rappa must keep a rappa's heart to the very instant of death: so Jūzō thought of this comrade of his. He told himself that his own strong interest in Kohagi was tied to that too. To Jūzō, Kohagi was a woman who was the very type of a heart that even he could not counterfeit. Toward her he had come to feel something like a clean kind of awe — and now that same Kohagi, for one instant, it was true, had crumbled miserably before him.

But Kohagi had crumbled not before some stranger; she had crumbled before Jūzō himself. Or perhaps that was not quite right. Before that, it was Jūzō who had crumbled toward her. Her keen heart had felt it, and to the man who had let his guard fall she had answered, reflexively, by letting fall her guard as a woman — Jūzō looked at himself with a start, as though waking.

(If that's so, then the one to be pitied . . .)

is myself. Out in the rain, Jūzō gave a wry laugh. Put that way, even his own going all the way to that noble house to meet Kohagi came to seem a riddle. What he had told himself at the outset was that he was driving a preemptive nail into Kohagi's little scheme. Kazama Gohei, in Jūzō's position, would probably have done the same. But Jūzō's true heart, he felt, had not been that at all. He had used the errand as a pretext to go and see the woman — his own heart he now saw through, as if for the first time.

(If that's the way of it, then it's only a man and a woman, no more. No wonder Kuroami laughed to himself.)

All at once Jūzō felt the ninja that was himself, walking there in the afternoon light, grow shabby by the moment before his own eyes.

By that time Kohagi stood beneath the camphor tree on the Nijō riverbank, face to face with Kazama Gohei.

"I come as Lord Jūzō's messenger."

"Oh? So that letter was Jūzō's doing."

To this the woman gave no answer, but said: "He has been so unlucky as to be otherwise engaged, and cannot meet you today. The next appointment is at the Jurakudai —"

"At the Jurakudai?"

"On the roof of the First Palace. The hour of the ox, ten days from now."

And with that she was gone, her white shins spattered and soiled, running off.

The eastern skirt of Amidagamine dips a little into a hollow that a bright growth of pines adorns. They call it Komatsudani. The name has been famous since the old days when Taira no Shigemori's mansion stood there. Imai Sōkyū's Kyoto villa lay within that pine grove. Yet Sōkyū himself almost never lived there.

For the villa of a great Sakai merchant it was oddly slight in its appointments, which is best taken as the working of Sōkyū's own taste — an attempt to gather the whole fashion for austerity into a single dwelling. The garden fenced off a plot a full chō across; it set no stones and planted no trees, but left the ground to its own natural rise and fall and to the wild rank growth of kaya grass. Half sunk in that field of grass stood a small house in the sukiya style, and Kohagi lived there as its mistress. That day the house in the grass field had a caller. He was an uncommonly tall samurai. Kohagi received him in the room, and bowing once to her guest, she said,

"I am Kohagi, Sōkyū's daughter."

"I am Watanabe Satoru. Pray remember me kindly."

"My father has told me of you. Lord Watanabe practises the Kashima school of swordsmanship, I understand."

"A little."

"You said, I think, that you were a shrine-servant of Kashima in Hitachi."

"That was my former station. I bound myself in a private quarrel within the shrine precinct, and now I lead the life of a wanderer through the provinces."

"For swordsmanship, they say, Kashima and Katori. Lord Watanabe must be very strong indeed."

"Somewhat."

Watanabe Satoru bowed his head lightly. He showed no sign of vanity. He was a well-made man of twenty-eight or nine; but for the breadth of his shoulders and the knotted forearms, thick as pine-boles, one might at a glance have taken him for a young page of the court nobility. Only, for one of those court retainers who go scurrying along the shaded side of the city avenues, this man's eyes were set differently. Now and then he showed the whites of them. There was a mad, blue light kindled in them that made even Kohagi, seated across from him, go cold.

He was, to begin with, a man who had come to Imai Sōkyū of Sakai bearing a letter of introduction from Ieyasu's lieutenant Sakakibara Yasumasa. The gist of what Yasumasa had written was that the man's origins were not clear, but that at a look he seemed one who could be put to use, and that he might be let stay a while in the capital to see the sights of the Kamigata.

For Watanabe Satoru's part, no doubt he meant, through Yasumasa, to take service with Ieyasu himself. Ieyasu was by now the master of a Kantō so vast one might call it the greatest holding since Masakado's day. The sons of the country gentry all across Bandō longed to serve this mild lord out of Mikawa, who might well be styled the realm's second sovereign.

Satoru was master of the Kashima blade-work, which in those days was still one of the rarer arts. That country is, one may say, the very wellspring of swordsmanship: all through the age of the warring provinces the shrine-families of Kashima refined it, until a man named Matsubayashi Samanosuke wove the techniques of the Kashima school — the Shinmei school, by another name — and became the fountainhead of the schools of arms that came after. Tsukahara Bokuden, who was sword-teacher to the shogun Ashikaga Yoshiteru, also rose out of these Kashima shrine-families.

Satoru too, following Bokuden's example, seems to have come up to the Kamigata to make his art widely known among the lords. The swordsmen of that day would for the most part halt in Kyoto, put on showy matches, and wait for the lucky day that would catch some lord's eye. It was for such a reason that the later Miyamoto Musashi challenged the Yoshioka house, one of the eight schools of the capital.

How this man came to rely on Sakakibara Yasumasa is not known. Nor is it known why Yasumasa entrusted him to Imai Sōkyū. Most likely Yasumasa gave the man to understand that since Sōkyū, as one of Hideyoshi's companions of the chamber, had a hand in the governance of the realm, there was an advantage to be had, even in seeking service, in being known to Sōkyū.

Sōkyū passed him on to Kohagi without ceremony. If a secret compact to overturn the realm did indeed stand behind Yasumasa and Sōkyū, then the letter must have carried, unspoken between the lines, the sense of: make use of this man somewhere. Needless to say, the man sent to Kohagi had no way of knowing what fate of his own was in it.

Kohagi took up the bell at her side and toyed with it between her fingers. Almost the instant it rang, an old serving-woman came in through the sliding door bearing an offering-stand raised to the height of her eyes. On the stand lay gold.

"Things are dear in Kyoto. I have made up just this, thinking it might help toward something; take your ease and see the sights of the Kamigata."

"What — ?"

He had, it seemed, come through a hard and needy journey; a man of that build was struck dumb, looking from the gold before him to Kohagi's face. Yet all the while, in spite of himself, his hand had gone out and taken hold of the mound of gold on the stand.

"My thanks. So this is the new-minted coin they make such a noise about in the world."

He set a large piece from the mix on his palm and lifted his face. A cheap fawning that had not been there a moment before now showed at the corners of his mouth.

"Oh — no need to make so much of it."

Kohagi laughed behind the back of her hand.

"Money like that is, you might say, ill-gotten coin. Used by a gentleman of prospects like yourself, it becomes a blessing on the coin itself. Kyoto has, too, the quarter of pleasure that gentlemen are fond of. Sharpen the arts of the sword, by all means — but it would do no harm to polish this other art as well."

"Hm."

The man wore his pleasure openly.

"I've been a man of the sword from the cradle. Of that sort of guiding I know nothing at all."

"Then this very evening I shall put men of the house at your service to lead you along the way. They too will be glad to share in the good fortune."

"You are kindness itself."

The man bowed his head. In the parting of the pate he had let grow out, white dandruff floated forlornly. That this frailty was the man's weakness Kohagi had seen through from the moment she looked into his eyes. If in the brothel quarter of Kyoto he came to have some woman he was fond of, it would be a great piece of luck for her. Let him take up residence at the woman's, and by and by, hard put to find the money he spent for her sake, come again and again to Komatsudani to beg for gold — and he was in the palm of Kohagi's hand.

The first thing, at all events, was to draw the Kantō spine out of the man. Happily, the pleasure quarter of Kyoto has a strange power to draw the spine out of eastern men and melt down their high spirits. There is no need to bring up Kurō Yoshitsune, or his cousin Yoshinaka, or, later, the Ashikaga warriors: one might say the whole history of this country has been a contest between the women of the capital and the men of the east. And in that contest the men of the east have not once, that anyone has heard, come off the victors.

By Sōkyū's letter, the man, unknown as he still was, had a blade-work such that once, in the precinct of Kashima, he had bound himself in a private quarrel with a fellow and cut him clean in two, and as he made his escape had struck down two or three skilled pursuers the shrine sent after him in the space of a breath. And he was young still. Even granting that among warriors it was grappling on the field that was prized, and the sword lightly held, an art like this could not fail to make a name in the world. The man himself had come up to the Kamigata ablaze with that keen ambition. But the woman of the capital who waited for him knew in her blood the way to gentle a wild thing. In time this man too would begin to learn that there was a life to melt a body sweet, quite apart from the flesh-cutting austerities, and one day would forget clean away the fierce purpose the soil of Bandō had bred in him. So thinking, Kohagi felt stir within her, toward the whole of what a man is, an aching, cruel appetite.

Just as Kohagi had reckoned, the man showed himself again at Komatsudani after a few days. He had come for money. Reading his feeling, Kohagi did not come out to receive him herself, but sent out an old woman named Kusu — to make it easier for him to come the next time and the time after — and handed over the sum he wished. The third time the man did not come; a servant of the brothel came in his stead. Kohagi smiled to herself, marking how the man had begun to grow easy in his position of one who takes money. The fourth time, he came bold-faced and on his own two feet, and she received him with all courtesy in the tea-pavilion.

"And how did you find the taste of the capital?"

"Ah. In truth, I never knew that living could be a thing so pleasant. It is as if the day had dawned for me."

"I am glad to hear it."

Watching the man's clumsy motions as he sipped his tea, she said, as though something had just come to mind,

"Lord Watanabe is very strong, they say."

"At drink?"

"At swordsmanship."

". . ."

The man set down his bowl and looked at Kohagi. He looked at her fixedly, in silence. An arrogant resentment had risen in his eyes. That he was strong in arms was a thing beyond question. And to have it confirmed all over again by this kinswoman of Sōkyū's whose money he was forever taking galled him bitterly, and,

"That's a matter women of the house cannot understand."

He spat it out.

"Ho, ho — I have said something unpardonable. If there is anything in it to have wounded you, I beg you will forgive me."

"What —"

The blood had risen to the man's cheeks; he had taken Kohagi's laugh for mockery. Say what boasts he might not in the capital, at the shrine of Kashima Gongen, called the very head-temple of swordsmanship in the whole of Japan, Watanabe Satoru had contended for first place, or second. True, he held no master's license. That was because he had been expelled. His own arm was in nothing inferior to this man or that of the school.

"That, of course, goes without saying. . . . Better still, might you not raise a school of your own here in the capital?"

"That too I have considered."

"It is forward of me to say it, but the cost of it, the calling-in of the great lords — for those, the Ōkurakyō Hōin" — Sōkyū — "would stand surety."

"Hm . . ."

His humour was beginning to soften a little now. Watching the change in his colour, Kohagi found she wanted to put the man's arm to the test.

(Now, then . . .)

Into the mind of Kohagi, as she tilted her small head, there rose in a living, vivid shape the image of Tsuzura Jūzō of Iga.

To cut down that man. . . .

At the thought of it Kohagi caught her breath, guest before her though he was. It was a thing that until a moment before had never entered her head; and by the mission in which she stood, this was no time to be killing the Iga man.

And yet she felt herself grow drunk on the brilliance of the notion, forgetting she was not alone.

(To cut down Jūzō . . .)

The mere thought sent the blood surging suddenly through her whole body, so that she felt a light swoon.

(To cut down Jūzō.)

Kohagi tried the words over softly in her heart. And in that instant, at the mystery of her own spirit, so made that it could love a man only in this strange fashion, she began at the same time to feel a loathing.

Kohagi could call up on the inside of her eyelids, vivid down to the colour of the split flesh, the very day the blade would touch Jūzō's body; and yet in this passion there was, besides, a strange fold of the spirit that only a rappa could understand.

Kohagi was already aware that she had begun to love Jūzō. But she had gone on thinking, with a chagrin that made her grind her teeth, of the self she had been in the moment she lost her rappa's heart through her longing for him. That moment was her disgraceful showing at the court noble's house at Nijō on the day of the rain.

That time, Jūzō had set his hand to the wall and was making to leave. In that instant, the self of Kohagi who had forgotten herself and called out "Lord Jūzō —" : in what country of the heart did that self dwell? She could still, even now, recall vividly the feeling of that moment, as of being wrapped in a mist of dream. And, strangely, it was with humiliation that she recalled it.

(That Iga man, most likely, despised me for it.)

That was why Jūzō had made no answer, had not turned, had vanished to the far side of the wall. From of old, a rappa is one who lives inside the disguised workings of a mind that deceives the other and the self alike. To make that disguise unbreakable was the shared honour of them all, and such a comrade they respected as a rappa above the rest. But in an unguarded moment Kohagi had let her own wet inward parts show.

Jūzō was on his guard against Kohagi. Kohagi knew it. But she knew, too, that a man of Jūzō's stamp granted her the awe due to a rappa. That she must in the end make an enemy of Jūzō was, it might be, the final destiny laid on her as a rappa. Yet for whatever time remained before that, she wanted to hold fast at least to Jūzō's respect. To cut down Jūzō would be to prove her own rappa's ruthlessness in a high, clear form. This too, perhaps, might be called a fierce kind of love.

So thinking, in the poise of Kohagi smiling like a Noh mask, the guest, seated across from her, felt something uncanny.

There came a soft pattering.

The sound of straw sandals crossing the dewy path stopped at the eaves of the tea-pavilion and died. The guest's ear told him that the owner of the footfall was the old woman Kusu. The footsteps had stopped, no doubt, because Kusu, who had brought the gold, had crouched by the stepping-stones to wait for the guest to come out.

Satoru's breast swelled. Kusu's footfall was the footfall of gold. In the country of the east, gold as a thing still barely circulated. All the more, this man risen from a shrine-servant, who had given himself to feats of arms alone, had a skin thin as an infant's where such enticement was concerned.

Let him take the gold in his hand and his fingers trembled. The cold touch of it set the blood of his skin astir, wantonly, deep in his breast.

Just because he was innocent of money, he could not feel himself being brought under money's dominion. Kusu's footfall rang, honestly, as his own pleasure.

At the moment her step stopped under the eaves, this man of the east felt the impulse to fling himself in a slave's rapture before Kohagi, the gold's serving-maiden. He raised a voice that was very nearly a shout of joy.

"Is there nothing I can do? It doesn't sit well with me only to take gold and silver. Whatever the task, I mean to do it."

"Ah. What a gentle-hearted man. But there is no need to think of it so stiffly; you have only to use it as you please. Your pocket-money will not thin the strong-room of the Ōkurakyō Hōin of Sakai."

"That may be so. Still, it does not ease my mind. . . . If there is anything I can do —"

The man drew his copper-fitted sword to him.

"This. Might this not serve the Hōin in some office?"

"Well —"

Behind her calm face Kohagi burst inwardly at the man's simplicity, and parting the shapely lips of her face — a face like the goddess Kisshōten's — she said,

"The Hōin is a merchant. He has no use for a sword. But Kohagi, bred in the Kamigata, would dearly like to see the mettle of an eastern gentleman. And Lord Watanabe is a hand called the foremost of Kashima. Will you not show me your skill, for Kohagi's sake?"

"Show it I will. Is the man a swordsman of Kyoto? Or one of Ōsaka? Of what school?"

"For reasons of my own I can tell you only that the man's name is Jūzō of Iga. But in his own country, they say, his arm stands out above the rest . . ."

"Good, good. Some Kamigata swordplay, no doubt. It will be no trouble."

"That man will show himself, this very night, around the Jurakudai from midnight on."

"Will you guide me there?"

"Yes. But be on no account off your guard."

"An idle caution."

That night, from dusk on, Watanabe Satoru lay in an upper room of the Wakasaya, a brothel of Yanagimachi in Nakagyō, holding a woman he was fond of, when through the dark window he happened to see the great waning midsummer moon going down. The moon hung on the lower boughs of a chinaberry at the far-off Konoe mansion, and would soon sink behind the roof — it was already past the hour of the rat. Satoru pushed aside the body of the woman who slept sprawled and shameless, and cast her white arm off onto the mats. The woman opened her eyes a slit.

"What is it? At this hour."

"I've remembered an errand. Bide quietly and keep the place."

"What errand?"

"A thing I forgot."

". . . how sleepy I am."

"There, sleep."

"When will you be back?"

"Well, now. Toward dawn, I should think."

"Then hold me once more toward dawn."

"Hm."

When he glanced back, the woman in the bedding was already fallen asleep.

(So this is what the women of the capital are — as sweet as this.)

Slipping his arm gently under the quilt, he half thought that in this small room, heavy with the smell of face-powder, he could let his high hopes rot away and not regret it. And yet, thinking so, at the woman's pillow the man's hands were binding fresh straw sandals fast to his feet.

"— my lord."

Out of nowhere the pool of dark in the lane stirred, and a man's voice, faint as the drone of a winged insect, reached Satoru's ear.

Satoru sprang back by reflex into a drawing crouch; toward him the shadow came at a queer hopping gait, tripping close like a sparrow after fallen grain.

"Wait, sir, wait. I'm the messenger from Komatsudani, Myōbei, a timid shop-clerk. And your guide this night as well. — This face."

He thrust out of the dark a small face like a wax gourd.

"Mark it well, if you please, for the times to come."

He had got himself up as a shop-clerk, but the set of his eyes, the carriage of his body — to an eye that could see, a rappa in disguise was plain. Myōbei was one of the rappa in Kohagi's employ.

"Is that so. — I put you to trouble."

Without a doubt Satoru fell in slowly behind Myōbei's small shadow. When they came to the northern turning of Yanagimachi, here too the dark of the crossing heaved up. Myōbei went close to that pool of shadow.

"You're Sunetsugi."

"Hm."

"How is it?"

"All clear, near enough."

By this he meant that the patrols through the streets of Kyoto were thin tonight. Hideyoshi, the master of the Jurakudai, had of late removed to Ōsaka Castle.

Going half a chō west from there, a townsman came suddenly out of the dark and passed them. As they passed, he said,

"All clear,"

and was gone.

It was Kohagi's arrangement. Besides the several rappa she had lying in wait around the Jurakudai to watch for Jūzō's approach, she was working to keep Satoru's party from being marked by the watch of the city. As the fineness of the arrangement came home to him, Satoru began at last to feel toward this woman named Kohagi, who lived at Komatsudani, something uncanny and beyond his fathoming.

At the crossing of Sakaimachi it was the fourth shadow, counting Myōbei. The man came up to Myōbei and said in a voice too low to catch,

"He's in sight."

"From where?"

"From Marutamachi toward Sakaimachi."

"Lie hidden in the grass of that old house-lot ahead. Before you have counted fifty, the man who comes down this road is Jūzō."

"And you?"

"My work ends here. Yours begins now. I take my leave, with your pardon. But guard your life well."

"What!"

"No offence — I don't know how great a master of arms you may be, but if you fancy Jūzō of Iga can be cut down by any half-measure, you make a grave mistake."

Not waiting for an answer, he flung the words back like a parting shot and whirled away. However well Myōbei had done his part as the guide who would bring Jūzō to be killed, once it came to the trading of lives, he surely felt, more strongly than for the swordsman before him, the pull of favour toward Jūzō, a rappa of his own kind.

At Myōbei's word Satoru stepped into the grass by the edge of the lane. The grassy waste, some five hundred tsubo, had been whose mansion and in what age there was no telling, but the foundation stones alone remained.

Satoru parted the roots of the grass and put his ear to the earth, but from the hushed ground came only the faint song of insects in the leaf-shadow. Giving it up, he raised his face and strained his eyes. Across the lane before him the long rear wall of Chōsokabe Kunai-no-shōyū rose up in the starlight like a single band of pale light.

Then, at the eastern end of that band of light, a faint stain floated up, barely to be told apart. In a moment the stain came running from east to west, tracing a pale horizontal line. It was like a bird in flight casting its shadow on a white wall.

(Jūzō?)

The thought was no sooner formed than Satoru drove straight at the wall, and without a cry drew and swept his blade across the shadow. The shadow was cut cleanly in two and dropped to the ground. It was the work of an instant. And in the instant after, the reason sweat broke over Satoru's whole body in a torrent was this: the thing that lay on the ground was nothing but a crimson sleeveless haori, and it had run like a wraith. When he understood what it was, Satoru felt the sweat freeze on him all at once. Terror woke in him. Not the terror of a wraith. It was the flinching of his Kashima swordsmanship, which since his shrine days had never met a stranger art.

Satoru's one thought was to be off the spot as fast as he could, to set himself to fend off the attack of the unseen enemy. By the time he knew it, he was already running blind. Along the wall he turned right, curved left. His feet floated off the ground.

Jūzō, for his part — Jūzō had of course used no sorcery. Running along the wall toward the Jurakudai, at the moment he sensed the sword-force come rushing at him out of the dark, he had swiftly stripped off his haori and flung it toward Satoru; and by the time he had thrown it his body was already off the ground. He had leapt the wall and was inside the Chōsokabe grounds.

Coming down on the moss of the garden, Jūzō lay flat to the earth, trying to read the signs beyond the wall; and when he made out that the footsteps had run off westward, Jūzō too ran like the wind west through the Chōsokabe garden, cleared the wall once more, and dropped into hiding in the lane.

"Hold."

The one who stopped short, startled — he had already slackened his pace, of a mind to slip away to the south — was Satoru. In the shadow of the lane where he turned stood a great-framed ninja in black, hooded in black, a long-hilted great-sword slung across his back.

"You there — was it you set the little trap just now? Who put you up to cutting me down?"

"No one put me up to it. You're the man called Jūzō, are you not."

Satoru answered so with his mouth, but by now, drawn up out of one strange turn after another, he was quietly getting back his breath, working the big toes of his feet, edging himself toward the wall. Not to let him take the wall for a shield, Jūzō pressed himself flat against the same wall and came slowly forward.

"If you won't say, then — I am sorry for it — I shall have to cut you down."

"Heh. You mean to cut down Watanabe Satoru?"

He had meant to say it with a smile, but the end of the word cracked so that he could not himself catch it. From the black-clad man edging nearer, grinding his right shoulder along the wall, there flowed a strange and murderous force, and it pushed Satoru's heel back a step in spite of him.

Even so, the man did not draw the sword at his back. Satoru too stayed bare-handed still. He feared that in the instant he laid hand to his hilt his stance would break.

"Mark this: I am no common warrior. I know the waste of killing a man. But if I must, there's no help for it. Fail to cut you down and my own life comes into danger by and by."

". . ."

Satoru could no longer answer; his face drained of blood, he sweated a greasy sweat, and only his eyes shone with a blue light. Satoru had a calculation. He did not believe the man's force could last for ever. Stand off against him like this and wait for it to flag, and a chance to flee would surely come — so he thought. But at the very moment the thought of flight rose in a corner of his heart, some faculty lay in the man's senses that caught it, and —

"Do not flee."

The man said it as though laying a spell. And in truth, when it was flung at him so, short, the whole of Satoru's body lost, from every joint, the will to move. Let the man's great-sword come down full on him now, and Satoru would be cut like a thing set out for the killing.

Then the man began, slowly, to raise his right hand.

At last Satoru checked his backward heel. A break was beginning to show in the man's body. The instant just before the grip closes on the hilt is the instant when a stance decisively loses its suppleness. Would the man spring back and draw, or leap forward and draw — for the man too, that was a moment of hell.

That hair's-breadth instant Satoru watched with the whole of his being gathered to a point. To widen further the opening that the man's body left, Satoru quietly matched his breathing to the man's. And when the man's right hand had crept a full shaku toward the hilt, lightning broke from Satoru's own right hand, and —

"Hyaa —"

with a roaring cry he sprang far back. But before Satoru's left foot, launched into the air, could come down to earth, the man's body stretched out before his eyes, and with the bared blade swung up overhead came down over Satoru's very head.

Satoru knew he had blundered. He had misjudged the distance. That the man would ignore the interval so boldly, with a leap so uncanny, he had not thought.

On his left was the wall. Though he himself had chosen the spot to guard his side, it had become his death-ground. He had lost the freedom to strike out from the left and sweep across the man's live torso from the right. Cornered, Satoru turned his edge upward and flung the blade straight up toward heaven.

The sword streamed away, uselessly, on the wind of the sky. Satoru knew that death had come. And in the instant after, the man's bared blade should have come down and cloven him in two. But a strange thing happened. The man did not come.

By the time Satoru had recovered his stance, the black-clad man stood far off, several ken to the rear, in the dark, motionless — and without a sword.

Only then did Satoru understand. From the first, the man had not so much as drawn his blade. In his leap he had passed clean over the spot where Satoru stood and come down behind him. In all that while, Satoru had been fighting, alone and for his life, against a phantom.

"I grant you your life. The next time we meet, be sure to tell me the name of the one who set you on."

And with that the man vanished into the dark.

From Satoru's whole body the strength drained away by the moment, and he felt the very ground under his feet begin to sway.
