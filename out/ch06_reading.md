## The Origins and Evolution of the Green Gang

{d} Jiang Hao

Before liberation I was active in public affairs in Shanghai for many years and had long dealings with gang people, and in 1936 I formally joined the Green Gang, entering under Wang Yucheng of the "Da" generation. When the Japanese army invaded Shanghai at the August Thirteenth fighting, Wang took refuge in Hong Kong and died soon after. Another man of the "Da" generation, Zhang Renkui, died of illness in Shanghai during the War of Resistance, and when his disciples in Chongqing held the memorial on the anniversary of his death, they took in ten disciples on his behalf, called "filial-mourner disciples before the coffin," and I was one of them; since I had already taken a master, this was called a "cross-adoption." On the rise and evolution of the Green Gang, I have now put together this piece from the materials I have gathered and what I know at first hand, for the reference of historians, and I ask their correction.

### I. The Green Gang's "Former Three Patriarchs" and Their Ties to Buddhism

The Green Gang was in fact formed in the early Yongzheng years of the Qing to carry the tribute grain, but gang men push its historical origins back to the Ming, taking Jin Youzi, a Grand Secretary of the Wenyuan Pavilion in the Ming Yongle reign, as their first-generation patriarch.

Jin Youzi, styled Bifeng, had his ancestral home at Jinjiabao, thirty li outside the Qilin Gate of Yingtian prefecture (Nanjing), and was born in the sixth year of Zhizheng under the Yuan emperor Shundi (1346), taking the metropolitan degree in the Ming Hongwu reign. He first served under the Prince of Yan, Zhu Di, at Beijing, then followed the army south, in charge of the commander's grain depot. After the Prince of Yan took the throne at Nanjing, Jin was made vice-minister on the left in the Ministry of Works; when the capital moved to Beijing in the Yongle years, he was transferred to Grand Secretary of the Wenyuan Pavilion. When Emperor Chengzu campaigned in person into Liaodong, Jin was ordered to superintend the grain transport, and when Liaodong was soon pacified, returned in triumph with Chengzu. Seeing then how perilous an official career was, he grew weary of the dusty world and set his heart on Bodhidharma, and so submitted a memorial resigning his office and retired to cultivate the Way in the Ziyun Cave on Qixia Mountain. Later he went on to Wutai Mountain to seek ordination, taking as his master the Chan monk Etou of the Linji school, the thirty-sixth in its transmission, and received the name Qingyuan; from then on he cultivated in seclusion in the Zixia Cave, and died some years later. This is the origin of the Green Gang's taking Bodhidharma as founding ancestor and honoring Jin Youzi as its first-generation patriarch.

The second-generation patriarch was Luo Qing, styled Jingqing, a man of Luojiazhuang in the eastern countryside of Weiqing county, Lanzhou prefecture, Gansu. At seventeen he passed as a provincial graduate in a special examination of the Ming Jiajing reign, was later granted the status of metropolitan graduate, and rose to be an investigating censor and vice-minister of revenue. When Turfan raided the border, the Jiajing emperor made him a field commandant and he led troops straight to the frontier. The legend goes that after a bloody battle he was unluckily trapped below Lianglang Mountain; with his grain run out for three days and his men killing horses for food, a monk suddenly came and told him that beneath the stone cliff behind the temple lay grain stored by the Chan master Qingyuan of the present dynasty on his northern campaign, and he went and found it just as the monk had said. His whole army well fed, he attacked the enemy camp the next day, cut down the enemy general, and pursued all the way to the enemy capital, where the enemy lord surrendered and pledged never again to rebel against the Ming; Luo accepted the surrender and returned in complete victory. On the way back he passed Wutai Mountain and sought out the traces of the Chan master Qingyuan; guided by Henxiu, abbot of the North Temple, he took from the Buddha-niche the scriptures and relics Jin Youzi had left, and learned that Jiajing had once enfeoffed Jin as Chan Master and Protector of the Realm. Deeply moved, Luo had Henxiu lead him to Jin's stupa and there took Jin as his master—the practice of later men taking a master after the master's death, called "filial mourning before the coffin," originates here. Later Luo was secretly done to death by Yan Song and his son, and spent twelve years in prison; in the Wanli years, the needs of the frontier led to his release, but having earned merit he would not accept a stipend, and went to cultivate the Way at the very place on Qixia Mountain, in the Ziyun Cave, where Jin Youzi had practiced, and there ended his days.

The third-generation patriarch was Lu Kui, styled Daoyuan, a man of Dantu in Zhenjiang prefecture, Jiangsu, who studied the martial arts from boyhood, was skilled in combat, and had served as regional commander of Jiangyou. After the fall of the Ming he lived in seclusion on Mount Mao. Later, admiring Luo Qing's feats in campaigning against the frontier and pacifying the Muslim regions, and his command of the Manchu, Mongol, Muslim, and Tibetan tongues and scripts, and Luo being besides of the direct Chan Buddhist line, Lu went to Wutai Mountain to seek the Way. In the early Qing he wandered to the Xinjiang and Gansu region, and seeing the Muslim and Han people come to armed quarrels over their differences of religion, he submitted to the Qing court a proposal to use religion to win them over, which Kangxi graciously accepted, granting him the title of Preaching Master of the Northwest and sending him to preach in the northwest, where he drew up a "Muslim-Han Compact" providing that Muslim and Han peoples should respect each other's customs, each keep to its own ways, and neither offend the other. On his return to the capital to report, Kangxi was greatly pleased and proposed to grant him office, but he begged leave to go home and study the Way, whereupon he was enfeoffed as Revered Man Who Pacifies the Realm, and his master Luo Qing further enfeoffed as the Yiqing Buddha-Patriarch. In his last years Lu preached the scriptures in the Liu Hermitage on Baohua Mountain outside the Wulin Gate of Hangzhou, to large audiences, and died in the seventh year of Yongzheng (1729).

These three men, Jin, Luo, and Lu, are honored by gang men as the "Former Three Patriarchs," and all had ties to Chan Buddhism, which is why the later Green Gang organization carried a certain religious coloring.

### II. The "Latter Three Patriarchs" and Their Ties to the Grain Transport

The true patriarchs of the Green Gang begin with three disciples of Lu Kui—Weng Yan, Qian Jian, and Pan Qing—the so-called "Latter Three Patriarchs."

Weng Yan, styled Futing, called Dehui, was a man of Changshu in Jiangsu, whose forebears had lived at Liaocheng in Dongchang prefecture, Shandong, and later moved to Nanyang prefecture in Henan. He came of a licentiate's background, but later gave up letters to learn the martial arts from the monks of the Shaolin Temple on Mount Song in Henan; fond of the company of greenwood stalwarts, he joined the Heaven and Earth Society and traveled the four quarters on its business, at home wherever he went—firm and unbending in temper, and no man of smooth words.

Qian Jian, styled Fuzhai, called Dezheng, was a man of Wujin in Jiangsu who had moved to Huizhou prefecture in Anhui. Shrewd and brave, he traded with his father from boyhood and moved to Kaifeng. At sixteen he lost both parents, and having no heart to carry on the family business, took up boxing instead and joined the Heaven and Earth Society, serving with Weng Yan under Zhang Yue, a chief of the Heaven and Earth Society.

Pan Qing, styled Qingyu, called Delin, was a man of Hangzhou in Zhejiang who first moved to live at Anqing and later to Kaifeng in Henan. Living on what his parents had left, he was rich in property; he studied as a boy, took no small pride in his verse and prose, and was good at the martial arts as well. Bold and righteous by nature and fond of society, he was known in his locality as the "Little Lord Mengchang." Weng Yan and Qian Jian, the two of them, on Zhang Yue's orders, went to Anhui to seek Pan out; being all three Heaven and Earth Society fellows of the Way, they took to one another at once and swore brotherhood as men of different surnames.

Having sworn brotherhood, the three set out together to seek like-minded men, and came to hear Lu Kui preach at Hangzhou; holding Lu in the deepest respect, they asked to enter his door, and Lu, seeing how sincere the three were in studying the Way, agreed to take them as disciples. In the third year of Yongzheng (1725), the Qing government posted a notice calling for worthy men to strengthen the grain transport, and the three, meaning to build a great body on the base of the grain gangs, went to the Henan governor's office and answered the notice to undertake the transport. The governor at the time, Tian Wenjing, was a Hangzhou man; the three laid before him a plan for putting the grain transport in order, and Tian, together with the Governor-General of Grain Transport, submitted a joint memorial. With Yongzheng's approval, the matter was placed under the direction of the Governor-General of Grain Transport, Zhang Dayou, and under the command of He Guozong, the imperial commissioner inspecting the river works, and they were permitted to open the gang and take in disciples, and by this to unify the grain business. Having received the charge, they first gathered the Heaven and Earth Society heads of the various regions at Pan Qing's home in Kaifeng to confer, won the support of a number of them, and, linking up the old grain gangs, unified the grain-gang organization, raising the three men Weng, Qian, and Pan as its heads and forming a "Fellowship of the Way," which enshrined Bodhidharma as founding ancestor, Jin Youzi as first-generation patriarch, Luo Qing as second-generation patriarch, and Lu Kui as third-generation patriarch.

In setting up the grain transport, they first established yards to build boats to a uniform size, drew up designs, and supervised the building in person, constructing in all, so the legend goes, 9,999 and a half boats (hence the saying "without the half there is no gang," the half being a foot-paddled skiff). The second step was to help the Qing court open works to dredge the rivers, mobilizing 165,000 laborers from Shandong and spending 1,100,000 taels of silver to dredge the channels and open through the north-south water route. This done, they held a grand opening of the incense hall and took in disciples on a large scale—Weng Yan taking in eight, after the number of the Eight Immortals; Qian Jian twenty-eight, after the number of the Twenty-eight Lunar Mansions; and Pan Qing thirty-six, after the number of the Thirty-six Heavenly Stars; the three taking in seventy-two in all, after the number of the Seventy-two Earthly Fiends. Thereafter disciples took in disciples of their own, and so the Green Gang organization grew.

After the Green Gang had been established to carry the tribute grain, the three—Weng and the others—asked Lu Kui's instruction, and Lu handed down to them the ancestral generation-sequence of twenty-four characters as the traditional "genealogy" of the gang. These twenty-four characters are: "Qing, Jing, Dao, De; Wen, Cheng, Fo, Fa; Neng, Ren, Zhi, Hui; Ben, Lai, Zi, Xing; Yuan, Ming, Xing, Li; Da, Tong, Wu [also given as Wu], Xue [also given as Jue]." Of these, the first three, "Qing, Jing, Dao," were used before the grain gang was founded, and the gang itself began with the character "De," so that in fact the first three characters mark the religious sect and only from the fourth character does the gang proper begin. After the gang was founded, disciples and their disciples grew ever more numerous, and there being reason to fear the original twenty-four characters would not suffice, Wang Dejiang (that is, Patriarch Wang Jiang, called within the gang the Little Patriarch) added a further twenty-four characters, namely: "Wan, Xiang, Gui, Yi; Jie, Lü, Chuan, Bao; Hua, Du, Xin, Hui; Lin, Chi, Guang, Tai; Pu, Men, Kai, Fang; Guang, Ming, Qian, Kun." Before liberation, Shanghai had most of all the "Da, Tong, Wu, Xue" generations, of which the "Da" generation was the highest. It is said that the latter twenty-four characters had also begun to be used, but which character exactly they had reached cannot be traced.

### III. The Organization of the Grain Gangs

The concrete organization of the grain gangs took the grain boats as its main body and followed a fixed system, so that wherever the grain boats went, there was a uniform organization. Its organization and the numbers of grain boats are said to have been as follows: Jiangsu province, 21 gangs, assigned 1,638 boats; Zhejiang province, 21 gangs, 1,538 boats; Jiangxi and the two Guang, 19 gangs, 1,492 boats; Hunan province, 18 gangs, 1,386 boats; Anhui province, 16 gangs, 1,258 boats; Songjiang prefecture in Jiangsu, 9 gangs, 686 boats; Henan province, 9 gangs, 702 boats; Zhili, 1 gang, 187 boats; the tung-oil-boat and hemp-sack gangs, 4 gangs, 310 boats; and Shandong, 10 and a half gangs, 800 and a half boats. This comes to 128 and a half gangs in all, and 9,999 and a half grain boats. Besides these, legend adds a half-gang bestowed by Qianlong on the Little Patriarch, not counted among the regular gangs.

As for the classification of the grain boats, it was much like that of a fleet: the loading points, the delivery points, the names of the receiving households, and the flags and marks used on the boats were all laid down in detail. The class-names included the Jiang-Huai Fourth (within Jiangsu's 21 gangs), the Xingwu Fourth (within Songjiang's 9 gangs), the Jiaxing Guard (within Zhejiang's 21 gangs), the Wuchang Guard (within Hunan's 18 gangs), the Nanchang Guard (within the Jiangxi and two-Guang 19 gangs), and 34 categories in all.

### IV. The Rite of Taking a Master, and the Gang Rules

Green Gang members stood to one another as master and disciple. The procedure for joining had as its first step "registration": the one registering asked a sponsor (anyone might serve as sponsor, whatever his generation) to submit a "small card" on his behalf, and once the master consented, a day was chosen to hold a "registration small incense hall," where he submitted a formal card of allegiance, and having burned the small incense, became a registered disciple. Only after burning the great incense did he count as a formal disciple, and from then on had the standing to "open the law and lead the flock," that is, to take in disciples for the first time.

To take a master one had to submit a card of allegiance, setting out three generations of one's forebears and one's own age, native place, and occupation, and hand it in person to the master; this was the token of allegiance, and could not afterward be repudiated.

Incense halls were of two kinds, great and small; more precisely, they fell into five kinds.

(1) The impromptu small incense, the simplest of all: whatever the size of the hall or the number registering, it could be held any time and any place, needing only a set of incense and candles and one set of the three patriarchs' spirit-tablets, with the rites duly performed.

(2) The formal small incense, of somewhat greater scale, required the two masters—the introducing master and the transmitting master—to be present, and was held according to the prescribed rite.

(3) The formal great incense hall, grand in scale and solemn in its arrangement, with many officiants, was a great ceremony of the gang.

(4) The special great incense hall, much like the formal great incense hall in its organization, differed in setting out several more censers, offering to several more patriarchs, making several more rounds of obeisance, employing several more officiants, and singing several more hymns.

(5) The special full-hall incense, larger still in organization, and lavish in the extreme.

The officiants of the incense hall came to twelve titles: first, the housemaster (assumed by the one taking in the disciple himself); second, the scripture-preaching master (concurrently the transmitting master); third, the hall-attending master (concurrently the introducing master); fourth, the law-enforcing master; fifth, the law-protecting master; sixth, the hall-patrolling master; seventh, the hall-keeping master; eighth, the incense-scattering master; ninth, the incense-holding master; tenth, the merit-and-virtue master; eleventh, the guest-receiving master; and twelfth, the hall-standing master.

Worship of the patriarchs in the incense hall was called "paying court to the patriarchs." The small incense hall had the spirit-tablets of the Dragon Kings of the Four Seas, of Heaven, Earth, Ruler, and Kin, and of the three patriarch-masters Weng, Qian, and Pan. The great incense hall's tablets included the founding ancestor Bodhidharma, the successive Buddha-patriarchs, the patriarchs Huike, Hongren, Sengcan, Daoxin, and Huineng, and the patriarchs Jin, Luo, Chen, Lin, Lu, Weng, Qian, and Pan, together with the successive departed forebears of the three houses. The incense hall's banner bore the four characters "loyalty to the end of time," and there was one couplet, its upper line "the An-Qing make no distinction of far or near," its lower line "the Three Patriarchs hand it down to this day."

The Green Gang was tightly organized and its rules complete, the chief of them being these:

(1) The Ten Great Gang Rules: no cheating one's master or renouncing the patriarchs; no slighting one's forebears; no incest or disorder of the generations; no lechery, theft, or vice; no talking loose in the brotherhood; no jumping the sequence to initiate on another's behalf; no throwing the gang rules into confusion; no setting the low above the high; no "opening the sluice and letting the water out" (betraying the gang); no bullying the weak.

(2) The Ten Prohibitions: no taking two masters; father and son may not take the same master; a master's death forbids taking another; once a door is closed it may not be reopened; if a disciple declines to take in, the master may not take in for him; brothers have their high and low by generation; within one's own gang one is introduced by one's own gang; a master past his time may initiate on the master's behalf; within the Way, no slandering the Way; one low in incense-standing may not climb above his place.

(3) The Ten Commandments: no vice and lechery; no waylaying and violence; no theft of goods; no wicked spells and curses; no ruining men by litigation; no harming life with poison; no cheating men under a mask of rectitude; no ganging up to bully the few; no using size to bully the small; no cursing men in drink.

(4) The Ten Musts: be filial to parents; be zealous in one's work; honor one's elders and betters; let elder brother be generous and younger forbearing; let husband and wife live in accord; be at peace with one's neighbors; keep faith with friends; set the heart right and cultivate the self; do a kindness whenever one can; and help the old and pity the poor.

(5) The Ten Articles of Transmission: obey the law; be filial to both parents; revere the gods; practice the right Way; guard the body; be ready to mend one's faults; establish one's character; be careful in speech; keep to an honest calling; and shun bad habits.

(6) The Ten Articles of House Law: In the days when the three men Weng, Qian, and Pan led the gang, because the organization was tightly knit, the house-law rod was seldom used, though it existed. Later, when Pan Qing's favorite disciple Shi Shixian made trouble and fled, the ten articles of house law were laid down, with the incense-board as the rod of punishment. When the Qianlong emperor made his southern tour, having taken Buddhist vows at Jinshan Temple, he went in disguise to Hangzhou, saw the Green Gang's ancestral shrine and the grain-gang hall, and, judging that Wang Dejiang managed things well, sent down a decree of commendation and bestowed a dragon-staff inscribed with the words "for breaking the gang law, beating to death is no crime," to be the gang's town-mountain treasure; thereafter, whenever a grave matter arose, punishment was dealt with this staff. The ten articles of house law were as follows:

(1) For a first breach of the gang rules: a reprimand in a light case, the house law in a grave one. On a repeat offense, the two characters "rule-breaker" were burned onto the arm with fixed incense, and he was expelled besides. For the crime of treason, he was bound to the iron anchor and burned to death.

(2) For a first offense of defying one's parents: a reprimand in a light case, the house law in a grave one. On a repeat offense, the two characters "unfilial" were burned onto the chest with fixed incense, and he was expelled besides. For the crime of violating the bonds of kin, he was bound to the iron anchor and burned to death.

(3) For a first offense of disobeying the master's instruction and speaking or acting wildly: a reprimand in a light case, the house law in a grave one. On a repeat offense, the two characters "stubborn churl" were burned onto the arm and he was expelled.

(4) For a first offense of disrespect to elders and betters: a reprimand in a light case, the house law in a grave one. On a repeat offense, the two characters "disrespectful" were burned onto the arm and he was expelled.

(5) For a first offense of using one's seniority to seize the property of gang members young or old: a reprimand in a light case, the house law in a grave one. On a repeat offense, the two characters "extortioner" were burned onto the arm and he was expelled.

(6) For a first offense of beating gang members young or old: a reprimand in a light case, the house law in a grave one. On a repeat offense, the two characters "brute" were burned onto the arm and he was expelled.

(7) For a first offense against the law of the land or an act contrary to morality: a reprimand in a light case, the house law in a grave one. On a repeat of a grave crime, the two characters "wicked churl" were burned onto the arm and he was expelled.

(8) For a first offense of slandering the immortals, the Buddha, the bodhisattvas, or any religion: a reprimand in a light case, the house law in a grave one. On a repeat offense, the two characters "reckless" were burned onto the arm and he was expelled.

(9) For a first offense of keeping to no honest calling but living by extortion and violent brawling and refusing all admonition: a reprimand in a light case, the house law in a grave one. On a repeat offense, the two characters "unrighteous" were burned onto the arm and he was expelled.

(10) For a first offense of lechery, theft, and vice, compounded by fabrication and false accusation that brought harm on gang members young or old: a reprimand in a light case, the house law in a grave one. On a repeat offense, the two characters "shameless" were burned onto the arm and he was expelled.

### V. Figures of the Shanghai Green Gang

The Green Gang's generation-sequence divides into twenty-four generation-ranks. Counting from Jin Youzi, he is of the "Qing" generation (styled Qingyuan); the second-generation Luo Qing is of the "Jing" generation (styled Jingqing); the third-generation Lu Kui is of the "Dao" generation (styled Daoyuan)—these three being in truth the patriarchs of the religious sect. From the fourth generation on, the grain gang was set up to carry the tribute grain, and its founders—the three men Weng Yan (called Dehui), Qian Jian (called Dezheng), and Pan Qing (called Delin)—are of the "De" generation. It was handed down thereafter without a break, and around the time of the War of Resistance there were still a few of the "Da" generation in Shanghai, with the "Tong" generation below them more numerous. Here I list those I know of.

#### The "Da" Generation

Gao Shikui, a Shandong man, was head of the Zhili water police in the days of the Northern Warlords, and later lived in Shanghai, where he took in a great many disciples, of whom Ma Xiangsheng, Yang Renquan, and others had some social standing.

Wang Yucheng, an Anhui man, had taken part in the 1911 Revolution, and when Sun Yat-sen was Provisional President served as captain of the presidential guard; he also held posts for many years in the Shanghai police bureau and at the *Shen Bao* office, and in the Hongmen was lord of the Wuxing Mountain lodge; he died of illness in Hong Kong during the War of Resistance.

Yuan Kewen, styled Hanyun, a man of Xiangcheng in Henan, was the second son of Yuan Shikai and wrote for the old Shanghai tabloid *Jing Bao*, so that the disciples he took in were mostly of the literary and theatrical world—men such as Yu Yifen and Jin Biyan.

Fan Jincheng, an Anhui man whose history is unclear, was Zhang Xiaolin's elder-master, kept by Zhang in Shanghai. It is said that after Zhang made his fortune he did not wish Fan to take in more disciples, so the disciples Fan took in were few.

Zhang Shusheng, a Jiangsu man nicknamed "the Heavenly King's Father" and also a boss of the Li sect, took in mostly Peking-opera actors as disciples and died in the 1920s. During the War of Resistance there was in Chongqing another "Da"-generation Zhang Shusheng, a northerner and a man of the Northwest Army, who took in a great many disciples, mostly of the transport trade.

Wang Delin, an Anhui man, was originally a soldier and later went into business in Shanghai, taking in a great many disciples, such as Jin Tingsu, Gao Xinbao, and Gu Jiatang.

Bu Zhangwu, styled Linwu Shanren, a man of Xiangcheng in Henan, is said to have been a private secretary to Yuan Shikai. Coming to Shanghai, he first practiced planchette divination at the China Relief Society, later hung out his shingle as a Chinese-medicine doctor, and ran the tabloid *Da Bao*, writing Mandarin-Duck-and-Butterfly sketches; fond too of writing opera notices and puffing actresses, he took in, besides idle literati as disciples, actresses as adopted daughters.

Zhang Renkui, styled Jinhu, a Shandong man, served in the days of the Northern Warlords as defense commissioner of Tong-Hai (stationed at Nantong), and later lived in Shanghai, where he took in several hundred disciples; his organization was called the Ren Society, its members largely former military and government men and heads of industrial and commercial enterprises, and he died of illness in Shanghai during the War of Resistance.

The Ren Society was in form a club, housed in a two-bay, three-story Western-style building near the corner of today's Yan'an Road and Chongqing Road, where people were always eating, drinking, and amusing themselves. Its members included Chen Shichang, Huang Jinrong, Zhang Zhuping, Sun Yixiang, Jiang Huaisu, Wang Yingbin, Xu Yimin, Yang Hu, Zhou Sizhong, Lü Cangyan, Yang Dezhao, Zhuang Zhujiu, Xia Qifeng, Shi Shouzhang, Chen Mingshu, Han Fuju, Fan Shaozeng, Sun Lianzhong, Huang Shaohong, Wei Zuomin, Dong Xianguang, Jiang Dingwen, Sun Tongxuan, Yang Qingshan, Jin Yuebo, Xu Liang, Ge Futian, Cao Zhigong, and Jiang Hao.

Xu Langxi, styled Yuyun, a Shaanxi man, studied in Japan in his early years, later joined the Revolutionary Alliance and took part in the 1911 Revolution, and held military posts; dissatisfied with Chiang Kai-shek after the Northern Expedition, he took no post in the Kuomintang government, but founded the Xinhua Art Academy in Shanghai and made himself its head. The disciples he took in were mostly intellectuals, and he died of illness in Shanghai after liberation. In the Hongmen he was lord of the Yuyun Mountain lodge.

Liu Dengjie, a Jiangsu man whose particulars are unclear, was Gu Zhuxuan's elder-master, made known through Gu and kept by him.

#### The "Tong" Generation

Jin Jiuling, a Jiangsu man, was a detective-sergeant of the French Concession police in Shanghai and ran a rickshaw company on the side; before liberation he went to Taiwan, where he ran a bathhouse, and died, probably before 1980.

Ma Xiangsheng, a Jiangsu man, was one of the opium-dealing "Eight-Stock Party" and opened the Jinmen Theater (now the Children's Art Theater); he was suppressed after liberation.

Chen Shichang, a Jiangsu man and a disciple of Zhang Renkui, was Du Yuesheng's elder-master, kept by Du after Du made his fortune.

Jin Tingsu, a Zhejiang man, ran the aviation lottery before the War of Resistance through his connection with Du Yuesheng.

Gao Xinbao, a disciple of Wang Delin, was before liberation manager of the Lido Garden (now the seat of the Municipal CPPCC), and one of the "Eight-Stock Party."

Dai Buxiang, a Nanjing man, was a plainclothesman (informer) of the International Settlement police and opened the Da Zhonghua Restaurant. During the fall of Shanghai his son was connected with the Japanese spy Kawashima Yoshiko.

Ji Yunqing, a disciple of Cao Youshan, had a certain power within the Yangtze gang, and when the puppet Wang regime's secret-service organization at No. 76 Jessfield Road in western Shanghai was set up during the War of Resistance, he backed it; the agent-chiefs Li Shiqun and Wu Shibao were both his disciples. He was later assassinated.

Gu Jiatang, a Shanghai man, made his start dealing opium and was one of the "Eight-Stock Party"; originally a gardener, he was Huang Jinrong's right-hand man and ran the Common Progress Society in the counterrevolutionary doings of April 12.

Gu Zhuxuan, a Jiangsu man, made his start renting out rickshaws; a disciple of Liu Dengjie, he was fairly close to Yu Qiaqing, later became manager of the Tianchan Stage, and before liberation was a Shanghai municipal councilor.

Zheng Ziliang, a Guangdong man, took in a great many disciples within the Guangdong gang, and during the War of Resistance, through his Juntong connections, joined the Loyal and Patriotic Army; he was suppressed after liberation.

Wang Binyan, a Jiangsu man, was an important figure in the Zhabei business world and head of the district defense corps.

Lü Cangyan, a Jiangsu man, was manager of the Daye Company.

Zhou Sizhong, a Jiangsu man, was deputy general manager of the China Travel Service.

Xu Yimin, a Guangdong man and a noted Western-medicine doctor, took in disciples widely; his organization was called the Yi Society, and in the Hongmen he was lord of the Wuxing Mountain lodge.

Wang Yingbin, an Anhui man and son of Wang Yucheng, studied journalism in America and served as manager of the *Shen Bao* office and general manager of the *Shishi Xinbao* office.

Jiang Huaisu, a Jiangsu man, had served as executive committeeman and secretary-general of the Kuomintang's Shanghai party office and deputy commander of the Shanghai volunteer police, and took in disciples too; he was suppressed after liberation.

Sun Yixiang, an Anhui man, was a senior staff member of the Fufeng Flour Mill and took in a great many disciples in western Shanghai; his organization was called the Yi Society.

Zhang Zhuping, a Jiangsu man, was before the war the head of the joint management office of the four concerns—the *China Press*, the *Shishi Xinbao*, the Shen-Shi News Agency, and the *Da Wanbao*; during the War of Resistance he opened the Da Hua Restaurant in Hong Kong, then ran the Bailing Restaurant in Chongqing, and after the victory returned to Hong Kong to run the Da Hua, dying there later.

Cao Ligong, an Anhui man, had served as secretary of the Shanghai Chamber of Commerce, head of the drug-administration section of the Shanghai health bureau, and chief secretary of the Western-medicine trade guild, and died before liberation.

Xu Xueyu, a Zhejiang man, was general manager of the China Merchants Steam Navigation Company; he went to Taiwan before liberation, and his recent circumstances are unknown.

Tang Jishan, a Guangdong man and a great tea merchant, had lived with the noted film actress Ruan Lingyu, who later took her own life. Tang was in Shanghai before liberation, and his circumstances afterward are unknown.

Sun Baiqun, an Anhui man, was manager of the Fufeng Flour Mill.

Zhuang Shuxia, a Jiangsu man, had served as secretary-general of the Southwest Transport Office of the Kuomintang Military Affairs Commission and general manager of the National Products Bank, and died after liberation.

Yang Hu, also named Xiaotian, an Anhui man, had served as Songhu garrison commander and chairman of the China Seamen's Union and other posts, and died of illness in Beijing after liberation; he took in a great many disciples, his organization called the Xingzhong Study Society, and the disciples he took in were mostly seamen, though he also took in Kuomintang military and government men.

Cheng Junguan, a Jiangsu man, was general manager of the Jianghai Bank; he was in Shanghai in the early days after liberation, and his circumstances afterward are unknown.

Huang Jinrong, a Jiangsu man, was a detective-sergeant of the French Concession police and made his start dealing opium; he took in a great many disciples, at first mostly police-house men and gangsters, later extending to industrialists and merchants, intellectuals, and others, his disciples' organization called the Rong Society.

Before liberation the chief men in charge of the Rong Society were Huang Zhenshi, Chen Peide, and others. Huang lorded it over the fishery trade at Shiliupu, was head of the Fishery Bank, and was a municipal councilor. Chen Peide had originally organized the union at the British-American Tobacco Company in Pudong, later held a certain position within the yellow federation of labor, and after the victory set up the Dade Printing House of his own; he too was a municipal councilor.

Dong Xianguang, a Zhejiang man, was head of the English-language *China Press* in Shanghai and had served as minister of the Kuomintang Central Propaganda Department.

Zheng Xitao, a Zhejiang man, had served as manager of the *Shishi Xinbao* office.

Xiao Zongjun, a Guangdong man, had served as general manager of the Sun Sun Company.

Ge Futian, a Jiangsu man, had before the war been active in the Roadside Business Federation, ran the Bailing Restaurant in Chongqing during the War of Resistance, and after the victory returned to Shanghai to serve as general manager of the Jingu Hotel, manager of the Jianghai Bank, and in other posts; he died before liberation.

Lu Youlan was the adopted son of Luo Jialing, wife of Silas Hardoon.

Wei Zuomin, a Zhejiang man, was of long standing in the transport-engineering field, served as chief engineer of the Ministry of Communications during the War of Resistance, and died of illness after the victory. He was a disciple of Zhang Renkui, and together with another Ren Society man, Zhang Binren (a Zhejiang man), founded the Wen Society, whose disciples were mostly of the transport trade.

The Wen Society was based in Shanghai, its chief men in charge being Tao Yishan, Wang Weijun, Wang Qingnian, and others. Tao Yishan was a Juntong man; after the victory he served as head of the inspection section of the Songhu garrison command in Shanghai, was transferred a few months before Shanghai's liberation to head of the civil-affairs bureau, and went to Taiwan on the eve of liberation. Wang Weijun was originally a man of the Youth Corps; after the victory he served as an officer of the Corps' Shanghai branch, was chief secretary of the civil-affairs bureau when Tao Yishan was transferred to head it, and, when Tao left Shanghai, took charge of the bureau's affairs, was after liberation kept on as a special commissioner by the civil-affairs bureau of the Municipal People's Government, and later left Shanghai for Hong Kong.

Zhang Xiaolin, a disciple of Fan Jincheng, was born in Cixi county in Zhejiang and at twenty moved to Hangzhou, where he first learned silk-weaving in a workshop, then entered a military-preparatory school and got to know Zhang Zaiyang, and later took as his master Li Xiutang, a detective-head of the Hangzhou yamen, going in for gangster doings. Presently he came to Shanghai and took Fan Jincheng as his master, and from then on took in disciples on a large scale. Before the Northern Expedition, Zhang Zaiyang was governor of Zhejiang, and through him Zhang Xiaolin colluded with the Zhejiang military governor Lu Yongxiang; and since the Songhu defense commissioner He Fenglin had been a subordinate of Lu Yongxiang, he took up with He Fenglin as well, and relying on these connections dealt in opium and swore brotherhood with Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng, becoming one of the "Three Big Bosses" among the Shanghai gangsters. At the April 12 counterrevolutionary coup they jointly ran the "China Progress Society" against the Communists, and thereafter stepped onto the political stage and took up posts in the industrial and commercial world. In 1932, on Du Yuesheng's recommendation, he became a supervisor of the Shanghai Chinese Cotton Goods Exchange. After the August Thirteenth Shanghai fighting began, Zhang, at the direction of Japanese agents, ran a "New Asia Peace Promotion Society," buying up war material for the Japanese army. In 1939 he plotted with Japanese agents to set up a puppet provincial government of Zhejiang, with himself as governor; Kuomintang agents failed in an attempt to assassinate him, but for that reason he dared not take up the puppet governorship. On August 14, 1940, he was killed by his own bodyguard Lin Huaibu.

Jiang Hao, a man of Baoshan county in Shanghai, held before and after the War of Resistance the posts of supervisory committeeman and executive committeeman of the Kuomintang's Shanghai party office, planning committeeman of the Central Organization Department, and Shanghai municipal councilor. After the victory he served as head of the Laozha district of Shanghai, and is at present a member of the Shanghai Research Institute of Culture and History.

#### The "Wu" Generation

Du Yuesheng, a man of Gaoqiao in Pudong, Shanghai, was a disciple of Chen Shichang. In his youth he was a fruit peddler at Shiliupu, later got to know Huang Jinrong and ran opium errands for him, and from then on rose meteorically to become one of Shanghai's "Three Big Bosses," his power surpassing even that of Huang Jinrong and Zhang Xiaolin both. Having done good service for the Kuomintang against the Communists in the April 12 counterrevolutionary coup, he stepped from then on onto the political stage, went on to set foot in industry and commerce, and became a leading figure in the commercial world. After the outbreak of the August Thirteenth War of Resistance in Shanghai, patriotic people of every quarter jointly formed the Shanghai All-Circles Association for the Support of Resistance, which Du Yuesheng joined as chairman of the Shanghai Local Association and of which he was a member of the presidium, jointly directing its affairs with Huang Yanpei, Wang Xiaolai, Qian Xinzhi, Pan Gongzhan, Zhang Shouyong, Ke Ganchen, and others. After the fall of Shanghai he went first to Hong Kong and then to Chongqing. During his time in Hong Kong and Chongqing he took direct part in directing the Kuomintang's secret organizations in Shanghai, such as the Unified Committee, and worked with the Juntong chief Dai Li on the "exchange of goods with the enemy," doing business with the occupied areas. After the victory he returned to Shanghai and, through his disciples, controlled the Shanghai Chamber of Commerce, the yellow labor unions, and other bodies, and, working with the CC Clique, controlled the Shanghai Municipal Council and swayed the local administration.

Du Yuesheng was adept at the many-sided method of the soft hand and the hard together and of wide-flung connections. On the surface he was close to Chiang Kai-shek, but in the successive anti-Chiang movements he drew the anti-Chiang factions to him at the same time. In April and May of 1930, when Wang Jingwei, Yan Xishan, and Feng Yuxiang joined against Chiang and held the Enlarged Conference in Beiping, I was then in charge of the Reorganization faction's labor movement in Shanghai and had gone to Beiping too, and Chen Gongbo arranged for me to return to Shanghai and coordinate anti-Chiang military activity with Du Yuesheng, saying Du had three divisions ready to answer Shi Yousan's anti-Chiang mutiny at Pukou. On my leaving, Chen Gongbo wrote a letter in his own hand to Du Yuesheng and had me carry it back to Shanghai to hand over in person; but by the time I reached Shanghai, Shi's mutiny had failed, and so I did not go to see Du Yuesheng—which shows Du's double-dealing method well enough.

Du Yuesheng took in disciples widely, his organization called the Heng Society, its members drawn from every trade and calling, some of whom already had considerable social standing. After the victory, Luo Qinghua and Wang Xianqing, in charge of the Kuomintang's industrial and commercial bodies in Shanghai, and Lu Jingshi and Shui Xiangyun, in charge of union work, were all Heng Society men. In industry and commerce, Du Yuesheng held the posts of director, chairman, and general manager of many enterprises, and moreover placed several of his sons and his ablest disciples as managers and deputy managers of those enterprises, keeping the real power in hand. In the newspaper world, Du Yuesheng was himself a director of both the *Shen Bao* and the *Xinwen Bao*, and with the newspapermen Tang Shichang, Zhao Junhao, Jin Xiongbai, and others also Heng Society men, he could sway Shanghai opinion. The Heng Society's influence across every quarter of Shanghai was very wide in those days.

The "Wu"-generation Green Gang men were very numerous and had great social influence. In principle the disciples taken in by "Tong"-generation men were all of the "Wu" generation, but strictly speaking, not all of them held a generation-rank. For taking in followers was of two kinds, "disciples" and "students": to take in a "disciple" was a Green Gang tie, and one could take only a single master; taking in a "student," or taking a "teacher," was of a different nature, and one could take several "teachers" at once. Among the members of the various gang-connected bodies of the day, some "took an elder-master" (that is, a master) and some took a "teacher," so that some held a Green Gang rank and some did not.

There are also a few men whose generation is unclear but who had a certain power in society, such as:

Xie Baosheng, a Suzhou man of donkey-driver origins, later came to Shanghai and opened the Carter Baths, the Carter Hotel, and the Ciro's ballroom; he was close to the traitor Chen Qun and served as police commissioner of the puppet Jiangsu province.

Lu Liankui, chief criminal inspector of the International Settlement police, was close to the traitor Chang Yuqing and was assassinated early in the War of Resistance.

Lu Wenying, a woman nicknamed "Lu the Seventh," a native of Baoshan county in Shanghai, dealt specially in the "flower-lottery" gambling and was known as the "Flower-Lottery King," taking in a great many disciples.

Xu Tieshan, a man of Shaoxing in Zhejiang, had studied at the Tongwen College in Shanghai and later served as a translation teacher at the Shanxi police school, and under the enemy and the puppets took in a great many Japanese rōnin as disciples.

### VI. The Green Gang's Influence on Shanghai Society

There are two different verdicts on the Green Gang's influence on society. Because the three men who founded the grain gang—Weng Yan, Qian Jian, and Pan Qing—had all originally been men of the Heaven and Earth Society, which was an organization within the Hongmen dedicated to "overthrow the Qing and restore the Ming," while Weng Yan and the others carried grain for the Qing government and so played a part politically in stabilizing Qing rule, they went against the Hongmen's aims, and Hongmen men therefore regard the Green Gang as a traitor to the Hongmen. But Green Gang men, for their part, say that in opening the gang and taking in disciples to build up strength, they too aimed at overthrowing the Qing and restoring the Ming. Still, during the period when the three men Weng, Qian, and Pan opened the gang and carried grain, there was no activity against the Qing government; only later were there Green Gang men who took part in anti-Qing risings here and there. By the time of the 1911 Revolution, no small number of men in both the Green and the Hong gates answered Sun Yat-sen's call, joined the ranks of the revolution, and made their contribution.

The Green Gang's influence on Shanghai society was far-reaching—especially as the imperialist concession authorities used the gang and gangster forces as the claws and fangs of their colonial rule. When the imperialists shipped in great quantities of opium, for instance, it was chiefly through gangsters—mostly Green Gang men—that they put it about. Besides this, the gangs merged with the gangster element and reached into every corner of society, opening gambling houses and brothels, marking off turf and lording it over it, doing every kind of evil and oppressing decent people, and became a force for evil in society.

Shanghai's gang figures had originally operated only in society, and in the lower reaches of society especially, and the taking of masters and transmitting to disciples was mostly done in secret. But once Chiang Kai-shek used Shanghai's gang and gangster forces to launch the April 12 counterrevolutionary coup, no small number of gang men and gangster bosses stepped onto the political stage, publicly forming bodies one after another, each setting up his own door and expanding his power, and in certain quarters a fusion of government and gang took shape, its arrogance beyond all bounds. In Shanghai's political, economic, and social life, and even in its cultural, educational, and publishing enterprises, they had a deep influence, which was not cleared away until after liberation.
