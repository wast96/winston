## Chapter 6

The next day, after breakfast, Stephen went off; I arranged to meet him at five at the Liti Café, then went to the bank to cash the cheque he had lent me, and, taking the money and following the address on Bai Ping's pawn ticket, went to that pawnshop to redeem the diamond ring. After lunch I went to Nanjing Road to buy a case to fit the ring, and chose one of white silk edged with silver. At half past three I telephoned Bai Ping from the Liti Café.

"Who is it?" — Bai Ping's voice.

"The gentleman who went from the gambling den to the church."

"The Liti Café again."

"Exactly so."

"Is it loneliness at your side again?"

"No," I said. "It is four thousand dollars at my side."

"Do you mean to give me back those four thousand?"

"Not at all."

"Do you mean to spend it?"

"No."

"Then you want me to pay for your tea?"

"Would that please you?"

"Of course," she said. "I'll come at once."

Less than half an hour after I hung up, the silver car had stopped before the Liti Café.

And sure enough it was the silver lady again — dressed just as she had been the day before last.

When she had sat down, I said:

"Will you allow me the honour of giving you a present today?"

"Is there any present more honourable than your red leaf?"

"There is," I said. "Second only to the red leaf you gave me."

"A cup of coffee," she told the waiter, then said with a soft, dreamy smile: "I thank you in advance."

So I took out the white-silk, silver-edged case, and said:

"Don't be surprised—" Before I could finish she cut in:

"Ah! So the four thousand dollars I staked has won my capital back."

Her cleverness overwhelmed me, and my pleasure vanished on the instant. I said:

"So your four thousand dollars and the red leaf were stakes laid on my 'red heart,'" I said, half smiling, half stinging.

"Yes," she said. "If it makes you angry, I thank you all the same — for you have still not treated me as a dance-hostess—"

The waiter brought the coffee, and the words broke off there. But she went on:

"Now I give the ring to you." Swirling her coffee cup, her eyes on the ripples within, she held the ring out to me: "A dance-hostess's heart can sometimes be as pure as it is."

"…" I fell silent, lit a cigarette, and blew the smoke before my eyes, to put a little more misty distance between us. But she blew the smoke aside, and said:

"Won't you accept this gift?"

"Do you really give away so lightly a thing another gave you?" I laughed, but not quite naturally.

"If that is what you think of me, then I truly regret, for your sake, the honour of the red leaf you gave me — how did it not occur to you that I would never give it to anyone else?"

"…" I said nothing, but my heart was shaken: could what Stephen had said to me really be so much to be trusted?

"Take my gift." She fixed her large round eyes on me. "And let us talk of other things."

Whether cowed by the threat of her gaze, or ruled by some feeling in myself I was not aware of, I reached out and took the white-silk, silver-edged case, and could not help saying:

"Thank you."

"Now that's a good boy." Her smile was a lily just opening.

"Good boy" — the word made me realize how childish and innocent the look on my face must be.

At once I blew smoke before me, to keep such a barrier between us for ever.

But even within that brief barrier I began to repent the absurdity of my accepting the gift — though it was now a thing past mending.

At last Stephen came. We fell into easy talk and happy laughter. All that day and night, but for my repenting now and again of the gift in my pocket, we were every one of us happy.

After that I was always afraid to go and see Bai Ping alone. On the third day I raised a sum of money and bought a diamond ring much like the one Bai Ping had given me, and put it in the silver-edged silk case I had bought that day. I had meant to take it to the Liti Café and ask her out, but in the end my inward timidity kept me from it; and since I did not wish Stephen to see when I handed it to her, I had no choice but to go with Stephen to the Paramount, and there, while I danced with Bai Ping, I said:

"Now it is my turn to have the honour of giving you a more lasting present."

"You haven't made the present I gave you into a stake of mine, I hope?"

"I have not," I said.

"Then thank you."

So I handed her the gift from my pocket; and when I got home, on the one hand I felt as light as if I had paid off a debt, and on the other as though I had pledged myself to a still larger loan.

After that I never once went to see Bai Ping alone — but today I was to ask her, for 18 March, to Stephen's dinner-dance.

Could Bai Ping, then, be the real Mrs. Stephen after all?

I thought not. The chance was at least smaller than for anyone else; and above all, on this point Bai Ping would not lie to me. So I picked up the telephone:

"Miss Bai Ping, please."

"Who is it?" — Bai Ping had come on.

"Your sweetheart, of course."

"Just so. I knew it was about time you called."

"Have you become Mrs. Stephen, then?"

"Is that someone's rumour, or Stephen's drunken raving?"

"It is my own nerves," I said.

"Don't you want to talk it over with me face to face?"

"I do," I said. "But the day is the 18th, half past three in the afternoon."

"Will you be alone?"

"Of course."

"How strange!"

"Don't be surprised," I said. "But could you give the whole of that day to spend together with us?"

"To do what?"

"To go to a very formal dinner-dance."

"I can."

"Then I thank you," I said. "And one more thing — when you see Stephen, say nothing of this."

"Of course."

"Goodbye, then. Remember: the afternoon of 18 March. At the Liti Café."

"At your command."

I heard her hang up.
