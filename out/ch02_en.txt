Chapter 2. The May Fourth Awakening
The second year after we moved to Xujiapeng in Wuchang was 1919, the year the May Fourth Movement broke out. That spring my father sent me to Changsha, the provincial capital of Hunan, to study at the Zhounan Girls' School. Though I was leaving home alone to board at school, my uncle worked in Changsha and could look after me close at hand, so my parents were fairly easy in their minds. The school name I used at Zhounan was Huang Zhangding, style name Shuyi.
The Zhounan Girls' School was founded by the progressive educator Zhu Jianfan and his wife. The couple, returned from study in Japan, sold their family property and beggared their house to build a school, founding this private girls' school in 1905. Zhu himself served as principal, and the teachers he engaged were all progressive intellectuals. This famous Zhounan Girls' School trained many outstanding people, among them Xiang Jingyu and Cai Chang, both pioneers of China's women's revolutionary movement. My father greatly admired Principal Zhu, and had a student of his own teaching there, so he decided to send me to study at the school, where I had the good fortune to receive the enlightenment of the May Fourth New Culture Movement.
Principal Zhu was very enlightened. He supported the students in forming a student union and encouraged us to read widely outside the curriculum; every week he even lectured to us himself, especially on China's modern history—laying bare how corrupt and incompetent the Qing court had been, how, unable to withstand foreign aggression, it had been forced to sign with the Western powers and Japan so many unequal treaties that stripped the country of its sovereignty and shamed it; and how dark and cruel feudal society was in prizing sons over daughters and in discriminating against and oppressing women. He said: Why do I run a girls' school? My wife and I have made a vow to do what we can for China's women's liberation movement. Though the movement has now been talked of and worked at for many years, and has raised all sorts of demands such as women's participation in politics, the most important thing is still to begin with the spread of women's education; only when women at large can be educated can they be brought to understand and to achieve self-respect, self-regard, self-reliance, and self-strengthening, and become people truly useful to society. He also told us of the revolutionary deeds of the heroine Qiu Jin, as a model for us to learn from. When I was in primary school in Liuyang I had heard my teachers speak of these principles, but never so thoroughly as Principal Zhu put them. From childhood I had loved to hear my father and my teachers tell of China's ancient history; now, hearing Principal Zhu explain China's present state, awakening our patriotic thought, I received a first grounding in the anti-feudal, anti-warlord, anti-imperialist national-democratic revolution. When one drinks water one thinks of the source: had Principal Zhu not given us that enlightenment then, had I not gone through the baptism of the May Fourth Movement at Zhounan, I would not later have consciously joined the great revolution of the Northern Expedition and thrown myself into the people's revolutionary movement led by the Chinese Communist Party; nor would I have walked out of my own family—from opposing an arranged marriage and seeking my own liberation, on to seeking the liberation of all women, and rising to seek the liberation of all humankind.
Principal Zhu and his wife were both progressive intellectuals. When Mao Zedong founded the *Xiang River Review*, they were already in contact with revolutionary pioneers such as Mao Zedong, Xu Teli, and Xiong Jinding; so during the May Fourth Movement the actions of the Zhounan student union (there was a citywide student federation in Changsha) all had Principal Zhu's support and guidance. In those days everyone, with high patriotic fervor, marched in the streets shouting slogans like "Oppose the secret Twenty-One Demands," "Down with the traitors," and "Promote native goods, ban Japanese goods"; we also went to inspect the shops in the streets, and wherever we found a shop still selling Japanese goods, we would seize the profiteering merchant to be paraded through the streets, confiscate all the Japanese goods, and pile them up in the street to be burned. Looking back now, when the student movement was just beginning, though everyone was full of patriotic ardor, our actions could not help being somewhat naive. Just as in the early, spontaneous workers' movements of the international labor movement, when workers did not yet understand that the working class must unite to overthrow the rule of the bourgeoisie and the capitalist system in order to win their own thorough liberation, and could only vent their hatred on the machines, smashing them wildly—so when the students went to inspect Japanese goods in the shops, seeing a glass cup they suspected to be imported, they would throw it to the ground; seeing cotton yarn or cloth made in Japan, all called "enemy goods," they would either cut it up with scissors or heap it up and burn it. Later the student federation issued a notice telling the students not to be so extreme: if a profiteering merchant were found selling or hiding Japanese goods, he was to register and report it truthfully first, and then these confiscated Japanese goods were all to be gathered on the lawn before the gate of the Education Bureau and auctioned. The proceeds of the auction, by resolution of the student federation, went either to subsidize students from poor families or to private schools as educational funds. In this way the profiteering merchants were punished, the goods were put to fitting use, and the cause of education was supported—several ends served at one stroke. The student union soon corrected its wild smashing and burning as this asked. In those days we were all young and ignorant, and did not ask who was directing us. Only later, after I joined the Northern Expedition revolution, did I hear our tutor tell us that Comrade Mao Zedong had by then already taken part in guiding the Hunan students' May Fourth patriotic movement.
In the May Fourth Movement every one of us was fired with fervor, high-spirited and full of fight. That spirit of uniting together and sharing a common hatred of the enemy was the most exciting, the most inspiring, the most deeply instructive thing—still fresh in my memory eighty years after the event. Yet I was not able to go out of the school gate to march in the streets. Why was that? At the time, after the whole student body had held a great meeting together, three people were chosen to stay behind at the school to run the school paper; I, a newly enrolled junior student, was unexpectedly among those chosen. Because in primary school, under my father's tutoring and urging, I had laid a fairly good foundation in composition; and after I entered Zhounan, the class teacher often posted my compositions on the wall to commend them to the other students; so everyone chose me to edit the school paper. The paper—more exactly a wall bulletin, or wall paper—was made from the fresh things the students who went out to join the demonstrations discovered in the mass movement; they would come back and tell us, and we would record and write it up, and the next day put it together into a morning edition, post it up, and let more people know, to raise everyone's fighting spirit. Doing both the recording and the editing, I got great practice in the doing, and my ability to take notes and write articles rose all at once. When the summer holidays came, the student union decided in discussion not to take a vacation that term: everyone would stay at the school, on the one hand continuing to take part in the May Fourth Movement, and on the other making up thoroughly the lessons neglected because of it, so as to move up smoothly the next term. I was ready to abide by the collective decision, to stay at school over the summer and study hard rather than go home, when my uncle unexpectedly received a telegram my father had sent—"Mother ill, return at once"—asking him to take me out of the school and send me back to Wuchang. There was a reason behind it: that year my mother, giving birth to my fourth brother, lost too much blood, and, weak to begin with, fell ill after the delivery; though she did not go to hospital, she was all along at home under a doctor's care, taking medicine and recuperating, and truly needed me, the eldest daughter, to go back and wait on her.
Only after I got back did I learn that this was merely a surface pretext. It turned out that the school's dean of studies (everyone called him "the pedant" behind his back), though a student of my father's, was rather conservative in his thinking and did not approve of the students striking classes to join the May Fourth Movement. He held a straw poll among the students—did they approve of a class strike or not?—requiring every student to vote by name rather than by secret ballot. Since even Principal Zhu approved of and supported the students' patriotic actions, and since over the summer everyone could study and make up the missing lessons while continuing to take part in the May Fourth Movement and to do social investigation and broaden their extracurricular knowledge, most of the students approved of the strike. Having learned through the poll the names of the students who approved of the strike, the dean wrote separately to those students' families; he did not openly say that he opposed the May Fourth patriotic anti-imperialist student movement, but only said vaguely, please take your daughter home to be educated. When my father got his letter, not knowing what had happened to me, he thought I had broken some school rule and was very anxious; and because my mother really was seriously ill, he telegraphed my uncle and asked him to send me home. My uncle was then a doctor on the railway, and railway employees and their families enjoyed free rail travel, so sending me back to Wuchang cost nothing in fares. I had only just spent one exhilarating term of new life at the Zhounan Girls' School, taking in new ideas, and was boundlessly attached to the school and to my teachers and classmates; but because my mother was ill and the family had wired to hurry me, I had no choice but to bid a reluctant farewell to classmates and teachers. When they saw me off, everyone hoped I could return to school in a few months—who could know that once I left that school gate I would never go back.
My mother was indeed ill. As the eldest daughter—sharing my father's cares, nursing my mother's illness, tutoring my younger brothers and sisters—I was in duty bound. So that my father could work with an easy mind, I stayed home to care for my sick mother and to tutor the younger children in their lessons, while also studying the classics and histories on my own. My father drew up a timetable of study for me, laying down that on odd days I read the classics and on even days the histories. Reading the classics meant the Four Books and Five Classics (the *Great Learning*, the *Doctrine of the Mean*, the *Analects*, the *Mencius*, and the *Classic of Poetry*, the *Classic of Documents*, the *Book of Changes*, the *Book of Rites*, and the *Spring and Autumn Annals*). Before he went to work in the morning he would explain a passage of the classics to me; when he came home from work in the afternoon he would have me recite what he had explained that day. My memory was very good, and even the *Book of Rites*, which I liked least, I could recite after reading it a few times. Reading the histories meant the *Twenty-Four Histories* and the *Comprehensive Mirror for Aid in Government*, which I studied on my own; since I already had some grounding in classical prose, ordinary histories and old texts gave me no trouble, and my father let me read them by myself. Having read the *Comprehensive Mirror* and the *Twenty-Four Histories*, I gained a first understanding of the rise and fall of China's dynasties, and to this day I remember firmly the particular historical circumstances of many of them. Among historical figures I admired Yue Fei most, and can still recite his lyric "The Whole River Red." My father was usually very grave, commanding awe without anger, but when he explained the classics to me his face was kind and his manner patient, so that his listener felt quite at ease; and so between us, father and daughter, the questioning and puzzling-out was very equal and relaxed.
I still clearly remember one time he taught a passage from the *Analects*: "The Master said: Of all people, girls and servants are the hardest to keep. Be familiar with them, and they grow unruly; keep them at a distance, and they grumble." I asked my father: "If Confucius looked on women like that, did he then have no mother?" My father said patiently: no; Confucius's mother was very worthy and good. He meant only that in many cases it is harder to educate women. He went on to draw me out: since ancient times, all famous people who accomplished anything owed it to the teaching and help of a worthy mother and a good wife, so women in ancient times were educated too. Women's education did not begin only now; it is only that women's education now has a new content. In ancient times there were many well-educated good mothers and worthy wives who not only taught their sons and helped their husbands but themselves left many fine words and noble deeds to shine for a hundred ages. My father ended: "You might read the *Biographies of Exemplary Women*." So I read the *Biographies of Exemplary Women* on my own, and of them all I liked best the story of Hua Mulan. I loved to chant the "Ballad of Mulan," and admired her filial piety, her love of kin, her wisdom and courage: to take her father's place in the army was filial; "Mulan has no elder brother," so she went in place of her young brother—that was love of kin; she fought bravely in the army, and when her merit was won she withdrew, and in twelve years of campaigning never let anyone find out she was a woman—was that not both wise and brave? And so, after I joined the revolution, I changed my name to Mulan-admiring, Mulan.
Besides my self-study of the classics and histories, my father subscribed for me to some extracurricular books and periodicals, and had me take the correspondence course of the English correspondence school run by the Commercial Press; the examination papers I sent in each time all got full marks. Besides this, my father engaged a Mr. Tang to teach me arithmetic, to prepare me to sit the university entrance examinations later as a candidate of equivalent standing. In those days taking the university examinations was not, as now, rigidly bound to the curriculum content laid down in the schools; one did not necessarily need a middle-school diploma, and as long as you really had the knowledge you could get into a university all the same. Many families' children did not attend middle school but studied at home, and could get into university just the same. Apart from studying on my own the courses my father laid down for me, what I liked best was reading novels. Because I slept in my father's study, where there were many books—among the many classics and histories were also not a few old novels—my favorite was the *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*; the *Dream of the Red Chamber*, however, I did not like. Perhaps, girl though I am, I have somewhat of a boy's masculine spirit, and do not much understand or care for those tender tales of love between men and women. In short, I did not lose myself in those romantic novels. But after I read Guo Moruo's translation of Goethe's *Sorrows of Young Werther* I did admire it, thinking it truly reflected the vexed and stifled psyche of young men and women who oppose the bondage of the feudal system and long for the liberation of the individual. Among the new fiction published by the Commercial Press I much liked the novels of Mao Dun. Through my years of self-study at home I laid a fairly solid foundation in Chinese, which let me, when I joined the revolution and took up secretarial work, keep the minutes of meetings, draft propaganda outlines and slogans, and formulate rules and regulations without any difficulty. This was owing on the one hand to my own diligent study, and on the other to my father's earnest teaching. In those days my mother also required me to set aside two or three hours every week to learn some needlework—embroidery, working the treadle sewing machine, and the like. Though I learned simple embroidery such as cross-stitch, I never mastered the fine handwork of Xiang embroidery; nor did I learn my mother's skill in cookery, which I still think a great pity.
Though my life of study at the Zhounan Girls' School lasted only a brief half-year, the enlightenment I received there had a great influence on my later taking the road of revolution, and sowed in my heart the revolutionary seed of a longing for liberation. Seven years later I formally became a member of the Chinese Communist Party, resolved to struggle my whole life on the road of China's revolution. And so, when one drinks water one thinks of the source: I cannot but credit it to the enlightenment of the May Fourth Movement.
