### Savings Swindles

Ever since the prize-savings scheme was set going, the public, unable to see clearly where its advantage and its harm lay, all threw themselves into it eagerly, hoping to win the great prize that is longed for but seldom got; as for whether they would come off well or ill, they had no leisure to reckon that. And the promoters, playing besides upon the people's fondness for a lucky windfall and their greed for a little gain, made a great noise of advertisement with their honeyed words; and so the victims grew past all counting.

Had the prize-savings but been run according to their own rules, without cheating or fraud, honestly managed, the principal returned at term, open and aboveboard, one might still have forgiven them. But as it fell out, of those who set up prize-savings only a few could be trusted, and the rest all harbored a cheating heart, so that the thing came to no good end at all. Among the prize-savings societies that have already gone under—the Dongfang Savings, the Dongya Savings, the Yuandong Savings, the Suzhou Bank and several more—every one has folded owing enormous sums. The depositors, aggrieved at being so defrauded, brought suit again and again; but the men in charge had all made themselves scarce, and it came in the end to nothing. Worst off of all were the many small depositors, who had sacrificed no end of their blood-and-sweat money into the society, meaning to build a little into much and hoping one day to draw a lump sum, only to find the promoter's maw already filled and himself fled far away without a trace, so that they could do nothing but choke down their sobs.

Dongfang copied the method of the Wanguo, its promoter, one So-and-so, being himself an old Wanguo man; Dongya dealt only in taking small deposits from the out-ports; and Yuandong's method was craftier still, its scheme the more poisonous, for the savings certificate it issued to a depositor required but a single cash payment, whereupon month by month he might hope for a prize, and at the end of five years still get his principal back. The term was short and the formalities easy, and so the poor flocked to it like ducks to water; who would have guessed that in the twinkling of an eye it too would decamp with the funds and shut its doors, working harm without end?

To take the blood-and-sweat wealth of the poor and wretched and hand it over to lawless men, for the raising of foreign houses, the buying of motor-cars, the taking of pretty concubines and all their squandering—savings in name, embezzlement in fact, with never a thought for the economy of society or for the depositors' blood and sweat: judge it by feeling or judge it by law, it is not to be pardoned either way!

Saving is in itself a virtue, and there is real need to promote it and to practice it; but with obstacles like these before us and warning examples like those behind, a man is at a loss which way to turn! Henceforth one had best cast away the vain hankering after prize-savings, and be quick to choose some long-reputed, solid and trustworthy bank for a compound-interest savings, and so avoid being hugely swindled and treading the same ruinous road over again.

The most laughable thing of all: when Dongfang went bankrupt, and the police station sent men to the society to look into it and search that great iron chest, there were found but three dollars and a few jiao in ready silver, and a heap of worthless scraps of paper. Where, one wonders, had those several hundred thousand in great funds got to? Ah—savings swindles indeed!
