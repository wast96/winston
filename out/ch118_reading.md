### The Greyhound-Racing Habit

There are many pleasure-loving Shanghai people whose enthusiasm for the dog-races is thick indeed: when the evenings of Wednesday and Saturday come round they simply must go, exactly as with a pull at the opium-pipe—not to go is to leave the craving unslaked. And so, over and above the opium habit, the morphine habit, the mahjong habit, the dancing habit, the jai-alai habit, the huahui habit and the rest, the Shanghai man has now added one more: the dog habit.

The forming of a dog habit, they say, calls for a patience beyond the ordinary man's; one must study a thick special paper on the racing dogs, much as the huahui gambler pores over his Complete Book of Getting Rich; one must draw out snow-white silver dollars and change them for the special scrip used inside the dog-track; one must appraise the form of the several dogs as they come out, and then reckon whether to bet a place, or a straight win, or a double, that this or that dog will come in first; and should one be lucky enough to hit it, one must still shove one's way through the crowd to collect—a troublesome procedure indeed. Yet the dog-race gambler never utters half a word of complaint, for the one reason that he has already caught the dog habit.

When the race begins, an electric hare runs out in front and a pack of dogs chases after it; it looks for all the world as though they were catching it up, yet they never do—but though the chase never catches, the hope is always there. And the upshot? A pack of dogs works up a good sweat, the electric hare rests at its leisure, and this is what they call a dog-race.

There used to be as many as three dog-tracks: (1) the Mingyuan on Ward Road, (2) the Shenyuan on Yanping Road, and (3) the Yiyuan on Avenue du Roi Albert—the Canidrome. Later the foreign ratepayers of the International Settlement moved to have them banned; the Municipal Council granted the request and ordered the Mingyuan and the Shenyuan to close. The track-owners, refusing to submit, once brought suit against the Council, but the outcome upheld the earlier ruling and they were not permitted to reopen. At present the sole survivor among the dog-tracks is the Yiyuan alone, for it stands on Avenue du Roi Albert in the French Concession, and the attitude of the French Concession authorities differs somewhat from that of the International Settlement, so the Yiyuan has been able to carry on as usual. Down to today, after the Mingyuan closed it was for a time turned into an amusement park, and when business fell slack it soon shut up shop for good; the Shenyuan has now been made over into a football ground. Those afflicted with the dog habit, looking back on the past from the present, must feel a boundless regret.
