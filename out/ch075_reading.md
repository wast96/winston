### Calling Back the Soul

There are people fond of dabbling in ghostly games. Let some small child of the house take an odd fright and sleep badly at night, and the father and mother will at once suspect the child has been startled out of its soul; and the soul once lost, there is nothing for it but to hold a soul-calling (also known as "calling back the joy"). Of this soul-calling there are three kinds in all: one is calling by the bedside, one is calling in the corner of the doorway, and one is calling under the eaves.

Calling by the bedside is done, most likely, when the child sleeps ill and cries and whimpers: as the sky is about to pale, the mother taps softly on the edge of the bed and calls under her breath, "A-nan, come home!" (the word here means "come back"). She calls it ten times over, and the thing is done.

Calling the soul in the corner of the doorway is for when the child has fallen ill, and the immortals' remedies have not availed, and prayers for the gods' help have not cured it, and the mother is sure the little soul has flown up into the sky, so that a soul-calling must be held. First she buys two sticks of benzoin incense and lights them, folds a small sheet of red paper into a packet, lights a set of incense and candles, and knocks four resounding kowtows to Heaven; then one person carries a garment the child usually wears, another holds the little red packet in the left hand and the benzoin in the right, and another lights the way with the candlestick, and together they go to the corner of the doorway, behind the window, along the foot of the wall. The first calls out loud, "A-nan, come home! . . ." and the second answers softly, "Oh . . . she's come home . . ."; at each cry of the one the other makes his reply, until a little spider is found, whereupon their faces break into smiles and they say together, "She's come home!" The spider is put at once into the red paper packet, carried solemnly back to the room, and laid beneath the sick child's pillow.

There is yet another kind, calling under the eaves, which is done only when a child or a grown person has fallen gravely ill. A ladder must be set against the eaves; one person lights a paper lantern and holds in one hand forty-nine sheets of yellow spirit-paper, and cries out, "A-nan (or the grown person's name), come home! . . ." and burns one sheet of the spirit-paper—crying and burning by turns—until a spider is found, and all answer, "Oh . . . she's come home!" Only then is the performance over.

To fall ill and, instead of sending for a doctor and taking medicine, to put one's strength into this—by what road is such a sickness ever to be cured? And soul and body may not part even for an instant; were the soul truly lost, you might shout your throats raw and never hope it back. Of this small piece of common sense they know nothing at all—which is a thing at once contemptible and pitiable.
