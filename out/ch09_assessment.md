# Chapter Nine — synopsis and reliability

*Printed pages 177–200. Sections 31 ("Soong Mei-ling Says: A Man Like That
Should Be Bought Over"), 32 ("The Mockery and Reproach of the Cultural World"),
33 ("The Empty-City Stratagem on the Ninth Floor"), 34 ("Two Sisters Into
Danger, and a Maid's Terrible Death"). Chapter title: "Gun Sights on the League
of Nations Representative Lytton."*

---

## Verdict first

**This chapter describes something that cannot have happened.** Not
embellished, not compressed — impossible on its own printed dates.

The book dates its action to **2 to 13 November 1932** (both dates verified
against the scan, not merely OCR). In that window:

- The Lytton Commission had **left China on 4 September 1932**, the day it
  signed its report at Peiping.
- **Lord Lytton was in London.** On **2 November 1932** — the very day the book
  has Wang Yaqiao receive the letter that sets the plot in motion — he was
  speaking in the House of Lords in the debate on his own commission's report.
  By 21 November he was in Geneva for the League Council. In 1932 the journey
  between London and Shanghai took weeks.

So on the day the book opens its plot, its target was demonstrably on another
continent, addressing Parliament. There is no reading of the chapter that
survives this.

Nor is there an independent record of any such plot. The story circulates in
Chinese popular writing about Wang Yaqiao in near-identical wording across
outlets — including a *People's Daily* magazine piece that appears to be the
respectability-laundering node — with no archive, police record, contemporary
newspaper or memoir behind any of it. It is one source propagated, not
independent attestation. The Chinese Wikipedia article on Wang Yaqiao, which
covers the Soong shooting and the Lushan plot, does not mention Lytton at all.
No account of the commission in any language mentions an attempt on any
commissioner.

The one genuine documented threat to the commission is a different thing
entirely: **bandits planned to attack the train carrying Schnee and Claudel on
their journey out, intending to kidnap them for ransom**, and aircraft escorted
the journey as a precaution. Not Lytton, not Shanghai, not assassination, not
political protest, not Wang Yaqiao. If the episode has any documentary seed at
all, this is the likeliest, and it has been transformed past recognition.

## What happens

### Nanjing considers buying him instead

Chiang drives out to Xuanwu Lake, and the proposition on the table comes from
Soong Mei-ling: a man of Wang Yaqiao's capabilities should be recruited rather
than hunted. Chiang asks Dai Li bluntly whether he has really burned the bridge.
Dai Li cannot read what Chiang is driving at — the two sworn brothers now pass
as strangers — and later reports back that Wang took the money and refused the
meeting. Chiang says nothing, and seems still to be hoping.

### The writers shame him into it

Wang is brought to a gathering of Shanghai literary figures by a lawyer who
introduces him not as a gangster but as the man who went into the Huangpu to
blow up the *Izumo*. The room is startled: this is the Wang Yaqiao who
frightens Chiang and T.V. Soong, and he looks like a man of letters.

Then they turn on him. A young woman holds up a newspaper and asks how he can
sit easy while Lytton speaks for the invaders. An older writer is blunter: you
are supposed to be the great assassin — why is your nerve only ever equal to
corrupt Chinese officials, never to foreigners?

Wang commits, and insists on going himself, arguing that success would carry
internationally and show the world the Chinese are not the feeble people the
Lytton Report describes.

### Three attempts, all failures

**By knife.** Wang and Xuan Jimin go in over a windowsill in the small hours,
up to the ninth floor, along thick carpet to a suite number an inside source
has given them. The room is lit and silent — the empty city of the section
title.

**By poison.** The commission moves to Sassoon House on the Bund. Wang's people
cannot move freely there, so Wang Yaying recruits her younger sister, Cong
Yunwen — a student who has never been near this world — to pass poison to a
maid on the commission's floor. The opening is a dinner: Lytton, who normally
eats with the whole commission, insists that evening on eating alone in his
room before a municipal ball. The maid gets the powder into his soup. What
nobody has allowed for is *why* he chose to eat alone — he already fears poison
and has acquired a taster.

**By ambush.** Wang Yaying and Niu Anru are meanwhile on the floor at the
Paramount posing as a dancing couple, waiting for a guest of honour who never
comes.

The night ends with Cong Yunwen sobbing on her sister's chest: the target
lives, and a maid has been destroyed for nothing.

### On the Lytton Report itself — the irony the chapter misses

The report *was* denounced across China, and the fury was real: it accepted
that the Northeast was Chinese and that Japan's action was not self-defence,
but it also blamed the anti-Japanese boycott and "the Red menace," never used
the word aggression, and proposed an autonomous Manchuria under international
supervision — read in China as swapping one Manchukuo for another. The CCP
Central Committee attacked it within three days of publication.

What the chapter never mentions is that **Chiang Kai-shek instructed China's
Geneva delegates to take a moderate line and accept the report in principle.**
For a book this eager to indict Chiang's appeasement, that is a remarkable
thing to leave out, and it was available.

### Sassoon House — accurate furniture, invented event

The book's building is mostly right, which is this author's signature. Built
for Sir Victor Sassoon, designed by Palmer & Turner, completed September 1929;
ten storeys with a thirteen-storey tower section, so "thirteen storeys" is
defensible; restaurants and bars on the eighth and ninth. Chinese name of the
hotel 华懋饭店, the Cathay.

Two details are wrong, and wrong in a way that matters, because they are what
make the plot mechanically possible:

- **Guest rooms on floors 1–4.** The ground floor was let to banks; floors 2–4
  were a shopping arcade. The hotel occupied floors 5–9 only.
- **VIP suites on 10 and above.** Floors 10 and up were **Sassoon's own private
  residence**, not guest accommodation. A commission member could not have been
  lodged there.

The commission was certainly *banqueted* at the Cathay by mayor Wu Tiecheng on
15–16 March 1932. Whether it lodged there in September rests on a local-history
source that misdates other things.

### One real person, correctly named

The lawyer who convenes the literary gathering is **Shen Junru** (1875–1963) —
the OCR mangled the name and the scan settles it. He is a considerable figure:
a leading progressive lawyer, later one of the "Seven Gentlemen" arrested in
1936, and after 1949 president of the Supreme People's Court. The book places a
real and distinguished man in an invented scene, which is the pattern
throughout.

---

## For the final map

Chapter nine is the clearest case yet of the book's method, because here the
invention has no documentary seed at all — no relocated conspiracy as in
chapter seven, no genuine event underneath. What it has instead is **furniture**:
a real hotel with a real owner, a real commission with real members and real
dates, a real report with real and well-recorded Chinese fury, and a real
lawyer. Everything a reader might spot-check is sound. The event in the middle
of it never happened, and could not have.
