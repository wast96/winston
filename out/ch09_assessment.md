# Chapter Nine — synopsis and reliability

*Printed pages 177–200. Sections 31 ("Soong Mei-ling Says: A Man Like That
Should Be Bought Over"), 32 ("The Mockery and Reproach of the Cultural World"),
33 ("The Empty-City Stratagem on the Ninth Floor"), 34 ("Two Sisters Into
Danger, and a Maid's Terrible Death"). Chapter title: "Gun Sights on the League
of Nations Representative Lytton."*

**The chapter's central claim.** That Wang Yaqiao mounted a sustained
assassination campaign against **Lord Lytton**, chairman of the League of
Nations commission of inquiry, in his Shanghai hotel — by knife, by poison and
by ambush — in protest at the commission's report being soft on Japan.

## What happens

### Nanjing considers buying him instead

Chiang drives out to Xuanwu Lake and, for the first time since the Shanghai
fighting, looks something like content. The proposition on the table comes from
Soong Mei-ling: a man of Wang Yaqiao's capabilities should be recruited, not
hunted. Chiang summons Dai Li and asks him bluntly whether he has really burned
the bridge with Wang. Dai Li cannot read what Chiang is driving at — the two
sworn brothers have reached the point of passing as strangers — and the
question hangs over the chapter.

### The writers shame him into it

Wang is taken to a gathering of Shanghai literary and cultural figures by a
lawyer named Shen, who introduces him not as a gangster but as the man who went
into the Huangpu to blow up the *Izumo*. The room is surprised: this is the
Wang Yaqiao who frightens Chiang and T.V. Soong, and he looks like an
intellectual.

Then they turn on him. A young woman writer holds up a newspaper and demands
how he can sit easy while Lytton speaks for the invaders, when the papers had
called him a man of blood during the January 28 fighting and a Zhong Kui who
kills the wicked. An older writer puts it more cuttingly still: you are
supposed to be the great assassin — why is your nerve only ever good enough for
corrupt Chinese officials, never for foreigners?

Wang commits, and insists on going himself. His argument to his own people is
about audience: success would carry internationally and show foreigners that
the Chinese are not the feeble people the Lytton Report describes, and even
failure would put a fright into them.

### Three attempts, all failures

**By knife.** Wang and Xuan Jimin go over a windowsill in the small hours and
up to the ninth floor, along thick carpet in a dim corridor, to a suite number
his people have had from an inside source. The room is lit and silent. The
"empty-city stratagem" of the section title is what they find.

**By poison.** The commission moves secretly to Sassoon House on the Bund —
thirteen storeys, restaurant and bar on the eighth, nightclub on the ninth,
suites above, ordinary rooms below, and Victor Sassoon's name on it. Wang's
people cannot come and go there freely, so Wang Yaying recruits her younger
sister, Cong Yunwen — a student who has never been near this world and goes
white at what is being asked — to pass poison to a maid working the
commission's floor, Lin Jindi.

The chance is a dinner. Lytton normally eats with the whole commission, but
that evening, before a ball the Shanghai municipal social bureau is giving for
them at the Paramount, he insists on eating alone in his room. The maid gets
the powder into his soup. What nobody has allowed for is *why* he chose to eat
alone: he is already afraid of poison, and has acquired a taster.

**By ambush.** Wang Yaying and Niu Anru are meanwhile on the floor at the
Paramount, posing as a dancing couple, waiting for a guest of honour who never
arrives. When the American number ends they look round an emptying ballroom and
understand that Lytton is not coming.

The night ends with Cong Yunwen sobbing on her sister's chest: the old villain
is not dead, and a good woman has been destroyed for nothing.

## The problem on its face

Before any archive is consulted, the chapter has a chronological difficulty
that may be fatal to it.

The Lytton Commission was in China from roughly **March to September 1932**.
Its report — the document that outraged Chinese opinion by treating Japanese
grievances as real while declining to bless Manchukuo — was published in
**October 1932**, after the commission had gone.

But this chapter's motive is explicitly the report. Writers wave newspapers at
Wang and demand to know how he can tolerate what Lytton has written; Wang tells
his men that success would prove the Chinese are not as feeble as *the Lytton
Report describes*; Wang Yaying tells her sister the target is the man who went
to the northeast and then handed the League a report supporting Japan.

That reasoning requires a published report and a Lytton still in a Shanghai
hotel, and those two conditions may never have overlapped. If they didn't, the
chapter is not merely embellished but built on an impossibility — and the
question becomes whether the author knew and did not care, which given the
compression already documented in chapters two, five, six and seven would be
entirely in character.

## Other questions logged

1. **Is any plot against Lytton documented at all**, by Wang Yaqiao or anyone?
2. **Did the commission stay at Sassoon House?** The building details — Victor
   Sassoon, thirteen storeys, the floor plan — are checkable and look right,
   which is the book's usual pattern of accurate furniture around an invented
   event.
3. **Soong Mei-ling's recruitment proposal.** Is there any record of the
   leadership trying to buy Wang over rather than kill him? Dai Li is said in
   some accounts to have tried repeatedly.
4. **Lin Jindi and Cong Yunwen.** Both are the kind of name that vanishes on
   checking. Cong Yunwen is presented as the sister of Wang Yaying, whose own
   original name the glossary carries as Cong Yunyu — internally consistent, at
   least.
5. **The Paramount ball.** A municipal reception for the commission is exactly
   the sort of event that would be in the newspapers if it happened.
