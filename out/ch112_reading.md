### The Doctor's Three Scares

The men who live off official business are in the habit of using the "three scares" on thieves and bad characters; without the three scares, they say, the rogues turn slippery and will not own to the crimes they have committed. Who would have thought that nowadays there is a breed of doctor who takes the very same three-scares approach to his patients? What are the "three scares"? Suppose a man falls ill and calls one of them in to treat him. In truth the ailment is a slight one, and a single dose would set it suddenly right; but the doctor makes the most of it, telling the patient how grave and how dangerous his condition is, and at the foot of the pulse-record he never fails to write "awaiting your correction" or "submitted for the wise physician's judgment." If the illness really were grave, already past all cure, then to handle it so might be forgiven; but when the case is nothing serious and he still calls it perilous and dire, that is truly not to be excused—and it happens often enough that he lays an extra layer of dread on the patient, and a light complaint turns into a heavy one.

If one guesses at the mind of these doctors, it is nothing but an attitude that shirks all responsibility. For when a trifling illness has been called perilous and a single dose then cures it, the patient must sing the doctor's praises without end and thank him past all measure; while if by misfortune the case should take a turn, he cannot so much as argue the point, since the man has said all along how very grave it was! And with "awaiting your correction" already written at the foot of the pulse-record, why—it was your own carelessness, your own want of caution, your bad luck for the year, your just deserts, and none of it has the least thing to do with this brilliant physician. In fact, though doctoring is a trade like any other, it ought surely to carry some measure of charity in it. Meeting a grave and hopeless case, one should still choose one's words kindly and not merely terrify, not pile an added layer of dread on the patient; and with a light case one ought all the more to give real comfort. That is the true calling of a doctor!
