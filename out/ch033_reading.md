### The Flower-Smoke Rooms

Among the women who sell themselves there is a sort of smoke-girl, a grade lower even than the pheasant, and just about the lowest kind of prostitute there is. Their place of business is not called a house but a "flower-smoke room"; the Small East Gate of former days, and Lanfang Li, Mill Street, and Dog-Beating Bridge, were their strongholds.

Why "flower-smoke room"? Because when a client comes to the door, they bring out a box of opium boiled up from cheap dross and invite him to a smoke; and while he smokes they trade banter with him, and when the foul smoke is done (this opium is all of it so rank it cannot be borne), a towel is passed over, and with that the client is seen off — he gives her no more than two mao in small silver and ten coppers. If he would stay the night, a dollar and two jiao will cover it. A smoke-girl of any little charm may have to shut her door ten times or eight in a single day, and that is nothing out of the way. As to whether such creatures are poxed or clean — that needs no arguing to be plain.

The clients who go to the flower-smoke rooms may be roughly divided into two sorts. One is the carters, porters, and hawkers and their like, who, being pinched for money, cannot afford the better houses and must make do as they can; the other is the raw country hick, newly come to Shanghai, who, chancing to pass her door, is hauled inside by these smoke-girls in the manner of a kidnapping and cannot get away though he would, and must respectfully obey the order and go through one "cooperative" turn.

Since opium was strictly banned, they no longer serve the foul smoke to their clients (what they use instead I have not gone to find out on the spot, and must leave blank); but people's tongues are used to the old words, and to this day they still call her a "flower-smoke room" and a "smoke-girl."

Their houses are all along the main roads or inside the lanes, with a staircase set right at the front door; of the flower-smoke rooms, nine in ten have the very same fitting-out, and it may be counted a mark peculiar to them.
