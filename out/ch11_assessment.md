# Chapter Eleven — synopsis and reliability

*Printed pages 219–238. Opening section 38, "Taking the 'Non-Resistance
General' by Night." Chapter title: 辗转粤闽, "Wanderings through Guangdong and
Fujian."*

**Coverage note.** Synopsis covers printed 219–222, read off the OCR. The rest
of the chapter was not yet processed and is not summarized. The reliability
findings below cover the chapter's whole arc, since they are research-driven.

---

## What happens

The 1933 Spring Festival passes under pressure. A betrayal has forced Wang to
abandon a move to Hangzhou and stay in a Shanghai under white terror. He finds
out what being at the end of his resources means: in the worst of the old Anhui
fights there had always been someone willing to shelter him, and now there is
nobody. Dai Li has closed a net over the city, and his photograph is on wanted
notices and reward posters at every station and wharf. Wherever he puts down,
somebody informs Zhao Lijun's action squad.

He shaves off his moustache, takes off the black-framed glasses, puts on a
qipao, and crosses the lanes at dusk with his wife as a pair of sisters, to
shelter with a pawnbroker he once rescued from a lawsuit.

His men come back to him — Xu Zhiyuan from Suzhou with a dozen, then Zheng
Baozhen, Zhang Xianting and Yu Yanong from Changzhou — with news of who is in
prison and who is dead. There is a great deal of weeping.

Then Rehe falls. Wang reads that Zhang Xueliang has gone to Baoding to meet
Chiang in secret, and days later that he has resigned. Like most patriotic
opinion in Shanghai he puts the loss of Manchuria *and* Rehe on Zhang's account
personally, and when the papers report Zhang's wife arriving in Shanghai he
proposes that a general who loses the country's territory to save his own skin
should be punished.

The room is with him. Zheng Baozhen cites Ma Junwu's newspaper poem calling
Zhang a playboy dancing while the country fell. Xu Zhiyuan wants to hit him
before he can get away to Europe. Only the bodyguard Zhao Shifa demurs: nobody
knows what passed between Zhang and Chiang, and if Zhang is taking the blame
for Chiang then killing him achieves the opposite of what they want. Wang is in
no mood to hear it.

## How it holds up

### The Zhang Xueliang chronology is sound, with one date to fix

- **Rehe.** The campaign opened 21 February 1933 and **Chengde fell on 4 March**.
  If the book dates the fall to 7 March, that is the date Zhang telegraphed
  asking to be relieved, not the date the province fell.
- **Baoding, 9 March: correct.** Zhang left Beiping on the evening of the 8th
  and arrived at six in the morning. T.V. Soong came first with Chiang's actual
  message — resign, hand everything to He Yingqin — and Chiang followed for a few
  minutes of consolation and an instruction to fly to Shanghai for treatment.
  Two meetings, under half an hour in total. The chronology is unusually well
  documented because Wang Zhuoran was in the room and wrote it up.
- **The resignation telegram of 11 March: correct as issued.** There are two
  telegrams — the 7 March request and the public circular of the 11th — which is
  where confusion enters.
- **What Zhang was doing in Shanghai.** Being cured of a morphine addiction, over
  the month from 12 March to his departure, by **Harry Willis Miller**, the
  American Seventh-day Adventist known as the China Doctor. Soong Mei-ling
  suggested him. Zhang afterwards financed a string of Adventist hospitals,
  which is good evidence the relationship was real. Chinese popular sources
  calling Miller German are wrong.
- **He did not flee.** He sailed from Shanghai on 11 April 1933 on an Italian
  liner, reaching Brindisi on 4 May, in a party of about seventeen, with the
  departing Italian envoy Ciano and Edda Mussolini aboard the same ship. This
  was a planned exile arranged by Chiang and Soong, on a date set by paperwork.

### The chapter's premise is unattested even in the legend

There *is* a story about Wang Yaqiao and Zhang Xueliang, and it is not this one.
In the popular corpus, Wang has a bomb **with the fuse already removed** left at
Zhang's Shanghai residence with an anonymous letter offering three choices — go
north and fight, go home and kill yourself in apology, or hand over your fortune
to arm the Manchurian volunteers — with a live one to follow. A warning, not an
attempt.

Even that fails every test. No primary, contemporary or scholarly source carries
it. The date migrates between 1931 and 1933. The sum demanded is 100,000 in some
tellings and 200,000 in others. The delivery is a mailbox in one version and a
front gate in another; the object is a bomb in one and a grenade in another.
Documented detail does not drift like that; invented detail does. And the "one
month" Wang supposedly allows maps exactly onto the real 12 March to 11 April
span, which is the signature of a story built backwards from a known date.

**The book goes further than the legend.** Its section title implies Wang's men
physically *took* Zhang by night. Searched specifically for a kidnapping,
seizure or nocturnal capture: **nothing, anywhere, in any version.** If the
chapter stages one, that is the author's invention on top of an already
undocumented story.

The one place the two men's records genuinely touch runs the other way: Zhang
Xueliang, in his own oral history, discusses Wang Yaqiao — as a killer for hire
bankrolled by Hu Hanmin's faction and aimed at T.V. Soong, not at himself.

### Ma Junwu's poem is real, and its charge is false

Two satirical verses published in Shanghai's *Shishi Xinbao* on **20 November
1931**, naming three women. The film star Butterfly Wu **placed denials in the
*Shenbao* on the 21st and 22nd**, the two days immediately after. Scholars hold
she and Zhang never met in their lives. Another of the three had married a year
earlier — **with Zhang himself presiding at her wedding**.

Where Zhang actually was on the night of 18 September 1931: convalescing from
typhoid at the Peking Union Medical College hospital, and out that evening at a
Mei Lanfang benefit performance with his wife and Zhao Yidi, having invited the
British minister along.

So the poem is right that he was at an entertainment and wrong in every
particular of who he was with. The non-resistance decision was genuinely his;
the dancing-with-a-film-star image was fabricated. Zhang called it the piece of
writing he hated most, and it is the direct source of the epithet in this
chapter's section title — which makes it worth a note wherever the book uses it.

### Zhao Lijun is a real man, plausibly but not attestedly placed

Zhao Lijun (1905–1942), a former Communist who defected to Dai Li in 1931 and
became one of Juntong's "four guardians." He genuinely led the team that killed
Yang Xingfo in June 1933. His end is worth knowing: caught in 1941 trading guns
to the Japanese for opium, implicated in the disappearance of the inspector who
seized the shipment, and **executed in 1942 on Chiang Kai-shek's personal
order**, with Dai Li unable to save him.

But no source names him as assigned against Wang Yaqiao. Right man, right city,
right job, right year — and not an attested assignment.

### The associates: two solid, five not established

**Zheng Baozhen** and **Yu Yanong** are well documented, and both have real
post-1949 careers — Zheng as Hefei's first mayor, Yu as a vice-governor of
Anhui. Zheng was quartermaster to the 19th Route Army in 1932, which is directly
relevant here. The standard roster of the Iron and Blood corps is four men:
these two plus Yu Likui and Hua Kezhi.

**Xu Zhiyuan, Zhang Xianting, Xuan Jimin, Wu Hongtai and Niu Anru** appear in
nothing outside the Wang Yaqiao popular writing, and none is on that roster.

### What is solid about his situation

The manhunt is real. **In November 1932 the Nanjing government posted notices
offering a reward for him**, and he was genuinely a hunted man throughout the
period this chapter covers — so the chapter's premise holds even where its
episodes do not. He left for Hong Kong in **August 1933**.

### "Guangdong and Fujian" is really Hong Kong and Fujian

Nothing connects Wang personally to Canton, to Chen Jitang, or to the Southwest
Political Council. The "southwestern faction" in this literature is shorthand
for Li Jishen and Chen Mingshu, who were **in Hong Kong**.

And on Fujian: Wang **held no position** in the revolutionary government
proclaimed at Fuzhou on 22 November 1933. He is not among its eleven committee
members, not among the ministers or committee chairs, not among the founding
signatories of its party. The recurring claim that he ran purge and security
work for it sits directly against the documented fact that **Li Zhangda headed
the political security bureau**. His presence in Fuzhou is asserted at reference
level with no documentation behind it.

## The provenance finding

This chapter's research closes the question the earlier ones kept circling.

The claim that Wang Yaqiao was behind the Hongkou Park bombing **first surfaces
in 1983**, in 文史资料 written by his own younger brother **Wang Shuqiao** and
his nephew. Nothing before 1949 connects him to it.

And the decisive negative: **Shen Zui's memoirs, which discuss Wang Yaqiao at
length, say nothing of it.** Shen Zui was a Juntong officer writing from the
other side, with every reason to inventory the man's operations, and the most
famous one is missing from his account.

That is the strongest single piece of evidence in this whole project that the
patriotic Wang Yaqiao was assembled after the fact, by his own family, in the
1980s — and that the mainstream-looking reprints which appear to corroborate it
are popular-book excerpt columns, not archival reporting. A *People's Daily*
masthead over a 书刊博览 reprint is not a citation.
