## Chapter 23

Should I steal it without seeing Bai Ping at all, or should I see Bai Ping first and then seize the chance to steal it? Bai Ping would certainly not be home now; I could take advantage of her absence and try to steal it. But once I reached her house, in all reason I could only leave when I found her out, or else wait there for her; I could by no means linger long, take the document, and go. And if I meant to see Bai Ping first, then I should have to go to the dance hall to look for her; yet she might well not be there, and even if she were, she would surely be surrounded by a crowd of people, so she would make an appointment to see me at some later time, and thus, bound by the time she set, if I went before the appointed hour it would be somewhat abrupt. I walked out of the Benner Inn, weighing these two plans, along Great Western Road.

It was only about eight o'clock, but the streets were already forlorn; the lamps looked especially bright, casting my shadow to grope along the ground. The clouds overhead were frozen fast, the faint gleam of the stars like traces of tears; the roadside trees now had only bare branches, which made the telegraph poles look the more gaunt. I walked on beside the trees and the poles, with an unspeakable feeling in my heart, like a traveler with no home to go to, and like a thief out to steal in the dead of night.

A car sped past, full of Japanese soldiers; this was just the beginning of their night's pleasures, and perhaps they had an engagement with Bai Ping, ready to dance wildly and drink deep till dawn!

The garages had been sealed up, and there were no rickshaws on the street either; I had to walk as far as Bubbling Well Temple before there was a tram to take. So I put away all these musings and quickened my steps.

As I neared Bubbling Well Temple, I saw a flower shop, very prettily arranged, which put it into my head to go in and pick out a beautiful bouquet. Near Bubbling Well Temple I bought some fruit as well, and only then took a tram to Bai Ping's place.

It was a long time since I had come to Bai Ping's, and as I reached the upper floor there was an unnatural feeling in my heart. But everything seemed unchanged. I knocked carefully; a thief's frame of mind set my heart pounding. It was Ah Mei who answered the door, and the moment she saw me she said:

"It's Mr. Xu; why haven't you come for so long?"

"I know Bai Ping is very busy," I said. "Is she at home?"

"No."

"May I come in?"

"Of course," she said.

I handed the flowers and fruit to Ah Mei and went alone into the drawing room. The arrangement of the drawing room had changed a little, but there was no sign that any guest came often. Ah Mei poured me tea. I said: "That room I used to live in, is it let out now too?"

"No," said Ah Mei. "It's become purely a study now."

"I'll go and have a look," I said, and stood up.

Ah Mei went ahead of me, and at the door of the room she came up to open it for me. At a glance I saw the books lining all four walls, and, as if startled, I could not help exclaiming:

"Books?"

"They belong to a friend of Miss Bai Ping's, left here for safekeeping."

The room had been done over. In the middle was a writing desk, and in front of it a small sofa. Further forward was a low, long table, with cushioned stools set about it; on the low table was an ashtray. On the writing desk were books and letters in disorder, as though someone were doing office work there. After a glance I walked over to the bookshelves; most of the books were on economics and politics, the greater part in English, no few in Japanese, and here and there a few French books as well.

In a twinkling I found that Ah Mei had gone out; a plan came to me all at once, and I hurried out, and, seeing Ah Mei just going into Bai Ping's bedroom, I followed her in and said:

"May I come in?"

Ah Mei smiled. "About what time will Miss Bai Ping be back?"

"There's no telling."

"Has she been coming back early lately?"

"Fairly early," she said. "Lately she's seldom back late."

"Then I'll wait for her here."

"Shall I telephone her?"

"No," I said. "I've nothing urgent; only it's a long time since I saw her, and today I'd like to talk with her the whole night. Would you buy a few things for me?"

"Buy what?"

"The best cigarettes, some fine wine, fresh pastries. Ah, and make some lavish sandwiches, some lovely fruit jelly, will you?"

"Why, so cheerful?"

"It's a long time since I came here," I said. "This place has become an old home I've long been parted from." So saying, I held out money to Ah Mei. But she said:

"There's money here."

"No," I said. "This is my affair."

Ah Mei took the money and came out with the vase from Bai Ping's room. She left me by myself; I sat down and began to take note of the room. The painting on the wall had been changed for a Shitao landscape, with a calligraphy slip by Ren Jinshu beside it. The furniture was slightly altered, and all the books had been carried out, moved to the study, I supposed; on the table were a few copies of American and Harper's, and just as I was about to take one up to leaf through, Ah Mei came in holding the vase, in which she had already arranged the bouquet I had brought. I said:

"Have there been many visitors lately?"

"Very few, very few."

"Does Miss Mei Yingzi come often?"

"She's never once been here."

As she spoke, Ah Mei carried the vase to the lamp table by Bai Ping's bed. Having set the flowers down, she said:

"Then I'll go and do the shopping."

"Good, thank you," I said. "Will you lock the door?"

"Are you going to stay here?"

"If you don't treat me as an outsider," I said. "This room makes one not want to leave once one has sat down."

"Then stay here," she said. "I'm off." After Ah Mei's figure had disappeared, I heard the outer door close, and I rose softly; my heart suddenly began to pound. I walked slowly to the outer room, went to the door to make sure Ah Mei had really gone. I made a round of every room and found that in all this world there was now only myself, but my heart beat the tighter. I went to Bai Ping's bedroom. The wardrobe door was locked, and the middle drawer of the writing desk was locked too; the other drawers that would open I examined one by one. In one lay two or three letters, one of them in Japanese; I longed to read her letters, to prove whether her identity was after all what Mei Yingzi supposed, but I had no time: I had to search quickly everything that could be searched. As for the locked places, I could only wheedle the keys out of Ah Mei, or get Bai Ping drunk and steal the keys off her person. I turned out every drawer, the five-drawer chest included, and found nothing of what Mei Yingzi had described. At last I went to the trunk room behind her chamber, but the door was locked and I could not get in; so I went to the study. Three of the desk drawers were locked; those that were not held nothing of note: one was full of letters, one held odds and ends, one some bills and envelopes and notepaper. The room was very simply furnished, and there was nowhere else to search. I thought the thing must certainly be in the locked drawers; the locks were of fine make, by no means to be opened at will. I thought of prying up the bottom board of a drawer, but though the prying seemed easy enough, setting it back would be hard. I reckoned Ah Mei would be gone half an hour, and more than half of it had passed already. It was impossible. I could only wait for Bai Ping to come back and find some way to get her to open that middle drawer, which I felt was the one most likely to hold the document, and to get her to open it before me by chance, so that I might know for certain the document was inside; then tomorrow I would come again with a scheme for opening the drawer worked out, and be sure of it. But how was I to get her to open the drawer for me? A wild fancy took me; I fished out a name card and, with the fountain pen on the desk, wrote:

"Whenever you open this drawer, please telephone me."

But I did not push this card into the drawer, for just then I suddenly thought of the little inner room where I had first put my luggage. I went over; the door was not locked, and inside it was very empty, piled with old newspapers and magazines, and underneath were two trunks that had always stood there, which before had seemed to be pressed beneath my own luggage and never to have been opened. I tried the trunks; they were locked, but they looked somewhat like my own trunks, so I took out my keys to try. Just then I noticed on the handle a strip of white cloth gone gray, on which were written the words "In safekeeping, the Tao residence." I tried my key, and by pure chance it fitted; sure enough, one turn and it opened. I was just about to shift the newspapers on top when the outer lock rattled. I started, came out at once, drew the door softly shut, pulled a book from the shelf as I passed, and sat down on the sofa; I had already heard Ah Mei's footsteps.

"Ah Mei, you're back?" I said, rather loudly, still sitting.

"Yes." I had left the room open so as to hear the lock outside, and when I heard her voice I had already caught her steps out of the corner of my eye.

"That was quick." I stood up and went out to meet her.

Ah Mei had indeed bought everything I asked for, and I very eagerly helped her carry the things to the kitchen. Not until Ah Mei was busy making the fruit jelly did I take a tin of Abdulla and a box of Era into the study, and this time I shut the door.

I reckoned Ah Mei would not leave the kitchen for a while, so I quickly took out the key, ran into the little room, and locked the trunk again. Anxious as I was to see what the trunk held, I had to be very careful and rouse no one's suspicion. So I came out quietly, shut the door, and browsed along the shelves. The books were plainly sorted: two walls held social-science works, on economics most of all; one wall was mixed, with books on philosophy, psychology, anthropology; one held only literary works. I pulled out a book at random, sat down on the sofa, and turned the pages, but could take in nothing at all. My watch showed it was past ten, and I began to feel uneasy and lonely. I opened the Abdulla, smoked one, and strolled out to find that Ah Mei had already put the fruit jelly in the icebox and was making sandwiches; she asked whether I wanted tea.

"No," I said.

"Are you tired of waiting?"

"No," I said. "Only I'm putting you to too much trouble; finish up and go to bed early."

"I never sleep before twelve any day," she said with a smile.

After a few words I went back to the study. I began to regret not having opened that trunk just now; if I had, I might already have found the document I wanted. But now it seemed I could move even less freely. I paced about the room, put the book I had been leafing through back in its place, and walked on along the shelves. Coming to the social-science shelf, at the level of my own eyes, was a row of economics books, and I read off the titles as I went, without thinking: Contemporary Theory of Monetary, Monopoly, Money, Faust. I was struck: how did a copy of Faust come to be here? Without thinking, I drew it out, and found something tucked inside. I opened it, and it was a white sealed envelope, made of thick paper, printed with the words of the Japanese Navy Ministry. My heart suddenly began to pound; on the back there was indeed sealing-wax, with a seal upon it, but I had no time to make it out closely. My heart was racing, as if someone in the doorway could see me at that very instant; but I held the book steadily, noting on the one hand that the page it was tucked between was page eighty-three, while stealing a glance to see whether Ah Mei might come in at the door.

No, the door was properly shut. Now there was no more room for hesitation; I took the envelope, but my inner coat pocket would not hold it, and the outer pockets were too small and too conspicuous besides. In the end I stowed it between my shirt and my woolen sweater, flat against my chest. The document was not thick; with my waistcoat buttoned there was no trace of it at all. But my heart went on pounding, as though I had committed some great crime, and as though someone were at the door; I looked, and the doorway was quite still. I drew a long, deep breath and began to put the Faust back in its place, taking care a time or two that it stood exactly as before.

Then I walked softly to the door, and suddenly heard the sound of someone outside; I started, and pulled the door open at once.

"Meow." It was only Jimi, the Persian cat, stretching lazily as it came in at the door.

I went out, but Ah Mei was not in the kitchen after all; a little alarmed, I called:

"Ah Mei."

Ah Mei answered from the bathroom, and in a moment she came out. I said:

"There was a sound at the door just now; I thought Bai Ping had come back, but when I looked it wasn't, so I wondered whether you had gone out."

"No, I was washing clothes; it must have been Jimi," she said with a smile. "Do you want anything?" "No."

"Are you fretting with the waiting?"

"No," I said. "I'm quite happy reading."

So saying, I lit a cigarette and went back to the study. By now my heart was somewhat calmer; I drew a book of literature from the shelf, sat down on the sofa, and in the most comfortable posture bent my mind to reading, hoping to forget for a while the unease within me. It was a book on imagination in literature; I cannot now recall its author. He divided imagination into four kinds: first, creative imagination; second, associative imagination; third, interpretive imagination; fourth, hypothetical imagination. He held that creative imagination selects elements out of various experiences to form a new whole; that associative imagination refines the spiritual elements in an object, or endows the object with spiritual value; that hypothetical imagination supposes in the object its own life, feeling, and sensation. In the book the author gave long arguments and examples, but I felt the classification too rigid; it might help somewhat in study, but was of no use in appreciation. The author spoke only of literature, but I thought that creative imagination seemed to find more use in religion, associative imagination was that most used by musicians, interpretive imagination that most used by painters and sculptors, and hypothetical imagination that most used by poets. If one spoke by schools, Romanticism seemed to use creative imagination most, Realism associative imagination, Symbolism interpretive imagination, and Expressionism, it seemed, associative imagination.

I laid the book on my knee, and as I sat alone letting my thoughts run wild like this, the door suddenly opened; I seemed to wake with a start from a dream, and my heart leapt.

It was Bai Ping!

"Did I startle you?" Bai Ping, in a navy-blue woolen cheongsam with red stripes, stood in the doorway, one hand slowly drawing the door shut.

"Ah, Bai Ping," I said. "You're back?"

"What are you thinking about, all alone?" she said.

"Reading this book," I said, taking up the book from my knee and standing up. "I was just thinking about its classification of imagination."

"Then will you talk with me about it?"

"Of course I can; but it's a long time since we met, and I want to discuss a more practical matter with you." I put the book in my hand back on the shelf.

Bai Ping had already sat down at the writing desk. I said:

"You don't think it forward of me to come to you?"

"You're most welcome."

"You've changed a great deal."

"In myself?"

"The place too," I said. "All these books."

"Left in keeping by someone else," she said.

Just then I suddenly became aware of the dust on my hands, and it flashed on me that I had picked it up in the inner room; there must be traces of me left in there, and I had to find some way to cover them. But I still looked at her and said:

"You seem to have put on flesh."

"Surely not?" she said. "It's a long time since you came."

"I often meant to come and see you, but because you'd said to wait for your call..."

"You've come at a good time today; these last few days I've been wanting to telephone you every day."

"I thought you must be too busy." I paced back and forth as I spoke, looking around, and said:

"This room, arranged like this, seems more dignified still." As if carelessly, I walked toward the inner room; as if carelessly, I opened the door, and going in, I said:

"Is this still the trunk room?"

"All left here for safekeeping by others," said Bai Ping, coming over. I deliberately shifted the newspapers about and said:

"You still keep old newspapers?"

"Mm..." she answered behind me; I turned my head and saw her faint smile, like a lily just opening.

That smile made me think of the feeling and the distance there had been between us in the past; and it dawned on me: did today's talk show too great a distance between us? Or had my own doings today thrown me off my usual manner? Or had her attitude toward me changed in some essential way?

For my part, standing on the side of justice, I was confident my actions were right; but before this person who had once trusted me utterly and borne me boundless friendship, I felt deeply ashamed of what I was doing. By my usual temper and disposition, I should certainly have poured it all out to her with the truest feeling, and won her over with the most upright reasoning; I should have got her to hand me the document of her own will, for me to carry to Mei Yingzi. But this was the very thing Mei Yingzi had warned me against again and again, a prohibition I was bound to keep; and at the same time, having already stolen the document, I had forfeited any right to be loyal to my friend. In the instant of her smile, a kind of inspiration seemed to come over me; with a look of the utmost sincerity, I traced from the corner of her mouth to her starlike, innocent eyes, took her arm in my hand, and led her out of the inner room, saying in a voice from the depths of my throat:

"Do you still count me your best friend?"

"Of course." She smiled.

"But I'm thinking now of leaving Shanghai."

"To the interior?"

"Yes."

"I urged you to, long ago."

"I hope you'll go with me."

"So it was for my sake you didn't go." She let go my hand, laughed ringingly, and threw herself onto the sofa.

"The truth is I can't feel easy about you." I sat gravely on the footstool before her and said coolly.

"It's you being here that makes me very uneasy," she said, suddenly grave.

"But you never once telephoned me to come and see you."

"Because I was busy."

"Busy," I said. "That is just what I can't feel easy about."

"Why should you be uneasy about me?" she said. "I'm a dance-hall girl; being busy is my income. You ought to be easy about it."

"You talk of income?"

"Of course. I tell you, you can go to the interior and do what ought to be done; if I went, I'd only be a drain." She said: "I hope you won't think anything on my account; go off properly by yourself, and if you need money, come and get it here."

"Do you think I've come to borrow money from you?" I stood up.

"Well," she said, "is it a disgrace to borrow money from me?"

"That's not the point," I said. "If I wanted to borrow money from you, I'd simply borrow it; why say all this other to you?"

"Then you've come to persuade me to go with you."

"Yes," I said. "I want to know your mind, for I've already put all my affairs in order; if you won't go, then I've decided not to go either, and afterward I mean to see you often. We ought not to be so hard to meet as this."

"Then it's as you please," she said, and stood up and went out.

I sat there alone, my heart full of disordered, uneasy feelings. Bai Ping's manner seemed to be a self-abandoning fall from grace, yet her earnest hopes for me were something I ought always to be grateful for; standing on the highest ground of friendship, I was bound to give her my most sincere counsel, frankly; but that was the very thing my duty forbade. I guessed she had come home at twelve, and that Ah Mei could not yet have gone to bed. She had come in and taken off her overcoat; perhaps she had seen Ah Mei, or seen my coat and hat on the rack, so that she could open the door and come in so composedly; and from her expression, she seemed to have no suspicion at all of my mission. I dearly wished I could leave here at once and go to Mei Yingzi's, so as to get the original back the sooner and put it in its place; but for the moment there seemed no way to get away. Now I turned over whether I had left any suspicious trace. I had gone to the trunk room in front of her, so that even if there were marks of me in the dust inside, she would surely no longer suspect I had searched anything before she came. What else? There seemed to be nothing in the drawers; and if there was any surface disturbance, it was only the chance doing of a man alone and waiting. Then I thought of the bookshelf, and my eyes went at once to the Faust. I had forgotten how it had looked before I took the document; I strove to call up how it had been and compare it with now, and it seemed to me the two covers stood a little loose, but I realized at once that this was perhaps the illusion of overwrought nerves.

"Xu, come and sit over here." These words recalled to me that Bai Ping had gone out just now; I stood up and went out through the door.

Bai Ping had changed into a gray cotton cheongsam and, carrying the food Ah Mei had prepared, was walking toward her own bedroom; I followed her in.

Bai Ping spread a cloth on the round table, and I helped set out the midnight supper. She took the white vase Ah Mei had set on the lamp table and put it on the round table; in the lamplight the flowers had a peculiar grace. Bai Ping sat down, every kind of serenity gathered at the corners of her eyes, a touch of weariness showing between her brows; she gave a faint sigh, drank a mouthful of tea, and said:

"Thank you for caring about me still."

"You've forgotten me."

"I've been so busy I've forgotten everything!" she said, leaning her head back on the sofa and closing her eyes.

This image brought back to me the way she had dozed lightly on the train coming back from Hangzhou, and I remembered the sketches I had made of her that day, which were in my notebook. I had long forgotten them; later the notebook had been tossed into a drawer, and I recalled that by the time I moved to Bai Ping's it was already nowhere to be seen, and now I had even less idea where it was. The memory was really rather strange, for on the one hand it stirred in me an indescribable feeling for Bai Ping. I felt that Bai Ping had never once done me the least wrong, and yet today I was using her old and constant feeling for me to steal her document; a shame rose up in my heart, and I felt I ought to explain it to her frankly. But on the other hand some force seemed to hold me back; I gazed at Bai Ping's weary figure and let the two different forces battle in my heart, until at last I spoke. I said:

"Bai Ping!"

This abrupt and bitter tone made Bai Ping open her eyes and rouse herself. I said: "If you go on like this in Shanghai, you're sure to be made a tool of by someone, and it may be that your best friend will become your enemy." My words were too stiff, my tone too bitter, as I felt only after I had said them.

"Do you mean you and me?" Bai Ping roused herself, sat up straight, and showed a faint smile.

"I was thinking, if I go into the interior and you stay on here..."

"I rather like having one friend among my enemies," she said. "And I very much hope that one day my enemy will become my friend again."

"Much as I like an enemy becoming my friend, I don't like a friend becoming my enemy."

Bai Ping bowed her head and was silent a long while, then suddenly stood up, paced away from her seat, and said, wide of the mark:

"Let us not talk of these things again. Between one person and another there may be love, but between one person and another there can be no understanding."

"You think I don't understand you?"

"I don't understand myself either," she said, coming back.

Suddenly she sat down on another sofa, a look of pain on her face, her head against the back of the sofa, both hands covering her face, and for a long moment did not move.

This expression struck me as an awakening of conscience, and now seemed the best chance to press my counsel. I resolved to disobey Mei Yingzi's warning, and prepared, in the most earnest manner, to make her tell me of her wrong course, and, with the most candid heart, to confess to her my mission tonight. I went softly over, bent down, and said close to her ear:

"Bai Ping, are you grieving?"

She made no sound and did not move; the document hidden at my breast made my posture very awkward. Stirred by a strange emotion, I knelt before her seat.

"Bai Ping, tell me, why are you suddenly like this?"

She began to sob.

"Bai Ping, take me for your friend, and tell me your heart."

She seemed to be mastering her feelings with her whole will, weeping in secret.

"Bai Ping, let us be frank with each other," I said. "Let us go together to the interior, to the mountain villages, and do educational work."

She seemed to have mastered her feelings and returned to a silent, motionless stillness.

"Bai Ping, if you really have an interest in political work..."

"Nonsense!" she cried out, rising at once and pushing me away, and said coldly: "Go home."

"Bai Ping..."

"Leave me alone."

"Bai Ping, surely..."

"I need to be alone," she said coldly, sitting down in another seat. "Get out!"

"Won't you let me say a few more words?"

"I won't listen!" She had grown angry; it was the first time I had seen her angry. Her great bell-like eyes gave off a scorching light, her lips locked in a look of resolve, her lashes glinting with the traces of her tears; she was all heat and force, like a lithe dragon coiling in a shallow marsh where it cannot stretch itself. She paced back and forth in the room, and said again:

"Get out; I detest you."

At any other time I believe I would have taken a more humorous line to soften her anger, and would have kept trying to calm her rage before leaving; but tonight, with my stolen goods hidden at my breast and a shame too painful to speak of ranged in my heart, I lost my very character in that scene. I said:

"Goodbye, then." I did not go over to her; as I bowed, the document at my breast let me lower my head only slightly, and I said in a low voice: "Forgive me, Bai Ping." On the surface this "forgive me" was for having brought her grief; but I meant it also for stealing her document. Whether it was conscience or some other inner impulse I do not know, but tears welled from the root of my nose to my eyes, and in what was left of my choked, sorrowful voice I said: "Sleep early; I'll come again tomorrow afternoon, and I'll bear any reproach you please."

Bai Ping did not give me a single glance; I walked quietly out the door, drew it shut, put on my coat and hat, and went down the desolate stair to the desolate street.
