## Chapter Twenty-Three. The Hour of the Dragon, Second Half (8 a.m.)

{v} By now the sun in the far east was bursting forth; the sky had grown bright, and the whole of the Yixiang Pavilion was beginning to fill with an intoxicating fragrance.

{d} The third year of Tianbao, the fifteenth day of the first month; the hour of the Dragon, second half.

{d} Chang’an; Chang’an County; Xinghua Ward.

Within the Jing’an Bureau, the runner of the main hall was a strange and contradictory figure.

He was everywhere in the Jing’an Bureau, and known to all. Every man there had seen the figure of him at a run; every man knew his sonorous, carrying voice. In and out of the main hall he went, again and again, again and again announcing the great matters that came and went. How much of the intelligence within the city of Chang’an had passed through his hands to reach the several officials in charge; and how many decisions, too, had passed through his hands to be scattered out to the watchtowers everywhere.

Yet the strange thing was that no one ever paid the least heed to his existence—did not so much as know his name. Everyone took him for granted, a thing that went without saying, for all the world like a summer cicada clinging to a tree in the Zhongnan Mountains: the more it sings, the stiller the wood. No one would ever deliberately bend his attention upon a mere runner.

And such a man as this was, of all people, the traitor within who had let the aphids in.

At first hearing it seemed a thing to shock the ears; but think it over with care, and nothing could be more reasonable. Who could pass freely, again and again, through every part of the Jing’an Bureau; who could grasp at the first instant the newest turns of the situation and every decision; and who, withal, drew not the least notice to himself—if not he, then who?

It was an artful illusion, and it had all but deceived every one of them. They had all been searching desperately, heads bent, far afield—while this traitor stood in the darkness beneath the very lamp, a mocking smile upon his face.

Adjutant Zhao looked down at the runner lying at the last gasp upon the ground, his face grave. He was not himself a man of the Jing’an Bureau, but he understood well that this man bore matters of the gravest weight, and that there must be no slip. To have caught the traitor did not mean the great work was done. The fellow must surely have his own connections; to find, by whatever means, the hidden hand that had directed him—that was the thing of first importance above all.

He must be got back to the Jingzhao Prefecture as fast as could be!

Yao Runeng’s arms still gripped the runner’s body fast, tight as an iron hoop. Adjutant Zhao gave the order to part the two of them, and several stout soldiers had to bear down by turns before they could pry the ten fingers loose at last—a measure of how deadly-hard a grip Yao Runeng had set before he fainted away.

The soldiers, all hands at once, bound the runner fast, gagged his mouth with a strip of cloth, got up a litter, and bore him off toward the Jingzhao Prefecture. Adjutant Zhao cast a glance at Yao Runeng, lying gravely wounded on the ground, and let out a deep, heavy sigh.

The wound in Yao Runeng’s back looked far from good; even should he wake, he was like to be a cripple for life. So spirited a young man, with a boundless future before him—and here, alas, he had been broken. Adjutant Zhao had once been coerced by this stripling in the Right Xiao Guard; yet now he could not but grant him, in his heart, a word of praise: well done. Had it not been for Yao Runeng’s flinging himself in without a thought for his own life, this traitor might well have got clean away.

What Adjutant Zhao could not make out was why the man had striven so desperately. Did the Jing’an Bureau pay so high a stipend as all that? Come to think of it, every man of the Jing’an Bureau he had met this day was some sort of freak: Yao Runeng one, Li Bi another, Zhang Xiaojing more than any—and even that woman was not altogether normal.

Adjutant Zhao shook his head, gathered in his wandering thoughts, and ordered a litter got ready to rush Yao Runeng off for treatment; then, thinking a moment, he dispatched a man besides to carry word of the traitor’s capture, as fast as might be, to Anye Ward. He knew Li Bi was there upon business, and that this news must be told him at the first instant.

These orders given, Adjutant Zhao at last found leisure to lift his head and look at the color of the sky. The light of dawn was growing ever brighter now, the black canopy of night faded to a pale blue-green. The day of the fifteenth of the first month was near to breaking; Chang’an, that had clamored the night long, would soon be bathed once more in sunlight.

Yet for some reason Adjutant Zhao felt a heaviness weighing at his heart, with none of the free, clear ease he might have looked for.

Wen Ran clapped her two hands together, knocking the last of the incense-ash from her palms, then carefully laid the newly pressed incense-stick within a hollow bamboo tube and slung it at her waist-pouch. Cen Shen stood behind her, his face grave:

‘Miss Wen Ran, are you certain you mean to do this?’

Wen Ran reverently lit a stick of spirit-summoning incense before Zhang Xiaojing’s memorial tablet, and only when she had watched the curling smoke truly rise did she answer: ‘Yes. I have thought it through clearly.’

‘You have only just, and with such trouble, escaped alive; you ought to be resting properly now,’ Cen Shen urged.

From yesterday morning on, this girl’s sufferings had never once let up. First carried off by the Bear Fire Gang, then locked up by the Jing’an Bureau, and at the first half of the Pig hour caught up in that great to-do at the Cibei Temple—driven from pillar to post, one fright hard upon another. An ordinary girl would surely have broken down long since.

Wen Ran, her face haggard, gave a stubborn shake of her head. Cen Shen sighed, knowing there was no more to be said.

Early on, at the Pig hour, Cen Shen had gone, as Wen Ran had charged him, straight to the Wen Incense Shop, taken down the shop-sign, and fetched away Zhang Xiaojing’s memorial tablet. He was just making ready to burn the two things when, to his surprise, Wen Ran herself came back.

A word of question and he learned that she had, all unlooked-for, come under Wang Yunxiu’s protection, so that Yuan Zai had at last given up the chase. But instead of staying on at the Wang residence, she had hurried back in all haste to the incense shop. Cen Shen was on the point of congratulating her on her escape alive when he saw that Wen Ran’s brows were knit with care. In the Jing’an Bureau she had caught a scattering of odd words and phrases, and had learned from them that her benefactor was fallen into grave trouble.

Cen Shen had supposed the girl would burst into loud weeping; he never dreamed she would come out instead with a wild, fantastical notion: Feng Dalun was the root of all the trouble, and if only she could seize and hold him hostage, she could wash her benefactor clean of the wrong done him.

The notion gave Cen Shen a start, and when he had heard out Wen Ran’s plan he was more astonished still. He had never thought that within that timid, frail-seeming frame there could lie hidden so tough and unyielding a temper. Yet, thinking it over, had it not been for just such a never-say-die tenacity, Wen Ran would likely have fallen long since into the hands of the Bear Fire Gang or of Yuan Zai, to await her death. Soft as this girl seemed on the surface, at the core she was hard as anything—and this, no doubt, was of a piece with her father’s ways.

‘My benefactor gave much for the house of Wen. Were he dead, I should tend his sacrifices through all four seasons and never forget him while I lived; but if there is even now one thread of life left in him, and I stand by with folded hands out of cowardice—how, when I am dead, should I ever face my father?’ Wen Ran said resolutely.

‘But to seize Feng Dalun as a hostage may not, even so, save your benefactor.’

‘This is all that I can do,’ Wen Ran answered, and raised her right fist to strike it against her left shoulder. Cen Shen asked what the gesture meant; Wen Ran said it was one her father Wen Wuji had taught her, and that it meant: nine deaths, and no regret.

Cen Shen was bold and open-handed by nature; turning it over again and again, he made up his mind to volunteer himself, and to help her carry through this righteous deed. For a scholar awaiting his examinations to plot the abduction of a court official—that was a grave crime indeed. But Cen Shen did not care; the affair was far too interesting, and would surely make a famous piece to be handed down through the ages.

He had all but settled on the title of the poem already.

The gate-commander of the Yanxing Gate was, just now, somewhat bewildered, and somewhat on edge as well.

The first thing he had heard and seen was the great blast from the direction of the Xingqing Palace, and the smoke and fire spreading across the sky. But he bore the charge of guarding the gate and dared not leave his post without orders; he could only wait, ill at ease, for word from above. Wait as he might, what came at last was an urgent letter sent down by the Gate Inspectorate, requiring a strict inspection of all who left the city. He had not yet set about arranging it when all at once he heard the street drums begin to boom. By the regulations, the gates were to be shut only when the drums had sounded six hundred beats. But soon there came in over the watchtower a command from the Jingzhao Prefecture besides, requiring that the gates be barred and locked at once, and all persons whatsoever forbidden to pass in or out.

These commands were much of a muchness, each more urgent than the last. But the gate-commander understood that they came from different offices, and that this meant the whole city of Chang’an was already in disorder: there was no one man holding the reins, and each office had no choice but to act upon its own judgment.

The Lantern Festival was not one day old, and already such a great commotion had blown up—what did those offices inside the city think they were about? The gate-commander grumbled a few words inwardly, and lifted down from its stand a suit of mountain-pattern armor, the little plates, each shaped like the character for ‘mountain,’ clashing and clattering as he did. In a time of crisis a military officer must put on his armor, and he dared not be slack about it.

His armor on, the gate-commander walked, a little clumsily, out of the duty-house, and in no good temper barked at the guard-soldiers to go and shut the gate at once. His personal attendant said in a low voice: ‘There’s no one over at the Gate Watch; those gate-servants are most likely still out looking at the lanterns and not back yet...’ The gate-commander glared: ‘Outrageous! Not one man left on duty? Do they want their heads off?’

To shut the gate was simple enough—a few soldiers pushing down the capstan and it was done—but to lock it was not so easy. The great Tang kept the strictest guard over its gates and doorways: the gate-commander might set the guard-soldiers in motion, but the keeping of the gate-keys was the charge of the Gate Watch. In this way the guarding of the gate and the keys to its lock lay in different hands, and the risk of anyone’s being bought off was lessened. If the gate-commander wished to shut the gate and lock it, he must send a man to find the Gate Watch, and have them dispatch a gate-servant to bring the keys over.

The night before, at the lantern-fair, there had been no curfew, and the city gates had stood open the whole night through. The gate-servants of the Gate Watch had, if you please, deserted their posts to go off and look at the lanterns, not one of them staying behind. The gate-commander ground his teeth with hatred of it, but for the moment there was nothing else for it; he could only get the gate shut first and see about the rest after.

Just then a guard-soldier came running up again: ‘There’s someone outside the wall asking to come into the city.’ The gate-commander thought to himself that this must be some idle fellow who had gone out to set river-lanterns afloat, and refused without a moment’s thought: ‘No! Tell them to clear off.’

‘Er... perhaps you had best go and take a look yourself?’ The guard-soldier’s face showed his difficulty.

The gate-commander knit his brows, gave his armor a shake, and strode up the steps to the top of the wall; he thrust his head over and looked down—and froze. By the light of dawn he saw, at the foot of the wall, a single rider on a single horse. The rider wore a bamboo rain-hat and the light-brown livery of an express courier—nothing out of the way in that. But the mount was another matter: the beast’s nostrils flared, a faint white froth at the corners of its mouth, plainly a post-horse just come off a long, hard road, and driven at a headlong gallop with no mercy for its strength. Slung crosswise on either flank were two huge baskets of yellow-green bamboo strips, their lids bound with brocade bands and sealed over with yellow paper; and behind the saddle there was planted a crimson Yinglong flag with a saw-toothed edge.

At the sight of that little flag, no more than a chi in length, the gate-commander’s expression changed utterly. He drew his head back in haste and, with his attendant, went clattering down from the wall-top, walked into the gate-tunnel, opened a narrow crack, and let this one rider in.

The gate-commander examined in person all the rider’s fish-tallies and credentials, and found nothing amiss; then he walked over beside the great baskets, but dared not lay a finger on the sealing-paper. He bent his head, saw slender wooden twigs poking out from the basket, and gave a sniff—a clear, fresh fragrance came to him. Straightening at once, he said to the envoy with a smile: ‘Your honor comes in the very nick of time; had you waited till the gates were locked, not even I could have opened for you.’ The envoy answered not a word, took back his credentials, gave his horse a squeeze of the belly, and passed through the gate-tunnel of the Yanxing Gate to charge straight into the city.

A guard, curious, asked who that had been; the gate-commander wiped away his sweat and said, dropping his voice: ‘That was an express courier come from Fuzhou. You saw the saw-teeth along the edge of that Yinglong flag? Seven of them in all—one tooth to a day; within seven days the goods must be brought to Chang’an.’

A soldier of Sichuan stock could not help crying out: ‘From Fuzhou to here must be a good two thousand li—seven days for it, and does that not mean there can be no resting even a moment on the way? What sort of goods could be worth so much?’ These soldiers watched the merchant caravans come and go day by day, and knew well the costs of freight and travel: to run so wildly as that, how many horses must be run to death along the road—so that, though the two great baskets were crammed with gold, it must still be at a loss?

To his men’s curiosity the gate-commander answered with a single word: ‘Lychees.’ The Sichuan soldier exclaimed again: ‘It’s only the first month—where would lychees come from now?’ The gate-commander said with a cold laugh: ‘An earth-walled room banked with fire, a warm shed close and steaming, silver and grain flung down by the fistful—what can they not force to grow? And that is nothing. Did you see the twigs poking out of the basket just now? To have the lychees reach Chang’an still fresh, they are not simply picked as fruit, but cut off branch and all. To send one basket of lychees, a whole lychee tree must be given up.’

The soldiers said, dumbfounded: ‘Then—then how dear must these lychees be? Who would ever buy them?’

The gate-commander turned his head, gazed off northward toward the palace city, and murmured: ‘There is one who loves to eat them; there is one who is willing to buy...’ but he did not spell it out. Instead he turned back and instructed them gravely: ‘An express courier flying the Yinglong flag comes once every month. In the ordinary way he goes by the Qixia Gate, which is why none of you knows him. Today the Qixia Gate was likely shut early, so he came round by the road to our Yanxing Gate. Mark it well for next time: however strict the order, before this flag it is nothing at all; on no account bar his way, or great calamity will come down upon your heads.’

The men all nodded, and the gate-commander waved a hand: ‘Enough gossip—get the gate shut, quick, and then go find those fools at the Gate Watch. If it can’t be locked, I’ll have the head off the man responsible!’

The rider, once through the Yanxing Gate, went straight on for about the span of two wards. The passersby all about were hurrying on their way, making for home amid the boom of the street drums; soldiers were already patrolling the streets and shouting, yet none dared bar that one Yinglong flag. The rider watched a moment, then spurred his horse into nearby Yongchong Ward. At the southeastern corner here there was an abandoned mercy-release pond, where, so the tale went, a fox-spirit had once made trouble, so that few people came near it.

At the edge of the mercy-release pond the rider took off his bamboo rain-hat, revealing the honest, guileless face of Aluoyue. He swung down from the horse and lifted off the great basket on the mount’s right side, and Zhang Xiaojing, curled up within it, at once tumbled out—and out with him tumbled several dozen fresh lychees and a few twigs besides.

Aluoyue led his camels out of the city to graze every day, and knew that once a month a flying courier bearing lychees would reach Chang’an; he knew too that the Yinglong flag carried more authority even than a military envoy’s, and went unhindered at any hour whatever. Today happened to be the very day the flying courier made his delivery. For his benefactor’s sake Aluoyue had made bold to waylay the courier and knock him senseless, then had played the rider himself, taken along the full set of credentials, and hidden Zhang Xiaojing away inside a basket. The yellow band across the top of the basket was an imperial seal, which no one dared open on his own authority—and so it was that he had got into the city in this fashion.

In all the world, only this one rider could still get in at the very moment Chang’an was sealed shut.

Zhang Xiaojing got up from the ground, brushed the fruit and leaves off himself, and looked about him, a shadow of gloom in his eyes. He had eaten a little game just before, and his condition had somewhat mended; only his voice still would not come. Aluoyue looked at his benefactor and felt that some change seemed to have come over him: the hair at his temples looked to have gone a shade grayer, and that one keen single eye had now lost all its edge, and held only a dim and turbid murk.

It was likely the death of his comrade that had grieved him so, Aluoyue guessed—but he did not dare to ask.

Zhang Xiaojing made a sign, bidding Aluoyue find him nearby a stick of burnt charcoal and a scrap of waste paper. He could not, to be sure, write ornate, flowery essays in balanced fours and sixes as the literati did, but he had a rough command of letters. The charcoal stick scratched across the paper, and soon a short note was written out.

Zhang Xiaojing folded the note and handed it to Aluoyue, then pointed toward the gate-tower in the distance. Aluoyue understood his meaning: he was to give the note to the guards at the Yanxing Gate. But he wondered greatly—if the note was so important, why did his benefactor not carry it over himself? Zhang Xiaojing shook his head and pointed off in another direction, signing that he had other business.

Zhang Xiaojing knew his own identity was far too sensitive; to appear rashly before the government troops would breed no end of complications. The peril to the Son of Heaven was lifted now, and to send Aluoyue with word was enough. As for himself, he must go with all speed to the Jing’an Bureau: if Li Bi was still alive, he would surely be there.

That sentence Xiao Gui had left with his dying breath was too dreadful; he could speak of it to no one, but come what might he must first let Li Bi know, and as soon as could be.

Aluoyue tucked the note away, made his benefactor a bow, and turned and went. Zhang Xiaojing led the fine horse over, stripped off the two lychee-baskets and flung them into the mercy-release pond, then swung himself up and, forcing his spirits to rally, charged off out of the ward.

By the might of the Yinglong flag, the guards dared not stop him. Zhang Xiaojing left Yongchong Ward and ran on westward along the highroad a stretch farther. Then all at once the mount gave a piteous whinny and toppled to the ground, foaming at the mouth, plainly at its end.

This swift horse had come out from the Ziwu Valley in Hu County, galloping the whole way, and by the time it reached Chang’an it was a spent arrow at the end of its flight. Now, far from being let rest, it had been driven on by Zhang Xiaojing’s whip a stretch farther, and at last it could hold out no longer, and went down with a crash. Zhang Xiaojing was a fine horseman, but his weakened body could not respond in time; he was thrown from the horse all at once, and the bamboo hat on his head fell to the ground and rolled far away.

Gritting his teeth, he clambered up from the ground and looked about him, hoping to find some other means to ride. Just then a sound of footsteps came from the way opposite—a patrol from the Wannian County yamen, urging the residents back into their wards.

The patrol, seeing a post-horse lying toppled in the middle of the road, and a man got up like an envoy standing beside it, thought it most suspicious, and raised their weapons one and all, shouting toward him. Zhang Xiaojing could not speak, and could only take up the Yinglong flag and wave it. There was one among the patrol who knew what was what; at the sight of the flag he understood its power, and his movements grew hesitant.

But the patrol-leader narrowed his eyes, gripped an iron rod, walked over, and struck Zhang Xiaojing a savage blow across the neck, knocking him flat to the ground: ‘Zhang the Yama? You pass yourself off as an imperial flying courier—did you really think we wouldn’t know you?’

That single eye was all too famous in Wannian County; everyone knew the tale of it. Zhang Xiaojing looked at this patrol-leader’s face and did not know it—likely a man newly promoted since he himself had gone into gaol. The patrol-leader grinned savagely: ‘The great Chief Zhang has dealt with too many small fry to know the likes of me. But I know a certain man—him you are sure to know, and he, for his part, is sure to be very glad to see you.’

Zhang Xiaojing was taken aback—could they mean to haul him back to the Wannian yamen? His heart leapt in alarm: this business touched on matters of the gravest weight; how could he be held up here!

The patrol-leader made no answer, but called two men to haul Zhang Xiaojing up by the arms and set off down a side road. Zhang Xiaojing tried to struggle, but the two patrolmen each had hold of an arm, and left him powerless to resist.

In the ordinary way these two would not have lasted a single bout with him. Zhang Xiaojing had fought the Türk Wolf Guards, had stopped the aphids, and yet here he was, pinned fast by this pair of small fry—a tiger, indeed, brought down onto the level plain.

The party threaded through the streets and lanes and soon came to a certain residence. The house had but a single courtyard-range; at its center was a small court, most exquisitely laid out, with nothing wanting—stone lanterns and a nanmu-wood pavilion, an old pine, a fish pond—and to the north there stood a pale-yellow pavilion-tower that gave off a faint fragrance. The patrol-leader waited a while at the courtyard gate, and soon there came out a middle-aged man in the pale-blue robe of an official, narrow of eye and hooked of nose; at the sight of Zhang Xiaojing held captive at the gate, his eyes could not help lighting up.

The patrol-leader said: ‘Knowing your honor has been looking for this man all along, the moment we took him we brought him to you first, without even passing by the yamen.’ The man handed him several strings of hard cash, and the patrol-leader went off in high glee.

‘Zhang Xiaojing, you’ve done no small things today. I truly underrated you.’ The middle-aged man spoke with a sinister, gloating malice, an irrepressible relish in his tone. Zhang Xiaojing looked up, and it was indeed someone he knew: none other than Feng Dalun, a recorder of the Forestry and Crafts Bureau and the boss of the Bear Fire Gang.

Feng Dalun feared Zhang Xiaojing to the marrow; all this while he had waited in the Yixiang Pavilion in fretful unease, and until he had the sure news of this ruffian’s utter death he could not be easy. The Bear Fire Gang had intelligence channels of its own: Zhang Xiaojing hunted through the whole city, the warrant then swiftly withdrawn, then the blast at the Xingqing Palace, the whole city under curfew and its gates shut—through all this chain of events there ran, faint but discernible, a thread of connection with this former buliang chief. He had even, dimly, got wind of it that Zhang Xiaojing seemed already to have turned traitor and gone over to the aphids. The very charge Yuan Zai had trumped up against him had, of all things, come true.

Who could have foreseen it—the course of things had proved too bizarre. Somehow or other, and out of nowhere, this fellow had been seized by a patrol; and as luck would have it this patrol-leader was one of the Bear Fire Gang’s inside men within the yamen, who had eagerly brought Zhang Xiaojing and delivered him up before his very eyes.

To see this fellow, once so awe-inspiring on every side, now kneeling meekly below the steps, his to butcher as he pleased—Feng Dalun’s heart, that had fretted the whole day long, was at last hugely eased.

‘That day you broke into my Bear Fire Gang and killed my men—did you ever think a day like this would come?’ Feng Dalun put out a foot and tilted Zhang Xiaojing’s chin up with it. But Zhang Xiaojing’s single eye glared, and it frightened Feng Dalun into a start of old habit, so that he all but lost his footing, and hastily caught at the veranda pillar beside him.

Feng Dalun, shamed into fury, drove a kick straight into the pit of Zhang Xiaojing’s chest and sent him thudding down onto the ground. Not yet content, Feng Dalun came over and kicked him savagely several times more, roaring as he kicked, for all the world like a madman.

‘Weren’t you the one whose righteousness towered to the very clouds, out to avenge your comrades? Weren’t you the one who’d throw your life away to tear my Bear Fire Gang up root and branch? Weren’t you the one bent on shielding that little whore Wen Ran?’

That one massacre had left too deep a shadow on Feng Dalun; to this very moment he held the name of Zhang Xiaojing in boundless dread. And now that too-long-pent fear turned into the pleasure of tormenting him, and poured itself out, every drop, upon Zhang Xiaojing.

Feng Dalun beat him till the sweat ran down his face, and only then stayed his hand. He crouched down and seized Zhang Xiaojing by the hair: ‘The wheel of Heaven’s law comes round; retribution never fails. That you fall into my hands today shows plainly that Heaven wills it. Do not look for me to hand you over to the magistrate for lawful execution—no, that is not enough. Only by taking your life with my own hands can I drive off the nightmare, and avenge my dead brothers of the gang!’

His face was worked into a kind of distortion by his agitation; now that he could at last, with his own hands, heave over the great stone that lay on his chest, Feng Dalun’s hands were faintly trembling.

Zhang Xiaojing’s face was without expression, but his fingers clenched tight, his heart burning with urgency. Feng Dalun marked the small detail: ‘You’re afraid? Even you can be afraid? Ha-ha-ha-ha—the mighty Five-Faced Yama, afraid at last!’

By now the sun in the far east was bursting forth; the sky had grown bright, and the whole of the Yixiang Pavilion was beginning to fill with an intoxicating fragrance. Feng Dalun hauled Zhang Xiaojing’s hair up high once more, forcing his head back to face the sunrise, his throat thrust taut. That single eye, meeting the strong light head-on, could only squint half-shut; but Feng Dalun reached out his other hand and forced the eyelid open, so that the golden radiance stabbed into the pupil, and reflex tears ran from the socket.

‘Weep, then, weep, you fiend—what you fear most is the sunlight of the mortal world, is it not?’ Feng Dalun cried out as if in a fit of madness, wholly unaware that a strange fragrance was stealing up into his nostrils. His hand bore down harder and harder, until he had all but torn Zhang Xiaojing’s scalp away—no, had torn it away already: Feng Dalun saw plainly that, as he ripped the scalp off inch by inch, there was laid bare beneath it a hideous demon’s head of a dark blood-red, pointed of crown and double-pupiled, with keen fangs and a high nose, and on its head two ox-horns besides.

‘Fiend of a Yama—now die!’

He drew the dagger at his waist and slashed it savagely at Zhang Xiaojing’s outstretched throat—and on the instant blood went spattering up before his eyes.

With his first step back into the Jingzhao Prefecture, Li Bi opened his mouth to ask: ‘Where is the traitor held?’ Adjutant Zhao bowed and said: ‘He is properly locked up, and has had contact with no one—only awaiting the Deputy Director’s return.’

Li Bi asked after the particulars of the arrest, and, not even returning the courtesy of a bow, made hurriedly for the cell where the man was held, his face an iron gray.

The moment he had Adjutant Zhao’s spoken message he had left that residence at once. Li Linfu remained there still, but the place was ringed about with soldiers of the Lüben Guards. Li Bi had, in any case, thrown all caution to the winds by now, and did not mind giving this great minister of the court one more offense.

Coming to the door of the cell, Li Bi cast a glance within through the bars—it was indeed the runner of the Jing’an Bureau’s main hall. His face burned on the instant: that this fellow should have run back and forth beneath his very eyes a whole day long was, for any commander, a shame beyond measure.

Yet there was something he could not work out. The record of service and station of every last man in the Jing’an Bureau had to pass a detailed vetting, and the runner of the main hall would of course be no exception. How in the world had this fellow slipped through so strict an examination and worked his way into the hall?

Li Bi did not believe the Türk Wolf Guards or the aphids could have managed this. It was not the same as killing and burning; whoever had done it must have known the bureaucratic system through and through, and have had deep roots within it, to smooth over every side of the matter and plant a man inside the Jing’an Bureau.

The pity was that all the files and records had gone up in flames with the main hall, and there was now no way to trace the matter back to its source.

Thinking back on it now, the one who had earlier given Li Bi the address of the residence in Anye Ward was this very runner. He had said at the time that the word came from one of the officials in charge, and Li Bi had had no leisure to verify it. It was plain enough: this was a stratagem of the hidden hand behind it all—first to lure Li Linfu there, and then to draw Li Bi there too, so that the calamity at the Xingqing Palace should have both an instigator and a witness.

This hidden hand played, sure enough, an exquisite game. With only a few words lightly passed along, he had driven the whole situation to this pass.

The heir apparent was, to be sure, the one who stood to gain the most; but could he really have played out a scheme like this? Li Bi had all along refused to believe it. He knew Li Heng too well—so honest and good-hearted a man, and with a touch of timidity besides, was truly not of a piece with this dark and sinister style.

Li Bi had meant to hurry at once to the physic garden of the Eastern Palace and confront the heir apparent again. But after turning it over again and again, he chose first to deal with the matter of the traitor. For, as he well knew, the turmoil at the Xingqing Palace was as yet unsettled, and whether the Son of Heaven lived or died was still unknown. Should the old man have ridden the dragon to Heaven and passed away, well and good; but if by some chance he had not died, and afterward looked into the matter and found that the heir apparent had, of all things, left the banquet early, then indeed disaster would be at hand.

Even were Li Bi willing to gamble on his own account, he dared not gamble with the heir apparent’s future. What he could do was to question the traitor as fast as might be, and drag out the true hidden hand—if indeed it was not the heir apparent after all.

These reflections flashed by in an instant. Li Bi pushed open the cell and stepped inside. The traitor had come back to his senses, but was trussed up hand and foot, a strip of cloth stuffed in his mouth.

‘Take out his gag,’ Li Bi ordered.

Adjutant Zhao said, with some misgiving, what if he should bite off his own tongue and kill himself? Li Bi said with a cold laugh: ‘To keep from betraying his own identity he tried to kill first Xu Bin and then Yao Runeng—a man so afraid to die, how should he take his own life?’

So a soldier went over and took the gag away. The traitor lifted his head at the last gasp, looked at Li Bi, and said not a word.

‘All this day you have brought me no end of tidings, good and bad. Now I would have you announce me one more piece of news—who was it that sent you to the Jing’an Bureau?’

The traitor forced out two words: ‘The aphids.’

‘Absurd!’ Li Bi raised his voice. ‘The aphids alone could never have done this.’ He stepped two paces nearer, a threat in his tone: ‘Do not imagine the Eight Methods of the house of Lai are a lost art! Speak! Who sent you to the Jing’an Bureau?’

The Eight Methods of the house of Lai, handed down from Lai Junchen, were eight cruel techniques of torture and interrogation; but these were current only among the torturers and gaolers, and men of letters had always scorned so much as to mention them. That Li Bi should utter even this threat showed how truly desperate he had become.

The runner was unmoved: ‘Deputy Director Li, you said just now that, to save myself, I did not scruple to murder two men and silence them—that I am a man afraid to die. But have you considered that there is yet another possibility?’

A light flickered in Li Bi’s eyes.

‘Everyone who knows must die.’ The runner split his mouth wide in a blood-chilling smile, his very tongue thrust out.

Li Bi grasped it at once and reached out in haste to stop him. But the runner clamped his two jaws shut and bit clean through his own tongue in a single stroke, then swallowed it down with all his might. The half-tongue slid into his gullet, but, being far too thick and fleshy, lodged fast in his windpipe. The men in the gaol rushed over and pounded on his back, but the runner kept his mouth clenched tight and let the blood pour freely out between his teeth. Before long, after a few agonized convulsions, he was choked, alive, to death.

Yes—everyone who knew must die, himself included.

The men within the cell and without were struck dumb for a moment; but it was Li Bi himself who had given the order to take out the gag, and they did not know how they ought to react. Li Bi turned his head, his face without expression: ‘Find out who, in the ordinary run of things, had private dealings with this runner. Every one of them still living—bring them all to me!’

The Jing’an Bureau’s records were destroyed, and now the runner had killed himself besides; to dig out his history, one could only pin one’s hopes on the faint traces and clues he had let slip in the ordinary course of things.

Both unluckily and luckily, after that great fire the men left of the Jing’an Bureau were not many, and most were gathered at the Jingzhao Prefecture, nursing their wounds. So it cost Adjutant Zhao no great effort to muster the ten-odd men who had had ordinary dealings with the runner. Li Bi ran his eye over them: ‘How is it they are all Tang men? Did he never have dealings with any of the Hu?’

Adjutant Zhao said that Ji Wen had earlier driven off all the Hu officials, on the pretext of guarding against Türk collaborators within. Li Bi glared: ‘Sheer folly—get them back here at once!’ Adjutant Zhao hurried out to see to it, while Li Bi stayed in the gaol to question these ten-odd men first.

These men were trembling with fear, thinking they were to be put to harsh torture. But to their surprise Li Bi’s manner was mild enough; he only had them tell what they knew of the runner in the ordinary way, the more fully the better. So the men, one putting in a word and then another, laid out all they knew.

It came out that the runner was surnamed Lu, third-born in his family, and a man of Yuezhou; for all that he was a big voice within the main hall, in ordinary life he was a man of few words. The men knew only that he was single, had never taken a wife, and had no kin to speak of here in the capital. As for how Lu San had come from Yuezhou to the capital, and how he had been chosen into the Jing’an Bureau, that scarcely anyone knew. Only one man mentioned that Lu San seemed once to have been in the army.

Li Bi asked over and over, several times, and got no answer of any worth. Somewhat vexed, he paced with his hands behind his back and bade them go on thinking. In the midst of this pressing questioning the door was pushed open, and several Hu petty clerks were brought in, ill at ease. They lived near Guangde Ward, and so had been the first to be fetched back.

Li Bi had them cast their memories back as well, but alas, what these clerks recalled differed little from the rest. In his bearing toward Tang and Hu alike, Lu San had shown no marked leaning. The verdict of all was quite of one accord: the man was steady and well-mannered, gentle of temper, and had a good deal of ordinary intercourse with his colleagues—but all of it mere passing acquaintance, not one bond especially close. When a colleague had some illness great or small, a wedding or a funeral, his contribution to the common purse was never wanting; and if now and then a man needed an urgent loan to tide him over, he was willing to put himself out to help—a man of kindness repaid and debt paid. Lu San himself had no particular pastimes: a little wine now and again, a game or two of double-sixes, and that was all.

Li Bi, standing to one side, all at once cried: ‘Stop!’ The men, in the full flow of their talk, were brought up short, and all were taken aback. Li Bi ran his eye round the circle and asked who it was that had just spoken. A Tang petty clerk raised his hand, trembling.

Li Bi shook his head: ‘The line before—the one about kindness repaid and debt paid.’ The men looked at one another; and an old Sogdian, a Hu of fifty-odd years, rose to his feet, his face somewhat fearful and unquiet.

‘“And if now and then a man needed an urgent loan to tide him over, he was willing to put himself out to help—a man of kindness repaid and debt paid”—that was you who said it, was it not?’

‘Yes—yes, it was I who said it... I once borrowed money from Lu San.’ His Tang speech came out stiff and halting; he must have learned it in his grown years.

‘How much did you borrow?’

‘Three thousand cash and two bolts of silk; I had them two months, and have paid it all back.’

Li Bi said: ‘Just now you called him a man of kindness repaid and debt paid—was that your own judgment of him, or something he said of himself?’ The old Sogdian was a little muddled by the question, and lifted his head; Li Bi went on: ‘The rest of us commonly say, “a kindness is always repaid, a debt is always paid.” Why do you say “kindness repaid, debt paid”?’

The old Hu did not well understand why the officer should fasten on such fine points of wording—it was only a chance turn of phrase, after all; what was there of why or wherefore in it? He hung there abashed, not knowing how to answer. Li Bi said: ‘That you should say it so without thinking—might it not be that you were influenced by Lu San?’

One who learns a foreign tongue in his grown years is easily swayed by those about him, and often without the least awareness of it. Prompted so by Li Bi, the old Hu remembered all at once: ‘Yes, yes—Lu San was forever fond of saying it, and before I knew it the words had slipped into my own mouth by rote.’

Li Bi, deep in thought, turned to Adjutant Zhao and said: ‘Dismiss them.’

‘Eh? Did you get something out of them?’ Adjutant Zhao was wholly at sea. Li Bi answered beside the question, reciting off-hand a scrap of a ballad: ‘Shouzhuolang, Shouzhuolang—kindness repaid, debt paid.’ And as he said it his look grew darker and darker.

‘A kindness is always repaid, a debt is always paid’—this was in origin a common saying of the marketplace, and very widely current. To square it with the three syllables of their own name, the Shouzhuolang had deliberately cut away the leading word, leaving only ‘kindness repaid, debt paid.’ In all the world, only they spoke it so.

Li Bi flung out his sleeve, his voice turning stern: ‘Muster a hundred-man mounted troop and come with me to the Pingkang Quarter!’

Feng Dalun’s Yixiang Pavilion stood in Jing’an Ward of the eastern city—ironically enough, of the very same name as the Jing’an Bureau—which marked, as it were, a dividing line across Wannian County: to the north of Jing’an Ward all was rich and thronging and prosperous; to the south lay either waste ground or pleasure-garden villas, with few residents, and mostly the idle young ruffians of the gangs moving about among them. That he should build his Yixiang Pavilion here was both respectable and convenient for directing the Bear Fire Gang from afar.

This house he had bought some years before from a merchant. Bought, they called it, but in truth it was got by cunning and by force. A recorder of the Forestry and Crafts Bureau held a low rank but a fat profit; play a few tricks in the matter of building-works, add to that the power of the underworld, and it was the easiest thing in the world to squeeze a small merchant who had no backing.

The Yixiang Pavilion Feng Dalun had spent great pains to refurbish, and nothing could be more elegant. For that reason he did not care to let the coarse fellows of the Bear Fire Gang come near it, and allowed only a few guards to stay at the gate.

Guards they were called, but they were really only a few idle youths and layabouts, squatting or lounging with no proper soldierly bearing to them. Hearing, from outside the gate, their master’s wild roaring and wild laughter come in bursts one after another from within the court, they could not help exchanging glances. One of them, an older and steadier head, said: ‘You can’t blame the master for being like this. You don’t know it, but that one-eyed Yama broke into the head-hall of our Bear Fire Gang a while back and killed several hundred of our best hands—he’s our great enemy.’

‘Several hundred?’ A few of the youths round about drew a cold breath. ‘Our whole Bear Fire Gang, top to bottom, hasn’t got several hundred men, has it?’

‘Aah! I only said it for the sake of saying it! Anyway, that madman gave us no light handling; now that he’s fallen into the master’s hands, there’s no telling how wretched he’ll be made.’ The steadier one sighed out the words—and just then another beside him suddenly wrinkled his nose: ‘What a lovely scent.’

‘Rubbish—is this your first day on duty? This place is called the Yixiang Pavilion; the very walls are kneaded through with Khotan fragrant grass, musk, and crumbled frankincense. The moment the sun shines on them, a rare perfume rises.’

‘No, it isn’t...’ The youth sniffed again. ‘The smell’s coming from over the way, opposite.’

The other guards smelled it too—a fragrance unlike that of the Yixiang Pavilion, richer and heavier, that no sooner drawn into the nose went straight of itself up to the brain. Before the men could so much as make out where the scent came from, their heads had begun to feel swollen and giddy, their sight a little blurred, and there seemed to rise before them fine wine, fair women, tall and splendid horses, and every other good thing. Leaning together upon one another, they fell to tittering foolishly.

Just then a figure came rushing swiftly up, a carpenter’s mallet in hand, and struck down at their heads. The guards’ wits were dulled and they could not react at all; a few muffled, heavy blows, and they all lay toppled on the ground, senseless. Straightaway a woman too appeared at the gate, a cloth over her mouth, cradling in her hands a burning coil of thick, coarse incense.

She pinched out the burning incense and gave a nod. Only then did the man with the mallet tear away the thin cloth covering his mouth and nose, showing the face of Cen Shen; and the woman, of course, was none other than Wen Ran.

Cen Shen watched the incense with a grave face: ‘So this is the fabled soul-stealing incense?’ Wen Ran shook her head: ‘There’s no such thing as a soul-stealing incense that drops a man at one whiff; the most it does is befuddle. This befuddling-incense is compounded of datura flower, hemp seed, and nutmeg; it can only make a man a little sluggish and set illusions before his eyes—that is the most of it.’

‘That is enough,’ said Cen Shen. He glanced up at the lintel over the gate, shook the mallet in his hand, and mocked himself: ‘I, Cen Shen, once meant to be a sword-bearing knight-errant; who would have thought I’d end up at the low, thievish trade of drugging men with incense.’

Wen Ran lowered her eyes: ‘You have seen me this far, young master—you have done all that kindness and duty could ask; the rest let me finish for myself.’ Cen Shen laughed aloud and stepped out before her: ‘An orphan girl repaying a kindness, the weak striking at the strong—with material as fine as this, how could I stand by with folded hands? I do it not for the great cause, but for the matter of my art!’

Their plan was crude, and simple too. Wen Ran was to loose the smoke and make the enemy sluggish; Cen Shen was to do the striking. The Yixiang Pavilion was small in its layout, and today, with the lantern-fair on, the guards would not be many. So long as the befuddling-incense truly answered, Cen Shen was confident he could carry Feng Dalun off bound, single-handed.

The guards at the gate disposed of, Wen Ran crouched down, set the befuddling-incense in the threshold, and lit it once more. When the fragrance had spread a little, she fanned it inward with a small round fan. This kind of incense had a coarse grain and a heavy smoke; it would first spread low along the ground, then slowly drift upward. So that, even in an open court, there was no fear of its being blown away by the wind.

Wen Ran let the smoke drift a while, reckoning it had by now spread through the whole of the Yixiang Pavilion, and then gave Cen Shen a nod. Cen Shen caught up the hem of his gown, took up the mallet, and rushed in at the gate, Wen Ran following close behind.

First he rounded the corner of the spirit-screen and saw a servant grinning foolishly, mouth agape, at a tree; he raised the mallet and felled him with a single blow, then charged into a courtyard paved with gray brick—and stopped short. Wen Ran, arriving behind him, let out a scream of rage.

The courtyard was not large, but exquisitely adorned, with trees and water both, and a fine scent-pavilion set to the north. Yet at the very center of this most elegant of courts there was a blood-soaked scene of savagery.

Feng Dalun had Zhang Xiaojing by the hair, and, crying ‘Fiend of a Yama—now die!’, was stabbing at him again and again with a dagger like a madman. Zhang Xiaojing’s two hands were bound, and he had no power to resist; he could only shift his muscles as best he might to keep his vital points out of the way. Perhaps because of his heaving agitation of spirit, the befuddling-incense worked upon Feng Dalun with a peculiar force. In his eyes, the shape of Zhang Xiaojing just then was likely that of a very fiend out of hell.

It was as well, too, that Feng Dalun, bewitched by the befuddling-incense, had lost his aim in striking. Zhang Xiaojing, though stabbed till the blood ran streaming, was as yet untouched in any vital place.

Cen Shen and Wen Ran had come here meaning only to seize Feng Dalun, and had never dreamed they might come upon Zhang Xiaojing. Cen Shen was the first to gather his wits; taking the lead, he rushed over and with one blow of the mallet knocked Feng Dalun’s dagger flying, then sent him sprawling with a kick. Wen Ran flung herself down upon Zhang Xiaojing and wept aloud.

Come to think of it, though the two of them had been seeking each other all this while, this was the first time in the whole twelve double-hours of the day that they had truly met.

Zhang Xiaojing opened his single eye and saw, out of the dimness, the face of Wen Wuji appear, wearing a look of solace. After him came the brothers of the Eighth Company, gathering one by one, fond and familiar, upon the clouds, their features blurred. But soon he saw, too, that beside Wen Wuji there appeared, abruptly, the face of Xiao Gui, chewing a mint leaf, glaring at him with a savage face, crimson flames boring their way out from within his body.

Zhang Xiaojing was seized with sudden fright, his body shuddering violently. In that instant his voice, before so numbed, all at once came clear, and a hoarse roar burst from his throat—whether of grief or of rage there was no telling.

Seeing this, Wen Ran knew that he too was under the sway of the befuddling-incense and was seeing the hidden pains at the bottom of his heart. She hurried to fetch some ice-cold water from the fish pond and dashed it over his face, then loosed his bonds. Only then did Zhang Xiaojing become aware of Wen Ran’s presence; trembling, he lifted his head, stroked her lovely hair, and for a long while could bring out no sound.

Feng Dalun leaned aslant before the Yixiang Pavilion, his gaze somewhat scattered. Cen Shen kept a wary eye on him all the while, to keep the fellow from escaping.

The befuddling-incense’s power was short-lived, and soon Feng Dalun came back to his senses. This recorder of the Forestry and Crafts Bureau grinned savagely: ‘The whole city, for whatever reason, is under martial guard now; bind me though you may, you can forget any easy leaving.’

Cen Shen’s face changed. He had heard something of the disturbance at the Xingqing Palace before this, and had heard the street drums as well. Feng Dalun was not wrong in the least: with the whole city now under martial guard, and they with a court official in their charge, they might well not get so much as past a ward gate.

The only course now was to kill Feng Dalun on the spot, then lie hidden until the martial guard was lifted, and after that contrive some way to get Zhang Xiaojing and Wen Ran out of the city. So Cen Shen reckoned inwardly—and in his mind there came a hair’s-breadth of slackening. Feng Dalun spied out this moment; his body sprang up all at once, and he darted back into the Yixiang Pavilion, raised a hand, and barred the great door fast.

Feng Dalun had run the underworld for many years, and was careful at every turn. This Yixiang Pavilion, for all its luxury, had certain life-preserving devices fitted to it besides. The wooden entrance door of the Yixiang Pavilion, for instance, had its two hinges fixed with four brass plates; once a man inside had dropped the iron bar, those outside, unless they took the whole door off, could by no means kick it in or batter it down.

Cen Shen rushed to the door and kicked it several times, but it did not budge a hair. Feng Dalun laughed aloud through the window-lattice and turned to go. Cen Shen knew there must be a hidden passage somewhere in the Yixiang Pavilion, leading off to some other place. But there was nothing he could do; he could only watch this arch-culprit slip quietly away.

Cen Shen gave the door a savage kick and turned to Wen Ran, saying urgently: ‘Quick, we must go—Feng Dalun’s got away, and he’ll be sure to bring men back.’ Wen Ran nodded, and the two of them, one on either side, helped Zhang Xiaojing to his feet and started outward.

‘Let’s get back to the Wen Incense Shop first; if we step quickly, we can still make it before the drums fall silent,’ Cen Shen said aloud. But just then Zhang Xiaojing spoke: ‘No—we go to Guangde Ward...’

‘Guangde Ward? Impossible—that’s far too far!’ Cen Shen stared.

‘I have a matter of the first urgency... I must go and tell Deputy Director Li. Come, quickly.’ Zhang Xiaojing’s voice was weak, but utterly resolute. Wen Ran hesitated a little, but Cen Shen gave no quarter: ‘What a time to think of that! Out of here first, and talk after!’

The two of them, propping Zhang Xiaojing between them, came quickly to the courtyard gate. No sooner had they stepped over the threshold than they heard, all at once, a shouted cry, and on the instant their eyes were dazzled by a wash of golden light. When their sight came back, they saw that there had appeared before them, abruptly, a great body of Longwu Army soldiers, the radiance being the dazzling glare of the morning sun off suit after suit of armor.

These soldiers stood ranged in a half-circle before the gate, crossbows leveled, bowstrings wound taut, in the posture of men facing a mighty foe. Should they loose an attack, in half a finger-snap the three of them would be shot into hedgehogs.

At the very front of the company stood three men. On the left was Chen Xuanli; on the right, Prince Yong; and Feng Dalun, but now escaped, stood foremost of all, his face one savage grin, pointing this way.

The Shouzhuolang’s foothold in the capital was at Liu’s Bookshop in the Pingkang Quarter, hard beside the resident-agent courtyards of the ten military commissioners. Today the Shouzhuolang had lost, one after another, two assassins and a firemaster, and had had their post besides turned upside down—a total loss of face, one might say.

Loss of face or no, the work had still to go on. Chang’an had been in the greatest turmoil the night before, and they must find some way to gather intelligence and learn what in the world had happened. The Shouzhuolang’s squad leader in the capital had all this while been buried in the work of clearing up the wreckage.

But just then, from beyond the lane, there came bursts of urgent hoofbeats, until the very ground was faintly trembling. The squad leader was a man who had been on the field of battle, and knew that cavalry was drawing near; hastily he ordered his men to go and reconnoiter.

But before they could make any response at all, the whole lane had been utterly sealed off.

By now the sky had grown light, the festival lanterns were put out, and the common folk had all been driven back into their wards, so that the six great avenues of the city were as open and clear as after nightfall. This troop of horse galloped on at full stretch, soon reached the Pingkang Quarter, and, with the help of the ward’s own garrison-soldiers, ringed the place tightly about.

The Shouzhuolang were greatly alarmed, not knowing what had happened. The squad leader knit his brows, rose, and walked out of the lane, and came face to face with an official forcing his way in; every Shouzhuolang who tried to bar his path was shoved aside by the soldiers at his side.

The squad leader was just about to clasp his hands and say a few formal words when, all unlooked-for, the official flung a round object at him. The thing rolled and rolled along the ground for a few turns and came to rest against the squad leader’s foot—and it was, of all things, a human head, and one but freshly cut off.

The official said in a loud voice: ‘I am Li Bi, Deputy Director of the Jing’an Bureau. This man was named Lu San—a man of yours, of the Shouzhuolang?’

The squad leader could see it: this official was calm on the surface, but within he was, like as not, on the very point of bursting. His instinct told him the matter must be closely bound up with the turmoil that had gone before; and in such a case the Shouzhuolang could no longer keep strictly to that code of theirs, or the court in its fury would tear them up root and branch.

The squad leader made his decision swiftly, and said honestly: ‘The Shouzhuolang in the capital are a known and numbered few; I do not recall this name, nor do I know this face.’

Without waiting for Li Bi to press him, the squad leader of his own accord fetched the name-register. Li Bi saw that the ink of this register was old, and that it could not have been got up in haste; it should be genuine, and indeed the name was not in it.

Li Bi thought a moment, then asked again: ‘Do the Shouzhuolang take on business on their own account?’

The squad leader said: ‘Impossible. Every commission must pass through a firemaster.’

‘And if it comes from outside—is that beyond the reach of the capital’s own ground?’

The squad leader was taken aback: Li Bi had hit the mark in one. Such a thing was indeed possible. If Shouzhuolang from elsewhere took on, from a client elsewhere, a commission touching the capital, and came to Chang’an, then in that case it need not pass through a capital firemaster. But the Chang’an branch would furnish a certain basic help—a foothold, say, or a guide and intelligence support—while as to the particulars of the matter they neither inquired nor took part.

If Lu San had taken on his commission elsewhere, and come to Chang’an to lie hidden within the Jing’an Bureau, then there would indeed be no trace of his history to be found among the capital’s firemasters.

‘And these clients from elsewhere—who make up the greater number of them?’

The squad leader made no concealment either: ‘Great wealthy merchants, frontier generals, old and powerful houses, the yamen of the provinces, and the like.’ Li Bi pursued it: ‘Then which sort of outside client commissions the most business in the capital?’ At last the squad leader began to hesitate, on the point of speaking and yet holding back. Li Bi pressed a step nearer, his tone fierce: ‘When you sent men before to assassinate the Türk Right Shad, you had already touched a court taboo; play me false any further, and this blame is one you Shouzhuolang will carry on your own backs!’

The squad leader sighed, knowing this official was not to be fobbed off at all; he cast a glance toward the east and said in a low voice: ‘The resident-agent courtyards.’

Across the street from Liu’s Bookshop stood the ten resident-agent courtyards. Behind each of these courtyards stood a military commissioner, and each stood for its master’s eyes and ears in the capital. The resident-agent courtyards were relatively independent of the court’s own system; they both passed word from the provinces to the central authority and reported the central authority’s doings, in good time, back to their commissioners.

If one asked which outside client had the greatest need of commissions touching the capital, it could be none other than these ten resident-agent courtyards.

A slight change came over Li Bi’s face: the moment the resident-agent courtyards were drawn in, the matter was hooked to frontier affairs, and grew more complicated by far. He asked: ‘Then how are the accounts between you and the resident-agent courtyards settled?’

It was an extremely precise question. Had he only kept pressing after the content of the commissions, the squad leader could have put him off by pleading ignorance; but to cut in at the point of the money-accounts—there the running records stood as witness, and it was hard to conceal on the spur of the moment.

The squad leader knew the question was a shrewd and searching one, and could only order someone to fetch the account-books from the firemasters’ side, explaining: ‘Our accounts with the resident-agent courtyards are settled once a month. Headquarters sends the vouchers over, and the resident-agent courtyards pay against the vouchers. As for what the several items are in detail—unless it is a commission handled in the capital, we do not know.’

The Shouzhuolang’s post in the capital had to take on the business of remittance and exchange—gathering together the payments from every quarter and converting them into grain, fodder, iron, and the like, to be sent back to the garrison-towns of the frontier—and so the great accounts were all settled from here.

‘Fetch them here and let me see.’

Li Bi did not lightly take the squad leader’s word. He had brought several old clerks with him, and had them fetch all the Shouzhuolang’s account-books of the past year, and checked them over in person. To a secret organization this was, quite simply, a public humiliation; but the squad leader ground his teeth and did not dare make bold to protest.

The instruction Li Bi gave was very simple: find, for the past year, all the dealings between the ten resident-agent courtyards and the Shouzhuolang; subtract the commissions handled by the capital branch; and see which resident-agent courtyard had the highest figure of dealings.

For, as anyone must know, to plant a spy inside the Jing’an Bureau was an exceedingly difficult thing, and the price of it would surely be very high; and to mount a scheme on so vast a scale as the aphids’, the outlay would be more staggering still. That figure would show itself in the sum of the dealings. One had only to find out which resident-agent courtyard had spent the most on commissioning outside Shouzhuolang to come to the capital and do its work, and the conclusion would stand plain as day.

Very soon the clerks reached their conclusion—the Pinglu resident-agent courtyard. In the second year of Tianbao alone, the fees it had paid the Shouzhuolang came to more than ten thousand strings of cash, of which the capital commissions made up not so much as two thousand strings.

‘Pinglu...’ Li Bi chewed the name over carefully.

Compared with the other nine military commissioners, the Pinglu commissioner was a relatively new one, set up barely two years before. It was in fact a lesser command split off from the Fanyang commissioner, having under it only eleven garrison-towns and a single army, with its seat at Yingzhou.

Just because it was so new, Li Bi could not for the moment call to mind who the Pinglu military commissioner even was, and could only turn an inquiring eye upon the squad leader. With this the squad leader was, of course, well acquainted, and he hastened to answer:

‘To report to the Deputy Director: the name of the Pinglu military commissioner is—An Lushan.’
