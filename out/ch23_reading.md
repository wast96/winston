### Concealment, Withdrawal, Relocation

### Reshuffling the Special Committee and Reorganizing the Special Section

After Gu Shunzhang's arrest and defection, Zhou Enlai's cool, resourceful command and meticulous planning had, in the gravest of circumstances, kept the Party Center safe and stamped out a catastrophe of the first order. The Central Committee moved at once to put Central Committee member Chen Yun in charge of the Central Special Section; with Zhou Enlai's help, the Special Section swiftly disposed of the crush of problems Gu's betrayal had brought on, and then of Xiang Zhongfa's defection, so that the Party came through the crisis intact and, in desperate conditions, quickly steadied itself and carried on. But Gu Shunzhang had run this vital department for four full years. He knew many of the Party's secrets and its organization inside out, and the retrenchment his betrayal forced still cost the Party beyond reckoning. Above all, the position it had built up on the hidden front over three hard years, painstakingly, across the Nanjing-Shanghai region, was shattered overnight: most of the strength planted inside the enemy's own ranks was destroyed, and the many comrades who had worked their way in at such cost could no longer operate and could scarcely even find a place to hide in Shanghai.

In May 1931 Zhou Enlai sought and won the Central Committee's approval to overhaul the Special Committee without delay, and to move the Central Special Section's senior cadres who could no longer safely work — step by step, into hiding, out of the city, and on to new posts. The reconstituted Special Committee had five members: Zhou Enlai, Chen Yun, Kang Sheng, Pan Hannian, and Kuang Hui'an. The Special Section itself was reorganized, its Fourth Branch handed over to the Central Committee's General Secretariat. Chen Yun (alias Li Jiesheng, known within the Party simply as "the Teacher") took charge of the Special Section — its head was styled "secretary of the Xinxin Company" — and headed the First Branch as well; Kang Sheng was his deputy and ran the Third Branch, and Pan Hannian the Second. To keep the Party Center safe, the counterintelligence work the enemy had wrecked had to be rebuilt. Chen Yun planned it himself, threw every resource into it, and worked every social connection until counterintelligence lines had again been established inside the Kuomintang's Songhu Garrison Command and the French and British concession police. It was decided at the same time that Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan would move to Tianjin (Chen Geng would end up in the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Soviet); that the "Three Heroes of Longtan" — Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di — would withdraw to the Central Soviet; that Li Qiang would be sent to study in Moscow; and that Liu Ding would carry on in the Special Section a while longer before leaving Shanghai too. Zhou Enlai went into hiding in time, and once the others were safely placed he too left Shanghai for the Central Soviet. That September the Special Committee was adjusted again, its members now Chen Yun, Kang Sheng, Pan Hannian, Chen Shouchang, and Yang Sen.

On June 10, 1931, Zhou Enlai chaired a meeting of the Central Committee's Politburo, which adopted the Central Committee's Review and Summary of the Special Committee's Work, drafted by Zhou himself. For all the Special Committee's achievements, the summary held, and all the protection it had given the Party, the defection of a single man, Gu Shunzhang, had shaken its entire operation; this had to be called a failure of the Committee's own work, and above all the Committee's want of political education was the chronic weakness that had left its foundations unsound. Zhou also offered a self-criticism: Comrade Wu Hao, who had directly overseen the work, must bear the main responsibility for the error. And he laid down principles for the Committee's future organization, its working methods, and its discipline.

Gu Shunzhang's betrayal, and the grave harm it did, was a hard lesson for the Party. Many years later, soon after the founding of New China, Zhou Enlai still called it a hard lesson at a meeting. "Gu Shunzhang's defection was no accident," he said. "The Li Lisan line and the Fourth Plenum both deepened his wavering, and we had not been on our guard against it in advance." He added: "A mortal blow like this can be avoided. It calls for stronger education, tight internal discipline, a clear line drawn between inside and out, and no lateral contacts." In this talk of May 1950 Zhou drew the lessons of the affair further still.

Du Ning (Yang Zhihua), then working in the Central Committee's Women's Commission, set down a detailed account of the affair in an essay written in Moscow on November 28, 1938, "The Course and Lessons of the Traitor Gu Shunzhang's Defection," and drew from it the lessons the Party ought to take. The Party's organizations, the essay argued, had to keep closer watch over their members' thinking, their political conduct, and their private lives; cadres had to be chosen for political quality; their political and ideological education had to be strengthened; and the gaps in security work had to be closed. On the ideological and political side: "Gu Shunzhang held an important post, yet day to day he read no documents and voiced no political opinion, and his comrades gave the matter no thought. He was an old Party member, and he had carried out the Party's decisions bravely in the past — but what was that bravery made of? We never troubled to look. He had no grasp of doctrine or of policy, and none of his work had any revolutionary consciousness behind it. Shanghai's lumpen proletariat is truly fond of a fight, but precisely because there was no revolutionary consciousness in him, he could not struggle to the end for the interests of the proletariat as a whole, and he even became a traitor to his class. Gu Shunzhang was the very type of the Shanghai lumpen proletariat." On his private life: "No one studied or heeded Gu Shunzhang's dissolute living — opium, even whoring, beating his wife, and the rest — so that on the pretext of his secret-service work he sank further by the day... His life alone tells you his betrayal was no accident." On the principles of choosing cadres: "Gu Shunzhang came up as a foreman and had a thick streak of the hoodlum in him. A man like that we should have trusted only within limits. We could use his strengths, of course, but we had to guard against his weaknesses, and we should never have let him hold the Party's most important leading work." The essay pressed the point: "The education of worker-cadres is vital, and above all of worker-cadres with a streak of the hoodlum. It can be said that in the past almost no work was done to educate Gu Shunzhang — one might even say none at all. From the day he joined the Party to the day he walked out, not one of his habits changed. Had we kept a firm hold on his political education, I do not believe a long life in the Party could have failed to change his character. The lesson is of the greatest importance to the Party. Had we educated him well, we might have changed not only him but, through him, the worker masses around him — for in Shanghai men of his sort, the lumpen-proletarian element, hold a considerable place."

The Chronicle of Chen Yun records that from May 1931 to March 1932, while Chen Yun ran it, "the Central Special Section did the following main work. First, it handled the fresh relocation of the central organs made necessary by Xiang Zhongfa's arrest and defection. Second, it reordered its internal structure: the Fourth Branch, which had linked the Center with the revolutionary base areas, was abolished and its radio work handed to the General Secretariat, and the remaining three branches were pared down. Chen Yun took on the First Branch, for general affairs; Pan Hannian the Second, for alerts and intelligence; and Kang Sheng the Third — the 'Red Squad' — which struck at traitors and informers. Third, it arranged for the Special Section people whom Gu Shunzhang's betrayal had exposed to leave Shanghai: Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di to the Central Soviet, Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan to Tianjin, Li Qiang to Moscow. Fourth, it drew on every kind of social connection to revive the Special Section work that Gu's betrayal had halted, above all the alert system, and built a batch of new intelligence lines, planting inside contacts in the French and British concession police, the Kuomintang's Shanghai Municipal Party Office, the Shanghai Garrison Command, and elsewhere: Chen Yun sent a colleague from the Commercial Press into the political department of the British concession police, a fellow townsman named Shen Shouya into the organization department of the Kuomintang's Shanghai office, Zhang Qiuyang into financial circles, and Xu Qiang into the Kuomintang army. Fifth, it went on punishing traitors — eliminating Wang Bin, the Shanghai Garrison Command inspector who specialized in wrecking Communist organizations, and executing Wang Shide, a former Special Section man who had turned. Sixth, it settled the other matters left over from Gu's betrayal." Though this phase lasted only a year, the work quickly recovered, and the results were plain.

When did the Central Special Section come to an end? Chen Yangshan, who stayed on in its Second Branch, once wrote: "The Central Special Section was wound up in early September 1935. From September on it no longer existed as an organization; most of its responsible cadres had been transferred away, the Party's organizations had all been broken up, and the task of guarding them was beyond its power — it could no longer even guard itself... Not until April 1936, when the Central Committee sent Comrade Feng Xuefeng to Shanghai, were the comrades in Shanghai's various quarters brought back into contact and placed under unified central control. But I never heard that Comrade Feng Xuefeng took over the Special Section, still less that the Special Section was rebuilt. Given the situation then, an organization of that kind was no longer needed; it had completed its historic mission. Winding up the Special Section was therefore in step with the situation and with the Center's thinking."

### Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan Move to Tianjin

Both Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan had joined the Central Special Section at the founding of its Intelligence Branch in early 1928. Before his arrest and defection, Gu Shunzhang had been closer to Chen Geng in his work than to anyone. The two had known each other early — they had gone to the Soviet Union together in 1926 to study intelligence and security work — and from early 1928 to 1931 they served together in the Special Section. Gu knew more about Chen Geng than about any other man: not only every professional dealing between them, but nearly all of Chen's relatives and friends (it was Gu who later had Chen's wife seized in Shanghai, sending men to her family home to take her). So once Gu turned, Zhou Enlai told Chen Geng to go to ground at once, before an assassin could reach him, and Chen quickly hid at a friend's house. Chen Yangshan had also met Gu Shunzhang many times during his own Special Section service and was likewise a cadre marked for withdrawal. He had been living under cover as a reporter for Shanghai's Shen Bao, in a lane at the corner of Zhejiang Road and Haining Road. When the Central Committee sounded the alarm over Gu's defection, the organization sent word for him to leave home immediately, and Chen Yangshan took refuge with a fellow townsman who was studying at the Shanghai Fine Arts Academy.

About a week later, so as not to endanger his friend, Chen Geng asked the organization to have Chen Yangshan look for a place where the two of them could stay together for a time. The place should be a cut above the ordinary — an office suite, ideally, to fit their merchant disguise — with doors front and back both giving onto the street, somewhere inconspicuous; better to spend a little more. Chen Yangshan asked around as instructed and found, beside the old Racecourse (now People's Square), behind the Hua'an Insurance Company on Nanjing Road, a small three-story building given over entirely to offices. It was a place of business, hung with company signboards, quiet, and little noticed; and it had passages front and back, handy in an emergency. He rented an office there with a way out at both ends, and brought Chen Geng in.

Not long after Gu Shunzhang's betrayal, the Central Committee had reconstituted the Central Special Section's leadership, with Chen Yun, Kang Sheng, and Pan Hannian directly in charge. Pan Hannian, taking over as head of the Intelligence Branch, moved at once to complete the handover with Chen Geng. They agreed to do it at Liu Ding's home. Liu Ding was the former deputy head of the Special Section's Second Branch; because he had not yet been exposed, he had not left Shanghai with Chen Geng, Li Kenong, and the others. He and his wife Wu Xianqing had stayed on at their work, living in a single detached house on the outskirts of the French Concession — a secluded spot that served as a Party safe house, run by the couple. Chen Geng and Pan Hannian arrived at the appointed time, and the three of them sat down formally with Liu Ding to transact the handover. First Chen Geng turned over to Pan Hannian, one by one, the intelligence contacts kept on from the old Second Branch — most of which Liu Ding already knew well; then it was decided that the few whose work centered on Shanghai would pass to Pan Hannian's direct charge, while the rest would stay in Liu Ding's hands, answerable to Pan.

Among the contacts Chen Geng handed over to Pan Hannian were two rather unusual figures. One was Yang Du; the other was called Jiao Dingkai. Yang Du has already been described. Jiao Dingkai had begun as a disciple of Yang Guangshan, a Red Gang boss; he had in turn taken on a whole crowd of followers among the low life of the French Concession's restaurants, teahouses, and bathhouses, and carried some weight of his own. His open profession was law clerk.

Once Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan had moved into their new quarters, they kept to their rooms by day and came out to work at night, and were safe enough. But Chen Geng knew a great many people in Shanghai, and had dealt in person with many of the Intelligence Branch's contacts; Zhou Enlai feared for him. Just then the Party organizations in the Beiping-Tianjin region were being broken up one after another and urgently needed someone sent to deal with it, so it was decided to send Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan to Tianjin.

In 1930 and 1931 the Party organizations of the Beiping-Tianjin region were shattered again and again. The disaster deepened once Wang Ming's "Left" line began to dominate the Party Center. After the Party's Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee in January 1931, a meeting was held in Tianjin that February to relay its decisions. In early April the Communist Shun-Zhi provincial committee was broken up: after its secretary, Xu Lanzhi, was arrested and turned, the provincial committee, its military commission, and its Communist Youth League were all badly hit. Chen Yuandao, An Ziwen, Zhou Zhongying, Liu Yaxiong, and dozens of others of the provincial committee were arrested and held at the Tianjin district court to await sentencing. After the Shun-Zhi provincial committee was smashed in Tianjin, it moved to Beiping; and in June the newly formed committee was broken up on a large scale there too. Its secretary Yin Jian, its secretary-general Guo Yaxian, its military commission secretary Liao Huaping, its Mutual Aid Society secretary Lai De, and many other responsible cadres were seized by the enemy. Yin Jian bore himself with great resolve under arrest, giving nothing away under torture; but Guo Yaxian, Liao Huaping, and Lai De defected soon after they were caught.

To meet this, the Central Committee sent Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan to Tianjin with three tasks: first, to rescue the arrested comrades — to find out where they were held and whether anything could be done to free them; second, to see whether Special-Section-type work could be set up in Tianjin to protect the Party organizations; and third, to investigate the traitors — to learn which of them most gravely threatened the Party and had to be eliminated.

Their orders in hand, Chen Geng and his party left Shanghai on June 16 aboard a British freighter bound for Tanggu. Traveling with Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan were Chen Geng's wife Wang Genying and their son Zhifei, and Shao Fumin of the Red Squad (also known as Zhang the Pockmarked and Pockmarked Cai). The freighter carried few passengers; there was only a common hold, men and women crowded together on one long sleeping platform. After four days and nights of pitching and rolling they reached Tanggu on June 20, the day of the Dragon Boat Festival. Early the next morning they changed to a train for Tianjin, and Shao Fumin settled them at the World Hotel in the French Concession. Shao Fumin was a tall, thin man, a graduate of a Shanghai medical school who had once run a small clinic; in the old days our wounded had gone to him for treatment. Later he joined the Central Special Section's Third Branch and became a noted crack shot. This time he had been posted to the Northeast, and since he knew Tianjin well it was on his way to see them there; once Chen Geng's party was settled he went on to Shenyang. Of the voyage Chen Yangshan later recalled: "We took a British freighter and slept on a big common platform. She was very slow — four days to reach Tianjin. Something happened aboard that I remember: there was a steward serving the passengers their water and food, and Comrade Shao Fumin, spotting that the man had leprosy, went at once to the ship's officers to insist he be kept off the job, for fear of contagion. Chen Geng and I even joked with Shao Fumin that it was a good thing we had brought a doctor along, or we'd have caught something. When the ship reached Tanggu the dock was deserted; we went ashore for a look and saw nothing but desolation, everything bare and poor — we couldn't even find a place to sit down to a meal. So we went back aboard, and reached Tianjin about eight or nine the next morning. Comrade Shao Fumin took us to the World Hotel by the Four-Faced Clock. Shao knew the proprietor well and introduced us, saying Chen Geng and I had come to Tianjin to do business. At the hotel Comrade Chen Geng and his family took one room and I another; Shao Fumin probably did not stay — he bought his train ticket and set off for the Northeast."

When they left Shanghai, Chen Geng was in a gray silk gown, silk trousers, and cloth shoes, every inch a merchant; Chen Yangshan too was got up as a businessman. They told the proprietor they had come to open a shop in Tianjin, and the man, taking in the dress and manner of the two, decided that Chen Geng was the boss and Chen Yangshan the clerk (the bookkeeper), and waited on them warmly and well.

Out early and back late, they cast about for a time and found that the Party in Tianjin had been broken up with unusual severity, the White Terror hanging over the whole city; only by immense effort did they at last find a Party contact and get a first sense of where the arrested were being held. Chen Geng then asked the Tianjin organization to send someone to make contact in Beiping — but as ill luck would have it, the man was arrested the moment he arrived.

Time passed quickly, and before long they had been a month at the World Hotel; fearing that a long stay would arouse suspicion, they moved out. Chen Geng and Wang Genying and the boy then took a rented house in the British Concession, while Chen Yangshan went alone to a flat in the Japanese Concession (soon finding a tiny room above a sesame-cake shop, into which he moved).

Before Chen Geng reached Tianjin, Hu Egong (alias Wu Nanhu) and Yang Xianzhen had already been sent there by the Central Committee and had begun work to rescue the arrested Shun-Zhi provincial committee comrades. One day Chen Geng brought Yang Xianzhen to Chen Yangshan's lodging to introduce them. Yang Xianzhen was then going by the alias "Yang Zhongren," and Chen Yangshan by "Old Wang." Chen Geng also met Hu Egong several times, mostly on the roof garden of the Zhongyuan Company in Tianjin. Through Liu Shaobai (alias "Liu Shaoxiang"), Hu Egong and Yang Xianzhen had opened dealings with the relevant Kuomintang quarters and smoothed the way. Yang Xianzhen now moved in with Chen Yangshan, and the two worked together to rescue the arrested comrades. Before long, Yang Xianzhen was suddenly arrested on his way to a meeting in Beiping, and the rescue work came to a halt. (The details are set down earlier in this book, in the third section of "Opening a New Situation," Part One.)

It was now, on Hu Egong's introduction, that Chen Geng met Wu Chengfang, who worked in the Communist Beiping Municipal Committee, in Beiping and asked him to take up intelligence and security work across the Beiping-Tianjin region — one of the three tasks Chen Geng had come north to carry out — and had him come to work with him in Tianjin for a while. Many years later Wu Chengfang recalled: "In August 1930 Hu Egong had Zhang Zhutang, secretary of the Communist Beiping Special Branch, seek me out and get me to talk over my past work. They thought I had some experience of local struggle and had achieved a certain amount. So, on Hu Egong's introduction, I met Comrade Chen Geng in Beiping. Chen Geng had been sent north by the Center to open up the work, and he invited me to Tianjin to be his assistant. Before long I was in Tianjin, staying at a hotel in the concessions. Comrade Chen Geng came to the hotel, laid out my tasks, and talked with me at length. He set out for me the experience and lessons of past clandestine work. He spoke of how, in the Great Revolution, Guangzhou and Wuhan had been the focal points, the political centers of the whole country; and how by the early 1930s, as the Japanese pressed deeper into China, Beiping and Tianjin had gradually become the political center. Miscellaneous warlord troops of every stripe were gathered in Beiping, Tianjin, and elsewhere in north China, and the spies sent by the Kuomintang's Nanjing government were many, and the traitors who served the enemy many too. But there were also many workers, peasants, students, intellectuals, townspeople, and patriotic soldiers ready to struggle against these reactionary forces, and our line of struggle was to meet them head-on. Chen Geng gave me forty work assignments written out in brush; reading them over, I grouped them simply under four heads: special military activity; special political activity (of a united-front character); protecting the organization; and intelligence work. By then Wang Ming had taken the Party's leadership, and the 'Left' line was being applied and pushed in practical work. Wang Ming and his people failed to see that the class relations in China had markedly changed, and branded the intermediate factions — which were at odds with the Kuomintang's reactionary rule and were working actively to bring down the Nanjing government — as the so-called 'most dangerous enemies of the Chinese revolution,' calling for 'down with everything.' Under that mistaken line, the work in the White areas suffered heavy losses. I had no theoretical grounding then, but in practical work I had my doubts about this line, and so I said to Comrade Chen Geng: 'These days some of our comrades, coming to a Party meeting, get out of the rickshaw a long way off — for fear another comrade will see them and report it, and they'll be expelled. We have to deal with the enemy, and we may have to do it in any number of ways. Under this line I cannot work.' Comrade Chen Geng said: 'Just get the work going. Act according to the actual situation; don't carry out the "down with everything" policy.' Only later did I learn that this important guiding idea had been dictated to Chen Geng by Comrade Zhou Enlai. Chen Geng did not come right out and say how "Left" the current central line was; he simply, and very tactfully, offered sound views on concrete working methods. To be able to raise even that much under the conditions of the time was very astute and far-sighted. It gave our work a thread to follow, and above all it laid the ideological ground for later dealings with Feng Yuxiang and the joint founding of the Anti-Japanese Allied Army. In the hour of danger to our Party and our nation, Comrade Zhou Enlai and Comrade Chen Geng, for the sake of the larger interest and with the breadth of proletarian revolutionaries, adopted a very wise strategy of struggle — one that looked to the overall interest of the revolution and that, in concrete action, resisted Wang Ming's 'Left' line. Had I not been clear in my own mind on this, I could not possibly have done the work — let alone had a hand in getting the Anti-Japanese Allied Army off the ground. Before long, Comrade Chen Geng left Tianjin and returned to Shanghai. Tianjin is a great city, but with the spies and traitors so rampant there, even with the concessions we found it very hard to open up any room to work. So I went back to Beiping."

Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan went on investigating traitors in Tianjin and turned up more and more. The surroundings were extraordinarily dangerous, but Chen Geng was bold and liked to be out and about. One day he took Chen Yangshan to the Quanyechang emporium, a very crowded place; Chen Yangshan was afraid something would go wrong, but Chen Geng said no one in Tianjin would know him. As it happened, at the Quanyechang they ran straight into a senior traitor who did. They were strolling about when a big, dark-faced, rather tall fellow in a shabby cloth gown came toward them. Chen Geng jerked his chin at the man and murmured, "That one's a traitor," and told Chen Yangshan to run. They both bolted, and the traitor at once set several men after them. Chen Yangshan recalled: "At one point Chen Geng and I split up, and they split into two to chase us. We didn't know Tianjin, so we just ran wherever we could. After more than two hours it finally got dark, and we shook off the tail at last. It was thanks to some of the little side streets in old Tianjin having no lamps — otherwise something might well have happened."

The traitor they had met at the Quanyechang turned out to be Zhang Keyun (Zhang Kaiyun), a notorious senior traitor of the Beiping-Tianjin region. He had gone to Moscow to study and, on his return, had turned in Tianjin, causing the deaths of many of our comrades and doing great harm to the Party in the north. When Chen Geng saw Chen Yangshan the next day he told him what he knew about the man. They decided the traitor should be dealt with first; and since Zhang Keyun knew Chen Geng, Chen could not show himself, so he had Chen Yangshan shadow the man and establish his circumstances and his routine. One day Chen Yangshan again saw Zhang Keyun at the French Park and began to trail him. After four or five days he had found that the traitor moved constantly among several hotels in the Chinese city and the French Concession, meeting other traitors and agents, very active and very brazen. Once it was established that he was staying at a small hotel in the Chinese city of Tianjin, Chen Geng reported to the Center and proposed that the traitor be eliminated.

During the days Chen Yangshan worked alone, he found that in Tianjin there were still some traitors gathering every dusk at the French Park in the French Concession (a key thoroughfare, through which ran many of the tram lines of the British, Japanese, and French concessions). Some were meeting contacts, others tailing people. After that he had Chen Geng come often to watch the traitors at work. There were too few of them to act; all they could do was learn to recognize the men, trail them to understand their movements, study their routines, and report to the Center so that force could be gathered to deal with them.

Before they knew it, high summer had come. Little by little the two of them had built up a file on a batch of traitors: names, distinguishing features, addresses, associations, routines. One evening after supper the two went for a stroll at the Tianjin Quanyechang and suddenly ran into four traitors. All these days Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan had been meeting these men almost daily, and by now they may have aroused the others' suspicions. The four kept a close watch on the two. Seeing that they were outnumbered and might come off worst, Chen Geng shot Chen Yangshan a glance, and the two slipped quickly away in different directions.

The next day Chen Geng met Chen Yangshan to talk over the work. They felt that Tianjin's traitors were brazen enough — barging about the streets, meeting contacts in the open, tailing people — to be a real threat to the Party's safety. In such surroundings the Party's underground workers had no room to maneuver and could hardly operate at all, so it would be very hard to build a Special-Section-type organization there. They compared notes, too, and reckoned that Shanghai's traitors had never dared be so insolent. So Chen Geng had Chen Yangshan go to Shanghai at once to report, and to propose that the Center send the "Dog-Beating Squad" to Tianjin to eliminate traitors and take some of the swagger out of them. Chen Yangshan returned to Shanghai, told Liu Ding about the traitors' doings in Tianjin, and pressed in particular for Zhang Keyun to be eliminated. Liu Ding told him to wait and he would let him know once the Center had considered it. Chen Yangshan stayed in Shanghai more than half a month, and the Central Special Section sent a comrade code-named "Old Wang" back to Tianjin with him to handle the matter. Chen Yangshan took "Old Wang" round several places to see the ground, and Chen Geng studied the situation with them, getting a firmer grasp of where the traitors gathered and how they moved, and providing detailed material toward a concrete plan for eliminating them. Once "Old Wang" had gathered this material in Tianjin, he went back to Shanghai to report to the Center. (A good while afterward, Chen Yangshan read in the Ta Kung Pao published in Tianjin that Zhang Keyun, that blood-soaked and utterly evil traitor, had at last been killed in a Tianjin hotel — the very hotel Chen Yangshan had gone to while shadowing Zhang Keyun in Tianjin.)

Late August came around. The Tianjin Party organization sent up from Tangshan a worker-Party member who made contact with Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan. His surname was Ju; he had a good record, a wife and children, and once they knew one another he wanted Chen Geng and the others to move into a Party safe house — his own home. He had some connection with the French Concession police, which made things easier. Within a few days Chen Yangshan alone moved in with Old Ju, the family living downstairs and Chen Yangshan up above. Chen Geng came round every day or two, sometimes to talk over the work, sometimes to go out with them.

This was just on the eve of the September 18 Incident; the situation was fairly tense, and Tianjin was rife with rumors that Japan was about to attack China. Chen Yangshan had not been long at the Ju house when suddenly Old Ju ran into trouble. One afternoon, around two or three, Chen Yangshan sent Old Ju out to buy a newspaper — ten minutes there and back should have done it — but by dark Old Ju had not returned. As Chen Yangshan grew anxious, Chen Geng arrived, judged from the circumstances that something had gone wrong, and left the place at once, telling Chen Yangshan to clear out too.

Having left in such haste that he could find nowhere to stay, Chen Yangshan had no choice but to go back to the World Hotel. The proprietor, seeing the "bookkeeper" return, greeted him warmly and put him up. Chen Yangshan told the man their business was about wound up and that they were going back to Shanghai to raise funds. The next day Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan made inquiries of the Tianjin comrades: Old Ju had indeed been arrested. Feeling it dangerous to stay any longer, they decided to return to Shanghai together to report and take instructions.

But how were they to travel from Tianjin to Shanghai? Chen Geng's main concern was that he knew so many people: by train, they might run into a traitor passing through Nanjing; by steamer, there was only one boat a week to Shanghai, and he feared trouble if they lingered. In the end they decided to take the ten o'clock night train, go by way of Jinan to Qingdao, and sail from there to Shanghai. The nearest station to the hotel and to Chen Geng's lodging was Tianjin Main; but with too many traitors about there, they changed to the out-of-the-way South Station to board. That night Chen Geng wrapped his head in gauze to look injured; his leg had in fact been hurt before, and now he leaned on a stick on purpose and walked with such a limp that he was every bit the cripple. The upshot was a smooth journey: by noon the next day they were safely in Jinan, and after a meal at the station they took an afternoon train to Qingdao and put up at a three- or four-story hotel — a decent-looking place that was in fact filthy and full of bedbugs. From Qingdao there were two kinds of boat to Shanghai: a Japanese mail steamer, which made it in a day and a night, and a slow freighter. They bought tickets on the Japanese steamer, returned to Shanghai, and put up together at the Qiantai Hotel on Tianjin Road in the International Settlement (a place where merchants gathered). About ten days after they got back, word came of the September 18 Incident.

Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan had been about a week at the Qiantai Hotel when Chen Yun came to see them. Chen Geng reported on the work he had done in Tianjin and what he had learned, and Chen Yun told him to stay in the hotel and not go out, for safety's sake. The Center had other plans for Chen Geng's work by then, and had him hand the Tianjin work over to another comrade. A few days later it decided to transfer Chen Geng to the Red Army in the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Soviet. Liu Ding now came to talk with Chen Yangshan, asking him to stay in Shanghai and go on doing intelligence work in the Central Special Section.

From 1928 to 1931, in his Special Section service, Chen Geng carried out his tasks brilliantly under the direct leadership of the Party and of Zhou Enlai. The work Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di did in penetrating the enemy's heart, and the counterintelligence tie built with Yang Dengying inside the enemy's own detective apparatus — all of it under Chen Geng's concrete part, planning, and leadership — was a great success, and stands as the two finest examples of the Central Special Section's whole record. Under a bloody White Terror, at the critical moment, it not only kept the Party Center safe and warded off the enemy's persecution, but also trained and tempered leading cadres for the growth of our Party's intelligence work and pioneered the practical experience of striking into the enemy's core — a thing of far-reaching significance.

Chen Geng was only twenty-eight when he left Shanghai. In these years the young Chen Geng, with a whole heart's loyalty, had gone deep into the tiger's den, run every kind of danger, come through the sternest trials, and grown fast in political maturity. Following the Party's instructions in this period, resolutely completing his tasks in the face of every peril, he showed the steadfast, quick-witted, and courageous mettle of a true Communist.

### Li Qiang Goes to Moscow for Advanced Study

This time Li Qiang's position in Shanghai was extremely dangerous too. He too had spent a fairly long time in Gu Shunzhang's company: they had worked together ever since the Central Military Commission set up its Special Services Branch back in the Wuhan days. On the organization's instructions, once he had taken part in the Central Committee's emergency measures after Gu's betrayal, he quickly moved house, relocated, and went to ground. To help meet the Party's shortage of funds, when he took his family back to their home in Changshu that May, he sold off part of the family property. Reckoning that Gu Shunzhang had worked with Li Qiang a long time and seen a great deal of him, so that it would be hard for him to stay hidden in Shanghai for long, Zhou Enlai told him to leave the city at once and study at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in the Soviet Union. Having seen his family home, Li Qiang changed his name to Zeng Zongda and set off for the Soviet Union straightaway.

The journey had its share of setbacks. To begin with, since the Nanjing government had broken relations with the Soviet Union, he could not go there directly; only through a friend working at the German consulate in Tianjin did he obtain a German passport and visa and get away. Soon after he reached Moscow, on the strength of a groundless slander by Wang Ming, he was barred from the Communist University of the Toilers of the East and forbidden to return to China, and was posted instead to the Institute of Communications Science of the Soviet People's Commissariat of Posts and Telegraphs. Li Qiang set all this down in an essay, "My Revolutionary Course." He wrote:

At the same time that the Central Military Commission sent Chen Shouchang to tell me to relocate, Zhou Enlai, after careful thought, decided to send me to the Soviet Union to study, to work, and to further my training. Before I left, Cai Shuhou gave me a letter and told me to look up a relative of his in Tianjin who would help me. I took a steamer to Tianjin and found him, and with his help obtained a visa and passport from the German consulate there and bought a train ticket to Berlin (I could get off partway, in Moscow). This roundabout route was necessary because the Chinese Eastern Railway Incident had just broken out and the Kuomintang government had severed relations with the Soviet Union.

In June 1931 I boarded the train north. At Harbin the Soviet consulate stamped my papers again. There I happened on Zhou Kui, a university classmate who worked in the Harbin customs, and with his help I soon boarded the train for Moscow.

In Moscow I hailed a carriage at once and drove straight to the seat of the Comintern. After its reception staff had asked about my circumstances, they notified Li Lisan right away. Li Lisan took me to the hotel where he was staying, gave me some living expenses, and told me to stay for now and see about work later. His spirits struck me as low, and I learned afterward that he had been criticized for the error of "Left" adventurism; the Comintern no longer trusted him, he was unsure of his own future work, and naturally he could hardly make concrete arrangements for me.

In November 1931 Wang Ming came to Moscow as the Chinese Communist representative to the Comintern. Politically he fought "Left" with "Left"; organizationally he practiced sectarianism, waging cruel struggle and merciless blows against any Party comrade who differed with him in the slightest. Wang Ming held that I was close to Gu Shunzhang, a diehard of Gu's, and trusted me not at all. He said privately: "Engineer Zhang" — my alias — "must not be allowed into the Communist University of the Toilers of the East, nor allowed to go home; once back, he is bound to betray the revolution."

So I was posted to the Soviet People's Commissariat of Posts and Telegraphs. I still lived in the Comintern's dormitory, though, going each day to the Commissariat's Institute of Communications Science. From then on I changed my name to "Li Qiang," and have used it ever since.

The next year Li Qiang was moved to the Comintern's radio training class as an instructor, teaching mainly mathematics, physics, and the fundamentals of radio. His students came from many countries of Europe and America, and there were Chinese students too from the various columns of the Northeast Anti-Japanese United Army. Li Qiang taught largely in English, and beyond the theory he had his students machine parts with their own hands: after class they used the small machines and workbench he had had installed in the classroom to turn and mill radio parts and wind coils. Because they put what they learned to use, the students soon had the essentials in hand. His teaching method won the praise of the Comintern leadership.

Li Qiang taught several classes in this course, and in 1934 returned to the Institute of Communications Science. He had seen 500-kilowatt transmitters there; even industrially advanced Japan could then build only 100-kilowatt sets. Determined to master the advanced radio technology, he wrote, after hard study, a paper, "The Transmitting Rhombic Antenna," which on publication caused a stir in the Soviet Union. In view of his research the Soviet Institute of Communications promoted him from engineer to research fellow, and Li Qiang thus became one of the seven noted radio experts of the whole Soviet Union; Soviet scientific circles called his finding "the Li Qiang Formula." Li Qiang recounted: "At the Institute of Communications Science I resolved to study radio theory. Once I saw in a magazine a report on a rhombic antenna made in America. The report gave only the experimental results, with no theoretical analysis. That, I knew, was the American way — they often prize experiment and slight theory. I work in basic science and have a special fondness for mathematics, so I set out to study antenna theory by mathematical analysis, to demonstrate how, within a rhombic antenna, the electrons and electromagnetic waves converge. I studied it for a full year and more and wrote up my findings in English as 'The Transmitting Rhombic Antenna.' I handed the paper to the institute, and the Soviets took great interest, since no other country yet had a theoretical treatment of the transmitting rhombic antenna. In 1935 a Ukrainian translated my paper into Russian, and it appeared in the journal Electrical Calculation of Antennas, published by the Soviet Communications Technology Press. In view of what I had achieved, the Soviet Institute of Communications Science promoted me from engineer to research fellow and called me a radio expert. My method of demonstration came to be known as 'the Li Qiang Formula.'"

The name Li Qiang used during his Central Special Section service was "Zeng Peihong." Before Gu Shunzhang defected, while the two were both in the Special Section, he and Li Qiang had agreed on aliases for Party liaison: Li Qiang was "Zhang Keming" and Gu Shunzhang "Zhang Keding," the two posing as brothers. After Gu's whole scheme to wreck the Party Center in Shanghai had failed and he could find no one there, he actually hit on the wild idea of running a "notice" in the Shanghai papers — the Shen Bao, the Xinwen Bao, and others — seeking one Zhang Keming: asking where his family had gone, and telling Zhang Keming to come find him at the Yamei Radio Company (that is, the Su Radio Company). It was in Moscow that Li Qiang saw this notice.

Before he knew it, July 7, 1937, had come, and the Lugou Bridge Incident broke out. At the news, which shook the world, Li Qiang's heart flew homeward; he had no more mind for scientific research and longed only to return and join the war of national liberation against Japanese imperialist aggression. He wrote: "The peril of the country and the suffering of the nation filled us with anxiety. After we had pressed our request again and again, the organization approved, and I returned home together with the Chinese students of the last training class. We left Moscow by train on December 12, and only after five or six days reached Alma-Ata, the capital of Uzbekistan. We stopped a few hours at Alma-Ata and pressed on. At Dihua (now Urumqi) we waited more than a month for a Soviet bomber, and only then flew on to Lanzhou. Then we changed to a car for Xi'an, and reached Yan'an at last."

### The "Three Heroes of Longtan" Win New Merit in the Central Soviet

By now the "Three Heroes of Longtan," the prime targets of the Kuomintang manhunt, could find it harder still to stay in Shanghai. The Center decided to move all three to the Central Soviet: Li Kenong first sent Qian Zhuangfei and Hu Di away, and left last himself.

After Qian Zhuangfei reached Shanghai from Nanjing, the Kuomintang sent every agent who knew him by sight to comb the streets and lanes for any trace of him. But the moment he arrived, Chen Geng arranged for him to go to ground, and under the close protection of the Party organization he came to no harm. When Qian Zhuangfei left Nanjing, his daughter Qian Jiaojiao stayed behind (his son-in-law Liu Qifu, having carried the intelligence of Gu Shunzhang's defection to Shanghai, also went straight back to Nanjing). To keep the Kuomintang agents from harming them, Qian Zhuangfei had left Xu Enzeng a letter, warning him that if he dared do his children any harm, Qian would lay bare to the world every one of Xu Enzeng's shameful and unspeakable crimes. Once Liu Qifu was back in Nanjing, he and Qian Jiaojiao were both arrested. Very likely the letter Qian had left did its work: the factions of the Kuomintang's secret-service apparatus were then at each other's throats, their rivalries sharp and their struggles fierce, and Xu Enzeng feared that exposing his secrets would tell against him. Besides, under the protection of Chen Lifu and Zhang Daofan, Xu Enzeng had employed Qian Zhuangfei as confidential secretary in the Investigation Section — and the fact that this had wrecked Gu Shunzhang's whole "net them all in one haul" scheme against the Party Center had been kept from Chiang Kai-shek all along; were it to leak, it would be the more dangerous for Xu Enzeng and his kind. So Xu Enzeng did not dare harm Qian Jiaojiao and Liu Qifu, and released them after holding them a while.

Qian Zhuangfei's family in Shanghai were of course a target Xu Enzeng went all out to seize. A book published in Shanghai in 1997, discussing Xu Enzeng's pursuit of Qian Zhuangfei, says: "Besides wrecking the Communist organs and hunting Zhou Enlai, Xu Enzeng had another important object on this trip to Shanghai: to capture Qian Zhuangfei and be rid of the danger for good. At the time Qian Zhuangfei's wife Zhang Zhenhua was a doctor in the obstetrics department of a Shanghai hospital, and Qian Zhuangfei's home was at No. 4, Xinxing Shunli, Xinxing Road, in the French Concession. Naturally all this vital information had been gathered by Xu Enzeng from Gu Shunzhang." This is simply wrong: Xu Enzeng had no need at all to "gather from Gu Shunzhang" the address of Qian Zhuangfei's home at "No. 4, Xinxing Shunli." The author wrote so because he did not know how deep the "friendship" between Qian Zhuangfei and Xu Enzeng ran, nor how far Xu Enzeng trusted Qian Zhuangfei. As the chapter "Deep in the Tiger's Den" in this book has already related, when Xu Enzeng, having taken up with a friend's wife, Wang Suqing, wanted to keep his "golden house" hidden in Shanghai, it was in the front rooms of this very "No. 4, Xinxing Shunli," Qian Zhuangfei's home, that he stashed her.

This time Xu Enzeng brought his whole Investigation Section down on Shanghai and went at once to "No. 4, Xinxing Shunli" to seize Qian Zhuangfei. The terror of that hour, full of peril at every turn, no one who lived through it ever forgot. Qian Zhuangfei's wife Zhang Zhenhua, recalling it thirty years later, remembered it as if it were yesterday. She said:

The very day Director Li (that is, Li Kenong, who after the founding of New China served as head of the Central Committee's Social Affairs Department) learned the intelligence of Gu Shunzhang's defection, he came to warn me: something has happened, be on your guard. That day, rather than wait for the train to reach Shanghai, Zhuangfei got off at Jiading (by another account Zhenru) and took a car into the city. I telephoned him from the hospital, said there was an emergency, and told him not to come. On hearing that, he went into hiding.

Early the next morning, before it was quite light, someone knocked and said: Doctor Qian! There's someone downstairs to see you. I went down and saw three men. I asked them in, then reached back for a coat, said I was just stepping out, and hurried down; at the door I saw a big truck with a load of men in copper helmets sitting in it, and a small car besides. I ran to the market, and on to Director Li's house (they were in a meeting), and told them, and they got away. Afterward we rented a garret in Shanghai and settled in.

Around that time another man came to the house and found the old lady (Qian Zhuangfei's mother), saying he was looking for Mr. Qian. The old lady said he was in Nanjing. The men said Mr. Qian was already back in Shanghai. There was nothing to it, they said, only a sum of money — a very large sum — that would become his if Mr. Qian would sign for it. The old lady truly did not know Zhuangfei was back in Shanghai; she had not seen him and did not know where he was. The man, at a loss, went away.

Zhuangfei was hiding in Jessfield Park in Shanghai; we met there, and after that he went to the Central Soviet, and we never saw each other again.

After Jiaojiao and her husband Liu Qifu were arrested, they were held three or four months and then let out. Jiaojiao now works at the Shanghai Museum. Liu Qifu, released, taught at a school and died of an infectious disease in 1949. A cousin of his gave his life for the revolution, and his younger brother was in 1956 secretary of the Communist Youth League's Hunan provincial committee.

Around August 1931 the "Three Heroes of Longtan" left Shanghai in succession for the Central Soviet. There Li Kenong stayed at the State Political Security Bureau in Ruijin as head of its executive bureau; Qian Zhuangfei was assigned to the forward troops, to the First Front Army of the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army; and Hu Di went to the front with him.

In the spring of 1932 the First Front Army set up a Security Bureau, and Qian Zhuangfei was named its head. He made a major contribution to establishing radio reconnaissance. "In 1933 Qian Zhuangfei was transferred to head the Second Bureau of the Central Revolutionary Military Commission. Soon afterward the Red Army General Headquarters' own Second Bureau came to the Military Commission's seat at Wushilong in Ruijin and merged with the Commission's Second Bureau, and he became deputy head of the combined Central Military Commission Second Bureau. In August 1933 the preparatory committee for the Second National Soviet Congress decided to raise a Red Army Martyrs' Memorial Tower at Yeping, the seat of the Chinese Soviet government in Ruijin, capital of the Central Revolutionary Base Area in Jiangxi. Qian Zhuangfei took on the design of this monument of historic significance. He shaped the tower as an artillery shell fired at the enemy's positions, a symbol of the Red Army martyrs' revolutionary heroism as they charged into battle and pressed ever forward. When the tower was completed in January 1934, Mao Zedong, Zhou Enlai, Zhu De, and other central leaders all inscribed words on the stone tablets at its base, and there were carved the names of the men who had guided and designed the work, Liang Baitai and Qian Zhuangfei. After the main Red Army force set out on the Long March, the tower was damaged by Kuomintang troops; it has now been restored to its original form and stands at the center of Yeping Square, one of the important landmarks of the red capital, Ruijin. Besides this, he was active in designing mastheads and drawing cartoons for the Red China newspaper. The Red Star Medal that the Central Military Commission awarded to outstanding Red Army officers and men on August 1, 1934, was also of his design."

Of Qian Zhuangfei's time in the Central Soviet, Li Yimang set down an account in his memoirs: "In the autumn of 1932 the State Political Security Bureau wanted Li Kenong to go to the front to replace Qian Zhuangfei as bureau head. Just then I reached Ruijin and was asked to take over from Li Kenong as head of the executive department, while Qian Zhuangfei came back to the rear as head of reconnaissance. It was then that I came to know him. Our work went smoothly enough, and with no major cases we were fairly at leisure. It was decided at that time to raise a Red Army Martyrs' Memorial Tower at Shapingba outside Ruijin, and, in preparation for the congress of the whole Soviet area, to build a great meeting hall; the design of both was handed to him. These became the shell-shaped Red Army Martyrs' Memorial Tower and the brick-and-timber Soviet Congress Hall. Sadly, after the Red Army moved out at the end of 1934 and the Kuomintang army entered Ruijin, both these memorial buildings were destroyed and burned. What we see now was rebuilt to the original pattern by the people of Jiangxi after liberation. When I returned to Ruijin I went to both places, and could not but hold their designer — Qian Zhuangfei — in deep remembrance. Take the tower: its base is a pentagon, and so has ten faces, each bearing an inscription by one of the leaders of the time, cut into stone slabs and set in. Facing the front of the tower is a red-earth path of fitting width, on which small white pebbles are set to read 'March on over the martyrs' blood.' The design was magnificent... He could do more than architectural design; he had other technical gifts too. The various seals of the Chinese Soviet and of its People's Commissariats, round and of silver, were all designed and cast under his supervision. These silver seals traveled with the Long March and through the War of Resistance, and are now kept intact in the Museum of the Chinese Revolution, relics of great historic value to the Chinese revolution."

In October 1934 the Red Army began the Long March, and Qian Zhuangfei was placed in the Military Commission column. After the Zunyi Conference he was named deputy secretary-general of the Red Army General Political Department (a post he never took up). On March 31, 1935, after the Red Army crossed the Wu River, Qian Zhuangfei fell behind while dodging enemy aircraft as he passed through Xifeng county, and was lost. The organization sent men everywhere to look for him, but he was never found. Li Yimang said: "On the Long March we forced the crossing of the Wu River in the Huangping region of Guizhou. Just as we were crossing, Kuomintang planes came; some had already crossed to the north bank and some were still on the south, and when the pontoon bridge had all been dismantled and a head count taken, Qian Zhuangfei was missing. Was he still on the south bank, not yet across? But by then it was late, the main force had to press on, and it was hard to send anyone back over the Wu to find him. This man's habit was, the moment planes came, to leave the crowd and run off to some out-of-the-way, empty spot. When we were dodging the planes on the south bank of the Wu, no one noticed where he ran to. If he had run far off, exhausted, he might have dropped down to sleep, and when it was time to cross again he might not yet have woken; and even if we had gone back for him, we would not have known where to look. Everyone thought him a resourceful man who would find his own way to catch up. But from then on there was no more trace of him. During the War of Resistance the Chongqing office sent men to search that region. From the circumstances, as we read them, it is very likely he was killed by the local landlord militia. Fortunately, Qian Zhuangfei, who designed that memorial tablet, still has his name on it to this day."

Li Yimang's reading was borne out in the end. On May 2, 1985, the Communist committee of Xifeng county, Guizhou, submitted to the Central Committee's Party History Materials Collection Committee an Investigation Report on the Death of the Martyr Qian Zhuangfei:

It has been established that on the night of March 29, 1935, the Central Red Army crossed the Wu River southward from the Shatu and Houshan area of Jinsha county; by the morning of the 30th the main force had entered the Liuchang area of Xifeng county. On the 31st the Central Column and the First, Third, and Fifth Army Groups of the Red Army were active around Liuchang, Luwo, and Jiuzhuang in Xifeng; at noon that day the units of the Third and Fifth Army Groups were bombed and strafed by four enemy planes at Jiuzhuang, and more than a hundred Red Army officers and men were killed. The Central Column, in which Qian Zhuangfei was, was not at Jiuzhuang at the time; but of the twenty-nine Red Army cadres and soldiers who had, in this period, fallen behind for various reasons and scattered about Liuchang, eighteen have now been found to have been killed by the local landlord die-hard militia. Among them, a man calling himself "Xia Shuyun," a "battalion secretary," came the day after the main Red Army force left Liuchang (that is, on April 2, 1935) to the Kehuzhai and Songjiazhai area of Liuchang township. According to eyewitnesses still living, this Red Army cadre looked very unwell in the face and walked with great difficulty as though ill; he was about forty, tall and thin, wore his hair in a "Japanese cut" (parted), spoke with an out-of-province accent, carried a bundle on his back, and held a cloth umbrella. That very evening he was killed in a plot laid by the Kuomintang anti-Communist "village-purge commissioner," the district head Song Zizhen, and his henchman Luo Shaoan and others (both Song and Luo were executed by the People's Government after liberation): on the pretense of guiding them to catch up with the Red Army units, they forced him, on a ridge about half a li to the right of Songjiazhai, down into a cave some fifty or sixty meters deep called "Meiliangkeng," and hurled great stones down onto the bottom, killing him then and there. Some years ago local people organized a party to go down into the cave to search for the martyr's remains, but after a lapse of several decades nothing could be found. Everything this Red Army cadre carried was taken by his killer Luo Shaoan; his books and seals Luo burned at once, and only a yellow woolen army blanket survives, now kept in the Zunyi Conference Memorial Hall, catalogue number 845.

The Xifeng county committee's report concluded: "On repeated investigation and comprehensive analysis of the build, age, accent, and baggage of the murdered Red Army cadre above, and of the eyewitnesses' recollections of his appearance, it can be established with fair confidence that the Red Army cadre calling himself Xia Shuyun, who died at Meiliangkeng in Liuchang township, Xifeng county, on April 2, 1935, was Comrade Qian Zhuangfei — at the time deputy head of the Central Military Commission's Second Bureau, and after the Zunyi Conference transferred to be deputy secretary-general of the Red Army General Political Department (a post he never took up)."

Hu Di had left Shanghai together with Qian Zhuangfei in August 1931, and after entering the Central Soviet had done security work throughout.

Truly accurate accounts of Hu Di's time in the Soviet are few. Some books and articles about the Central Special Section, written any which way, saw the record that Hu Di had been killed by Zhang Guotao and, hearing wind and calling it rain, spun stories as they pleased. A book published in Chengdu in 1996 flatly asserts that "Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and the others left Shanghai one after another and moved to the Central Red Area, while Hu Di, who together with them had made a great contribution to protecting the Party Center in Shanghai, was moved to the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Red Area to work (and was later secretly killed by Zhang Guotao)." Another book, published in Hefei in 1996, says: "Li Kenong and Qian Zhuangfei crossed over into the Jiangxi Soviet, while Hu Di crossed over to the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Soviet." Many articles say the same.

In fact, Hu Di moved to the Central Soviet just as Li Kenong and Qian Zhuangfei did, in succession. On the Long March he was head of reconnaissance in the State Political Security Bureau, and after the First Front Army and Fourth Front Army joined he stayed with the Left Route Army. He was deeply dissatisfied with Zhang Guotao's splitting of the Party and the Red Army and his setting up of a "second Central Committee," and Zhang Guotao had him secretly killed at Caodi on a trumped-up charge of being a Kuomintang agent. Li Yimang, who had worked with Hu Di in the Central Soviet's State Political Security Bureau and gone on the Long March with him, gives a detailed account in his memoirs of Hu Di's work after reaching the Central Soviet and of how he was killed by Zhang Guotao:

In Ruijin I was head of the executive department and Hu Di head of its interrogation section. He wrote a fine hand with the brush, and the documents he drafted read very smoothly. He came from a well-known wealthy family of Tongcheng (properly Shucheng), knew something of art, and had by him two or three scrolls of landscape painting picked up from somewhere or other; I at the time had neither knowledge of such things nor interest in them. There was not much interrogation work, and the two of us had many chances to chat idly; I felt he was a man of some quality.

On the Long March, before Zhang Guotao's split, he worked in the Security Bureau and followed Comrade Zhu De. Once Zhang Guotao made his split, Hu Di broke off from the Central Column and stayed with the Fourth Front Army. He was deeply dissatisfied with Zhang Guotao's anti-Party conduct, and even gave it voice, which came to Zhang Guotao's ears. Zhang Guotao framed him as a "counterrevolutionary" and ordered the Fourth Front Army's General Security Bureau to seize him at Aba and send him back to Chali Temple under guard. In mid-September 1936 Zhang Guotao proclaimed his Plan for a Grand Southward Advance and Political Guarantee, and the Fourth Front Army headquarters and all its units moved south from Aba by way of Chali Temple and Sidaba toward Suijing county, concentrating in the Dangba, Songgang, and Matang area to prepare an attack on Suijing, Danba, Gonggong, and other places. On the road from Sidaba to Songgang, the General Security Bureau reported to Zhang Guotao: "Hu Di can't walk any farther. What do we do?" Zhang Guotao said: "See to it yourselves." So, before the troops made camp at Songgang, the General Security Bureau sent men to kill Comrade Hu Di and bury his body by the roadside. The head of the General Security Bureau then was Zeng Chuanliu. Of this affair Zhou Enlai spoke at a meeting in 1968: "In Gu Shunzhang's defection in 1931, it was fortunate that Comrade Qian Zhuangfei, at Xu Enzeng's Zhongtong, got the word soonest, so that the central organs were spared destruction. Qian Zhuangfei, Li Kenong, and Hu Di were together, and so were sent to the Central Soviet. Later Qian Zhuangfei died dodging aircraft on the Long March, and Hu Di was killed when he joined up with Zhang Guotao. Because Zhang Guotao was giving a speech to the Fourth Front Army and Hu Di, down in the audience, said Zhang was like a fascist, one of Zhang Guotao's guards heard it and reported it to Zhang, and afterward he was killed." Because he was an ordinary Party member, few now mention him. But he was a steadfast member who opposed Zhang Guotao's line. To die in abnormal circumstances for holding to the truth in an inner-Party struggle — such a comrade is very much to be honored. It is not for the two years of private friendship between us that I speak well of him; this is simply how it was.

Li Kenong was the last of the "Three Heroes of Longtan" to leave Shanghai and move to the Central Soviet. On learning the intelligence of Gu Shunzhang's defection, Li Kenong wired to recall Hu Di from Tianjin to Shanghai, warned Qian Zhuangfei's family, and then took a tram back to his lodging at the Fenghuang Hotel — already with someone on his tail. Dangerous as his position was, he still had to see Qian Zhuangfei and Hu Di off and settle the responsible central comrades before he could go to ground himself.

Not long after Li Kenong was brought into the Central Special Section, to make his cover easier and to throw off the enemy, he had his wife Zhao Ying and their two children, who lived in Wuhu, come to Shanghai. This time the blow fell suddenly; with the central organs in danger, Li Kenong bent every effort to ensure their safe relocation and to remove the threats to the Party, and could not see to Zhao Ying and the children. Only when the most urgent matters had been dealt with did he send a courier to take word to his home for Zhao Ying to hide. His bundle of clothes was still at the house: "If anyone comes to arrest me, say I've gone to Hangzhou." That morning Zhao Ying was still at work in the kitchen when the landlady told her to "go out and buy some food." Seeing the plainclothes agents multiply at the door, and having lost touch with Li Kenong, she was for a time adrift in the streets, sleeping nights at the market; the two children she owed to the landlady's care. One day at the North Sichuan Road market Zhao Ying ran into Comrade Wang Shaochun, who went pale at the sight of her and told her not to wander about. Wang Shaochun placed Zhao Ying safely in hiding for more than a month, and had a letter passed through the branch to the Central Committee and on to Li Kenong, before the two could meet. After that Li Kenong moved to the Central Soviet, and Zhao Ying took the children back to Wuhu, hard put to make a living and enduring every hardship. From then on all word was cut off; only after the peaceful settlement of the Xi'an Incident did Li Kenong regain contact with his family.

In August 1931, setting out for the Central Soviet, Li Kenong took off his Western suit and put on a coarse cloth gown to make himself "unrecognizable," hard to place. With two other comrades he went by steamer by way of Hong Kong. Turned back by landlord militia near Dapu, he returned to Hong Kong to find Elder Sister Kan, ran a joint trading firm with his companions for a time, and only in winter entered the Soviet. The long, hard journey left him so worn that he fell ill the moment he reached the Soviet. Li Kenong came to Tingzhou on a stretcher, and the man who came to meet him was none other than Qian Zhuangfei — an old comrade-in-arms with whom he had fought shoulder to shoulder for years deep in the tiger's den, met again now in the red area, to his boundless joy.

In Ruijin, Li Kenong was first assigned to the Jiangxi provincial security sub-bureau as head of its executive department. In 1932 he was transferred to head the Political Security Bureau of the First Front Army of the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army. With his brisk, decisive way, he soon set up political security sub-bureaus in turn in the five army groups under the First Front Army. Later the Red Army Security Bureau became the Red Army Work Department, and Li Kenong became its head. In this period he, Qian Zhuangfei, Hu Di, and the others often wrote, directed, and acted in their own plays, living newspapers, and two-man comic turns — he once starred in Long Live the Secretary-General — and did much to organize and lead the Red Army's cultural and recreational life.

In October 1934 the Red Army began the Long March, and Li Kenong led the Red Army Work Department with the vanguard of the Central Committee and the Military Commission, his task to investigate the enemy's dispositions and clear out enemy scouts. During the historic Zunyi Conference, Li Kenong mounted a tight guard and kept the meeting safe. On reaching northern Shaanxi he became head of the Central Committee's Liaison Bureau (also called the Northwest Liaison Bureau, and known outwardly as the Foreign Affairs Department), handling united-front work with the outside, liaison with the Northeast Army and the Northwest Army, and clandestine courier work in the White areas; he received Snow, Ma Haide, Ding Ling, and others. In the War of Resistance and the Second Revolutionary Civil War he did arduous work in learning the enemy's situation, protecting our own, and suppressing counterrevolution. After liberation he achieved notable results in diplomatic work and in the struggle to safeguard socialist construction and consolidate national defense.

### Liu Ding's Troubled Withdrawal

Liu Ding, who worked in the Intelligence Branch, had had no direct dealings with Gu Shunzhang in his work, and so for the moment went neither into hiding nor away; he stayed on for a time after the Central Special Section was reorganized, and was unlucky enough to be arrested in October. Released, he was sent by the Party organization to the Central Soviet, and on the way met with more setbacks yet. With a whole heart's loyalty to the Party and an indomitable fighting spirit, Liu Ding weathered a winding, rough life and made an outstanding contribution to the Party's cause.

Faced with the grave threat Gu Shunzhang's betrayal posed to the Party, Liu Ding fought a hard fight under Zhou Enlai's command. Together with the other Central Special Section comrades, he successfully covered the relocation of the central organs and the responsible comrades, then held on at his work through the crisis. Qian Zhuangfei, back in Shanghai from Nanjing, was a prime target of the enemy manhunt; after he reported to the Central Committee, Chen Geng arranged for him to go to ground, and charged Liu Ding with keeping in touch with him and looking after him. Li Kenong too was a prime target of Xu Enzeng's manhunt: he wired to recall Hu Di from Tianjin, settled Qian Zhuangfei's family, and "with one hand pulled Wang Ming and with the other Bogu" into safe hiding, then went to ground himself — Li Kenong, too, being looked after through Liu Ding. All the while Liu Ding was also taking part, in that terror-filled atmosphere of peril at every turn, in the round of hard fighting to counter Gu Shunzhang and remove the danger.

After the Central Special Section was reorganized, Pan Hannian took over the Second Branch, and Liu Ding handed over, one by one, the intelligence contacts in his charge, keeping for the moment only the work of liaison with the Kuomintang Reorganizationist "Mr. Gao." Since the September 18 Incident, factional strife within the Kuomintang had sharpened, and the Kuomintang Reorganizationists were stepping up their anti-Chiang activity. The Central Committee urgently needed intelligence on this, and Liu Ding kept at it. On the morning of October 10, 1931, Liu Ding went to the Bund Park in Shanghai to meet "Mr. Gao," but agents had long been lying in wait, and the moment he reached the park he was seized.

Liu Ding was tortured after his arrest, but flatly refused to reveal his true identity. To get word of his arrest out in time, so that his family and the comrades staying at his home could relocate at once, he led the agents to a courier point he had once run — a fruit shop — hinting to the shopkeeper to slip away and pass on the warning. The agents ransacked the fruit shop for a while, got nothing, realized they had been had, boxed his ears twice, and took him back to the police station. Under arrest, he went by the alias "Gan Zuomin," invented a personal history and doings, and admitted only to being an ordinary courier. After examination by the Second Branch Court of the Jiangsu High Court, Liu Ding was extradited to the Songhu Garrison Command, held more than ten days in Longhua Prison, then sent to the Nanjing Gendarmerie Command, given a provisional two-year sentence, and imprisoned in the Nanjing Army Prison. Through the trial and confinement Liu Ding met the enemy's carrot-and-stick, its threats and inducements, with an unbending will and a clear head, keeping strict silence about the central leaders he knew, the many activities of the Central Special Section, and its important outer contacts, and never letting the enemy get a single usable lead. The traitor Gu Shunzhang came to the prison to talk him into surrendering; Liu Ding, knowing that Gu did not understand his work very well and playing on Gu's eagerness to raise his own worth before his new masters, flattered him with feigned humility: "You're the boss — whatever I did, you know all about it; without you, nothing can get done; I sit idle at home with nothing to do." Gu, hearing this, was indeed carried away with delight, for he had all along boasted to the Kuomintang that once the Communists lost him their intelligence work was finished, to show how able he was. Liu Ding's words struck exactly the right chord, and Gu thumped his chest and told the judge: "I know all about his case; leave it all to me." So the enemy questioned Liu Ding no further.

Liu Ding got in touch with the Central Special Section organization in Shanghai from the Nanjing prison by a secret means of communication, and reported what he had learned inside. Once, the Special Section head Pan Hannian sent two comrades to visit him under the guise of relatives at a prison visit and passed on the instruction that he should "work to get out early" — or plant himself in the enemy secret-service organization in Nanjing, or return to Shanghai to work. Acting on this, he deceived the enemy and strove to get out as fast as he could. In September 1932 Liu Ding was released on bail put up by Kang Ze and others. Out of prison, he first lodged at the China Daily office, then moved to a hotel. On the surface the enemy seemed to have given Liu Ding his freedom, but in fact they watched him very closely. One evening at dusk he slipped quietly out of the hotel, shook off the tail, found a Party courier station in Nanjing, and with the help of the comrades there was put on a train and hurried through the night to Shanghai. In Shanghai he found the Communist organization, reported on his time since his arrest, and asked to be sent to the Central Soviet. Before long Pan Hannian, on behalf of the organization, granted his request and arranged for him to move to the Soviet.

That Liu Ding managed to get out "on bail" so early owed a great deal to his wife Wu Xianqing. The "two comrades sent by Pan Hannian to visit him under the guise of relatives," who had passed on the Party's instruction, were Wu Xianqing and her sister-in-law Sun Ruzhen. Zhao Zijie, in his essay "Wu Xianqing," recounts the affair: "Wu Xianqing was good at reading her surroundings and cautious and quick in her dealings. In October 1931, when Liu Ding was arrested in the park and she came home from an errand, she at once sensed something amiss around her, made up her mind on the spot not to go in the door, and so escaped. After that Wu Xianqing worked under Pan Hannian's leadership, and was also responsible for keeping in touch with Liu Ding in prison.

Wu Xianqing's sister-in-law Sun Ruzhen had graduated in the 1920s from the Beijing Higher Normal School, and while studying in Beijing had come to know some fellow townsmen then at the Baoding Military Academy, men who by the 1930s all held important posts in the Kuomintang army. Wu Xianqing judged that these connections could be used, and so had her sister-in-law go to Nanjing to call on the notables she knew. Sun Ruzhen first found Li Jinde, chief of the inspection division of the Kuomintang capital police, explained her purpose, and won the official's help — he escorted her to the prison visit in his own car. The second time the two women came to Nanjing, they first put up at the Central Hotel and then telephoned Li Jinde. Fearing danger for them, Li Jinde took them to stay at his own home, then found the Kuomintang air force commander Zhou Zhirou and asked him to step in and smooth things over. Since Wu Xianqing's brother Wu Quanqing was a fellow townsman of Zhou's and had served as his confidential secretary, Zhou Zhirou knew Wu Xianqing well and knew she was a Communist. On seeing Wu Xianqing, Zhou burst out laughing and said: 'Here comes the bandit's wife' (in the Linhai dialect, a term for a brigand's wife). Wu Xianqing said calmly: 'That's right, I've come, and today it's either you let Liu Ding out or you lock me up with him too.' Getting Liu Ding out was of course not so simple, but this 'prison visit' did great good. On the one hand, Wu Xianqing used these connections to pass the organization's instructions cleverly to Liu Ding, so that he was later (in the autumn of 1932) released on bail and returned to Shanghai; on the other, under the 'protection' of these connections she got safely back to Shanghai herself."

At the start of 1933 Liu Ding, under the alias Dai Liang, set out from Shanghai for the Central Soviet. It was just then the Kuomintang's fourth "encirclement and suppression" of the Central Soviet, and when Liu Ding passed through the Soviet of northeastern Jiangxi he found the routes to the Central Soviet tightly blockaded by the enemy and had to stop for a time. Fang Zhimin, learning that Liu Ding had studied politics, military affairs, and industry in Germany and the Soviet Union and had come through the stern tests of struggle against the enemy — a rare talent — wired for the Central Committee's consent and kept him to work in the northeast Jiangxi Soviet. There he served as head of the organization department of the Min-Zhe-Gan Soviet's political department, and as political commissar of the Fifth Branch School of the Red Army School. Later, appointed by Fang Zhimin political commissar of an arsenal, he set his own hand to developing and organizing the production of the Red Army's first gun and shells, pioneering our Party's early arms industry. In October 1934, on the orders of the Central Military Commission, the northeast Jiangxi Tenth Red Army and the Seventh Red Army Group from the Central Soviet were combined into the Tenth Red Army Group, and, as the Red Army's Anti-Japanese Vanguard for the March North, pushed toward southern Anhui under Fang Zhimin and others. No sooner had the main Red Army force left than the enemy launched a great "mopping-up" of the Soviet. The arsenal where Liu Ding was broke into small units for guerrilla fighting and skirmished with the enemy. In May or June 1935 Liu Ding was captured in the mountain forests of Nanshan at Yiyang.

Passed from hand to hand by the enemy, Liu Ding was held in the Jiujiang prisoner-of-war camp in Jiangxi. Under interrogation he hid his identity, saying only that he was an engineer taken prisoner by the Red Army while passing through the Soviet. He often used his skills to do a little technical work for the enemy — repairing cars, clocks, and the like. Believing him an engineer, the enemy gradually relaxed their watch, sometimes even taking him out to buy food and mend tools; and he used these chances to learn the lay of the land and prepare his escape.

One weekend in the autumn of 1935 Liu Ding fled to the riverside wharf, found a steamer bound for Shanghai, gave the fare that the camp mess steward had entrusted him to buy vegetables to a crewman who took smuggled passengers, and was hidden aboard and carried away from Jiujiang. By then the Shanghai Party organization had been badly broken up, and the Central Committee had moved to the Central Soviet two years before. Unable for the moment to reconnect with the organization, Liu Ding went to find "Boss Cai" of the Shaodun Electrical Company — Cai Shuhou, formerly of the Central Special Section, later transferred to the Comintern's China group. Cai Shuhou first put Liu Ding up at an inn, and introduced him to the American journalist Agnes Smedley.

Smedley was a friend of the Chinese people, deeply sympathetic to the Chinese revolution, and had joined the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights, often shielding and rescuing Communists and revolutionaries. She took Liu Ding to the home of her New Zealand friend Rewi Alley, under the alias Zhou Shu, and told Alley: "He'll stay with you until he goes elsewhere." Alley had all along kept his home as a refuge for Communists; living with him at the time was Gumperson, a British Communist, who ran a secret Comintern radio in Shanghai. Liu Ding hid at Alley's for close to a year, and dangerous as the surroundings were, he went on with his revolutionary work without pause under Alley's active support. Alley wrote in his memoirs:

My home was now a place where revolutionary cadres could stay, and we soon grew used to strangers coming and going whose real names we never knew. When Agnes brought them, she always gave each an English name.

One evening in 1935 Agnes came with a short man in her company, whom she introduced as Charles. "He'll stay with you until he goes elsewhere," she said. So Charles became one of our household, and only later did I learn his name was Liu Ding. During this time he would sometimes have my schoolboy son Mike take my own Ford to carry things. For after The Voice of China was left free to publish openly, we still secretly printed and distributed many leaflets and pamphlets in support of the united-front policy. Later, hearing that the police were looking for a car of my number, I hurried to sell it. Since I still worked at the fire department, my red official car was sometimes especially useful, able to carry fugitives past the police checkpoints or out of the International Settlement; sitting in the back, they escaped inspection.

Liu Ding stayed close to a year, then went to the Northwest. When he came to Shanghai again he brought an orderly, a "Little Devil" — a boy in the Red Army too young to serve in a fighting unit. The child wore Northwest peasant clothes. I went out and bought him a pale-blue short-sleeved shirt, khaki shorts, white socks, and leather shoes — the casual dress of upper-class Shanghai children then — so he could change. When he had put on the shirt, Agnes, who was there to see these two visitors, caught sight of a hammer and sickle tattooed on his forearm, and seized the boy's arm and, laughing and moved, kissed the emblem. I then went out again and bought him a long-sleeved shirt with buttoned cuffs.

One evening we went with Agnes by motor launch up the Huangpu, passing on purpose the foreign warships moored on the river so they could see them. Liu Ding talked earnestly with Agnes for a long while, and afterward asked me to buy a set of medical instruments and other supplies for the Red Army. We filled a large trunk with these things. A few days later he took the trunk and his little orderly back to Xi'an. He would be based in Xi'an from now on, he said. I promised to visit him on my summer holiday; I planned to spend it there.

After the Long March, when the Red Army had joined up in the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia border region, we set to work buying and shipping large quantities of medicine to the far-off Communist areas of the Northwest. The problem was how to buy it in Shanghai. Unless an order was countersigned by some acceptable person, the pharmacy would not fill it. But a pharmacy would accept the signature of the League of Nations health experts sent to the Nanjing government. The Austrian doctor Erich Landauer was an expert of that standing, and signed several orders. Another expert, the Yugoslav doctor Bocic, countersigned the order for the medicines that Edgar Snow and Ma Haide took to Yan'an. To get their signatures one had to take the train from Shanghai to Nanjing, find them, and get back as fast as possible. The next problem was whom to deliver the goods to in Xi'an. Agnes Smedley found a way and acted on it decisively. She found an anti-fascist fighter, a Jewish refugee from Germany, Dr. Herbert Wunsch, who agreed to set up a dental clinic in Xi'an for the purpose; he would notify Liu Ding to collect the goods. Liu Ding was becoming more and more an important figure behind the scenes.

Liu Ding stayed at Alley's until the start of 1936. In that time Zhang Xueliang came to Shanghai to meet in secret Li Du, a commander of the Northeast Anti-Japanese Volunteers. Zhang Xueliang, thinking the Central Committee was still in Shanghai, had solemnly asked Li Du to help find a Communist connection to discuss joint resistance to Japan in the Northwest. That March, after Liu Ding met and talked with Li Du, he agreed at Zhang Xueliang's invitation to go to Xi'an to see him, and Zhang Xueliang at once sent men to Shanghai to fetch him. On March 20, Liu Ding reached Xi'an, escorted by Zhao Yi, whom Zhang Xueliang had sent. In Xi'an Liu Ding was warmly received by Zhang Xueliang; his work there bore good results and won the Central Committee's praise. On July 25, 1986, Liu Ding died of illness in Beijing. The Life of Comrade Liu Ding, released nationwide by Xinhua on September 2, states:

In March 1936 Comrade Liu Ding went to Xi'an and, with his learning and his rich experience of struggle, gave General Zhang Xueliang a systematic account of the Chinese Communist Party's policy of a national united front against Japan, so that General Zhang Xueliang came to a truer understanding of the Communist Party and the Red Army, and strengthened his confidence and resolve to cooperate fully with the Communists against Japan; this prepared the way for Comrade Zhou Enlai's talks with Zhang Xueliang at Fushi (Yan'an). Comrade Liu Ding took part in those talks and was afterward appointed our Party's representative to the Northeast Army. He helped General Zhang Xueliang educate the Northeast Army in the patriotic spirit of uniting to resist Japan, and strengthened the unity of the Northeast Army with the Red Army and the Seventeenth Route Army. The Party Center fully affirmed Comrade Liu Ding's work at the time. Comrade Mao Zedong said again and again: "In the Xi'an Incident, Comrade Liu Ding rendered service." In 1961 Comrade Zhou Enlai personally appointed Comrade Liu Ding head of the leading group for the history of the "Xi'an Incident."

Liu Ding was the founder and chief pioneer of our country's arms industry. From the Second Revolutionary Civil War and the War of Resistance onward, he threw himself heart and soul, gave his whole life's strength, to founding the arms industry and building national defense industry, and won imperishable merit. After the founding of the People's Republic, "he gave all his strength to the socialist construction of New China; to the building and growth of our country's arms, machine-building, and aviation industries; to the realization of a modern national defense; and to industrial, agricultural, and educational work, he made a major contribution. He turned the strengths of the defense enterprises to the development of civilian goods, and organized the design and trial manufacture of a 10,000-ton hydraulic press, contributing to the advance of socialist construction. To his work he brought unfailing warmth, diligence, and dedication, a readiness to break new ground, shrewdness and competence, and a steadfast, fact-seeking working style. He was good at joining Marxism-Leninism and Mao Zedong Thought to production technique, often going deep into the front lines of research and production to investigate and solve problems, and won the support and love of the great body of engineers, technicians, workers, and cadres. Comrade Liu Ding was the founder and chief pioneer of our country's defense industry, and enjoyed high prestige in the arms and machine-building world." (The Life of Comrade Liu Ding)

After Liu Ding passed away, on the desk in his bedroom people saw a yellowed newspaper clipping bearing words of Zhou Enlai's: "We must do solid, real work — do real things, get real results — to be of use to the people. Speak the truth, put in real effort, do real things, get real results. These four lines come down to one: seek truth from facts. The wisest people in the world are the most honest, for only honest people can stand the test of fact and of history." Liu Ding and Zhou Enlai were both honest men who "stood the test of fact and of history" in just this way.

### Zhou Enlai Reaches the Red Capital, Ruijin, Safely

Xiang Zhongfa, the provisional Party general secretary who lived with Zhou Enlai, was sold out by Gu Shunzhang and arrested on June 22; he then gave up Zhou Enlai and their lodging on Xiaoshadu Road. When the enemy sent men to seize them, Zhou Enlai and Deng Yingchao had already left. But the enemy would not give up: they kept agents watching the house and pressed the search and pursuit on every side, so that Zhou Enlai had to hide the more strictly, vanishing from the enemy's sight without a trace. He, Deng Yingchao, and Deng Yingchao's mother, old Mrs. Yang Zhende, moved into a small tobacco-and-sundries shop at the corner of Haining Road and Shanxi Road near Suzhou Creek, the three of them crowded into a ten-square-meter garret. Only a very few in the Party knew where he lived, and he kept apart from the other leaders. With the enemy searching everywhere, he could scarcely go on staying in Shanghai. Before long the Center decided he should stop work for the time being and prepare to move to the Central Soviet.

Dangerous as his position now was, Zhou Enlai did not stop work before leaving Shanghai; he still took important reports and drafted documents for the Center. It was now that Japan seized the Northeast in the "September 18 Incident." Chiang Kai-shek ordered "no resistance," the Kuomintang government's army retreated without a fight, and China faced a national crisis of unprecedented gravity. Though under strict cover and preparing to relocate, Zhou Enlai still watched the developing situation closely, and even wrote several articles on the "September 18 Incident."

Ever since Gu Shunzhang's defection, Zhou Enlai had been moving the central organs and some Central Special Section comrades who could no longer safely work in Shanghai to various places, one after another, while also giving thought to his own move to the Central Soviet. In those days there was one main route from the Kuomintang-controlled area into the Central Revolutionary Base: from Shantou, through Dapu, across the Kuomintang blockade line, into the guerrilla area of Yongding in Fujian, and then by way of Changting into southern Jiangxi. Three or four months earlier Zhou Enlai had had Lu Weiliang, head of the Dapu courier station, come to Shanghai to report, and had questioned him in detail about the conditions along this route, so as to prepare.

In early December Zhou Enlai left Shanghai for the Central Soviet. The Center sent Huang Ping, lately back from the Soviet Union, to escort him. Huang Ping later recalled how they left Shanghai:

Comrade Zhou Enlai left Shanghai in November 1931. Before that, with the White Terror rampant, the Party Center decided he should stop work for a while and wait to relocate, going to the Soviet once preparations were made. In September, on account of my work, I called on Li Fuchun and on Cai Chang and Nie Rongzhen. They were living at Qingyuli on today's Changle Road, waiting to go to the Soviet. I arrived in the afternoon and saw Enlai there too; besides them, the central courier Gong Muhua (?) was there. They were playing mahjong, which was of course a cover. It was dark before Enlai left; he had come at first light, to lessen the danger, and waited until dark to leave for the same reason. Plainly Enlai had something urgent to tell them, to have risked coming.

At the end of October that year I took a letter of introduction into the Soviet to Comrade Enlai's home. He was then living above a little shop at the corner of Haining Road and Shanxi Road (a tobacco-and-sundries shop, I think, though it was hard to make out at night); Enlai and his wife and his mother-in-law, three of them, lived in a room of about ten square meters, lit by a single fifteen-watt bulb. The light in the room was dim. At the time Zhang Wentian lived in a two-story Western house, and Zhao Yun (Kang Sheng) too had a bright second-floor room the width of the building; and as for Qin Bangxian, one could tell from the fine Western suits he wore that he lived none too badly — yet the hard-working, meritorious Enlai lived in this cramped, gloomy little room! It was only to save the Party a little expense. Enlai was frugal all his life, unchanging over decades; no wonder the people love and cherish him so to this day.

I gave him the letter of introduction. It began: "Dear Sir, nothing else..." The Premier did not understand what "nothing else" meant, and asked Deng Yingchao, but she could not make it out either. The old mother-in-law, hearing this, said it was a merchant's phrase, meaning: there is nothing else in the letter, only the following matter to say. Enlai said: since it puzzles us, it may puzzle the enemy too, and that would not do; better to change it. As he wished, I took the letter back and changed it, and delivered it to him again the next night.

The day Enlai left Shanghai, I reached his home about eight in the evening. He was wearing a lined blue serge Chinese jacket buttoned down the front and a pair of blue serge Chinese trousers — the dress of a skilled Cantonese worker. The room was dim, and I could not see what shoes he wore. He had me go buy a cap. I found a shop on nearby North Sichuan Road and bought him a blue cloth cap, which he put on and found a good fit. His disguise ready, he took a small suitcase, and the two of us went downstairs together, hired two rickshaws, and set off. To avoid drawing notice, Deng Yingchao did not come down to see him off. At Shiliupu we boarded at once a steamer of Butterfield & Swire or Jardine Matheson, going by way of Hong Kong or straight to Shantou — I cannot be sure — but never at the risk of arrest, going ashore at Hong Kong. In the common hold we found a courier nicknamed "Little Cantonese," whom Enlai knew. I handed Enlai over to "Little Cantonese" and took my leave and went ashore.

The steamer he took sailed two days and two nights to Shantou. "Little Cantonese" made contact with Huang Hua, the secret courier of the Dapu courier station, and Huang Hua and "Little Cantonese" led Zhou Enlai, meaning to put up at the Jinling Inn arranged beforehand. But no sooner had they stepped in the door than they saw hanging in the inn's parlor a photograph from 1925, of the welcome rally held by all quarters of Shantou during the Whampoa student army's Eastern Expedition, with Zhou Enlai seated in the middle. Afraid of being recognized, they moved at once to a small hotel on Mian'an Street and spent the night. Here Zhou Enlai changed his disguise again, got up as a physiognomist. Early the next morning they left the small hotel in a heavy fog and boarded the train from Shantou to Chao'an. Reaching Chao'an at two in the afternoon, they got off and took a steamer up the river toward Dapu; partway they got off again and changed to a small launch bound for Hutousha. At daybreak, passing Qingxi, they went ashore there and reached the site of the Dapu courier station.

From here to the entry into the Soviet was the crucial stretch of the whole journey. Along it lay the tight blockade line of Kuomintang troops and local militia, with blockhouses and watchtowers and many checkpoints and sentry posts. To slip across, one could not take the main roads, only the seldom-trodden mountain paths and gullies; one could not travel by day, only feel one's way through the dark of night. Lu Weiliang, head of the Dapu courier station, had made preparations early, sending six couriers to escort Zhou Enlai across the blockade line under arms. In the black of night they crossed hills and ridges, clinging to vines and creepers, and passed the blockade line, reaching Wushixia village in Yongding by mid-December. Couriers of the Yongding station carried on the escort and, two days later, reached Hexi; then they entered Shanghang county.

The moment he entered the Soviet, everything around Zhou Enlai was so fresh that it filled him with excitement. Along the way he used the rest stops to talk with peasants and township and district cadres, learning from them the real state of the Soviet's work. On December 22 he reached Changting (formerly Tingzhou), the seat of the Communist Fujian-Guangdong-Jiangxi border provincial committee and provincial Soviet. In a letter to the Politburo Zhou Enlai wrote with delight: "The prosperity of Tingzhou is simply the first among all the Soviet areas of the country (the Central Area has nine towns, but none like Tingzhou; the other Soviet areas have no fixed towns yet)." At Changting he called together the leaders of the provincial committee, the provincial Soviet, and the Changting county committee, and gave an eight-hour report, setting out in detail the present situation and the Party's tasks.

At the end of December 1931 Zhou Enlai reached the end of this journey — Ruijin, capital of the Central Revolutionary Base Area — where he met Mao Zedong and Zhu De, already there, and Ren Bishi, Xiang Ying, and Wang Jiaxiang, who had arrived earlier, and took office as secretary of the Central Committee's Soviet Area Bureau.
