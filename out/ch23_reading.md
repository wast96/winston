## 9

When Father Brown threw himself from a window of the military police headquarters, a salute of guns was being fired over Suzhou Creek. The day was 29 April 1942, and the Japanese troops garrisoned in Shanghai were all celebrating the forty-first birthday of their emperor. Father Brown chose this day to end his life. He had said to the intelligence officer Nakamura Nobuo, “I will tell you everything you want to know, but you must promise to let me return to Rome.” Nakamura Nobuo agreed at once. To display the so-called generosity and mercy of the Imperial Japanese Army, he even had a proper English afternoon tea prepared. The priest paid it no mind; he asked only to be allowed a bath and a change into a clean shirt. “God will not permit me,” the priest said, “to enjoy so fine a tea stinking like a pig.”

Nakamura Nobuo nodded, and had a guard take the priest up to the officers' bathhouse on the floor above. Just then his aide reminded him that they ought to guard against the prisoner's taking his own life. Nakamura Nobuo said with a smile that a Catholic priest might kill, but would never kill himself. He instructed the aide further: to subjugate an enemy, the whip and the bullet were not enough; one had also to understand their history and their culture. Nakamura the intelligence officer had always held it as an article of faith that the courage and the glory of suicide belonged to the samurai of their Yamato race alone.

It was from the window of the officers' bathhouse that Father Brown jumped. When he had washed his scar-covered body clean, he climbed naked onto the sill without so much as a prayer. Father Brown closed his eyes and spread his arms, and, like a crucifix dropping out of the empty air, he fell naked to his death on the concrete road.

A few days later, when Zhongliang carried an ecclesiastical letter into St. Thérèse's Church, Father Kruger, standing before the altar, told him the news. With a helpless look the priest raised his eyes to the image of the virgin saint high on the wall and said that for a Catholic, suicide was a sin from which there was no rising again. Zhongliang stood there and thought once more of his father. He gave a light smile and said to Father Kruger, “It is nothing. He only gave up one faith for the sake of another.”

Father Kruger looked at him in shock, as though he had seen the devil, and after making the sign of the cross over his breast, said, “My God.”

Within himself Zhongliang gave a cold laugh and turned to go. He heard Father Kruger's voice reach him from far behind: “Believe in God, and have eternal life.”

It was the summer of the following year when a postal inspector took up his post at the Jing'an post office. Early one morning two Japanese military policemen drove the inspector in through the great iron gate on a three-wheeled motorcycle, and the whole office fell silent in an instant. The inspector did not get down; he stood in the sidecar, ran the eyes behind his black-framed glasses over every face, and then said to them all, in fluent Chinese, “I am Itō Kinji. I ask for your kind indulgence.”

That said, Itō made a bow that lasted a full half-minute before he straightened, stepped down from the sidecar, and walked straight into the postmaster's office.

His face dark, the postmaster flung out a hand and followed him in. By dusk his face was dark still, and at the front gate he stopped Zhongliang and pressed him to come for a drink or two. Zhongliang looked at the postmaster in surprise. This stiff, buttoned-up man would not, on an ordinary day, waste half a sentence of idle talk on a postman, much less drink with one; but this evening he drank a great deal and said a great deal, and every sentence struck Zhongliang to the heart.

The postmaster sat in a small tavern, and when the dishes were all laid out he poured Zhongliang's wine with his own hand. Uneasy, Zhongliang said, “Postmaster, say whatever it is you have to say.”

The postmaster nodded, and told him to resign the moment he came in to work the next day. Zhongliang's eyes went wide, and he asked why. The postmaster said, “You still don't know why?”

Zhongliang said, “How would I know?”

The postmaster said, “What sort of man are you? What sort of man was your father? And that Zhou San — you people know best of all.”

“They're all dead men now.” Zhongliang said, “I'm a postman who delivers letters.”

The postmaster shook his head and said he had come into the postal trade to eat this bowl of rice back in the second year of the Xuantong reign. “I've seen more people than you've delivered letters.” As he spoke he pointed out toward the street. “The concessions are full of every walk of life, full of men everywhere who set no store by their lives. But whether you're Chongqing's, or Nanjing's, or Yan'an's — whatever you do, you mustn't drag others down with you.”

Zhongliang said, “Postmaster, you've had too much to drink.”

The postmaster waved a hand. “If I can see this much of what's going on, do you imagine that Itō Kinji can't? Listen to the way he speaks Chinese and you'll know he's no mere postal inspector.” The postmaster gave Zhongliang a look full of meaning and went on, “I'm saying this for your good, and for everyone's. You must know better than I do — the Japanese will kill a whole street of people over a single sack of flour.”

Zhongliang could not get out a single word; his face began to go white, but he could still smile, could still lift his cup and drink — only the wine had lost every trace of its taste.

At parting, the postmaster clapped Zhongliang on the shoulder in the street. “You needn't worry. If I meant to inform on you, I wouldn't have waited until today, and I certainly wouldn't have stood you this meal.” Emboldened by the wine, the postmaster said, “I'm a Chinese too. My old home is in Hubei. The Japanese dug up my ancestors' graves and tore down my family's ancestral hall — all because they'd heard that a forebear of mine had served two terms as a provincial governor under the Daoguang emperor.”

The glint of tears in the postmaster's eyes shone under the street lamp, but Zhongliang was unmoved. He stood in the street, watched the postmaster's back disappear around the corner, then hurried home, and the moment he sat down he told Xiufen the whole thing.

“You know the rule.” Xiufen said it before he had finished.

“But I've never so much as killed a chicken.” Zhongliang looked at his woman, his eyes like an innocent child's.

Xiufen thought a moment, stood up, and said, “I'll go.”

Zhongliang said, “Let me think.”

Xiufen said, “The longer the night, the more the dreams.”

Zhongliang said nothing more, and reached out to draw Xiufen back onto the stool. That night he lay thinking in bed until past midnight, then shook Xiufen awake and said he had made up his mind. Heavy with sleep, Xiufen said, “Then take me to the post office at daybreak — let me learn the face first.”

Zhongliang said, “Never mind.”

Xiufen came wide awake at once. “It's not as if you'd have to do it yourself.”

“Better let it go.” Zhongliang turned over. “For now I'm only a postman.”

But Zhongliang was soon dismissed from the Jing'an post office all the same. The reason given was his carelessness — the letters of the Japanese residents above all, which either went missing for no reason or were misdelivered to the wrong hands. Yet Itō Kinji, who received the complaints, was not in the least angry; he sat behind his desk smiling at Zhongliang and asked why a postman could not deliver letters properly. Zhongliang seemed a little nervous, and wore, too, a touch of shame. Itō Kinji next asked whether he was unwilling to serve the Japanese. Zhongliang shook his head; he had already realized that to end a postman's career in this way was an unforgivable mistake. Itō Kinji stood up smiling, came before him, stared into his eyes, and said, “Why do you want me to fire you?”

“Need you even ask? He wants to pack up and go.” The postmaster spoke up suddenly. “There are plenty of people out there who'd like to be postmen.”

The tense air eased a little. Itō Kinji turned his head and glared fiercely at the postmaster.

The postmaster, in turn, turned and glared at Zhongliang. “Do I have to spell it out for you? The finance office door is open. Settle up and go.”

Itō Kinji's face turned iron-gray after Zhongliang had gone. He fixed his eyes on the postmaster. “What are you afraid of?”

“Afraid?” The postmaster gave a small laugh. “What have I got to be afraid of?”

“Then go and keep him on. I want him to go on being a postman here.”

“That won't do. We can't let one turd spoil the whole pot.”

“It's not for you to say what goes here now.”

“Lose letters and you pack up and go — that's the post office's rule.”

Itō Kinji gave a cold smile. “Then you don't know the rule of the military police.”

The postmaster's face went white all at once, and he murmured, “Inspector — over a single postman, you'd send me to the military police?”

Itō Kinji was taken aback and said nothing; and until the postmaster had bowed his way out of the office, he still stood there stiff and straight, looking at the Map of China that hung on the wall. This Japanese agent, who had lived twenty years in Shanghai, had lost heart the very moment the three-wheeled motorcycle drove into the Jing'an post office. He had been dismissed his post for spreading defeatist talk about the war while drunk. The last order the command had given him was the fittest punishment for a pessimist: to remain on this Chinese soil until the day this war was won.

Itō Kinji knew that he would pass the whole of his life in longing for his native Nagoya.
