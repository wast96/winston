CHAPTER UNDER REVIEW

Chapter 9. The "Rescue Campaign": How a Political Campaign Broadened the Hunt for Agents

To unify the thinking and the action of the whole Party, Yan'an launched a Rectification Movement without precedent in its history. Rectification had two kinds of target. First, the people of "half a heart": those who carried non-proletarian ideas or had made mistakes. Second, the people of "two hearts": agents and traitors. The half-hearted would be cured by ideological education; the two-hearted, by vetting the cadres.

And so a logic took shape: "Rectification must lead to cadre vetting, and cadre vetting must lead to rooting out traitors."

1. "Case-Cracking" in Step with the "Campaign"

Let us look at this stretch of the timeline.

From the spring of 1938 to the spring of 1942, the years in which Juntong set up and ran its Hanzhong class, were precisely the years Yan'an spent preparing its Rectification.

From the spring of 1942 to the spring of 1943, the year Yan'an spent cracking the "Dai case," was precisely the year in which general Rectification unfolded.

The synchrony is worth pondering.

Early 1938: the Nationalists and the Communists were negotiating their alliance.

That April the Nationalists set up their two great secret services, Zhongtong and Juntong. Dai Li, who ran the Juntong bureau, told Cheng Muyi, head of the Shanghai district's operations section, to build a "special reconnaissance group" aimed squarely at the Communist base areas. To train his agents, Cheng Muyi opened a "special training class" at his old home in Wenzhou.

In the same months the Communists were incubating a Rectification of their own. In September 1938 the Sixth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee conveyed the Comintern's position and confirmed Mao Zedong as the core of the Party Center. The senior cadres then took up the study of Rectification and of Party line, correcting the "Left" line of the Second Revolutionary Civil War.

In February 1939 the Party Center resolved to establish the Central Social Affairs Department to run the whole Party's intelligence and security work.

In September, Juntong shifted its class for the Communist base areas from Zhejiang to Hanzhong and began enrolling and training educated young people from the Border Region in numbers.

Even as they adjusted their policies, neither side let the hidden struggle slide.

In October 1940 Wu Nanshan came forward on his own to confess how he had been tricked into the Hanzhong class. The Longdong Security Section counter-used him to watch the work of Juntong's Northwest Special Reconnaissance Station.

In December, Mao Zedong drafted the Party Center's internal directive "On Policy," judging the policies of the Second Revolutionary Civil War too "Left," summing up the Party's policy and strategy for the War of Resistance, and laying down how traitors and agents were to be handled.

In the Communist base areas, both the study of Rectification and the counter-espionage work were deepening.

Through 1941 the graduates of Juntong's Hanzhong class, posing as progressive youths, sat the entrance exams for the Yan'an schools and infiltrated the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region and the others. In August, Dai Li ordered his fruitful "special reconnaissance group" enlarged into a "special reconnaissance station." In October, Juntong's Northwest Special Reconnaissance Station convened a meeting in Xi'an and resolved to send couriers, gather up its sleepers in the various Yan'an organs, and drive its intelligence work hard.

On May 19, 1941, Mao Zedong delivered his report "Reform Our Study" to a conference of senior cadres. In August the Communists set up the Central Investigation and Research Bureau, with Mao Zedong himself as its head and Ren Bishi as his deputy, and under it an intelligence department, a political research office, and a Party-affairs research office. In September the Party Center held a Politburo meeting to examine the political-line questions in the Party's history, and the Center decided to form a senior study group.

In October the Longdong Security Sub-office arrested Qi Sanyi, the courier for Juntong's Yan'an group. In January 1942 Qi Sanyi picked out the courier Yang Chao at Yan'an's New Market.

Investigation and research were the ground on which Rectification would unify thinking, the ground on which the counter-espionage work would proceed, the ground under every task. These arrangements marked the Party Center bearing down, across the board, on the preparations for a general Rectification.

On February 1, 1942, at the opening of the Central Party School, Mao Zedong gave his address "Rectify the Party's Style of Work."

The very next day, February 2, the Central Social Affairs Department issued its "Directive on a Campaign Against the Nationalists' High-Grade Intelligence Blitz." The Border Security pressed harder at the "Dai case" and turned up yet another Juntong courier, Li Chunmao.

Mao Zedong's address marked the passage of Yan'an's Rectification from its preparatory stage into general Rectification; it is also called the opening of the Yan'an Rectification. In the same weeks the Social Affairs Department was setting out its counter-espionage work, and the Border Security scored a major breakthrough in its watch on Juntong's Hanzhong class.

Which is to say: the general spread of Rectification and the breakthrough on the Dai case ran on the same clock.

In the spring of 1942, once the Yan'an Rectification had entered its general, Party-wide phase, everything sped up at once: Rectification, cadre vetting, the rooting out of traitors.

On February 17 Wang Shiwei published his essay "The Politician and the Artist" in the magazine *Grain Rain*. On February 21 Kang Sheng relayed Mao Zedong's "Rectify the Party's Style of Work" to more than 2,200 Yan'an cadres in the Eighth Route Army auditorium. In March a poisoning scare broke out at the General Office's compound, and Wang Ming, then under criticism, claimed his doctor was poisoning him. On March 9 Ding Ling published "Thoughts on March 8" in the *Liberation Daily*. On March 13 Wang Shiwei published "Wild Lily" in the same paper. In late March Wang Shiwei ran a string of pieces on the wall paper "Arrow and Target" at the Central Research Institute. On April 3 the Central Propaganda Department issued its "April 3 Directive" to steer the Rectification. On April 5 the *Liberation Daily* printed "Our Self-Criticism," a piece of soul-searching from the wall paper "Light Cavalry" of the Central Youth Committee.

On May 1 Yan'an held its May Day rally. There the Border Security identified Zhao Xiu, chief courier for Juntong's Yan'an group; with that, every courier of the group was in Border Security's hands. Later that month the Border Security swept up the "Dai case" agents scattered across the Border Region, taking eleven in the first haul.

On May 2 Mao Zedong convened the Yan'an Forum on Literature and Art; on the 23rd he gave his "Conclusions." On May 27 the Central Research Institute opened its reckoning with Wang Shiwei.

One battle by force of arms, one by force of the pen: Yan'an fought both at once.

On June 2 the Party Center set up a General Study Committee, with Mao Zedong himself as chairman and Kang Sheng as his deputy. Kang Sheng, the head of the Central Social Affairs Department in charge of intelligence and security, now also had charge of the Party's central task, Rectification.

On June 10 the Border Security formally took onto its field staff five turned "Dai case" men, Qi Sanyi and Li Chunmao among them.

On June 19 Wang Shiwei was labeled a "Trotskyite."

In October the Social Affairs Department cracked the most dangerous nest of the "Dai case," the sleeper cell inside the Military Commission's Second Bureau.

On October 19 the Party Center's Northwest Bureau convened a senior-cadre conference to sum up the history of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region.

On December 6 the Central General Study Committee issued its "Circular Against the 'Small Broadcast,'" holding that it was precisely through these "small broadcasts" that agents pried loose the Party's secrets and spread "plausible rumors" as cover for counter-revolutionary work. The circular is reckoned the herald of the "cadre vetting." By year's end the Juntong Hanzhong class case was cracked entire, with thirty-two sleeper agents found across the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region.

Was it by design that the case-cracking and the Rectification ran together?

Rectification was carried out to a plan; the Dai case surfaced by chance. Who could have ordered Wu Nanshan to confess of his own accord in October 1940? Who could have appointed Cheng Muyi to send Qi Sanyi to Yan'an in October 1941?

Yet the later cracking of the case was driven by plan. The mass roundup of May 1942 above all was the leadership's own choice of the moment. The explanation given at the time was that the situation had grown tense and the net had to be drawn in without delay.

What was so tense in the situation? Take in the whole board, and the sharpest tension lay not in the fighting fronts but at a turning point in the Rectification. The Yan'an Rectification was passing, just then, from a rectifying of Party style into an assault on Wang Shiwei and his kind.

Seen this way, the synchrony of "Rectification" and "rooting out traitors" was not wholly a coincidence. There was a thread of cause and effect in it.

The "Dai case" broke out amid "Rectification," and "Rectification" in turn drove the "Dai case" on; the fruits of the "Dai case" reminded people to watch for "cadre vetting" within the "Rectification," and the progress of "cadre vetting" in its turn pointed up how much the "rooting out of traitors" mattered.

To say the cracking of the "Dai case" decided the launching of Rectification and cadre vetting would surely be to overstate the case's weight. But that the discovery of it made the Party Center put more store by vetting cadres in the course of Rectification, that much follows logically.

"Rectification must lead to cadre vetting, and cadre vetting must lead to rooting out traitors." This conclusion, all the rage in its day, came from Kang Sheng.

Kang Sheng, who wore two hats, deputy chairman of the Central General Study Committee and head of the Social Affairs Department, led both the Rectification and the rooting out of traitors at once. Whether from the habit of office or from raw nerves, he was forever raising a matter of thought to a matter of politics. There is evidence that it was Kang Sheng who drove hardest to reclassify Wang Shiwei and his like from men of mistaken thought to men of mistaken politics, and then to men of counter-revolution.

When Kang Sheng learned how the "Dai case" was being cracked, he made a point of reporting it to Mao Zedong, who of course praised the work. But Kang Sheng drew his own lesson from the case: those caught up in it were all young, especially outside intellectuals, and they had wormed into the Border Region's Party, government, and army. Therefore the vetting and the counter-revolutionary hunt should be cast wide, and their main quarry should be the outside intellectuals.

Kang Sheng put it plainly: "The 'Dai case' has rung an alarm bell for us." And: "At least half of the outside intellectuals were sent in by the Nationalists!"

2. From the "Old-Case Suspects" to the "Shandong Anti-Trotskyist Purge"

Even before the "Dai case" surfaced, the Communist security organs had kept a steady watch for "Nationalist agents."

The Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region had two judicial bodies in the War of Resistance, the High Court and the Border Region Security Office, and the Border Security also discharged the duties of a procuracy. The army system had, besides, its anti-traitor department and its judicial office. Of the whole judicial apparatus, the Border Security ran the largest establishment. Its detention house sat at the foot of Phoenix Mountain, with four wards named Heaven, Earth, Man, and Harmony; before the "Dai case" broke, some suspects were already held there, known as the "old cases."

Public order in the Border Region was good, with few thieves or killers, and most of the "prisoners" in the "old cases" were men merely suspected of being "agents."

Into Yan'an, deep in the loess plateau, came four white men who called themselves "White Russians." White Russians were the Russians who had fled to China after the October Revolution, tsarist loyalists all. What had brought these erstwhile anti-Communists to Yan'an?

There was also a Japanese woman, passed hand to hand from a base area behind enemy lines until she reached Yan'an. On examination she proved a mere commoner, not worth pursuing. Release her? She had moved about the base areas a long while; who could promise she would let nothing slip? So she was kept at the Border Security detention house. Call it imprisonment, and she lived in no cell; call her a guest, and she had to ask leave to go out. Now and then this Japanese woman even minded the children of the women cadres of the rear third section.

What is worth study is that a few Communist Party members, too, were held in the old cases.

Cai Ziwei had been branded a Trotskyite in the Shaanbei purge, and his case had dragged in Liu Zhidan and others. After the Center corrected that mistaken purge, Cai Ziwei served as principal of the Yan'an Middle School and later as head of the Border Region's Finance and Economics Committee. Yet on the eve of the cadre-vetting campaign he was suspected once more of being an internal enemy and held by the Border Security.

Lin Lifu had been a "long-term resident" there since 1939. In Shanghai he had been Party-group secretary and propaganda chief of the Chinese National Armed Self-Defense Committee, the "Self-Defense Committee"; when the organization was smashed he alone got away, reaching Yan'an in 1937 to serve as secretary of the interior department at the Northwest Office. Lin Lifu suspected that a traitor within the Party had wrecked the Shanghai Self-Defense Committee, and he kept reporting his suspicions to the organization. To his astonishment, the man who denounced others as internal enemies came under suspicion himself. Kang Sheng ruled that Lin Lifu, who had once studied in Japan, was a "double agent": a Nationalist agent and a Japanese agent both.

The affairs of the white-area Party, and of the Shanghai underground in particular, were tangled indeed. The case dragged on unresolved, and Lin Lifu stayed in Border Security custody. Later a man named He Guiren was locked in the same cave dwelling.

He Guiren's family background was more tangled still. His grandfather had been Zhang Taiyan's teacher, his father a Hanlin scholar of the Qing; he himself was born in England and had, while working in the white areas, once helped rescue Liu Shaoqi and Kang Sheng. In 1934 He Guiren went abroad, took a doctorate in law, and traveled through Europe and America; in 1937 he led a group of returning students home to join the war, reaching Yan'an in 1938. A man of great learning, he was chosen as one of Mao Zedong's secretaries. He wrote a novel that glanced at Jiang Qing, sensed his own footing turning treacherous, and asked to be transferred out of Yan'an to the Nationalist areas. Just as he was boarding the vehicle with his wife and children, Kang Sheng ordered him held. Kang Sheng's explanation: a telegram had come from the Nationalist areas saying there was an international figure in Yan'an to be arrested. Soon enough it was found the arrest was a mistake, but Kang Sheng then said He Guiren had too many ties in the Nationalist areas, and his going out would do harm. So He Guiren stayed on in Border Security custody for years. Later Britain's Reuters offered him charge of its North China news agency, and his father took a post at the American State Department. Kang Sheng then demanded that He Guiren confess to being an agent.

In the old cases there was also one Wang Wenyuan. This Wang Wenyuan, who had joined the revolution in 1927, was originally a Special Branch man; after the Shanghai Special Branch collapsed, he made his way to Yan'an. Just then Wang Ming's wife, Meng Qingshu, also arrived in Yan'an, and the name Wang Wenyuan pricked her ears at once: China's Trotskyite organization had a Central Committee member by exactly that name!

This Wang Wenyuan was not that Wang Wenyuan. The Yan'an Wang Wenyuan's real name was Jin Shuwang; his elder brother Jin Cheng was head of the Border Region's liaison office, and his uncle by marriage, Xuan Xiafu, was also a Special Branch man. The Shanghai Wang Wenyuan really was a Trotskyite Central Committee member; his real name was Wang Fanxi. The two Wang Wenyuans were nothing but namesakes. Yet even knowing they had the wrong man, those above would not let him go.

If Lin Lifu and He Guiren really had some history of grievance with Kang Sheng, Li Yuchao's ties to him were close and personal, a fellow townsman, a schoolmate, and a colleague. And still, once in Yan'an, Li Yuchao too was set aside for the Border Security to examine.

Li Yuchao was another Special Branch veteran. In 1931, when Gu Shunzhang defected, the imperiled Zhou Enlai and his wife had taken refuge in Li Yuchao's home. A man once so deeply trusted was, once he reached Yan'an, examined all the same, for reasons of one kind and another. Li Yuchao, to be sure, was not locked up in the Border Security; he lived as an ordinary cadre, free to move about and to join in labor, but he was given no work.

In Yan'an it was still a matter of the odd interrogation; in Shandong it had already grown to scale.

In June 1939 a cadre school run by the Huxi prefectural committee graduated its students, and some of them complained about their assignments. Wang Xuren, the committee's organization chief, decided a Trotskyite ring was stirring up trouble in the school, and put men to the torture.

The case swelled fast. Not only was there a Trotskyite ring in the school, there was an "International Proletarian League," and a school instructor named Wei Dingyuan was the "Trotskyite secretary of the Su-Lu-Yu Border Region special committee." Wang Xuren conspired with Wang Hongming, political commissar of the Fourth Battalion of the Eighth Route Army's Su-Lu-Yu Detachment, and they put nearly three hundred Huxi cadres to death.

The Huxi anti-Trotskyist purge spread quickly, reaching the southeastern and southwestern Shandong districts, Jiaodong, Taishan, Qinghe, and elsewhere in the Shandong base area, with three false cases one after another.

The infighting badly wounded the base area's unity, and the Japanese and puppet forces seized the moment to sweep in. In November, Peng Mingzhi, commander of the Eighth Route Army's Su-Lu-Yu Detachment, looked into the Huxi affair himself and reported it urgently to the 115th Division headquarters. Luo Ronghuan, the 115th's political commissar, and Guo Hongtao, secretary of the Shandong Bureau, galloped to Huxi and swiftly stopped the purge, freeing the detained cadres. Liu Juying, the Shandong Bureau's social affairs chief, led an inspection team to Huxi to investigate and wrote a summary report. The Shandong Bureau issued an order punishing those responsible for the Huxi errors and commending the wrongly killed Party members. But it did not yet rehabilitate everyone who had been wrongly handled.

The Party Center sent a five-man group under Chen Yun to investigate and settle the Huxi affair, and on February 20, 1941, it issued the "Central Decision on the Anti-Traitor Errors in the Huxi Border Region," calling it a grievous affair that should put the whole Party on guard, and setting out six points for the aftermath. Liu Juying, the Shandong Bureau's social affairs chief, went to Huxi, convened a rally to rehabilitate and mourn the dead, announced Wang Xuren's crimes, and disciplined Wang Hongming and Bai Ziming.

A "Trotskyite" meant a member of the "Trotskyist Opposition." This faction, native to the Soviet Union, was slight in China both in numbers and in reach. So why would cadres in China's Huxi go "subjectively exaggerating the strength of the Trotskyites"?

Fairly stated, China's war years were a tangled affair. Some Trotskyites opposed the Nationalist-Communist alliance, declaring that to oppose the Nationalists was revolution while to resist Japan was not. Such an extreme "Left" stance was hard to make sense of, and one could not help wondering whether Japanese agents worked behind it. The struggle in Shandong was cruel beyond the ordinary; there was even a case in which a sub-district commander set up his own mountain kingdom and murdered his political commissar.

The blame for subjectively exaggerating the strength of the Trotskyites falls plainly to Kang Sheng.

Kang Sheng had worked long in the Soviet Union. In the Communist delegation to the Comintern, Wang Ming was head and Kang Sheng deputy. In August 1936 the Soviets opened their campaign against the Trotskyites; in 1937 the Comintern issued a directive requiring the Communist parties of every country to take up the struggle against them. Only then did the Chinese Party learn there even was a Trotskyite faction, though it still tried to win over Chen Duxiu, China's Trotskyite leader, and split him from them. In November 1937 Wang Ming and Kang Sheng returned to China and pushed the anti-Trotskyite line hard, Kang Sheng publishing his long screed "Root Out the Trotskyite Bandits, Spies of the Japanese Invaders and Enemies of the Nation." On February 26, 1938, the Party Center's Secretariat issued its "Decision on Broadening the Campaign to Root Out the Trotskyite Bandits and Traitors."

In February 1939 Kang Sheng became head of the Central Social Affairs Department, in charge of the whole Party's counter-espionage and security work. That June the anti-Trotskyist purge broke out in Huxi, Shandong.

The Huxi affair was of no small moment. Yan'an had not yet launched a campaign, and Huxi had already turned its anti-Trotskyist hunt into one!

3. The "Four Great Agents" and the "Red-Flag Party"

From the suspects in Yan'an's old cases one can pick out a common thread: all came from the white areas, all were intellectuals, all had fairly tangled family and social ties.

When the relevant organs vetted a cadre, they had to establish his family origins, his own record, and his social ties. The "base-area cadres" mostly came of worker or peasant stock, had grown up on the spot, and were of clear provenance. But of some of the "outside cadres" the security organs knew rather little. Most of these came from what used to be called the "white areas," the "Nationalist areas," were of the upper strata of society, some with overseas connections, and had comparatively tangled records; naturally they became the focus of the security organs' scrutiny. Outside intellectual cadres like Cai Ziwei and Li Qiming had already fallen under suspicion in the Shaanbei purge.

In the early years of the war more and more educated young people threw in their lot with Yan'an, and among them were no few of tangled record. So, were there agents among these people?

If the residents of the old cases were no more than "suspects," the newly arrived guests were "agents" confirmed.

Before the general Rectification, Yan'an had already seized its "three great agents": Qian Weiren, Li Ning, and Wang Zunji. Add Zhang Keqin, taken during the Rectification, and there you have Yan'an's famous "four great agents."

Qian Weiren, an engineer with the Border Region highway bureau, had come to Yan'an with his father, Qian Laisu, whose aliases were Qian Jiaji and Peng Erning for the son, Qian Zheng for the father. Qian Laisu, who had once served as a major-general adviser in the Nationalists' Second War Zone, had brought his children secretly over to Yan'an out of disgust with the Nationalists' policy of non-resistance. Father and son were not Communists; both had held good posts in the Nationalist areas, yet they resolved to leave home to resist Japan and to cast their lot with Yan'an and its hard life. This should have stood as a model for China's intellectuals; instead they were suspected of ulterior motives. Qian Laisu, it was said, had ties to the Japanese, and someone further charged that in his work at the highway bureau Qian Weiren had dealings with the Nationalist side and links to Nationalist agents.

Li Ning, from Shanghai, was strikingly good-looking and a figure much noticed in Yan'an. Material forwarded from Shanghai suggested she had a problem, and Kang Sheng added that she walked like a Japanese woman; on this she was suspected of being a Japanese agent.

The Wang Zunji case caused an even greater stir in Yan'an. "Wang Zunji's uncle is North China's number-one traitor." "Wang Zunji is really Kawashima Yoshiko in disguise." And so on.

When Wang Zunji was ten her father died of a stroke, and her mother placed her in the household of her uncle, Wang Kemin. The wealthy Wang Kemin was a Beiping banker, and he sent the Wang sisters to an aristocratic school. At school Wang Zunji threw herself into the anti-Japanese student movement and joined the "Vanguards." What she did not yet know was that this "V" organization was the "Chinese National Liberation Vanguard," under the Communists' secret leadership.

When the Japanese seized Beiping, Wang Kemin, who had once studied in Japan, stayed behind. The Japanese set up a North China autonomous government and chose this president of the Beiping chamber of commerce to chair the North China Political Council. So it was that Wang Zunji's own uncle became North China's greatest traitor.

The shame of it! At school Wang Zunji could not face anyone, and, unwilling to go back to a traitor's household, she begged the Vanguards to help her escape into the Communist-led Jizhong base area. Before long she traveled on with the wife of Lü Zhengcao, the Jizhong military commander, to the rear office in Shaanxi.

Wang Zunji, arriving happily in Yan'an, was sent straight to the Border Region Security Office to be examined. No sooner had she left the Jizhong rear office than a Nationalist force struck it, calling by name for the rescue of Wang Zunji. This one was a great traitor's niece, and she could work a wireless set besides: worth pursuing.

The young Wang Zunji could not stand up to the intimidation, and she had to invent lies to get through. And so the stories she had heard and the novels she had read became the stuff of her confession. The Japanese agent Wang Zunji had put out poison; she had signaled Japanese aircraft with a lantern to bomb Shanghai's Nanjing Road; her signals to the Nationalist secret service were knitted into a sweater!

North China's number-one traitor had an agent ring inside the Jizhong base area! The security people believed they had caught a great double agent, Nationalist and Japanese both.

The "three great agents" set Yan'an buzzing, and yet all three cases wanted for evidence. It is easy to suspect; hard to unsuspect. The Border Region's security organs had limited means of reaching outside, and it was hard to verify anything in the Nationalist areas. So the "three great agents" stayed locked up under examination.

The fourth great agent was more startling still, and dragged in a many-province "Red-Flag Party"!

At the very crux of Yan'an's Rectification, at the very moment the Border Security drew in its net on the Hanzhong class, on May 26, 1942, Zhou Enlai reported an emergency from Chongqing: the Communists' Southern Committee had been wrecked by Nationalist agents! Its deputy secretary Zhang Wenbin, its member Liao Chengzhi, and more than twenty other senior cadres were arrested, and the underground Party in Guangdong and Guangxi was gravely damaged.

Nationalist agents in Ji'an, Jiangxi, had stopped and searched travelers and pounced on two young women. Now that Nationalists and Communists were allied against Japan, they said, every Communist must support the government, and secret work like theirs was illegal. This line left the two peasant women at a loss, and they admitted they were underground couriers for the Communists. Following the vine to the melon, Zhongtong seized seventeen members of the Communists' southwest-Jiangxi special committee, and without alarming a soul.

Under the wartime alliance, the Nationalists had not yet found the Communists' organization in their own areas. Xu Enzeng set great store by this lead and at once sent the Zhongtong master agent Xu Xigen to Ji'an to direct matters on the spot. Xu Xigen had been a Communist Central Standing Committee member and had worked in the Special Branch, and he knew the Communists' secret work through and through. Rich in the experience of the turncoat, he first talked around the special committee's propaganda chief, old Yang, then lured in the committee's organization chief, Li Zhaoxian, and had Li Zhaoxian lead the search for the Jiangxi Provincial Committee.

To reach the provincial committee's seat at Yangxi Mountain, one had to be guided by an old courier, "Old Iron-Crutch." He was a Party member since 1925, firm of revolutionary will. Zhongtong played on his love for his little son, catching and freeing him three times over, until Old Iron-Crutch had no choice but to lead Li Zhaoxian up the mountain. Halfway there Old Iron-Crutch repented and urged Li Zhaoxian to come clean to the organization; but it was Li Zhaoxian who talked him around once more: better to work for the Nationalists. The new conditions of the alliance were setting even firm Communists to wavering.

In January 1942 Li Zhaoxian lured the provincial-committee leaders Xie Yucai and Luo Qiding down the mountain to visit their wives, and Zhongtong pounced on the pair at once. With his wife near term, Xie Yucai reluctantly signed a bond, and Luo Qiding offered to lead them to the Southern Bureau. Zhongtong meant to use this tie to get into the Southern Bureau, but Luo Qiding and his wife slipped away in the dark of night. Reckoning that Luo Qiding would not dare go back and report to the provincial committee, Zhongtong laid a further plan: it used Old Iron-Crutch to lure more than forty of the committee's people down the mountain in batches, and received them under the pretense of united-front ties. The committee's secret wireless up the mountain was worked by a husband and wife, and when childbirth forced them down, they were installed with the agents. Zhongtong sent a woman agent to tend the new mother with care, and only once feeling had ripened did it strike in the open. By this cunning method Zhongtong won over the underground Communists in its net one by one, then had the turncoats do the persuading, until step by step it turned the committee's leaders and its guards, and even the Red Army wireless operator and cipher clerk sent from Yan'an gave themselves up. Huang Luping, secretary of the southwest-Jiangxi special committee, after his own defection even helped Zhongtong lay its plans and take control of the county organizations under the committee.

Through 1942 the forty-four county committees and more than two hundred district committees under the Communists' Jiangxi Provincial Committee were all wrecked; over two thousand Party members were arrested and over two thousand rural Party members put under "supervised training," and all the while the higher organ far off in Guangdong, the "Southern Committee," knew nothing of it!

Zhongtong then laid plans to work upward, meaning to wreck all the Communists' organization in the south and reach at last into the Yan'an Center. The Jiangxi Provincial Committee's wireless, now under Zhongtong's control, suddenly called the Southern Committee's set, claiming it had just been repaired and asking besides for Yan'an's call sign and wavelength. The "Southern Committee" was a body under the Communists' Southern Bureau, charged with the southeast and the south. Wary that the Jiangxi station had gone silent for three or four months, the Southern Committee's secretary Fang Fang sent his organization chief Guo Qian to Jiangxi to check, and sent Provincial Committee secretary Xie Yucai a coded letter in plain-language cipher.

Seeing the Southern Committee in danger, Xie Yucai flung down his infant and leapt out a window to flee. Judging it could no longer play the long game of penetration, Zhongtong at once sent men to move ahead of Xie Yucai. On May 26 Fang Fang learned what Xie Yucai had reported and wired Guo Qian. Guo Qian was arrested before he could decode it, turned that same night, and the next day led agents to arrest the northern-Guangdong provincial secretary Li Dalin and others!

When Zhou Enlai saw Fang Fang's wire, his face changed, and he kept saying, this is bad. He thought a moment, then had Tong Xiaopeng wire the Southern Committee at once: Liao Chengzhi, then at the committee's seat in Qujiang, was to come to Chongqing at once, or else stay at the home of his mother, He Xiangning.

The Southern Committee could not reach Liao Chengzhi. On May 30 Liao Chengzhi was arrested; on June 6 the committee's deputy secretary Zhang Wenbin, its propaganda chief Tu Zhennong, and more than twenty senior cadres of the northern-Guangdong provincial committee were arrested, along with more than forty county-level cadres. On June 8 Zhou Enlai, learning of Liao Chengzhi's arrest, at once had the committee's leaders scatter and go to ground and break off all open ties. But the committee's wireless had already been sold out by turncoats, and it never received Chongqing's call.

Zhongtong then sent a dozen-odd armed men to the committee's secret seat at Dabu on the Dong River to arrest the Southern Committee secretary Fang Fang. This was old base-area country, and when the people spotted the agents they beat their gongs to raise the alarm at once; a thousand and more surged out to surround the agents and cover the escape of Fang Fang and the rest.

Worn out in body and mind, Zhou Enlai had a recurrence of his intestinal hernia and entered hospital for surgery in late June. Mao Zedong wired from Yan'an urging him to rest. In hospital Zhou Enlai still kept asking for word of the Southern Committee, until Dong Biwu, standing in for him at the Southern Bureau, ordered that no one was to speak to him of work. On July 10 Zhou Enlai's father died of illness, and Dong Biwu, after conferring with Deng Yingchao, kept it from him for the time. On July 13 Zhou Enlai left the hospital, learned his father had been dead three days, and wept without stop. Even in his grief he gave his orders at once: except for the Party organizations in the occupied and guerrilla zones, which were to carry on as usual, all Party work in the Nationalist areas was to halt and await the Center's decision.

Zhongtong had broken the underground organizations of the Communists across three southern provinces, Jiangxi, Guangdong, and Guangxi, and Xu Enzeng was mightily pleased with himself: "This is my one victory over the Communists in the fighting of the war years. It is also the victory in all my record of battles that took the longest and was the most successful in technique."

Zhou Enlai rebuilt with painstaking care, re-forming a Guangdong provisional provincial committee at the end of 1942 and reviving organizational work everywhere by 1944. When the Japanese drove open the Canton-Hankou railway, the Communists' underground in the south again roused the people to resist, and played a great part.

The lesson of the Southern Committee's undoing was staggering: a whole provincial committee, Jiangxi's, in the grip of the Nationalist secret service!

There is the enemy within our ranks, and our own within the enemy's; even a familiar comrade could not be wholly trusted, and denouncing agents became a chief occupation in Yan'an. The Social Affairs Department received a flood of denunciation letters daily, and among them one forwarded from the Lu Xun Academy of Arts denounced Zhang Keqin as an agent. Just then the Social Affairs Department was itself looking into the man. After Zhang Keqin transferred from Lanzhou to Yan'an, his father and his wife, who had joined the Party at the same time as he, were arrested and turned, and the Gansu Work Committee was wrecked soon after. There were leads, too, that Zhongtong agents were working to turn Zhang Keqin.

Under three days and three nights of unbroken interrogation, Zhang Keqin, who at first denied being an agent, could hold out no longer. He confessed: the Gansu underground Party was one that "waved the red flag to oppose the red flag," a creature of the Nationalists' red-flag policy, in fact a Nationalist agent organization.

4. Were the "Outside Intellectuals" "Riddled with Agents"?

"Waving the red flag to oppose the red flag"?

Kang Sheng at once made Zhang Keqin the model of the "four great agents" and promoted him everywhere.

The vetting for the "Red-Flag Party" soon spread to the Communists' underground organizations across every Nationalist-area province: Gansu, Henan, Shaanxi, Sichuan, Hunan, Hubei, Yunnan, Guizhou, Zhejiang, Guangxi, and more, all had their "Red-Flag Party," and most of the Communists' white-area organizations were held to be tainted!

During the Yan'an cadre vetting, the suspicion of the white-area Party, the suspicion of the outside intellectuals, reached a pitch to freeze the blood.

One night at the end of March 1943, Kang Sheng summoned Zhou Xing and Shi Zhe to the cave dwelling where he lived, drew out a name list, and marked it up with his pen: this one an "agent," this one a "traitor," this one a "renegade," this one a "Japanese agent." Everyone whose name he circled was to be arrested by the Border Security; everyone whose name he dotted was to be sent to the Administrative Academy for collective training.

That same night the Social Affairs Department and the General Political Department's anti-traitor department made arrests too. The Social Affairs Department took chiefly the people of the organs directly under the Center, holding them at Zaoyuan. The anti-traitor department took chiefly the army's, holding them at Xiaobiangou. The Border Security took chiefly the Border Region system's, holding them at Hougou.

On the night of April 1, 1943, through the dark of Yan'an town, a secret mass arrest went forward without a sound; more than two hundred and sixty were taken in a single night, and the next day, the arrests not yet done, they went on.

To be arrested is one of the searing experiences of a person's whole life.

On April 1 Zhao Xiaochen received notice from his school: he was being transferred, and was to report to the Northwest Bureau.

That morning a great rain fell suddenly on Yan'an, with hail in places. Only toward noon did the sky clear, and Zhao Xiaochen set out with twenty-odd schoolmates. There was a stretch of road to walk from Yan'an University to the Northwest Bureau, and the man leading them called a rest. So the party happened to sit down at the gate of the Security Office. No sooner were they seated than the Security Office's guards came swiftly out and penned the whole group in.

That night snow whirled down over Yan'an town.

April 1, 1943: a town-wide mass arrest in Yan'an. That same day, rain and hail in Yan'an in the morning, snow at night.

Snow in spring?

In the Shanxi puju opera *The Injustice to Dou E*, when Dou E is beheaded, snow falls suddenly from the sky in the sixth month of summer, when snow could never fall. It moves Heaven and Earth: a sign of the great injustice of her case.

Yan'an's April snow was an omen of a disordered season.

In the spring of 1943 the Soviet Union stood at a disadvantage on the world's fronts, while at home Japan was working to lure Chiang Kai-shek into surrender. Abroad and at home, inside the Border Region and out, all pointed toward a sharpening of contradictions.

The mass arrest of April 1 could not have lain within Kang Sheng's personal authority.

On April 9 and 12, Ren Bishi, secretary of the Party Center's Secretariat, reported to a mass meeting of the organs directly under the Center on "Agent Activity and the Center's Policy Toward Agents." Ren Bishi held: "Of those the Nationalists have sent into the Border Region and the Eighth Route and New Fourth Armies to do agent work, the great majority are simple, upright young people, lured or coerced. Having lived the real life of the revolutionary environment and studied through Rectification, they are willing to turn over a new leaf."

Ren Bishi set out the Center's decision: "We should give these young people, who were coerced for a time onto the wrong road, a guaranteed way out."

This policy toward agents was plainly more lenient, more enlightened, than the purges of counter-revolutionaries and of Trotskyites before it.

In the past, agents were locked up as a rule and mostly beheaded; now they were allowed to turn over a new leaf!

Every organ in Yan'an at once began a movement to confess.

April had barely passed when, in May 1943, the Comintern declared its own dissolution!

Stalin hoped by this to reassure the West and to spur America to open a second front against Germany.

Chiang Kai-shek seized the chance to strike at the Communists: your higher body has dissolved itself, so the Communists should dissolve too!

Hu Zongnan was ordered to fall on the pouch-shaped salient of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, and on July 7 his troops shelled the garrison of the Guanzhong sub-district.

War between Nationalists and Communists hung by a hair, and the Communists reacted at once and strongly.

On July 9 Yan'an published Zhu De's telegrams to Hu Zongnan and to Chiang Kai-shek and He Yingqin, protesting the Nationalist army's encroachment on the Border Region. That same day Yan'an held a "Rally to Mark the Sixth Anniversary of the People's War of Resistance" and issued its circular telegram "Appealing for Unity and Against Civil War." The whole Border Region, Party, government, army, and people, was thrown into emergency mobilization to force Chiang Kai-shek to halt his assault.

With opinion at home and abroad running against any wrecking of the anti-Japanese united front, on July 11 Chiang Kai-shek and Hu Zongnan wired Zhu De back to say they had no intent of attacking the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region. The next day Hu Zongnan ordered the two army headquarters and one division that had closed on the Border Region to pull back to their old stations.

The Nationalists' third anti-Communist upsurge died down, but the Party Center did not relax its watch.

On July 13 the Party Center's Politburo again weighed its response. Mao Zedong proposed five tasks to be carried out at speed: first, a political offensive; second, the necessary military preparations; third, harder work on a general campaign to hunt out agents and traitors, and on propaganda and education against agents; fourth, more class education among the Party and the people; and fifth, propaganda work exposing the Nationalists' every kind of crime and their reactionary thought and policy.

5. "Mass Campaigns" Plus "Coerce, Confess, Believe"

Since the war began, for all the friction between them, the Nationalists and Communists had, for the sake of the nation, kept up an outward respect and cooperation, and this had blurred the class line for some Communists. Now that the Nationalists had been first to tear off the mask and come out openly against the Communists, the Communists could take the chance to seize back a long-neglected class education and to clear the Nationalists' influence from within. This beating-back of the anti-Communist upsurge had turned above all on accurate intelligence, and so had won the initiative in propaganda. The importance of intelligence and counter-intelligence thus stood out the more, and Yan'an put yet more store by its counter-espionage work.

In July 1943, with a great enemy bearing down, the people of Yan'an had enemies filling their minds, and feared the more that enemies hid among their own. In this dread of the enemy Kang Sheng said: "Agents are like ants: standing up you cannot see them, but crouch down and the ground is crawling with them!"

This was the "agents riddling everything"!

The "Rescue Campaign" was called the "Rescue Campaign" because of a famous speech of Kang Sheng's.

On July 15, 1943, the organs directly under the Center convened a thousand-strong cadre meeting. Kang Sheng, deputy chairman of the Central General Study Committee and chairman of the study committee of the central organs, gave a long speech, and the speech was titled "Rescue the Fallen."

"The fallen"?

To those who came from the Nationalist areas the phrase was not strange. When Nationalist agents secretly murdered a Communist, the pretext they later put in the papers ran often enough: "It is established that the man, having strayed, fell into the water and drowned."

Why did Kang Sheng use the phrase too?

Kang Sheng opened by saying: today's meeting is a meeting of an emergency time, a meeting of a time of military mobilization. The Nationalists run an agent policy, dragging many promising young people down into the foul pit of agentry to serve the Japanese invaders' fifth column. Since April 10 the Communist Center has once more called, with a policy of leniency, on these young people to rise, turn over a new leaf, and get free of the agents' trap. As of today, four hundred and fifty have confessed and repented to the Party.

Kang Sheng struck a figure that stayed with his hearers: "A child slips and falls into a river. In ordinary times you can go and pull him out at your leisure; but when the flood is up, you have to rescue him!"

Rescue? Which of those who have fallen in does not long to be rescued?

Zhang Keqin, planted in advance, went up first to confess and lead the way. One after another, people who had already confessed went up again, denouncing their own crimes in tears. Some were driven up onto the platform; but the very passivity of these only proved the more how necessary the rescue was. And so confession became, of all things, a "conscious" act; people vied to get up on the platform, vied to speak, vied to clap the agent's cap onto their own heads!

Who would not long to be "rescued"...

"Rescued"!

The day after Kang Sheng's speech, every organ in Yan'an began its own roaring "rescue." Party, government, army, people, schools, all were to hold "rescue rallies." From this the "cadre-vetting campaign" turned into the "Rescue Campaign."

Every circle in Yan'an had its Nationalist agents; whoever did not believe it, Kang Sheng would pull one out of his very organ! Kang Sheng's pockets were full of magic weapons like the Heaven-Toppling Seal: a list of 450 agents, of whom 33 came from the "Dai case" alone! At the Zaoyuan confession rally the "Dai case" agent Hu Shiyuan spoke amid streaming tears, laying out the agent work of his own cell inside the Second Bureau, and threw the whole hall into an uproar. Walking into a unit, Kang Sheng could actually call out a name on the spot, and a "Dai case" man would rise in answer; this cowed the leaders of every unit, and no one dared fail to find an agent in his own.

"Yan'an's New Market has become an agents' market!"

"Three barbers at the Center's seat at Yangjialing have turned themselves in; the heads of the Center's leaders have been rolling under the agents' razors!"

"At least half of the outside intellectuals are agents!"

The Rescue Campaign swept forward like a whirlwind, and in a dozen days had taken the whole town of Yan'an by storm.

An invisible great contest of rescue took shape inside Yan'an and out; every organ and unit vied to catch agents, and agents duly arose to be caught, more and more of them, until in a dozen days over 1,400 had been seized!

To confirm an agent still called for a legal procedure.

Interrogation is the judicial inquiry a legal organ makes into a criminal suspect, its aim to obtain a confession and check the evidence against it. The Nationalists' secret services and courts made corporal punishment a chief means of getting confessions: whipping, burning, the tiger bench, chili water forced down the throat, whatever was cruelest.

How did Yan'an interrogate?

A document summing up a month of the rescue's interrogation work noted: "Some work was done to reform prisoners by persuasion, but beating and cursing, corporal punishment, disguised corporal punishment, dictated confessions, and coerced confessions were also gravely present."

One Guanzhong county counted twenty-four kinds of corporal punishment in use: pressing a man with a pole, slapping the face, hoisting him up and dashing him to the ground, and more. More common still was the disguised kind, chiefly relay interrogation, the low stool, trussing a man up, and mock execution.

In fact, as a matter of rule, the Communists had long strictly forbidden corporal punishment.

In January 1941 the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office issued its "Directive on Anti-Traitor Policy," which in laying down "interrogation authority" held: "Interrogation work must rely on the battle of wits and on political persuasion, and must resolutely abolish all corporal punishment (such as forced standing at attention, chilling the body, exposure to the sun, denial of sleep); violators are to be dealt with by law, and their superiors punished too if they fail to pursue it." "A case must not be closed on confession alone; charges must not be fabricated to implicate the innocent; a prisoner's weaknesses must not be exploited (especially with women prisoners) to extort, by naming names and facts, a confession that traps others as traitors and harms oneself." "Interrogators shall respect the spirit of human rights and treat prisoners in the spirit of a civilized legal order, and shall not adopt any attitude of unlawful humiliation." These provisions were quite specific and showed a certain consciousness of legality. On May 1, 1941, the "Governing Program of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region" laid down: resolutely abolish corporal punishment, and weigh evidence over confession. This program was also the Communists' platform in their electoral contest with the Nationalists, and in making its stand for the abolition of corporal punishment public, the Communists plainly held it up as a governing principle of democratic politics.

By the rules, beating and cursing, corporal punishment, disguised corporal punishment, dictated confessions, coerced confessions, all were expressly forbidden. But when the campaign came, this "forbidding" may have weakened. In December 1942 Zhou Xing made a pointed review of "the deviations that arose in carrying out the lenient policy," noting: "The abolition of corporal punishment was misunderstood; tying a man up came to count as corporal punishment, and the upshot was that prisoners we had caught got away."

It seems the leadership's read of a campaign's temper shaped the means of interrogation too. When a campaign was launched, the stress fell on not being so lenient as to slacken repression; when it crested, breaches of discipline in interrogation might be indulged as "the ardor of the masses"; and in a given unit, unlawful and undisciplined methods might even be encouraged, by a leader of lower caliber, as "effective means."

For the unlawful and undisciplined excesses that arose within a campaign, the Communists had a summary all their own: "coerce, confess, believe"!

This was Mao Zedong's own coinage. The Party Center's "Decision on Vetting Cadres" held that its nine-point policy "stands fundamentally opposed to the mistaken purge line committed in many places during the civil-war years. That mistaken line, put simply, comes down to three words: coerce, confess, believe. The interrogator uses corporal punishment, disguised corporal punishment, and other means of coercion on agents and suspects; the interrogated then confesses wildly and at random, framing good people; and the interrogator and those in charge then believe, without a second thought, this utterly unreliable testimony, and go arresting, beating, and killing at random."

The purges of the civil-war years had done the Communists vast internal harm, and anyone who took up random arresting, beating, and killing again would surely lose the people's hearts. But what caused that random arresting, beating, and killing, many cadres never saw clearly. So long as the root sickness of "coerce, confess, believe" was not cut out, the madness of "random arresting, beating, and killing" was bound to return.

But rooting out "coerce, confess, believe" was no easy thing.

In the minds of many cadres, security cadres above all, lay a stubborn conviction: no one will speak out what harms himself, and no one under examination confesses unless coerced. Practice seemed to bear it out: persuasion worked slowly, coercion cracked cases fast.

As for whether a coerced confession was true, that crucial question seemed lost on the people caught up in the campaign.

And if someone with ill intent set out to manufacture false cases, then "coerce, confess, believe" was a magic wand made to order.

Kang Sheng had an invention: "analyzing an agent."

You don't know you're an agent? Very well, let me analyze it for you. The Party organization you joined is a Red-Flag Party, so aren't you a member of the Red-Flag Party? The Red-Flag Party is an agent organization, so aren't you an agent?

A lone person under examination had no way to prove the major premise, the Red-Flag Party, false. So he had no choice but to accept the minor conclusion drawn from it: that he was an agent. And to admit to being an agent was to have to name accomplices, which dragged in a fresh crowd of agents.

Analyzing an agent. And while they analyzed, they would not hear your defense.

When Su Ping was "analyzed" into an agent, they analyzed it thus: you came to Yan'an from the Nationalist areas, so the travel pass for your road was surely one the Nationalists issued. How were these red-area cadres, who had never gone far from home, to know that outside you simply bought a ticket to ride? They analyzed again: the Shaanxi Party is a Red-Flag Party, and you are an underground member, so you must be a Nationalist agent. It never occurred to these cadres from elsewhere that, if the Shaanxi Party were a Red-Flag Party, how could the Center, come to Shaanbei on the Long March, have found any footing? They analyzed again: your cousin is a secretary-general at a Nationalist county Party office, so your coming to Yan'an must mean the Nationalists sent you in as an agent! It never crossed the minds of these cadres of clean worker-peasant stock that drawing in people of every origin was the very charm of a revolutionary party!

Since it was analysis, it needed no evidence.

Whoever came from a Red-Flag Party province was an agent; whoever came from the Nationalist areas was an agent; and even one who came from the Border Region, if a superior or a colleague was an agent, was "developed" into an agent too! To have joined the CC Clique was to be an agent; to have joined the Fuxingshe was to be an agent; to have been in the Nationalists' Three People's Principles Youth League was to be an agent; and even one who had joined nothing, but had a relative or friend in the Nationalists, was "developed" into an agent! Behaving badly was an agent's sabotage; behaving keenly was an agent's disguise; however you behaved, you were an agent! Confessing of your own will was an agent; admitting under pressure was an agent; refusing to confess made you a greater agent still! To blush at the sight of another being struggled was to be an agent; to walk like a Japanese woman was to be an agent; weeping, brooding, an unnatural face, an off manner, sleeplessness, all were signs of wavering. A White Russian from the Northeast was surely a Japanese agent; an envoy of a Beiyang minister from Inner Mongolia was Japan's Mongol scout; an ethnic-Korean interpreter come over from the Japanese army was surely a spy sent in by Japan.

A famous line of Kang Sheng's: "The way I see it, you look just like an agent!"

A man who had joined no agent organization was an agent, a man who had done no agent work was an agent: a line of reasoning past all imagining.

Everyone who lived through it could tell several tales of the "Rescue Campaign," stories so absurd they might be jokes.

In every age and every land, the chronic disease of the police and security trade is just this: "coerce, confess, believe"!

This old ailment turns malignant in the heat of a campaign. The coerced confessions of the Rescue Campaign manufactured more and more agents.

With so many agents turning up inside the Communist Party, how then was one to judge the Party's organization, how to carry on the Party's work?

A great share of the members had become agent suspects, a great share of the cadres feared for themselves, and the few keen ones left were busy hunting traitors. And so the daily work of many Party and government organs ground to a halt.

People tend to think the security organs cut the finest figure in the hunt for traitors and agents; in truth the security organs themselves suffered vast losses in the campaign. The very organs charged with hunting traitors were snarled in guarding against traitors of their own.

The Social Affairs Department's first catch was Yu Bingran. This veteran Party member, sent on the Special Branch's orders to penetrate the Nationalists' Juntong, had somehow become a Japanese agent, and had to confess in the open at the Zaoyuan rally. The Social Affairs Department, small in numbers, locked up a dozen or twenty of its own.

The outside intellectuals of the Security Office system were all gathered up for collective study and examination. The Security Office and its sub-offices had kept field groups in the border zones, running intelligence against the Nationalist areas; now all the field men were called back, and even the moles planted inside Nationalist secret services were pulled home for examination. Of the men left doing intelligence in the border zones there remained only two, Qin Ping in Guanzhong and Zhao Qufei at Luochuan, and most of the work had to be abandoned. Yang Hongchao and others, so hard-won as penetration agents inside the Nationalist secret services, were branded agents in the campaign and put on show as models everywhere, and so their cover was blown. Some of the men who had been drawn out of the Nationalist secret services no longer dared make contact with Yan'an.

In the second half of 1943 the Border Security's intelligence work all but stopped. The once-steady stream of intelligence from the Nationalist areas dwindled to a trickle.

The campaign's reach ran far beyond the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region; the Jin-Sui and Jin-Cha-Ji and other border regions drove their own campaigns hard, and even the intelligence work in the Nationalist and occupied areas was badly disrupted.

Pan Hannian, deputy head of the Social Affairs Department, who had long directed underground intelligence in Shanghai and Hong Kong, was likewise recalled to the New Fourth Army's headquarters at Huanghuatang for Rectification. When Rao Shushi, the army's political commissar, organized criticism of army commander Chen Yi, Pan Hannian, a friend of Chen Yi's, was criticized too as a "small broadcast" and a "liberal," with no leisure to go into enemy country on intelligence work. A student whom Yang Fan, the New Fourth Army's Third Division security chief, had recommended to Yan'an was branded a CC man in the cadre vetting; a telegram came from Yan'an, and here in central China Yang Fan was arrested. Yang Fan's arrest set every intelligence cadre behind enemy lines fearing for himself.

The decline of intelligence work told in turn on security work. In the past the Social Affairs Department and the Border Security could, through moles planted inside the Nationalist secret services, keep track of the agents sent into the Border Region. Now that most of the moles were cut off, this way of countering agents became hard to work.

A mass anti-agent campaign could, of course, partly halt the Nationalist agents' work in Yan'an. Yet the Nationalists could still get Border Region intelligence through special channels. On March 31, 1944, Zhongtong's Unity Press brought out a book, *The Inside Story of the Recent Inner-Party Struggle of the Chinese Communists*, drawing heavily on the Social Affairs Department's internal journal *Anti-Traitor Experience*; and one Guo Hualun then wrote his *A History of the Chinese Communists* on the strength of it, this Guo Hualun being none other than the great turncoat Guo Qian!

A campaign got up to hunt traitors had instead brought intelligence work to a stop; and the decline of intelligence work had in turn hampered security work. A campaign run this way not only disrupted the hunt for traitors, it harmed the whole of the Party's work.

A political campaign that told against the great cause of the revolution could not but draw doubt.

"Agents as thick as hemp." Were these hemp-thick "agents" all real?

If these people were not agents but were treated as agents, then was it not time this campaign was brought to a close?

6. Mao Zedong Apologizes

Many cadres began to doubt this "Rescue Campaign."

In its early days the campaign had no lack of zealots, firm in denouncing agents! The more agents confessed, the more it proved the Party right and themselves keen. Only when they too were denounced as agents did it dawn on them: since their own agent-hood was false, the agent-hood of others might be false as well.

Many senior cadres brought the problem to the Center, and Mao Zedong saw the problem himself.

The "rescue" began on July 15, 1943; on August 15 the Party Center issued its "Nine-Point Policy," criticizing the "Left" phenomena in the campaign. On October 9 Mao Zedong wrote his instruction, "Not one to be killed, and most not to be arrested," to keep the wrongful killings of a purge from recurring. On December 22 the Party Center decided to turn the campaign toward a phase of screening. The "Nine-Point Policy" cooled it, "Not one to be killed, and most not to be arrested" braked it, and "screening" turned it about.

But the "Rescue Campaign" was traveling at a furious speed; could such a turn be made cleanly?

Mao Zedong took the platform at a Yan'an cadre rally, admitted the Rescue Campaign had gone wrong, took the responsibility on himself, and, doffing his cap and bowing, apologized to the persecuted cadres. For the highest leader of a political organization to bow his head and own his error before his own people was a rare thing in China's political history.

Mao Zedong apologized in public again and again, easing the feelings of the persecuted cadres and driving the screening forward.

Cases of a counter-revolutionary character were all handled by the security organs, and the screening of these cases turned, again, on the security departments. To correct one's own errors was a test for the security organs too.

The highest security organ was the Central Social Affairs Department, and the man who ran this key department, Kang Sheng, was the very begetter of the Rescue Campaign!

On December 24, 1943, the third day after the Center decided on screening, Kang Sheng gave a report on the screening work. On March 29, 1944, he addressed the Northwest Bureau's senior-cadre conference. He spoke first of "consolidating the gains of a year's anti-traitor struggle," second of "thoroughly correcting the errors of coerce-confess-believe," third of "the new anti-traitor line," and last of "how to carry out the screening work."

The Party Center decided to work the speech up into a pamphlet, "On the Course of the Anti-Traitor Struggle and the Present Tasks," and send it out everywhere as study material for the screening.

Kang Sheng, so good at reading the wind, could not only trim his sails to it but seize the initiative first!

By Shi Zhe's recollection, this speech of Kang Sheng's was drafted by the Security Office. Kang Sheng made a point of adding his own view, insisting: "That so great a movement should touch and wound a few, what is strange in that? What is odd in that? What is there in it that cannot be understood?"

Zhou Xing sighed: "A report like that, we couldn't make, and wouldn't dare."

If Kang Sheng the man may have paid the screening lip service while working against it, the Central Social Affairs Department, as a Party organ, took an active attitude toward it all the same.

Li Kenong, the department's deputy head, who had run a little hot himself in the campaign's early days, soon saw its errors and reported the problem to Mao Zedong on his own initiative. Once Mao Zedong had put him on notice, he pushed the screening harder still.

Li Kenong questioned Huang Gang himself. This was another case Mao Zedong refused to credit. Huang Gang of the *Liberation Daily* was the orphaned son of Huang Fusheng, a martyr of the Party's founding years, and had been cared for from childhood by Yun Daiying, Chen Tanqiu, Ren Bishi, and others. Yet this red seedling, because in the white areas he had once been placed in a job by the Nationalist propaganda chief Zhang Daofan, and after coming to Yan'an had had contact with the Nationalist delegation, was "rescued" into a Nationalist agent. Huang Gang thought at the time, it is an inner-Party examination in any case; let me duck the storm first. And so he admitted to being an agent, and even gave a detailed account of how Zhang Daofan had sent him.

Li Kenong opened by saying: I've reported your case to Chairman Mao. Flesh and blood of the Communist Party, fallen into the enemy's mire, and back now in the Party's embrace! Seeing a Social Affairs Department leader before him, and hearing that even Chairman Mao knew of his case, Huang Gang recanted on the spot, saying he had lied under the pressure of the rescue.

Li Kenong said kindly: if it's so, it's so, and if it isn't, it isn't! This petty-bourgeois way of thinking is just not honest.

This Huang Gang, once "rescued" by the Social Affairs Department, later became a writer with a gift for the intelligence front, and wrote the screenplay for the film *The Unfailing Radio Waves*.

The Border Security's screening load was heavier than the Social Affairs Department's: it was holding over five hundred people!

Zhou Xing had lived through the Soviet-area purges of the civil-war years. As secretary of the Jiangxi Provincial Security Bureau then, he had watched, helpless, as many revolutionary comrades were tortured into confession, and even his own younger brother was killed; his own life had hung in the balance, until the Center corrected the purge's errors. After coming to Yan'an, Zhou Xing never forgot the lesson, and reviewed his own faults from time to time. When the Rescue Campaign began, as head of the Border Region's largest security organ he had, of course, to carry it out in earnest; but Zhou Xing's head did not run too hot, and he often reminded those below him of the old purges. When the Center decided on screening, he bore down at once on the screening of the Border Region government system.

On December 25, 1943, the third day after the Center decided on screening, the Northwest Bureau's Social Affairs Department issued its "Basic Regulations for Interrogation Work," opening by stressing the "prevention of certain deviations," its first item being "the practices strictly forbidden in interrogation work." What was strictly forbidden included: relying on subjective conjecture alone; relay interrogation; corporal punishment; disguised corporal punishment (forced standing or sitting, denial of sleep, denial of food, forced chilling, exposure to the sun, pulling out the beard, seizing the hair, restricting drinking water, restricting the use of the latrine); mock shooting, mock stabbing, mock trial, mock torture-sounds (feigned electric torture, the display of instruments, the feigned sounds of torture being applied); dictated confession (naming the names, the facts, the charges, and making the prisoner assent); beating and cursing; humiliation (trussing and hanging, slapping, kicking and punching, abuse, defilement, spitting in the face); exploiting a prisoner's physiological weakness (the changes of body and mind in a woman prisoner's menstrual period); exploiting a prisoner's bodily weakness (the changes of body and mind during and after illness); crediting a confession too readily; adding to or subtracting from testimony; and so on. These "strictly forbidden" items were quite specific: on the one hand they prove that coerced confessions were indeed rife in the Rescue Campaign, and on the other they show that the Border Security corrected its errors in earnest and in detail.

The Yan'an Administrative Academy was where cadres were gathered to be examined; 908 cadres of every level, from Border Region down through sub-district, county, township, and district, were held there for collective training. The Border Security made it a focus of its work and screened them in three batches. By April 1944 the academy's screening had reached conclusions on 25 people.

Break through at one point, gather the experience, and the screening of the Border Region system could go forward.

Those caught up in the "Dai case" were screened too.

In the campaign's early days Wu Nanshan, then working in Longdong, was also called up to Yan'an for collective training, and his field work was cut off for a time. Qi Sanyi and Li Chunmao in Yan'an were gathered into the Security Office, on the pretext of a senior-cadre training course, though in truth it was confinement and examination. The Social Affairs Department and the Security Office had learned the whole course of the case at first hand, and Wu Nanshan and the others had made outstanding contributions. The Longdong sub-office soon reached the right conclusion on Wu Nanshan, and he returned to Qingyang to resume as education-section head, still head of the Qingyang field group. Qi Sanyi had not turned himself in of his own accord, but had declared for the Communists only after arrest and education, and his confession had not been complete: he had not volunteered that Dai Li had come to inspect Hanzhong, and so he was held for examination in the Security Office again. Qi Sanyi acquitted himself well there, even winning honors as a labor model in the great production drive, and in November 1944 he went with a Border Security work team to Longdong, using the post of deputy chief of the Zhenyuan county tax bureau as cover for his field work. Li Chunmao had been transferred to the Administrative Academy to study during the Rectification, and in July 1944 was taken to Longdong by the Border Security field-group head Ouyang Tian, using the post of manager of the Xihuachi native-produce company as cover for his field work.

In the Rescue Campaign it was not only the leaders' heads that ran hot, and the masses' heads that ran hot; the specialist organs ran hot to the point of dizziness. People reasoned: the Fuxingshe became Juntong, the CC Clique became Zhongtong, and so anyone who had once joined the Fuxingshe or the CC Clique had joined a Nationalist agent organization. They reasoned further that the Nationalists' agent organizations had a mass character, and that the Three People's Principles Youth League was an agent organization too. And so there were more agents still.

As the screening phase came on, the work of the specialist organs grew finer and deeper too.

Whether the screening went smoothly turned on more than attitude. The competence of the specialist organs was an important factor too.

Suide was a newly won part of the base area, where the Nationalists had left some foundations, and some security cadres made too much of the enemy threat. At the Suide Normal School there was a "black-ink poster," and there was a "stone case," in which a teacher named Yang Dian had been hurt by a thrown stone. In the Rescue Campaign a great many of the school's teachers and students became agents.

Xi Zhongxun, the Suide prefectural Party secretary, looked into it himself, and Qi Xin, the normal school's Party branch secretary, also reported the over-reach of the hunt: how could a handful of teenagers be agents? Xi Zhongxun sent Bu Lu, the newly appointed head of the Suide Security Sub-office, to the school to investigate.

In the earlier stage Bu Lu had already established that not one of the school's teachers, students, or staff was a real agent. Yet the black-ink poster and the stone case still hung over Suide like a dark cloud.

Bu Lu went to survey the scene. The anonymous slogan ran to seven characters: "Streamline the army, streamline the government, and give us democracy." Streamlining the army and the government had nothing to do with the students; the Party members among the staff were ruled out; the odd-job workers would not raise a point of policy. The field narrowed to three or four non-Party teachers, of whom Yang Dian was the only outside intellectual and the only one with a historical question still uncleared, and thus the one most likely to fear being cut in the streamlining. And yet Yang Dian had suffered the enemy's secret assault besides!

Yang Dian worked hard at the school, head down, and had always been valued by his leaders. But because a stretch of his past, when he had served in a puppet force, could not be cleared, he had never been able to join the Party. Before the Rescue Campaign had even reached him, the incident of the stone occurred, which turned Yang Dian, of all things, into the toast of Suide.

No Party membership, so no political enemies; unmarried, so no private grudges; at the scene only the weapon, a stone, and no assailant to be seen. The school's head, Yang Bin, laughed ruefully: to crack this one, I'm afraid we'd have to send for Sherlock Holmes!

Bu Lu judged that a stone so small, struck to the chest, could hardly knock a man down, let alone out. He gripped the stone and struck his own chest with it, and the bruise it raised was very like Yang Dian's. This was a clumsy self-injury!

After a talk with Bu Lu, Yang Dian honestly admitted that he had written the black-ink poster out of fear of being cut in the streamlining, and had injured himself out of fear that the cadre vetting would dig into his past.

With the case cracked, the prefectural secretary Xi Zhongxun was greatly pleased. Had it been enemy sabotage, the man should be arrested and his political background traced. Being private selfishness, it was a matter for education.

The cracking of the false stone case caused another stir in Suide. More than three hundred implicated students and their parents cheered, and the great mass of wronged and false cases in Suide were overturned root and branch.

One case faked by a single teacher had somehow dragged most of a school's teachers and students into agent-hood, set off the over-reach of a whole district, and even become a model for the entire Border Region! Without the social condition of a political campaign, one isolated case could never have borne such poisoned fruit.

The lesson of the screening proved that the cracking of cases should still rest chiefly on the specialist security organs, and that the attitude and efficiency of those organs, in turn, played the decisive part in the screening.

Luo Qingchang of the Social Affairs Department had once worked for a time under cover inside the Three People's Principles Youth League organization in Xi'an, and from questioning many suspects, together with what he had seen for himself, he wrote a report on the League. Luo Qingchang held that the League was a Nationalist youth body, not to be equated with an agent organization; it had its various levels and various cases, and work could be done on it. The report won Mao Zedong's regard.

The Central Social Affairs Department issued a document holding: the Fuxingshe and the CC Clique were originally factions within the Nationalist Party, small inner-Party groupings, and the Three People's Principles Youth League was the Nationalists' youth body. That these organizations were firmly anti-Communist, and that many of their people did anti-Communist agent work, was beyond doubt; but not every Fuxingshe or CC man, and not every League member, was an agent operative. CC men like Chen Lifu and Xu Enzeng, and Fuxingshe men like Dai Li and Zheng Jiemin, each held a proper, lawful secret service of his own, and Kang Ze did agent work within the Youth League too, all of this beyond doubt; but the Fuxingshe, the CC Clique, and the Youth League as wholes were still not the same as specialist, professional agent organizations.

The document also analyzed the Nationalists' secret services systematically. At the central level there were chiefly two systems: the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Central Party Headquarters, and the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Military Affairs Commission. In Xi'an alone there were 14 agent agencies, and in the Longdong town of Xifeng, 9.

The document stressed: in political propaganda we say the Nationalists, the CC Clique, and the Fuxingshe practice agent politics, rule by agents, run an agent-ridden education and an agent-ridden Party apparatus; but in the anti-traitor work we must make concrete distinctions. It required: do not efface the difference between the Nationalists' small groupings and their professional secret services, and do not confuse a question of faction with a question of agentry.

From ants filling the eyes and agents thick as hemp, to concrete analysis and treatment case by case: this was a plain advance. Each unit screened by the bounds of the Center's "six categories of persons," and the first category, the "professional agents," proved to be quite few. Even the most famous figure of all, Wang Shiwei, no longer looked like an agent. Though the Nationalists had printed his essays, Wang Shiwei had never had dealings with a Nationalist agent organization, and the one question left was of a Trotskyite ring. Though he had confessed to being a Trotskyite Central Committee member and deputy propaganda chief, that too was self-slander wrung out under coerce-confess-believe.

While Yan'an was screening, Xi'an got up a stunt. The Nationalists insisted Yan'an had already executed Wang Shiwei, and staged a grand memorial rally for him in Xi'an, which many Trotskyites attended. When word reached Yan'an, people joked that this was a "memorial service for a live man." Mao Zedong proposed: bring Wang Shiwei out and let them see him!

Chinese and foreign correspondents gathered at the Border Region's liaison office, and Wang Shiwei appeared! The camera shutters clicked without a pause. Whatever treatment the man had met in Yan'an, that he was not dead was at least a fact.

Wang Shiwei said earnestly: I am a Trotskyite, I made mistakes, I ought to be shot, but Chairman Mao does not want me to die and has let me work. He also asked the correspondents to tell his relatives and friends in the Nationalist areas: I live well in Yan'an; do not worry over me, and do not be taken in by the Nationalists.

Wang Shiwei, already given a memorial service in Xi'an, had risen from the dead in Yan'an! Yan'an did not need to publicize it; this delicious piece of news spread across China at once.

The Nationalists' propaganda had lost another round. Yet some keen correspondents, such as Zhao Chaogou of the *Xinmin Evening News*, could still see that Wang Shiwei spoke somewhat against his will.

After the press conference Wang Shiwei went back to the Social Affairs Department's detention house. He lay down on his bed, clenched his fists, and said bitterly: "Today I sacrificed myself for the Party's interests. I am not a Trotskyite! I am not an agent!"

Mu Fengyun, the Social Affairs Department cadre who lived alongside him, was turning it over too: this Wang Shiwei, what exactly was his problem?

Most Border Security cadres, too, had known little of the inner workings of the Nationalist secret services. Zhou Xing made a point of inviting Wang Yantang of the Social Affairs Department to lecture at the Border Security on how the Nationalist agent system worked. The Border Security cadres profited greatly, and in passing hung a nickname on Wang Yantang: "the counter-revolutionary section chief."

As the screening went on, people came little by little to feel that this cadre-vetting campaign, too, had over-reached, just as the old purges had. At some point the detained ceased to be called "prisoners" and became "trainees" of a "cadre training team"; first the cell doors went unlocked, and later the guards moved out to the yard beyond.

7. "Liu Qiao'er Brings Suit"

By now the focus of Yan'an was shifting too. The Border Region held a labor-model conference, and the arts world raised a yangge-opera movement in answer to Mao Zedong's talks at the Yan'an Forum on Literature and Art. The work of the Social Affairs Department and the Security Office shifted with it: a few cadres took charge of the training teams, and most threw themselves into the production drive. The Social Affairs Department turned out Victory-brand soap, and the Border Security's training team wove woolen serge.

More surprising was another undertaking: the Social Affairs Department even had a "Zaoyuan Art Troupe"!

The Rescue Campaign that Kang Sheng had pushed so hard had bred a tense air in the Social Affairs Department, and the sizable training team was a heavy burden besides. Again it was deputy head Li Kenong who came out to mend matters, adding an entertainment section to the department. Wang Dongxing, who had run the cadre vetting, became its section chief; one of his deputies was the Red Army cadre Duan Daming, and the other was that famous "Red-Flag Party" man, Zhang Keqin. The Border Security's training team formed a yangge troupe too, led by the interrogation-section cadre Yang Gang, with the "trainee" Yan Yong as deputy leader and, for its players, the "prisoner-trainees" locked up under examination.

Putting on shows is habit-forming too. The Zaoyuan Art Troupe got ahead of the Lu Xun Academy and staged the big Soviet play *The Front*, in three acts and five scenes. The Security Office's yangge troupe was rich in talent and wanted to mount a big piece of its own. Wronged people all, they resolved on a theme drawn from the screening, and just then they came upon an editorial in the *Liberation Daily*: "Comrade Ma Xiwu's Way of Judging."

Commissioner Ma Xiwu was the highest administrative officer of Longdong, but his way of governing was nothing like a Nationalist commissioner's. He would often shoulder a mattock and go down to the countryside, lending the villagers a hand wherever he went; hoeing and chatting, he would get the whole matter clear. Ma Xiwu also served as presiding judge of the Border Region High Court's Longdong branch, yet he rarely held court in Qingyang town. Judge Ma's courtroom was more often set at the scene of the case, and wherever there was a case he went there to sit and talk with the parties.

Feng Yangui, a peasant of Zhangbangyuan at Chenghao in Huachi county, Longdong, had a daughter named Feng Peng'er. When Peng'er was four her father had betrothed her to Zhang Bo'er, second son of Zhang Jincai, a peasant of Zhangwan village at Shangpuzi in Huachi. But by the time his daughter had grown into an eighteen-year-old, Feng Yangui had come to regret it: he had taken no bride-price at the betrothal! So Feng Yangui put his daughter up to seeking an end to the engagement on the pretext of "freedom to choose in marriage." At the same time he secretly betrothed her to the son of Zhang Xianzhi of Nanyuan, and got for himself 2,400 yuan in fabi and 48 silver dollars. When Zhang Jincai's family learned of it, they reported it to the Huachi county government. Child betrothal and the buying and selling of brides were both feudal marriages forbidden by the democratic government, and the county judicial office ruled that both engagements be annulled. Zhang Jincai's family still would not have it, and Feng Peng'er could not bear to give up Zhang Bo'er; but her greedy father would by no means agree, and betrothed her instead to the landlord Zhu Shouchang, getting 8,000 yuan in fabi and 20 silver dollars, and four bolts of serge besides. Feng Peng'er, desperate, told Zhang Bo'er to think of something fast. And so Zhang Jincai gathered a band of twenty-odd men, came to the door to seize the bride, and married them that same night. A bride-seizing? A child betrothal? The county judicial office took it for the Zhang family practicing feudal marriage, sentenced Zhang Jincai to six months' imprisonment, and declared the marriage of Zhang Bo'er and Feng Peng'er void.

A happy match, broken up just like that, and the people talked and talked. Feng Peng'er above all was heartbroken; recalling that Longdong had a Commissioner Ma who judged clearly, she walked the eighty li to Qingyang alone to bring her suit.

On the seventeenth of the fourth lunar month, the Yuele district convened a mass meeting of three townships. On the platform, in coarse cloth, Commissioner Ma helped the Huachi county judicial office hear the case in the open. Before a thousand and more of the people, Ma Xiwu questioned each party in turn and canvassed the views of the crowd, then handed down his verdict on the spot. First: Feng Yangui had broken the Border Region's marriage law and had sold his daughter again and again; the bride-prices he had taken were all confiscated, and he was sentenced to half a year's labor as a warning. Second: gathering a crowd to seize a bride by night, alarming the neighbors and disturbing public order, the ringleader Zhang Jincai was sentenced to half a year's imprisonment, and the others who had gone along were sternly criticized and educated, that the law might be made plain. Third: Feng Peng'er and Zhang Bo'er, having married of their own will out of free love, were, under the Border Region's marriage law, granted a valid marriage. Ma Xiwu then issued the two their marriage certificate on the spot, and praised them for breaking the bonds of feudal ritual, choosing their own marriage, and setting an example worth following. For the Huachi county judicial office's earlier wrong verdict, Commissioner Ma took the responsibility upon himself. He wished the couple a happy marriage besides, and urged the two families to mend their relations.

A tangled civil case, judged by Commissioner Ma in a way at once humane, reasonable, and lawful. The people there exclaimed: "A very Ma Qingtian!"

In truth, before judging a case Commissioner Ma would go about incognito, get the facts clear, and only then rule. The method this case created, of joining adjudication with mediation, Ma Xiwu used to settle many a civil dispute. Through his practice on the bench he worked out, step by step, a set of judging principles. First, carry through the Party's mass line: do not lightly credit a written plaint or a confession, but investigate, tell right from wrong, and fix the crime and the sentence. Second, join adjudication with mediation, using mediation wherever possible in ordinary civil cases. Third, keep the procedure simple and easy: do not always hold court on the bench, but go down as far as possible to the grass roots, for the people's convenience.

The Party Center summed up and spread Ma Xiwu's experience in good time, and the *Liberation Daily* named it "Comrade Ma Xiwu's Way of Judging." With this, judicial work took another great step forward in the Communist-led Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region and the other base areas.

This practice, formed in the Yan'an years, of settling ordinary civil disputes by mediation was a pioneering thing even in world jurisprudence. The Western countries, with their long legal history, had never had mediation; a civil dispute went straight to court, until the courts could not keep up and it had become a judicial affliction. To this day foreign jurists visiting China still rate this Chinese feature highly.

This true story struck a chord in the Border Security's yangge troupe: had they not all suffered the harm of a wrong case?

By collective work, with Yuan Jing holding the pen, they wrote a qinqiang libretto, *Liu Qiao'er Brings Suit*. The heroine was played by Guo Suping, the woman who to the death would not confess to being an agent, and the hero by Wang Xingwen, a real Hanzhong-class agent.

This *Liu Qiao'er Brings Suit* was a hit at its first performance, and it even dared to sell tickets at Yan'an's New Market! The Shaanbei storyteller Han Qixiang, after hearing it, made a Shaanbei qinshu ballad of it and sang it everywhere. After the founding of the state, the Beijing Pingju Theater adapted it again into a pingju. The pingju *Liu Qiao'er* played up its theme of opposing feudal marriage, caught the tide of the new Marriage Law's championing of marriage freedom, and, with the famous actress Xin Fengxia in the title role, swept the whole country for a time. Later a published dictionary of drama, in its note on the pingju *Liu Qiao'er*, forgot the qinqiang original and mistakenly credited the first authorship of the subject to Han Qixiang's storytelling.

In the Border Security detention house, the first to be let out was Li Rui. In early June 1944 Li Rui read in a newspaper that the rear areas were about to send a delegation of Chinese and foreign correspondents to Yan'an, and that among them was a primary-school classmate of his. Li Rui seized the chance to write to the Center, saying he could do some united-front work. Zhou Enlai, who had charge of the united front, had the Border Security release him, and in mid-June Li Rui walked out the Border Security gate.

Mindful of the coerce-confess-believe errors of the earlier interrogation work, the Border Security's interrogation section brought in some new people. Hao Su, who had come from Longdong to Yan'an to be examined, was, after his own screening at the Administrative Academy, moved to the interrogation section as head of a screening group. Cadres who had themselves been under examination, once put in charge of screening, naturally brought a keener attitude and a different eye. Hao Su took the case of Hu Sha, and the written material alone ran to a hundred thousand-odd characters.

With a fuller and more accurate grasp of how the Nationalist secret services really worked, it became possible to draw concrete lines of policy, and so the screening had a ground to stand on. By year's end, 60 percent of the cadres and 30 percent of the masses had been screened.

Screening, some held, was simply rehabilitation. This view met at once with Kang Sheng's fierce rebuttal. Kang Sheng stressed first that screening was to consolidate the gains of Rectification and correct some of its errors, and was not, as some said, a mere matter of rehabilitation or of taking off caps. There had been errors in the Rescue Campaign, he stressed, this was a fact and should be corrected; but it had not been wholly wrong.

The stock phrase of the screening was: "There was cause behind it, but no evidence was found."

Generally speaking, every case had some cause, in greater or lesser degree. Among those examined, some had once joined a Nationalist organization, some had once been arrested and turned themselves in, some had been made use of by others; most had some doubtful point in their political history. This was the so-called "cause behind it."

And after "no evidence was found"? The easy way was to let the person out to work.

Zhao Xiaochen, once released, ran back to the Border Security to demand a conclusion, and so a conclusion of "was made use of" went into his file, to follow him the rest of his life; at every political campaign thereafter, someone would bring out this historical question to use against him.

Su Ping's fate was better. For one thing, he worked a while in the Border Security, and to do security work was itself proof enough of a clean record; for another, he did not chase after a conclusion, and no mistaken conclusion was left in his file.

Wang Zunji even said: thank goodness for the Rescue Campaign! In China from of old there is such a thing as a wrongful arrest but no such thing as a wrongful release; a few of us cadres held in the old cases had plainly cleared our questions, and still those above would not let us go. I had thought I would be locked up till I died in prison; but with the Rescue Campaign stirring things up so, the great crowd of cadres brought in brought hope with them: with so many cadres wronged, they could not but be rehabilitated.

And indeed the screening phase released the great majority of the detained, the people of the old cases among them. Lin Lifu was elected a delegate to the Seventh Party Congress. Jin He was asked back by the Korean Communist Party and, it is said, later became chair of the national women's federation. Jin Shuwang became secretary to the Border Region's non-Party public figure Xu Fanting.

Of the famous "four great agents," Wang Zunji joined a research group in the Border Security, while Zhang Keqin, Qian Weiren, and Li Ning all stayed on to work in the Social Affairs Department.

8. Go to the Front and Draw Your Own Conclusions!

Those who came through the screening all found a way ahead. From the Border Security "cadre training team" alone came, after the founding of the state, more than twenty ministers and vice-ministers of the State Council.

Fate is a tangled thing...

"There was cause behind it, but no evidence was found": a flat-seeming phrase, and how much it carried!

Some said: this form of words does not get snarled in historical questions, and helps free a cadre quickly.

Some said: you finish persecuting a man and then let him off with an airy phrase like that; who bears the responsibility?

Some said: it is a sword of Damocles, forever hanging over the head of one who has been persecuted, and there is no telling when it might become the pretext for persecuting him again.

And some said: how the conclusion is written does not matter; what matters is how a cadre is regarded and used!

Yan'an, in those days, held no debate on the point.

Yan'an, in those days, was in a hurry to free its cadres fast.

As 1945 came, the War of Resistance had been endured through eight bitter years, and the prospect of victory was plainly in view. The Communists were planning their Seventh National Congress, preparing to take over great stretches of territory and, in the end, to seize national power.

It is when hands are wanted that one grudges having so few. The greatest spur to the screening came from the needs of revolutionary work.

In early 1945, with the counter-offensive under way, the war went well everywhere. The Eighth Route and New Fourth Armies had grown to 910,000 men, the nineteen base areas held a population near a hundred million, and the Communists already held one part of the realm in three! The Yan'an Rectification, too, had completed its three great phases: the rectification of thought, the organizational vetting of cadres, and the summing-up of Party history. The Seventh Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee opened on May 21, 1944; this preparatory meeting ran on for eleven months and passed the "Resolution on Certain Questions in the History of the Party." By the spring of 1945 every condition for the Congress was fully met.

The understanding of the Rescue Campaign, too, grew clearer by degrees. In March 1945 Jiang Nanxiang wrote to Liu Shaoqi and the Party Center an "Opinion on the Rescue Campaign," stating plainly: from the narrow standpoint of security work, it had of course its gains; but weighed against the Party's overall gains and losses, the loss outran the gain. In this blind sort of fighting, though one might by chance strike an enemy or two, one wounded oneself far more. This opinion may have been the first writing within the Communist Party to reject the Rescue Campaign outright, but it spoke for the inward view of many cadres of the day.

The watchword of the Seventh Congress was "Unite as one and win victory." The preparatory meeting opened on April 21, and Mao Zedong said: the Congress must look forward, not back, or it will spoil the Congress's success. The meeting approved the list for the Congress presidium and resolved to set up a delegate-credentials review committee under Peng Zhen.

The Seventh Congress delegates who had come to Yan'an from all over had mostly passed a preliminary examination by small committees the Center set up in 1940 and 1941. Later, as ordinary Party cadres, they had gone through the Yan'an cadre vetting in the First Section of the Central Party School, in the Northwest Bureau's organization department, and in the organization department of the Joint Defense Army's political department. These examinations turned up political questions of one degree or another in a portion of the delegates, which bore on their credentials for the Congress and were troublesome to resolve.

The Congress was set for April 23, and the vetting of credentials brooked no delay. As a result, the credentials of a whole class were revoked: agents, traitors, suspected agents, suspected traitors, those who had turned themselves in, the politically wavering, the politically unreliable. Some delegates had made mistakes in the past, or had erred when arrested or captured, but had stood the test of long work, had shown results, and had rendered the Party service; the facts on every side proved these comrades sound in politics and reliable, and so their credentials were kept. The final tally: 544 qualified full delegates and 208 alternates, 752 in all. Those suspended or stripped of their credentials, or recalled by the units that had elected them, numbered 49, of whom 2 had no political problem at all.

Ren Bishi pointed out to Mao Zedong that the localities were far apart, and to replace a delegate would take months, even a year. Mao Zedong decided at a stroke: hold the Congress, seat every delegate, and vet no further.

So it was that a good many of the persecuted got to attend the Seventh Congress. Delegates like Liu Zijiu and Li Yu were not replaced. Lin Lifu, held so long in the Border Security's old cases, was elected a delegate too. Bai Dongcai, earlier denounced as a member of the Third Party, also had his question cleared just then and was elected a delegate.

The Congress delegates were mostly rather senior cadres, and screening them in time drove forward the liberation of the ordinary cadres too. By April 1945, when the Congress opened, 2,475 people had received their conclusions.

At the Seventh Congress Mao Zedong gave the political report, "On Coalition Government," setting out the Communist Party's program and policy for solving China's problems. Zhu De gave the military report, "On the Battlefields of the Liberated Areas," setting out the Communists' strategy and tactics for the War of Resistance. Liu Shaoqi gave the report on revising the Party constitution, and the new constitution laid down that the guide for all the Party's work would be the thought that unites the theory of Marxism-Leninism with the practice of the Chinese revolution: Mao Zedong Thought.

It is worth noting that in his speech to the Congress Mao Zedong took up the "question of Rectification, cadre vetting, and rooting out traitors": in the vetting we wronged many people, which was very bad; it grieved some comrades, and it grieved us too. To the comrades we wronged, we should apologize. Where we wronged them, there we should apologize. Why did we go wrong? It should have been few and precise, for agents were few to begin with, and the method should have been fine, not coarse. But we did it in a way that was many and coarse, and there lay the error. So, as to agents, our old estimate was a blind man groping for fish, with no notion how many there really were; now we know it was only a very few.

Mao Zedong had once said that "so many agents was, after all, no wonder"; now he said agents were "only a very few." The whole estimate had changed.

The Congress had many floor speeches besides, their theme the summing-up of lessons and the meeting of new conditions and tasks. Their content touched every side of the Communists' work, and the anti-traitor work was but one item among them. Kang Sheng's speech was titled "Understanding the Political Report, and the Lessons of More Than Two Years of Anti-Traitor Work," and the Congress also circulated his written speech, "On the Question of Rooting Out Hidden Wreckers of the Nation."

Kang Sheng began by setting Yan'an's two years of anti-traitor work apart from the purges of the civil-war years, so that the anti-traitor work of 1943 and 1944 all fell under the correct line Mao Zedong had put forward, and stood fundamentally opposed to the mistaken coerce-confess-believe line of the civil-war years. From this Kang Sheng summed up: to sum up, the anti-traitor struggle of the past two years had created a new and correct anti-traitor line; in carrying out this line we had won vast gains, made many mistakes as well, and then corrected the mistakes and consolidated the gains. In this way we had won much precious experience, and it grew fuller and more solid by the day.

The Seventh Congress was full of the air of self-criticism. Kang Sheng was not so stupid as to say nothing at all of personal responsibility.

Kang Sheng said: comrades in the localities may have made mistakes in their work too, but the overall responsibility is mine to bear, for I directly led this work. Yet these mistakes were excesses that arose in carrying out a correct line; they were not errors of the line.

Against the voices criticizing the Rescue Campaign, Kang Sheng defended it: even as to the Rescue Campaign, one cannot deny, as mistaken, the ideological-demolition work that exposed Chiang Kai-shek's crime of attacking and wrecking the Border Region; one cannot deny, as mistaken, the mass line that roused the broad masses; one cannot deny the great achievement that, of the 219 agents rooted out across the Border Region, 165 were rooted out during and after the rescue...

The Congress's theme was to achieve Party-wide unity and win national victory. The "anti-traitor" work, Kang Sheng's special subject, did not become the focus of discussion. In his political report Mao Zedong held: toward hidden wreckers of the nation one must take a stern attitude, and in dealing with them a prudent one.

The reckoning with the Rescue Campaign, with the Yan'an cadre vetting, stood at the time essentially there.

Unite and look forward! This Seventh Congress could not have come at a better time.

While it sat, the Soviet Red Army took Berlin, and the German army announced its unconditional surrender.

Two months after it closed, America dropped atomic bombs on Japan, and the Soviet Union declared war on Japan and marched into China's Northeast.

On August 9 the First Plenum of the Seventh Central Committee opened, and Mao Zedong set out the tasks of the war's final stage. The Party Center issued a directive: mobilize and deploy every force at once for a broad offensive against the enemy and puppet troops, enlarge the liberated areas, and prepare, when Japan surrendered, to seize with speed whatever cities and communication arteries could be seized.

Eight hard years of resistance, and the Communists would not let the fruits of victory fall into others' hands. With the whole realm the prize, the Party Center urgently needed to send cadres out to every quarter in numbers.

But no few cadres had not yet been screened, and their many tangled questions were hard to settle at short notice. Mao Zedong grew impatient: the Northeast will soon be liberated, we need cadres in numbers, so let them go to the front and draw their own conclusions. A Communist will stay in the Communist Party; a Nationalist, let him run off to the Nationalists, what is there to fear!

The Border Security sent its sub-offices one directive after another "on summing up the screening work": let these cadres screen themselves, be answerable to the Party, and make their own questions clear; the organization would trust the cadre's account, and if a problem came to light later he would answer for it himself.

Screen yourself? An enlightened way of screening, enlightened to the hilt. Soon 99 percent of the cadres were freed, and the few for whom no conclusion could be reached at once were set to be tested in real work.

Great numbers of cadres for whom no conclusion had yet been reached were able to leave the cadre training teams of every name and set out for the front!

These cadres, resolved to wash the stain off with their lives, fought with more than ordinary courage. Two of them, an uncle and his nephew, went to Sanbian, led a guerrilla band against a brigade of Hu Zongnan's for a day and a night, and were still unrehabilitated when they fell.

With hands so wanted, the organization's policy ran unusually loose. When Wang Ning, chief of the Border Security's Second Section, led his people to the Northeast, the section still had with it that "Japanese agent" Wang Yutian. Wang Yutian had been an interpreter for the Japanese army and had passed intelligence to the Jin-Sui Military Region's anti-traitor department. Later, under Japanese suspicion, he had fled to the base area without waiting for the organization's leave, and had been sent to Yan'an for examination, and branded a Japanese agent in the Rescue Campaign. Now, though no conclusion had been reached, he was already working alongside the cadres of the Border Security's reconnaissance section.

In late 1945 and early 1946 the Yan'an screening sped greatly, and except for a handful like Wang Shiwei, the great majority of cadres were set free. Wartime, extraordinary measures: much was swept clean at a single stroke.

Were all the agent suspicions turned up in the vetting, then, false?

In the Social Affairs Department's cadre column bound for the Northeast, two men defected and fled along the way, were caught and brought back and shot. And there were a few real agents, too, who had come under suspicion in the vetting but could not be proven out, and who won their release by going with the tide.

A turning point in history had come, and the whole Communist Party mobilized to meet, with all its strength, the hard new tasks ahead.

In the rush of it all, there was no attending to so much. No attending to the odd agent who had slipped through, and no attending, either, to the gravely mistaken "Rescue Campaign."

Twenty-odd years on, those who lived through the "Cultural Revolution" would think: the Yan'an "Rescue Campaign" and the "Cultural Revolution" were all too alike!

Both struggled their own cadres as enemies; both distrusted the intellectuals; both took the form of a mass campaign; both practiced coerce-confess-believe; both produced a vast crop of wronged and false cases; both were launched by the same leader; and both had the same "strategist," Kang Sheng.

The past should not be left a blank, and neither should the drawing of lessons from history.

Principal Sources

Li Qiming, former standing secretary of the Yunnan Provincial Party Committee; interviewed October 18, 1995. In Yan'an, Li Qiming took part in cracking the Hanzhong training-class case, and had charge of taking Wu Nanshan and the others onto the Border Security's rolls.

Shi Zhe, *Peaks and Valleys* (Red Flag Press). In January 1943, to strengthen the Border Security's work, the Center moved Shi Zhe from the General Office to be chief of the security bureau of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office. Shi Zhe was thus able to observe something of how Kang Sheng, the Social Affairs Department head, conducted himself in the "Rescue Campaign." Shi Zhe's book was the first to raise the connection between the Hanzhong training-class case and the "Rescue Campaign."

Wu Wenzao, former deputy president of the Beijing High Court; interviewed June 20, 2001. Wu Wenzao was the only surviving member of the old Border Security interrogation section, and had taken shorthand at many interrogations, the Hanzhong class case and the Wang Zunji case among them. That detained Japanese woman had even minded Wu Wenzao's children. The Border Security's screening was also handled by the interrogation section; under section chief Ye Yungao were three screening groups, led by Yang Gang, Yu Sang, and Hao Su. During the "Cultural Revolution" Hao Su received many outside investigators and vouched for the Yan'an screening of many cadres.

Hu Baiqin, Lin Lifu's wife; interviewed June 26, 2001. When the author called to interview, Lin Lifu had died just two months before, but had fortunately left memoir writings. Lin Lifu was held by the Border Security for seven years and was screened before the Seventh Congress in 1945. After the founding of the state he was a research fellow at the Institute of Economics of the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, and was the first to argue that socialist enterprises must practice economic accounting; in 1957 he was branded part of the "Di Chaobai–Lin Lifu anti-Party clique," and in 1984 his name was cleared. From Yan'an to Beijing, Lin Lifu never stopped reporting to the Center his suspicion that Kang Sheng and others were turncoats.

Lin Lifu, "The Party Should Clear the Names of Comrades He Guiren and Fang Jin: A Lifelong Political Frame-up Concocted by the Counter-revolutionary Kang Sheng." This document, written by Lin Lifu on July 8, 1994, recounts the life he and He Guiren led in Border Security custody. In October 1945, when the great majority of those held by the Border Security during the "Rescue Campaign" had been screened, He Guiren, with several White Russians, was transferred to the Border Region court and held. At parting He Guiren gave his manuscripts to his fellow prisoner Lin Lifu for safekeeping. Lin Lifu judged that He Guiren was put to death in the War of Liberation.

Jin Shuwang, former counselor of the State Council; interviewed February 16, 2000. Wang Wenyuan later took back his original name, Jin Shuwang; his wife Wang Zunji was another famous figure wronged by Kang Sheng.

Qin Ping, former deputy Party secretary and security chief of the Ministry of Petroleum's central organs; interviewed October 5, 1994. Qin Ping said that Lin Lifu, who had studied abroad, was suspected of being a Japanese agent and secretly arrested before the Rectification. Qin Ping was on good terms with Li Yuchao in the Border Security, and had once wondered why this old revolutionary was living idle there. After a time under scrutiny, Li Yuchao was trusted again and assigned as political-department head of the Southward Detachment led by Wang Zhen.

Wang Zunji, former office director of the Chinese Academy of Sciences' earth-sciences department; interviewed February 16, 2000. Speaking of being wronged in those days, Wang Zunji did not hide that she too had confessed wildly under coerce-confess-believe. After Xu Jianguo reached the Jin-Cha-Ji base area he corrected one wrong case; only Xiong Dazheng, the most deeply implicated, was not freed in time, and was put to death by his escorts during a transfer. Wang Zunji said plainly that Kang Sheng never took part directly in her own interrogation.

Liu Xiangyi, former office director of the Central Organization Department; interviewed March 7, 2011. Liu Xiangyi worked in Shandong during the war and led the Weishan Lake guerrillas; his elder brother, a primary-school principal, was wrongly killed in the anti-Trotskyist purge!

Tong Xiaopeng, *Forty Years of Wind and Rain* (Central Party Literature Press). This book records how Zhou Enlai handled the wrecking of the Southern Committee.

Li Yimin, "Fragments of Recollection of Taking Part in the Yan'an 'Rescue Campaign,'" in *Materials on Revolutionary History* (3), Literature and History Materials Press. Most memoirs tell of their author's own merits, but Li Yimin frankly wrote of his own failings. As one of those in charge of the Zhang Keqin case, Li Yimin did not shirk responsibility, but dwelt above all on reviewing his own faults; and, with a historian's eye, he summed up the far-reaching influence the "Rescue Campaign" had on the later political campaigns, the "Cultural Revolution" in particular. Li Yimin later headed the Third Office of the Social Affairs Department, and after the founding of the state was political-department head of the General Staff.

Wang Suyuan, "The Whole Story of the 'Rescue Campaign.'" This piece narrates the "Rescue Campaign" in full, a rare thing, and its author seems to have interviewed Zhang Keqin and others who took part.

*A Short Biography of Sun Zuobin* (Gansu People's Press). This book appends a Party Center General Office document of September 9, 1981, in which the Center decided to grant formal rehabilitation to the underground Party organizations of Gansu, Henan, Shaanxi, Sichuan, Hunan, Hubei, Yunnan, Guizhou, Zhejiang, Guangxi, and other regions that had been falsely charged as "Red-Flag Parties." This is exactly what shows how wide the "Red-Flag Party" wrong reached.

Zhao Xiaochen, former cadre of the Ministry of Construction; interviewed January 26, 2000. Zhao Xiaochen was only fourteen when arrested, and told his story with a vivid charge of feeling, the great snow of that day fresh in his memory still.

Jin Chongji, ed., *A Biography of Mao Zedong* (Central Party Literature Press). This book acknowledges that Mao Zedong overestimated the enemy threat, and also relates how Mao Zedong discovered and corrected the Rescue Campaign.

*The Memoirs of Yang Shangkun* (Central Party Literature Press). Yang Shangkun, then working in the General Office, later held too that "Chairman Mao's estimate of the enemy threat had gone too far; else Kang Sheng would not have dared convene the great meeting and give the report 'Rescue the Fallen.'"

Kang Sheng, "Rescue the Fallen," from Zhou Xing's own files. This preserves the transcript of Kang Sheng's speech of July 15. Some who attended the meetings also spoke of parts of what Kang Sheng said at three such gatherings.

Li Fushan, former deputy chief procurator of the Shanxi Provincial Procuratorate; interviewed March 1998. Li Fushan was office director of the Border Security during the Rescue Campaign, and remembered that in his mobilizing speech Kang Sheng spoke of the Hanzhong class case.

Tu Zhankui, former deputy director of the Qinghai Provincial Machinery Department; interviewed 1999. Tu Zhankui heard that Kang Sheng once named Qi Sanyi on the spot at a meeting.

Kang Sheng, "A Review of a Month of Interrogation Work," from Zhou Xing's own files. From this piece one can see the coerce-confess-believe of the Rescue Campaign's early days.

"The Party Center's Decision on Vetting Cadres," in *Selected Documents of the Party Center* (Central Party School Press). This document is the one the Communists customarily called the "Nine-Point Policy."

Li Rui, former deputy head of the Central Organization Department; interviewed March 13, 2001. Li Rui heard that Shuai Mengqi and others had jointly petitioned to report the problems of the Rescue Campaign. Li Rui held that "analyzing an agent" was a disguised form of coerce-confess-believe.

Guo Suping, former deputy head of the Chaoyang District cooperative in Beijing; interviewed October 26, 1998. Kang Sheng's wife Cao Yi'ou led the Rescue Campaign in Yan'an county in person, and Guo Suping, for firmly refusing to confess to being an agent, was judged a hardened agent and put into Border Security custody. The Taiwan-published *The Shadow of Yan'an*, quoting Yan'an's *Anti-Traitor Experience*, says Guo Suping was the only one among Yan'an county's rescue targets who held out and never confessed, and openly called others spineless, and so was reckoned to be answering to Wang Shiwei from afar.

Yin Qi, *A Biography of Pan Hannian* (Chinese People's Public Security University Press). The consequences the campaign brought Pan Hannian did not stop there. In the tension of the campaign, Pan Hannian did not report to the organization that he had been forced, in Nanjing, into a meeting with Wang Jingwei, and this sowed the seed of his later persecution.

Chen Yongfa, *The Shadow of Yan'an*, a monograph of the "Academia Sinica" Institute of Modern History. This Taiwan-published book treats the Yan'an cadre vetting at length, drawing on a great deal of historical material said to come from the Communist Social Affairs Department's organ journal *Anti-Traitor Experience*.

Kai Cheng, *Li Kenong: An Outstanding Leader of the Communists' Hidden Front* (China Friendship Publishing). This book records the attitude of Li Kenong, deputy head of the Social Affairs Department, during the Rescue Campaign, and its account of the Huang Gang case is quite vivid.

The Northwest Bureau Social Affairs Department, "Basic Regulations for Interrogation Work," from Zhou Xing's own files. This document's provisions are relatively specific, reflecting that the Border Security had already grasped the problem of "coerce, confess, believe."

The Border Region Security Office, "Experience of the Preliminary Screening at the Administrative Academy," from Zhou Xing's own files. Hao Su, then also in collective training at the Administrative Academy, was freed rather early and moved to the Border Security to do screening work.

*A Biography of Xi Zhongxun*. This book records the many cases Xi Zhongxun screened and rehabilitated in the Suide district.

"The Central Social Affairs Department to the Central Bureaus and Sub-bureaus, Against the Error of Coerce-Confess-Believe," from Zhou Xing's own files. This document, issued March 23, 1944, gives a realistic analysis of the Nationalist agent system.

Wang Yantang, former deputy head of the Central Investigation Department; interviewed January 28, 2003. Wang Yantang held that the Social Affairs Department knew the organization of the Nationalist secret services like the back of its hand, but did not brief the local security cadres enough.

Mu Fengyun, former head of the General Bureau of Border Defense; interviewed July 10, 2001. The Social Affairs Department appointed Mu Fengyun to live alongside Wang Shiwei and to work on him.

Kang Sheng, "Report on the Progress of the Screening Work," from Zhou Xing's own files. On December 27, 1944, Kang Sheng spoke on the screening work at a Northwest Bureau meeting. *A Chronicle of Peng Zhen* (Central Party Literature Press). This book records in detail the schedule of the Yan'an Rectification and cadre vetting, the review of the Seventh Congress delegates' credentials among them.

Kang Sheng, "On the Question of Rooting Out Hidden Wreckers of the Nation," from Zhou Xing's own files. Kang Sheng's written speech to the Seventh Congress gives a preliminary summing-up of the cadre vetting and the screening, and supplies some figures.

The Party Center's Northwest Bureau, "Regulations on the Field Forces Resolving Party-Membership Questions," from Zhou Xing's own files. In this period the Communists' relevant policy provisions were clear and specific. Wang Ji, former public-security cadre of Shanghai; interviewed October 24, 2000. In the cadre column Chen Long led to the Northeast, a defection occurred. This accords with the logic of life: whether in examination or in screening, no work can ever be done wholly to the last.

Yang Dexiu, former organization head of the Party committee of the Central Party School; interviewed June 16, 2005. NOTES (these are prose too; judge them):

- [because of a famous speech of Kang Sheng's] The “Rescue Campaign” (qiangjiu yundong) was the coercive spy-hunt into which the Yan’an Rectification’s cadre-screening phase escalated in 1943. Kang Sheng, chairing it from the Central Social Affairs Department, cast the purge as the “rescue” of misled youths from espionage; in practice it ran on forced confessions and manufactured evidence. Scholarship on the period—Gao Hua’s How the Red Sun Rose, Dai Qing’s study of Wang Shiwei, Chen Yung-fa’s work, which the author himself cites—finds the great majority of the thousands accused innocent; Mao soon conceded that real agents were “only a very few.” CORROBORATED: the campaign drew in on the order of 30,000 cadres, and the Party later disowned it. The author’s own verdict, that it prefigured the Cultural Revolution, matches the scholarly mainstream.
- [the very begetter of the Rescue Campaign] That Kang Sheng drove the campaign is the single most scrutinized fact of this chapter, and the consensus is firm (on the man, see the note at Chapter 2). It was he who generalized Zhang Keqin’s coerced confession into the “Red-Flag Party” theory, gave the July 1943 “Rescue the Fallen” speech, and let his wife Cao Yi’ou run the campaign in Yan’an county in person. In 1980 the Party posthumously expelled him and named him, with Lin Biao and Jiang Qing, a chief author of the era’s persecutions. CORROBORATED. The author assigns him the blame plainly—unusual candor for so Party-sympathetic a book, and consistent with the record.
- [On February 17 Wang Shiwei published] Wang Shiwei (1906–1947), a writer and translator at the Central Research Institute, whose essay “Wild Lily” (野百合花) attacked the privilege and hierarchy of Yan’an’s elite. He was made the literary target of the Rectification, denounced as a “Trotskyite,” and imprisoned. CONTRADICTED, and gravely: the “living memorial” scene later in this chapter shows him alive and “living well” in 1944, but Wang Shiwei was in fact secretly killed during the Communists’ retreat from Yan’an on 1 July 1947, his body thrown down a well. In February 1991 the Ministry of Public Security overturned the verdict, called the killing a wartime error, and rehabilitated him. The author’s text holds to the 1944 moment; the record does not end there.
- [put nearly three hundred Huxi cadres to death] The Huxi (“west-of-the-lake”) anti-Trotskyist purge of late 1939, in the base area west of Weishan Lake on the Shandong–Jiangsu border, is a documented atrocity in Party historiography: on fabricated charges of a Trotskyite ring, several hundred cadres were tortured and killed before Luo Ronghuan and Guo Hongtao rode in to stop it. The Party Center’s February 1941 “Decision on the Anti-Traitor Errors in the Huxi Border Region,” which the author cites, formally condemned it. CORROBORATED.
- [Root Out the Trotskyite Bandits] Kang Sheng’s article, published in Liberation in early 1938, notoriously branded Chen Duxiu—co-founder and first general secretary of the Chinese Communist Party, by then close to the Trotskyists—a paid agent of Japanese intelligence. The charge was baseless and never substantiated; historians read it as a political smear. It set the “Trotskyite equals Japanese spy” template the later purges would exploit.
- [Kawashima Yoshiko in disguise] Kawashima Yoshiko (1907–1948), born Aisin Gioro Xianyu, a Manchu princess raised in Japan who became a Japanese intelligence agent famed as the “Eastern Jewel.” The Nationalist government executed her for treason in Beiping in 1948. The rumor that Wang Zunji “was really Kawashima Yoshiko in disguise” the author offers as a specimen of the fantastical charges the campaign bred.
- [her uncle, Wang Kemin] Wang Kemin (1873–1945), a Beiping banker who headed the Japanese-sponsored Provisional Government of North China and then chaired its successor, the North China Political Council—among the most senior collaborators of the occupation. Arrested after Japan’s surrender, he died in a Beiping prison in December 1945.
- [the Communists' Southern Committee had been wrecked] The wrecking of the Southern Committee (南委) in 1942 was one of the gravest penetrations the CCP underground suffered in the war. Zhongtong turned captured cadres in Jiangxi and rolled the network up through Guangdong and Guangxi; by the author’s account the whole Jiangxi provincial organization was gutted and over two thousand members arrested. The secretary, Fang Fang, escaped; the deputy secretary Zhang Wenbin died in prison in 1944. CORROBORATED in the standard accounts.
- [waved the red flag to oppose the red flag] This forced confession by Zhang Keqin, a young Gansu cadre broken after three days and nights of interrogation in early 1943, was the spark Kang Sheng seized: he generalized it into the theory of a “Red-Flag Party” (红旗党), a supposed Nationalist front “flying the red flag to oppose the red flag,” and extended the hunt to underground organizations across a dozen provinces. The “Red-Flag Party” was a fabrication; on 9 September 1981 the Party Center formally rehabilitated the organizations so charged—the document the author cites from the biography of Sun Zuobin. CORROBORATED.
- [The Injustice to Dou E] The Injustice to Dou E (答娴冠), Guan Hanqing’s Yuan-dynasty tragedy. Wrongly condemned and beheaded, the chaste widow Dou E swears three portents will prove her innocence, one of them snow falling in the sixth month of summer. “Snow in June” became the stock image of a great injustice crying out to Heaven, which is why the author reads Yan’an’s freak April snow as an omen.
- [the Comintern declared its own dissolution] The Communist International dissolved itself in May 1943, a wartime gesture by Stalin to reassure his Western allies. Chiang Kai-shek seized on it to argue that the Chinese Communists, their “higher body” gone, should dissolve too.
- [doffing his cap and bowing] Mao Zedong’s public apology for the campaign’s excesses is attested in several participants’ memoirs. In October 1943 he laid down the corrective “not one to be killed, and most not to be arrested” (一个不杀，大部不抓), and at the Seventh Congress in 1945 he apologized again for the wrongs of the vetting. CORROBORATED. Historians add that the gesture cost him little: the blame fell to Kang Sheng, and Mao’s own authority emerged from the Rectification enlarged.
- [Pan Hannian, deputy head of the Social Affairs Department] Pan Hannian (1906–1977), the CCP’s master intelligence operative (Chapters 5–6). The campaign’s shadow reached far past 1943: as the author’s own sources note, Pan never reported a 1943 meeting into which the collaborationist Wang Jingwei had drawn him, and in 1955 that silence was used to charge him as a traitor. He and Yang Fan, named here, were imprisoned for life; Pan died in a labor camp in 1977. The Party rehabilitated both in 1982, ruling the charges a fabrication. CORROBORATED.
- [Jiang Nanxiang wrote to Liu Shaoqi] Jiang Nanxiang (1913–1988), a leader of the 1935 December 9th student movement and later the People’s Republic’s minister of higher education and president of Tsinghua University. His March 1945 “Opinion on the Rescue Campaign” is remembered as one of the earliest reasoned repudiations of the campaign from within the Party.
- [with the famous actress Xin Fengxia] The opera grew from a real 1943 Longdong case: the marriage-freedom suit of the peasant girl Feng Zhiqin (childhood name Feng Peng’er), decided by Judge Ma Xiwu. Reworked as the pingju Liu Qiao’er and, with Xin Fengxia (1927–1998) in the title role, made into a hugely popular 1956 film, it became the best-known celebration of the 1950 Marriage Law. Feng Zhiqin was still living when the author wrote.
- [only Xiong Dazheng, the most deeply implicated] Xiong Dazheng—better known as Xiong Dazhen (熊大缝); the source prints a homophone variant—was a Western-trained physicist and a favored student of Ye Qishun who left Tsinghua to build munitions for the Jin-Cha-Ji resistance. Accused as a “Nationalist agent” in a 1939 anti-espionage purge, he was killed by his escorts during a transfer. His case, like the Huxi purge, is a documented instance of “coerce, confess, believe” predating the Rescue Campaign; he was rehabilitated in the 1980s. CORROBORATED.
