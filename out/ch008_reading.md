### Zhou Hao, Bold and Shrewd

In the last years of the Qing, Zhou Hao ran a paper in Harbin, and having in some matter crossed the authorities, found himself on the wanted list. Mr. Dai Jitao, hearing of it, wired Zhou specially with warm words of comfort and encouragement, and further called him down to Shanghai to take up editorial work at the Tianduo Bao. When the Wuchang Uprising came and toppled the Qing house, Zhou set up a paper of his own, the Minquan Bao, on Jiangxi Road, engaging Dai Jitao, Liu Minwei, Niu Pisheng, Jiang Zhuchao, Wu Shuangre, Xu Zhenya, Guan Yihua and others as editors and writers. Scarcely had it begun to appear when, on its repute for plain speaking, it swept into vogue and spread of itself like wildfire.

After the Song case broke in the second year of the Republic, the Minquan Bao was fiercer than any, reviling Yuan till not a whole patch of skin was left on him, and printing plate photographs of Yuan Shikai and Zhao Bingjun in its pages, each captioned "the felon Yuan Shikai" and "the felon Zhao Bingjun." A few days later Zhou ran a special notice besides, which said in substance: "The traitor Yuan Shikai has sent men south to buy up the Minquan with one hundred thousand dollars in cash; but while Hao has a breath left in him, he vows to fight to the last and never swerve from his first purpose" — and readers one and all admired Zhou Hao for his courage and his judgment. Only when the guichou year's "Second Revolution" had failed did the paper wind up and cease.

Zhou Hao was styled Shaoheng; short of stature, he wrote a fine hand in the Su manner, and on Qipan Street there used to hang a signboard for the "Zhonghua Library" that had come from his brush. Later he went up to Beiping and ran a paper there, the Zhongbao. In the seventeenth year of the Republic he served a term as magistrate of Jiangning county at Nanjing, and a year later, his views not prevailing, hung up his cap and left.
