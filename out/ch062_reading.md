### The Little Pawnshops

The little pawnshop is also called the "pledge-head shop." Its proprietors are, more than any other kind, men of the two provinces of Guangdong and Guangxi, for in those two provinces there was of old a thing called the "pay-pawn": at the outset it was got up expressly for soldiers who had not yet drawn their pay, that they might pledge their goods for ready cash against some pressing need — whence the name "pay-pawn." By now its business has long ceased to be confined to soldiers; yet the old usage has carried down, and the sign "×× Pay-Pawn" is unchanged.

The little pawnshops of Shanghai number something over a thousand. Their interest has always been reckoned by a term of ten days, three fen to the term, with the pledge good for up to six months. Some decades ago the Qing marquis Zuo Zongtang came to Shanghai and, appalled at the pawnbrokers' extortion, ordered every one of them shut — the blackest time the little pawnshops ever knew. Later, when the ban was relaxed, they opened again one after another, and so they run to this day. Only, whereas the term used mostly to be ten days, some have now made it a month; where the interest was three fen for ten days, some now take three fen for a month; and where the pledge ran six months, many have stretched it to eight months, ten months, or twelve. There remain, besides, a good many old shops that keep to the old rules still. Their signboards, which used all to read "×× Pledge" — the character for "pledge" written especially large — have long since been changed to "×× Pawn," and one seldom now sees the "pledge" character used for a shop-sign. The little pawnshops now and then fail to look sharp and take in stolen goods by mistake; and when the case comes out and the court sends its detectives to impound them, by the rule the shop forfeits its principal along with its interest — whereas with a pledge-house or a great pawn-house the principal is returned but not the interest: in this, again, the two differ.

By the pawnbroking interest now fixed by the National Government, a month may not exceed two fen. To take three fen a month is already against the law; to take nine fen is lawlessness of the rankest kind. Their capital, it is said, runs from a few thousand dollars at the least to some tens of thousands, or up to a hundred thousand and more, with no fixed ceiling — the sum turning on how large or small a trade the shop drives.
