### A Cup of Tea for Five Silver Dollars

Go to amuse yourself at one of the amusement halls that keep waitresses (tea-girls, that is), and in whichever hall it may be, you have only to plant your feet a moment, or settle your backside on a bench, and one of them will come up with a hot towel. Wave the first off unmoved and another comes; slight the one girl and she is swapped for a second; and once you have deigned to be moved, a cup of hot tea (a "glass-cup") is brewed and set before you. The price of this cup of tea is fixed: two mao in small silver at the very least, and should they run up against some green country bumpkin who hands over a trifle less, they will not stand on ceremony but argue it out with you, and nothing will do but you make the two mao good.

Then there is the class of tippler whose heart is not on the wine, who takes a sudden fancy to one of the waitresses and, on leaving, presses a dollar into her hand, at which she will just breathe a "thank you"; and if he comes three or four times running and gives a dollar each time, by then she has taken his meaning, and a secret negotiation may afterward be opened, whether it ripens or not depending on each man's own arts.

Last year a certain amusement hall had a young gentleman of fashion who took a fancy to a waitress got up in full bloom and dripping with coquetry. His first cup of tea, and he was bewitched; on leaving he gave her five dollars, and got for it only the three words "thank you kindly." After that he came again and again to have his tea brewed, five dollars every time. In the end this young blade and that waitress became, if you please, a pair of lovebirds, and consummated their sweet dream at a certain grand hotel. He is said to have spent several hundred dollars on her; and meanwhile the whole sisterhood (the girls who serve as waitresses in the same hall) envied to a woman this one's no small luck. A tippler who pays a dollar or two in silver for a cup of tea, they said, is common enough and no wonder at all; but to redeem a single cup of tea at the rate of five silver dollars — now that is luck indeed!

Of the amusement halls that keep waitresses, the Wing On, the Sincere, and the Sun Sun are the three with the most; the girls draw no wages and live entirely on what they can pick up on the side. And your seasoned tea-drinker, one and all, calls this troop of waitresses "glass-cups" — using, that is, the name of a thing outright in place of the name of a person.
