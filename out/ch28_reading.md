## Chapter 28. Not a Hair’s Breadth Between Life and Death

“Wait.” Xia Yuechun came running up, gasping for breath. “Wait.” He had been running too fast, and his whole body skidded and went down on the corridor floor.

Cong Feng turned his head and saw the pistol in the nurse’s hand, its black muzzle pointed straight at his chest.

“Open the door, Comrade Special-Committee.” Li Qinhong took a step closer.

“You’ve got it wrong…” Xia Yuechun scrambled up, flustered.

“It’s no use. Dr. Xia, he has already opened a door he should never have opened,” Li Qinhong said. “And another thing, Dr. Xia: you are no good at acting. Charging so recklessly into a place of danger was very unwise of you.”

“Let me explain. He is a schoolmate of mine. From England. He…” Xia Yuechun broke off abruptly, for to tell Li Qinhong that the man was a schoolmate from England was, beyond doubt, to tell her that he had only just come back from abroad. A mistake. He should not have put it that way.

“Dr. Xia, thank you for handing me a fresh and valuable lead,” Li Qinhong said. “In that case, perhaps I should address you as Mr. Envoy?”

“I don’t understand what you are saying,” Cong Feng said.

“Open the door.” Li Qinhong gave the order. “You will understand soon enough.”

Cong Feng had no choice now; mechanically he opened the door…

Li Qinhong made no further move, for the sense of foreboding was still upon her; only when she saw Yang Muci’s face did the taut string in her mind at last slacken.

“In you go, Mr. Envoy. Adjutant Yang can hardly wait.” Li Qinhong shoved Cong Feng inside, then, with the muzzle of her gun, signalled Xia Yuechun in as well, and shut the door tight behind them.

“Who’s there?” She had caught movement behind the wooden screen in the ward. “Come out.”

The wooden screen was pushed aside, and she saw Yu Xiaojiang.

Yu Xiaojiang was dressed in a neat, spotless nurse’s uniform, a faint smile playing over her face; through that smile, of course, seeped a certain inexplicable chill.

Yu Xiaojiang’s appearance astonished Li Qinhong greatly.

“Section Chief Li, it has been a long time,” Yu Xiaojiang said.

“A surprise. A real surprise.” Li Qinhong laughed without restraint. “Your reach has grown rather long. Didn’t Du Luning ever teach you? A mighty dragon cannot crush the snake that owns the ground.”

“I think you have misunderstood. I have come to help you,” Yu Xiaojiang said, unhurried.

Yang Muci sat calmly in the wheelchair. There was nothing he could do now: the gun he had hidden was at this moment in Yu Xiaojiang’s hand.

He was calm only because he had made a “new discovery.” Though he could not be sure what Yu Xiaojiang intended, he chose, for the time being, to keep silent.

“Fearless in the face of peril, unbending in the face of ruin.” Li Qinhong walked once around Muci’s wheelchair. “Adjutant Yang, what am I to make of you? Are you brave, or are you a fool? Won’t you explain it to me? Explain how this Communist envoy comes to be here in your sickroom?”

“One moment, please; allow me to speak.” Xia Yuechun broke in. “This is Mr. Cong Feng, an overseas Chinese from England. He is a doctor of medicine, a schoolmate of mine; I invited him here to consult on Mr. Yang’s case. Yes, that is all it is. You have all misunderstood.”

“Misunderstood? Dr. Xia, do you know where else this friend of yours went on his way to the hospital?” Li Qinhong said. “Plum Blossom Lane. Do you know what Plum Blossom Lane is? It is one of the Communists’ underground contact points.”

“I think you really have misunderstood.” Cong Feng spoke at last. “I did go to Plum Blossom Lane. But the place I went to was not any Party point of yours; it was the home of a friend.”

“Is that so? Then what a coincidence. Are there such coincidences in this world?” Li Qinhong fixed her gaze on Yu Xiaojiang. “Do you believe it, Major Yu? Oh, I hear you have been promoted to Colonel. You are always so hungry for merit, so eager to rise above the crowd.” Li Qinhong shook her head in contempt. “Do you know? My people are only twenty metres from here. This whole room lies within the field of vision I command.”

“Not necessarily.”

“What did you say?”

“I have no intention of stealing your credit,” Yu Xiaojiang said.

“Then why are you pointing a gun at him?”

“The gun I am pointing is not at him. It is at you, Section Chief Li Qinhong.” The gun spoke.

A muffled “phut,” low and heavy; plainly there was a silencer fitted to the muzzle. Li Qinhong’s astonished eyes went wide, her mouth fell open in shocked disbelief, and she saw the dark-red blood come gushing from her chest. She lost consciousness and, with another soft “phut,” toppled at Muci’s feet and stopped breathing.

One shot, and death.

Yu Xiaojiang had killed Li Qinhong with the speed of a thunderclap that leaves no time to cover the ears.

Then Yu Xiaojiang put two more rounds into her, in the invariable manner in which Juntong kills.

All of this happened in a single instant, and it caught Muci utterly off guard. Something went off in his mind with a flash of light; a half-second of freezing, only a half-second, and every guess he had made was confirmed.

“Proceed as planned,” Yu Xiaojiang said to Xia Yuechun.

Xia Yuechun said to Cong Feng, “Go down the right-hand corridor, out the entrance of the inpatient wing; someone will meet you and get you into a car.” He pulled the door swiftly open.

“Wait.” Cong Feng hesitated for half a second. Xia Yuechun understood, and said, “Sir, have you read the English edition of A Short History of Chinese Philosophy?”

“I have. The one published by the Macmillan Company.” Cong Feng looked extraordinarily moved. Moved too, of course, was Yang Muci, for he knew that this was the organisation’s rule: the second set of recognition passwords, to be used if anything should happen to Ronghua. Ronghua had devised these passwords herself.

“Do as I told you,” Xia Yuechun said gravely.

Cong Feng hesitated no longer, and crossed the corridor swiftly.

“He’s yours now.” Yu Xiaojiang put on a mask and followed close behind Cong Feng. Muci understood that she meant to pass for Li Qinhong and vanish “alive” from the watchers’ line of sight.

Cong Feng walked out of the inpatient building as fast as he could. An unremarkable little car pulled up before him; the door opened, and he got in quickly.

They exchanged the pre-arranged code words as a matter of routine.

“Snow Wolf” reached out both hands warmly to Cong Feng. “How do you do. I am Snow Wolf, secretary-general in charge of this Special-Committee conference. We have waited a long time for you.”

“How do you do!” Cong Feng’s spirits ran high. “I am Cong Feng, head of the Comintern’s Far Eastern Intelligence Bureau. My task in coming this time is to restore contact with the Shanghai underground Party organisation and to open the channel of communication with Moscow.”

“Welcome. Your coming is the sign that our ‘Red Silk Road’ is opening again.”

“Has the conference convened?” Cong Feng asked.

“It opens formally this morning, at a restaurant on Fourth Avenue. Under the pretext of a birthday feast, we have rented it for three days; upstairs and down, everyone is our own. Tonight’s agenda has you giving the report on the ‘Comintern Alliance.’”

The little car sped on and was soon lost in the vast sea of people.

By now Xia Yuechun had dragged Li Qinhong’s body behind the wooden screen; the floor was still spattered with blood.

“Let us go back to the ward.” Xia Yuechun set Muci’s wheelchair moving.

“And this? What is to be done here?” Muci asked.

“Your elder brother will help see to it.” Xia Yuechun stepped out of the door and, with a backhand turn, flipped over the “Bed 19” plate that hung at the entrance; on its other side was written: Dissection Room.

Yang Muci understood at last the whole process by which the organisation had carried out its plan.

About ten minutes earlier.

Under Yu Xiaojiang’s coercion he had handed over Lili’s gun; then they had returned to the ring-shaped corridor on the second floor of the inpatient wing. Muci had not gone back to his own ward but had walked the other way, to the more out-of-the-way right-hand curving corridor. With his own eyes he had watched Yu Xiaojiang flip the “Dissection Room” door-plate over to its other side, Bed 19, second floor, and he had grown suspicious.

So he had begun to hold his tongue and, without quite meaning to, to play along with Yu Xiaojiang.

The inpatient building of the Chunhe Hospital was a typical European structure, ring-shaped, like a curled hairpin. Yang Muci’s ward lay in the left-hand curve, where the sunlight was ample and the view wide open, but which was also, for that very reason, easy to keep under watch: both Li Qinhong’s team and Yu Xiaojiang’s team had placed their observation posts in the building facing the left curve, so that nothing Yang Muci did could escape the agents’ eyes. The right-hand curve, by contrast, lay in deep shade, where two enormous camphor trees blotted out the sun. The trees were said to be “relics of the former Ming,” and their spreading, unchecked branches and leaves shut the two rooms of the right curve in on every side.

Xia Yuechun and Yu Xiaojiang had made bold and shrewd use of this ideal “blind spot” to do the deed. There was, to be sure, an element of chance in it, for no one could guarantee that Cong Feng would lead Li Qinhong into the blind spot; but, happily, Cong Feng managed it. Guided by the bed-number positions Xia Yuechun had arranged in advance at the nurses’ station, he brought Li Qinhong, smoothly, to precisely the place she was meant to go.

This was the price Li Qinhong paid for feeling all along that something was amiss, that somewhere a mistake had been made, and yet refusing to let slip the best chance she would ever have to take Cong Feng.

And of course, throughout the whole operation, without Xia Yuechun weaving in and out to scatter Li Qinhong’s attention, without Yang Muci’s melancholy gaze drawing her into the dead corner, without Yu Xiaojiang’s unfathomable smile loosening her guard, the task of receiving and spiriting away the envoy could never have gone off so smoothly.

Thinking so far, Muci felt his spirits leap. The kite whose string had snapped was joined to its line again.

“You still have a slight fever. The inflammation hasn’t cleared.”

Back in the ward, Xia Yuechun resumed his duties as a physician.

“Dr. Xia,” Muci said. “Are you my superior?”

“Between you and me there is no relationship whatsoever.” Xia Yuechun ordered him to lie down.

Muci asked no more questions.

He had, in fact, already known the answer: his superior must be Yu Xiaojiang. She was the new “Timely Rain.”

Outside the door a young nurse called, “Director Xia, Mr. Yang is here.”

Yang Muchu came in, his face grave, his step steady; the men he had brought all remained standing outside the ward.

“Where is he?” A-Chu asked.

“Gone,” Xia Yuechun answered, closing the door behind him.

“Is he safe?”

“Safe.”

“I came too late.”

“Not too late.”

“Do you have trouble on your hands?”

“I do.”

“Where?”

“The dissection room.”

“I’ll send someone,” A-Chu said.

“A-Chu.” Xia Yuechun stopped him.

“What is it?”

“You must go yourself.”

“All right.” Yang Muchu looked deep into Xia Yuechun’s eyes and said, “Set your mind at ease.”

It had all come too fast, like a sudden storm. And it all seemed strangely cold and still, as calm as a windless sea. In the observation room the junior agents gazed dumbly out the window. They had plainly seen their section chief follow a man upstairs; less than ten minutes later they had seen the section chief come downstairs with that same man. Before they could make out what exactly was going on, the thing they had never anticipated happened: the hospital’s fire alarm began to ring…

Doctors and nurses set about evacuating the patients; stretcher after stretcher was carried out; the junior agents had no attention to spare for surveillance and all went rushing downstairs, darting about like headless flies.

In the back alley of the Chunhe Hospital, A-Chu’s car sped away; in its boot lay the body of Li Qinhong.

Half an hour later the Chunhe Hospital was back to normal order: the fire alarm, it turned out, had malfunctioned, a false alarm and nothing more.

Three days later, Yu Xiaojiang, having traced a thread of evidence, led Nationalist gendarmes and military police in a raid on a restaurant on Fourth Avenue. The place was empty, the people gone; in the passage were the plain black marks of documents burned in haste, and the water in the restaurant’s vacuum flasks was still scalding hot…

Yu Xiaojiang made a great show of dejection: more than a week of crushing overwork had yielded nothing at all, so much so that Du Luning had to offer a few soothing words to steady the ranks.

Another week passed, and Du Luning received the report submitted by Xiong Zida of the Detective Division of the Central Shanghai Commandant’s Office concerning the strange disappearance of Section Chief Li Qinhong. It threw Du Luning into a state of anxious unease; his feelings were exceedingly delicate.

Li Qinhong had once loved Du Luning to the point of madness, and her feverish adoration had filled Du Luning with “boundless disgust.” This mad woman was like a pair of shoes he had once tried on: no matter where he threw them away, the shoes still held the scent of his feet, even the warmth of them.

And yet, once Li Qinhong had truly vanished, once those shoes were gone forever, an inexplicable sorrow rose in Du Luning; he even began to recall the style and colour of the shoes, and the idle carelessness with which, in those days, he had tried them on.

“Chief.”

Just as Du Luning sat lost in his musings, Yu Xiaojiang knocked and opened the door of his office.

“Chief, we have detected unidentified radio signals in this area.” Yu Xiaojiang pointed at the map of Shanghai spread out on Du Luning’s desk.

“Have they ever been picked up before?” What concerned Du Luning was whether this transmitter had existed in the past.

“Yes, but that was a year ago.”

“Which is to say, this secret transmitter has fallen silent for a whole year.”

“Yes, Chief.”

“What on earth is Xiong Zida good for!” In his anger Du Luning flung the red pencil in his hand hard at the map; a spot of scarlet stained the drab-coloured paper. It was glaring, and it stood out.

“Whose do you think this secret station is? Yan’an? The Far East? The Japanese?”

“The Japanese.” Yu Xiaojiang answered without the slightest hesitation.

“The Japanese? Why are you so certain?”

“It is intuition.”

“A woman’s intuition is often reliable.”

“Chief, ever since the eighteenth of September, when the Japanese Kwantung Army blew up the rails on the Liutiaohu stretch of the South Manchuria Railway, then shelled the Northeast Army’s barracks and seized Shenyang. This year they have set up the puppet Manchukuo, in their vain design to swallow the Northeast whole, and the pattern of a partial war of resistance has already been drawn open like a curtain. Shanghai is China’s most flourishing city, and a place where the intelligence of the whole world converges; that the Japanese should have planted espionage agents in Shanghai must have been long premeditated.”

“Liaoning, Jilin, Heilongjiang, and Beiping too?” Du Luning drew a deep breath. “The great mansion is about to fall.”

“Chief? You are very pessimistic.”

“I am very realistic,” Du Luning said. “Look into it, keep looking, and see whether we can catch this venomous snake.”

“Yes, Chief. But the Communists?”

“They are not to be relaxed on either. Their conference may be over, but their people still have to leave Shanghai in batches. Watch every harbour and railway station, watch them closely; not a shred of a lead is to be let slip.”

“Yes, Chief.”

Just then the sky turned a hazy grey, and a fine, unbroken drizzle began to fall.

On the Huangpu River, an ocean liner was about to weigh anchor.

Cong Feng, in a seaman’s work clothes, carried his hand luggage along with the ship’s crew and went toward the liner by the internal staff passage.

Then he mounted the deck and entered the cabin.

“Snow Wolf,” holding up a black umbrella, watched the departing figure, and listened to the sounds of the liner setting sail; the shrill “hwah! hwah!” of the whistle was, to his ear, most pleasant to hear.

Silently he turned and got into Zhong Yundi’s car; the car and the liner drew slowly away from the wharf at almost the same moment.

The rain gradually eased. Along the corridor, where its traces wound and lingered, there was a breath of coolness; the inpatient wing of the Chunhe Hospital was steeped in the rich fragrance of the camphor trees, an evergreen that, once washed by rain, looks especially fresh and alive. Muci’s ward was very quiet. Muci was asleep; the weather was fine, and so his mood was fine, and he slept sweetly and deep.

Xia Yuechun and A-Chu both leaned against the windowsill, watching the sky, watching their watches. Between them was an unspoken understanding, for Cong Feng’s berth as a seaman had been arranged by A-Chu, who had had Han Zhengqi see to it through a friend in the Customs.

“There. Now you can rest easy,” A-Chu said.

“That’s an odd way to put it. Isn’t he a friend of yours?” Xia Yuechun protested.

A strange smile came over A-Chu’s face; Xia Yuechun simply pretended not to see it.

“Have you heard an old Persian legend?” A-Chu asked.

Xia Yuechun shook his head.

“A king wagered on a game of chess with one of his ministers. Since it was a wager, there had of course to be a winner and a loser, and the loser would grant the other a single request. As it fell out, the king was defeated in the match. The minister’s request was this: that grains of rice be laid on the sixty-four squares of the board by powers of two, one grain on the first square, two on the second, and so on. By the time all sixty-four squares were filled, the tally had reached a trillion grains of rice.”

Xia Yuechun understood now what he was driving at: A-Chu was accusing him of having secured his help by “deceit,” of having used his “brotherly feeling” to attain his own ends.

“Wait.” Xia Yuechun said. “I am not the minister you speak of; the one you mean is lying in that bed.” Xia Yuechun pursed his lips toward Muci on the sickbed.

“No. You are the minister who truly won, the man standing behind the curtain. You used my brother’s life as your stake and gambled for mine. I am the king, who, all unknowing, walked into the snare you had laid and went forward in the direction you appointed. To put it plainly: I work for you. A fair wager, and I take my loss.”

“You have a fine tongue,” Xia Yuechun said.

“And strong judgment,” said A-Chu, well pleased with himself.

“I am a man who values his friends and does his duty.”

“And a man who beats the drums of war as well,” A-Chu said.

“In this clamorous, war-torn world, ought we not to seek our own way out?”

“Then you admit it?”

“Admit what? I have never killed a man, never once.” Xia Yuechun was plainly correcting some flight of A-Chu’s fancy. “I believe in Jesus.”

“I believe in myself.”

“You and he are of one family.” Xia Yuechun kept pressing the blood tie between A-Ci and A-Chu.

“As I see it, quite the opposite.”

“You are far too suspicious.”

“Do you dare say that you and he…” A-Chu’s finger pointed at Muci, “…have no entanglement of any kind?”

“It’s you I have a bond with,” Xia Yuechun said, smiling.

“You won’t slip past me with such light brushstrokes. Look at the way he’s treated you lately; he respects you.”

“I am his attending physician.”

“And he has begun to hold you in awe.”

“That is on your account: he has come to feel for me the respect one feels for an elder brother.”

“Rubbish.” A trace of scorn touched the corner of A-Chu’s mouth. He held up his fingers, making a “three.” “My brother’s respect for you may come from just this… these three shots.”

“You counted them?”

“You were on the scene.”

“Let each of us… sweep the snow from his own doorstep.”

“Are you telling me… not to mind the frost on another man’s roof-tiles?” A-Chu drew three cartridge cases from his pocket.

“This was merely a small flaw that arose in the course of the work,” Xia Yuechun said.

“In my work I strive for perfection.” He pressed the cartridge cases firmly into Xia Yuechun’s hand. “Let the thing return to its owner.”

“Have you buried her?”

“I don’t care for manual labour.” A-Chu tilted his head. “Ten days, a fortnight perhaps, and she may float up to the surface.”
