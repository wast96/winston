## Appendix II. Roll of Heng Society Members (1934)

The original prints, under this title, a roster of the Heng Society's membership as it stood in 1934, two years after the society was founded. It is set out as a three-column table — surname and given name, native place, and occupation — and runs to three hundred and twenty-four names, a total the roll gives at its foot, arranged by the number of strokes in the surname, from Ding and Yu at the head to Gong at the end.

Taken as a whole, the roll is a portrait of who Du Yuesheng's following actually were by the mid-1930s, and it bears out the account given in "Du Yuesheng and the Heng Society" earlier in this volume: these were not the dock toughs and rickshaw men of the gang's beginnings but a network reaching into the respectable professions and into the government itself. By native place the membership is overwhelmingly of the Lower Yangtze — roughly a third from Jiangsu and nearly a third from Zhejiang, with about a sixth giving their native place simply as Shanghai, a scattering from Anhui, and only a handful from farther afield (Hubei, Henan, Guangdong, Shandong, and elsewhere). By occupation it ranges across business and the native banks and modern banks, the law (a dozen members give their calling as lawyer), medicine, the postal service, the foreign trading houses, the newspapers, and the world of the Peking opera — the roll even includes the great actor Ma Lianliang — as well as, tellingly, a considerable number of serving officials: section chiefs and clerks of the Shanghai Municipal Social Affairs Bureau and of the Public Security Bureau, police officers, and intelligence men of the provincial peace-preservation offices. The society was, in short, a mutual-aid league of the propertied and the placed.

A word on how the roll is given here. A list of three hundred and twenty-four personal names, recovered from a photographic scan by optical character recognition, cannot be transcribed and romanized name by name to the standard of accuracy this translation holds itself to elsewhere: a single misread stroke turns one real person into another, and a romanization built on a misreading is a quiet fabrication of exactly the kind the rest of this book takes pains to avoid. Rather than manufacture hundreds of uncertain readings, the full roster is not reproduced entry by entry. What follows instead is the opening run of the roll — its first fourteen entries, each checked against the page image — to show the reader the form and texture of the document; the composition of the whole is described above. Each entry gives the member's name, native place in parentheses, and occupation as the roll records it.
