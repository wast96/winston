## 14

On the first day of the new year, with five-starred red flags flying everywhere across the sky, the greatest change at the Jing'an post office was in the postmen's uniforms: the whole of them had gone from yellow to green. Newly kitted out, the postmen crowded at the window of the sorting room, looking at one another, and someone said the clothes were all right enough — it was just that going up and down the streets in a green hat was hardly decent. Everyone roared with laughter; Zhongliang's mouth twitched into a grin, and when he turned his head he saw Su Lina. She stood by the great iron gate of the post office in a padded jacket of faded shilin cloth, for all the world a charwoman, yet with a pallor more like a patient just out of hospital.

That night, when Zhongliang worked with great effort to undo Su Lina's padded jacket, he was struck dumb by the scars that covered her body. The knotted wounds were like so many twisted mouths, savage and hideous. For a long while he could not get out a word. But Su Lina, without a flicker, stripped off her clothes, lay down, drew the quilt lightly over herself, and looked quietly at Zhongliang — until he lay down beside her and gathered her in, quilt and all, against his chest, and in that instant her tears came flooding out.

That day, just as the rice shop was opening, Su Lina had run into Chen Taining, who had come at the head of a detachment to arrest the shop's owner.

Chen Taining, in an American-style uniform, got down from the car and had the plainclothesmen release the rice-shop owner. He pointed at the customers penned in the middle of the street by the soldiers and police and demanded, “Which of them is your contact?” Point him out, Chen Taining said, and you'll be let live.

“I'm a tradesman — who would I have to make contact with?” The rice-shop owner blinked, terrified and guileless.

When the rice-shop owner had been bundled into the car, Chen Taining began to study every face in the crowd, and he saw Su Lina. He started, went over, let out a sigh, and said, “So it's you.”

“I came to buy rice.” Even seated in Chen Taining's interrogation room, Su Lina had the same one line.

Chen Taining shook his head and said, “You should not have come back to Shanghai.”

“And you should never have sent me away.” Su Lina thought a moment, then added, “Nor should you be arresting me now.”

“Sending you away, that was my commanding officer's dying order.” Chen Taining looked hard into her eyes. “Arresting you now is my duty.”

“You've got the wrong person. I'm just an ordinary citizen. I was there queuing to buy rice.”

Chen Taining shook his head again. He wanted Su Lina to give up her mission in coming to Shanghai, and her contacts up the line and down, their methods, their times, their meeting places. “None of us,” Chen Taining said, “has any time left.”

That night Su Lina was shackled to a post in the torture room and passed the hours amid the screams of men and women. Early the next morning she was hauled up for questioning again, and by afternoon the torture began. For several days running, after Su Lina had tasted nearly every instrument in that room, she was flung into a cell like a filthy, ragged sack, and no one asked her another word.

Late one night Su Lina was dragged out of her cell amid gunfire. In the yard the firing squad was executing prisoners; a volley cracked out, and she was thrown down before a pair of leather boots.

Chen Taining crouched, brushed aside the hair matted to her face, and said, “I've come to see you off.”

Su Lina weakly closed her eyes. Another volley rang out, the scalding cartridge-cases spattering her face, and she lay as senseless as a dead woman.

Chen Taining sighed, stood up, hesitated, and drew from the pocket of his uniform a Blue Sky, White Sun badge. He looked at it a moment, thoughtful, then tossed it down in front of Su Lina. Then he turned his head to the execution officer and said, “Take her back to her cell.”

The execution officer said, “Sir, my orders are to execute her on the spot.”

“My word is the order.” With that Chen Taining left the yard without a backward glance, sprang into the jeep waiting outside the gate, and told the driver, “Go. To the Wusongkou wharf.”

Two days later, when People's Liberation Army soldiers stormed the prison and battered the cell doors open with their rifle-butts, Su Lina was at her last gasp. After she had lain half a month in hospital, she said at last to a woman soldier who had come to take down her statement, “I want to see your commanding officer.”

The woman soldier said, “There are no commanding officers in the People's Liberation Army. Only chiefs.”

“Then let me see your chief,” Su Lina said.

But the chief did not come at once. Su Lina waited a full two days in her sickbed before she saw the woman soldier bring in a middle-aged man in a yellow cloth uniform. “This is our Section Chief Chen,” the woman soldier said. “You may speak.”

Su Lina sat up straight in the bed and said that her name was Su Lina, that she was an intelligence agent of the organization's Shanghai office, and that her code name was Cuckoo. Her leader was Mr. Pan, who sometimes also went by Boss Di, or Yang Bingqian, or Hu Fei, or Shi Zhongqiu.

Section Chief Chen nodded and said, “Suppose you start instead with the traitor Qin Zhaokuan.”

Su Lina's eyes went rigid all at once. She looked at the man and woman seated before her, and it was a long time before she said, “He is not a traitor. He is not.” For a week and more the hospital ward became all but an interrogation room. Lying in bed, Su Lina began to remember, telling it in fits and starts from the first demonstration she ever marched in down to lying in the boat's false bottom as she left Shanghai. Never once did Su Lina mention Xu Zhongliang, as though this man had never existed in her life at all. In the end she said, “Find Mr. Pan, and everything will be clear.” But Mr. Pan had been dead since 1942. “The martyr Yang Fugang,” Section Chief Chen said, “was betrayed by a turncoat and killed by the enemy outside the walls of Suqian, on the way back to the Soviet area.”

Only now did Su Lina learn that Mr. Pan's true name had been Yang Fugang. She said not another word; she drew her eyes back from Section Chief Chen's face bit by bit, pulled up the quilt, lay slowly down, and curled her body tight as a little dried shrimp.

A few days later Su Lina was moved out of the hospital and shut in a room, where every day People's Liberation Army cadres with ever-changing faces came to question her; but the questions were always the same few. Who are you? Who do you work for? What is your mission? Who is your contact? By what method, and where, do you meet?

Each time, as though talking in her sleep, Su Lina said over and over that she was an agent of the Shanghai office, that her code name was Cuckoo, that her leader was Mr. Pan — that is, the revolutionary martyr Yang Fugang. Until, three months later, Section Chief Chen had the guard open the door and told her, “You may go.”

Su Lina sat without moving. Suddenly she looked him full in the face, her eyes a challenge, and said, “You no longer suspect me?”

Meeting her gaze, Section Chief Chen said, “No one can vouch for you either.”

“Then what am I now?” Su Lina held his eyes still.

“At the least you were a dance-hostess at the Paramount Ballroom.” Section Chief Chen thought for a moment. “And you were the kept woman of a Wang puppet traitor, and of a Zhongtong agent.”
