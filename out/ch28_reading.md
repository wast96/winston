## Chapter 28

Mei Yingzi's mystery was now forever a question in my heart. She fooled people, she used people, and yet she still made everyone feel her beauty and her charm. She not only manipulated other people's lives, but controlled their feelings besides; she understood the character and cultivation of every person, and disposed of them as a painter disposes of his colors — so harmonious, so natural.

And so I turned to examine myself, recalling how I had come to know Stephen, how I had come to know Bai Ping, how I had come to know Helen and Mei Yingzi at Mrs. Stephen's home. It dawned on me all at once that Stephen and Mrs. Stephen had fostered the feeling between Bai Ping and me, and that the manufacturing of an atmosphere in which Bai Ping loved me had all been work laid out in their plans. I thought again of that talk Mrs. Stephen had had with me — had she not all along suspected me of being an agent of the Chinese? And in bidding me draw close to Bai Ping, was that not to say she was handing Bai Ping over to me? I thought again of Hangzhou, of Mei Yingzi's strange provocations and incitements, thought of how Mei Yingzi had spoiled things while Helen and I were seeing each other — these were all facts within my own experience; but as for how she manipulated Mrs. Manfield and her daughter, that was a thing beyond my imagining. Besides all this, the workings of the Benner Inn, Mrs. Stephen's residence, and her socializing with big merchants and soldiers of every kind — there was no knowing what sorcery she wielded there.

Turning these thoughts over, I got back to my lodgings at nine, after dinner, and on the table there was a note from Mei Yingzi:

"A good friend of yours lies in Ward 12, the Gaolang Hospital, on Gaoye Road; I hope you will go and see him tomorrow morning."

Who could this be, that Mei Yingzi should have to bring the word? My feelings grew tense at once; the first I thought of was Bai Ping — could Bai Ping have been stabbed again? If not, then Helen — she had gone out at five o'clock in Nomura's car; had something happened in these four hours? And Mei Yingzi, of course, had come here earlier still; so in under four hours, for something to happen, for a person to be taken to hospital, for Mei Yingzi to hear of it and come to my place to tell me — was that possible? I rang the bell and asked the servant:

"Has the young lady who came the other day been here?"

"Yes."

"When did she come?"

"About six o'clock."

It was six o'clock; then it was certainly not Helen that had come to harm, but Bai Ping, beyond doubt. It was not that in my heart I made light of Helen and much of Bai Ping; but Bai Ping had come to grief now for the second time, and this time, I feared, it was of Mei Yingzi's contriving. My heart pounding, I hastily rose, tucked an evening paper under my arm — the evening paper carried no such dreadful news as last time, but this could not comfort me, for very likely the newspaper office did not yet know of it. I got into a rickshaw and set off for Bai Ping's; how dreadfully long the road was! All the way I grew surer and surer of my supposition; then Bai Ping would of course not be at home, but it seemed that if I could see Ah Mei I could learn the details. I must get there quickly!

At last I reached Route Prosper Paris, and Ah Mei came to the door. I asked:

"Is there any news of Bai Ping?"

Seeing how flustered I was, she was startled a moment, and asked:

"What's the matter?"

"Bai Ping — Bai Ping —"

"She's asleep inside!"

"Asleep inside?" I thought she had been brought back from the hospital, and asked: "Brought back home?"

"She's a little unwell, so she didn't go out."

"..." I said nothing more, but pushed past her in my agitation and burst inside. The door of Bai Ping's bedroom stood open, the lamp lit.

"Who's there?" Before Bai Ping's question was out, I had already rushed over the threshold.

"You?" Bai Ping raised herself to look, then lay down again. This silver bed, this silver room, brought back to me the day at her apartment on Avenue Joffre, when I had put out her bed-lamp for her and come away, and a kind of silver air seeped into my breast, and made me feel a latent desolation and a faint melancholy. Now, though she had moved, the furniture was still the same: the same girl slept in the same silver quilt, and yet the changes in our affairs had been too many — she was a friend I ought to cherish, and yet was my enemy. I fell silent.

"How is it you've come at this hour?"

"I heard someone was at the Gaolang Hospital." I sat on the sofa beside her and said, laughing in jest: "I thought the business of your being stabbed had happened again."

"How should that have come round onto my head?" She laughed. "That telephone call was mine, in fact."

"The telephone call?" I was surprised.

"I telephoned your home; you were out, and I told them to pass on to you that a friend was at the Gaolang Hospital."

"Then who is it, after all?"

"It's Stephen!"

"It's Stephen?" I was overjoyed. "How did you know?"

"I've been to see him."

"How did he get out?"

"He's very seriously ill; Mrs. Stephen hired a Japanese lawyer, exhausted every method, spent a great deal of money, and got him out on bail."

"Is he very seriously ill?" I asked. "What illness?"

"It hasn't been diagnosed yet."

"Is it dangerous?"

"He was rather better when I came away," she said. "But the doctor says the danger is not yet past."

"!?" Bai Ping had gone to see Stephen? Mei Yingzi had left the note at my place? — I had ten thousand doubts, and wanted to question Mei Yingzi; but my astonishment and my feelings far outran my doubts, and I fell silent.

"How do you feel about it?"

"I don't feel anything in particular," I said. "I feel that in the unseen there seems to be a terrible fate directing everything; I pray that Stephen may recover his health soon."

"Of course," said Bai Ping. "All we can do now is pray!"

Though Bai Ping too was somewhat mournful, she was on the whole very cool, and this made me feel Bai Ping was not warm enough. But there was no help for it: was Bai Ping born lacking this quality, or had she acquired the lack later?

After a long pause, I asked:

"Are you unwell?"

"I've slept too little!" she said coolly. "The impression of Stephen has affected my spirits a great deal too."

"Then sleep early; I'll go."

Bai Ping did not press me to stay.

A smile like a lily just opening saw me off; at the door I looked back a moment and said:

"Shall I put out the lamp?"

"No," she said. "Thank you."

I came out of that silver room into the gray street; the sky was very dark, and a pattering sleet came down. I felt the cold, but I felt at ease. My head seemed much clearer, and I began to think: how, after all, had Bai Ping known that Stephen was out? Or had she, once Stephen was out, lent her strength to the rescue too? Or had Mei Yingzi at first not known, and, coming to my place and learning from the servant of Bai Ping's telephone message, left the note in her stead; or had Mei Yingzi known first, and then come herself to tell me, so that her word and Bai Ping's telephone call were two notices arriving one after the other, without prior arrangement? Then in this one matter was she working hand in hand with Mei Yingzi? Stephen, in any case, was not merely an army surgeon, nor merely an officer who was also a doctor; he was a spy. Then if Bai Ping was an agent of the Japanese, this was precisely a matter between enemies — how should Bai Ping go to rescue him? Not only would she not rescue him, but by reason she ought to wreck any rescue by others; then did Bai Ping not suspect that Stephen had some other mission? I believed that when Stephen and I frequented the dance halls and chose to draw close to the dance-girls near the Japanese, his aim had been wholly to make use of them; but with Bai Ping, when he suspected she was an enemy agent, he gave up the ordinary buying-over and took another method: on the one hand seeing that Bai Ping was an enemy agent, on the other feeling that I was an agent of the Chinese, he did his utmost to bring us close. Perhaps his reading of the two of our backgrounds was only a guess, and so he wished, in our drawing close, to observe how matters truly stood on both our sides —

I walked along the gray street, the sleet striking my face with a faintly painful pleasure. The road was a little pale, and the street-lamps shining on it made it seem the more cold and bright. The shop-doors on both sides were shut, not a soul was about, and my footsteps were unmingled with any other sound — clear, simple, heavy, and grave.

Then, Bai Ping had not taken part in the rescue; perhaps she had known beforehand, perhaps by chance, of Stephen's coming out; perhaps Mrs. Stephen had told her, perhaps Mei Yingzi had told her — perhaps, I thought, Bai Ping did not know of the connection between Mei Yingzi and Mrs. Stephen. Yes, that was it: she knew Mei Yingzi, but had never known that Stephen and his wife were people of the same organization. This was of course no dullness in Bai Ping; had I myself not joined in their work, how should I have known?

A flash of light like lightning rose in my mind, and a chill ran over me: it dawned on me that the style of "Stephen and his wife" was only a smoke-screen of their work, that there was no relation of husband and wife in fact at all. Many of a man's intuitive perceptions are indeed sometimes swifter and truer than the analysis of reason, and out of such a judgment there often comes an obstinacy, a firm conviction, or even a kind of faith. A scientific conjecture is the product of intuitive perception, but it needs the analysis of reason to prove it; and now, one had only to recall the past — Stephen's suddenly using the style of husband and wife to invite me to their birthday feast, Stephen's ordinary manner of life, and Mrs. Stephen's attitude toward him — were these not all things that could serve as the ground of my conjecture?

Carrying these thoughts, I walked all the way home; carrying these thoughts, I lay down in bed; and about Stephen's fate at the hospital I found I did not think at all.

The long walk had tired me; the sleet struck the windowpane, seeming thicker than before, and its pattering sound slowly swept away my broken threads of thought, and I fell asleep in a kind of empty blankness.

It was not early when I woke, and in the midst of a hurried wash there was suddenly a telephone call for me, and I went downstairs after the servant.

"Who is it?" I asked, taking the receiver.

"It's me." It was Mei Yingzi's voice: "Will you come at once to the Gaolang Hospital? I am waiting for you."

So I dressed, and without eating breakfast hurried to Gaoye Road.

The Gaolang Hospital was a very small private hospital, but clean, lovely, and tranquil. Ward 12 was upstairs, and I hurried up; on the broad veranda, with its rattan chairs and round table, sat Mei Yingzi, with Mrs. Stephen standing beside her, and by the railing leaned Dr. Philip, to whom a doctor in a white gown, his hands in his pockets, was speaking low.

Mei Yingzi saw me first, and rose gravely; Mrs. Stephen too turned round gravely; as I came up, Mei Yingzi said to me as if in reproach:

"You've come too late."

"Stephen —?"

"The pastor is in with him now..." with a glance at the door of Ward 12.

I fell silent and stood aside.

"Sit a while," said Mrs. Stephen.

I sat down slowly, and watched the two doctors before me; I saw Dr. Philip shake his head, fish a pipe from his pocket, slowly fill it, slowly light it, and then the curling smoke drifted in the air; the talk seemed to be over, and everyone watched this smoke dissolving into the air.

At last Dr. Philip saw me, came over and shook my hand, and then introduced me to the doctor in the white gown, Dr. Gaolang. Just then the door of Ward 12 opened, and a pastor of fifty-odd years came out; everyone watched him, and Mrs. Stephen, covering her face with both hands and sobbing, stepped forward two paces. I saw the pastor pat her gently on the shoulder and say:

"Go in now, and do not grieve; let this brave child enter Heaven in peace." So saying, he went off with Dr. Gaolang. Then Mrs. Stephen, sobbing, followed the two nurses in; I wished to be with Stephen once more, but Mei Yingzi stopped me and said in a low voice:

"This is the last talk between husband and wife."

So I stood, and saw the door close softly; a myriad griefs gathered in my heart, and for an instant I lost feeling and thought, and my tears streamed down. As I reached into my pocket for a handkerchief, I found that Mei Yingzi was already seated in a rattan chair, a handkerchief pressed to her eyes; and Dr. Philip was at the railing, both elbows on it, his face buried in his hands.

At last the door opened, and Mrs. Stephen came out weeping; holding back my tears, I helped her to Mei Yingzi's side. The two nurses came out after her. Just then some extraordinary power roused me, and I pushed open the door and walked into the ward.

Stephen lay stiff on the bed; the nurses had already drawn the sheet over his face. I went softly over and lifted the sheet from his face.

Tousled hair, a disordered stubble, an iron-gray cheek, deep-purple lips. His teeth were clenched, his eyes slightly open; gaunt, he lay there stiff. Was this the healthy, lively, young and dauntless Stephen? And yet this was Stephen.

I stroked his eyelids gently with my hand, and said:

"You've seen your friend now, haven't you? Then close your eyes and go back to Heaven in peace! I shall pray for you forever."

Stephen's eyes did indeed close! A grave and gloomy feeling froze my tears, and naturally I knelt before his bed. It was a man without religion beginning to feel that in the distance between life and death only religion is our bridge.
