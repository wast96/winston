Appendix IV. My Grandmother, a Daughter of the Party
After a wait of more than twenty years, my grandmother's autobiography has at last been published. As the granddaughter she has always cared for and cherished so deeply, besides sending a great heavy bundle of congratulations from the southern hemisphere, what I offer more than anything are my deep prayers and warm blessings, wishing the dear old lady health and happiness. Her health is a blessing to us of the younger generation, and her happiness is the strength that has sustained her through all the ordeals of her life.
I have long wanted to write a few words, for the publication of my grandmother's autobiography and to fulfill a wish of my own. In our family, among my generation, I was the first child to watch her begin writing her autobiography — to watch her write it over and over, piece after piece, from Daye in Hubei to Beijing and Shanghai, and on to Hangzhou.
In 1975, when my grandmother came out of Qincheng Prison, under the rule then in force that she "may not stay in the capital, and may not go to a big city or the frontier," my mother brought her from Beijing to Hubei. It was an evening of pouring rain; again and again the wind blew the oilcloth umbrella inside out as my grandmother led me, soaked through, step by step along the sleepers of the railway track toward our home with the geological team on the edge of the Daye county town. The next day she took up her pen.
It was a home without a table. Besides the bed, all there was to sit on were two bamboo chairs and two small wooden stools — the stools one each for my younger sister and me, and of the bamboo chairs one for my mother's regular use and one kept for my father, who came to visit once a year. My grandmother's earliest writing was done sitting on a little wooden stool, with a bamboo chair for a desk.
Though I had only just started school, I could still make out perfectly the large characters "Beloved Chairman Mao." I knew she was writing letters, always thick and long — sheet after sheet of double-ruled report paper, from the red double rules and the white blank spaces to whole pages and whole pads filled with blue ballpoint writing. Only years later did I learn that she was writing petition materials. Those thick materials recorded her lifetime of work, her lifelong pursuit and hopes, following the Chinese Communist revolution from 1926, when she was nineteen.
Perhaps because every letter had sunk without a trace like a stone in the sea, once my grandmother took me and marched straight into the Organization Department of the Huangshi Municipal Party Committee in Hubei. I remember that morning we got up at five o'clock, and only after changing vehicles three times — train, bus — did we reach Huangshi. Two men who looked like cadres argued loudly with my grandmother about something in their office, and I kept worrying that they would come to blows. In those few hours I understood almost nothing of what they said, and only one thing circled in my mind: "If they hit my grandmother, I'll bite their hands." For at the time my height was just right for such an action…
My grandmother was fortunate. She inherited her own grandfather's largeness of spirit, her father's wisdom, and her mother's kindness; gifted and quick, with an extraordinary memory, she was acknowledged a great beauty well into middle age. Her life was full of twists and reverses, yet there was always a discerning patron or a kindred spirit to help her, and again and again she turned danger into safety. From the age of nineteen she threw herself into the stirring Chinese revolution; as one of the earliest members of the Chinese Communist Party, from the Great Revolution in Wuhan, the Nanchang Uprising, and the early Shanghai workers' movement, through the War of Resistance, the Pacific War, and the civil war between the Kuomintang and the Communists, she was always an important participant, an organizer, and the most direct witness of history. On the crests and in the troughs of the waves she lived through the whole upheaval of twentieth-century Chinese society in her own person, through the growth, the setbacks, and the development of the Communist Party.
My grandmother was also unfortunate. Her greatest misfortune was a life of marriage beset by troubles. She was a beautiful and virtuous daughter of a great house, and her father was a most enlightened man of learning. In his day he had been a chief supporter and helper of Tan Sitong in the movement for democratic reform and renewal. Precisely because of this, he never held rigidly to convention or clung to dogma in educating his children, and so shaped in his daughter a character both well-read and reasonable, warm and lively, and yet wholly free of the haughtiness of a young lady of a noble house.
From the time my grandmother gave herself to the revolution, her personal life too became a part of the revolution. For the needs of revolutionary work she gave up a chance to study further in the Soviet Union in order to marry; again for the needs of the revolution she parted, time after time, from her sons and her husbands, setting out through her tears to struggle on stubbornly and alone in the torrent; and because the work required it, she followed the organization's arrangements without a word of complaint, putting her husband's family estate, property, and social connections all to the service of the Communist cause. That she could make such great sacrifices over the decades was because she would not betray the trust of the elders she revered — Dong Biwu, Qu Qiubai, and Zhou Enlai, her old superiors; because she could not forget the teachings of her revolutionary companions Wan Xiyan and He Chang, who were dear to her as kin and were both mentors and friends; and still more because of her own utterly sincere, open, and selfless heart. As a woman, and as a granddaughter who lived with her for more than ten years, I dare say that she surely had her longings, her hopes, her grievances, and her loneliness too. But in those years revolutionary conviction and passion held her personal wishes in check; later the years smoothed away the creases in her memory; and now her greatest joy is often to count on her fingers "how many good things there are in the family this year" — and these "good things" are mostly the successes of her children and grandchildren, or even the smallest bit of progress by the younger ones.
My grandmother was forgiving. I do not know whether it was the special experience of those decades that shaped her character, or whether it was her nature. From joining the revolution in 1926 to 1955, when she was first shut into Qincheng Prison and held in isolation for investigation — in those nearly thirty years — she was envied and framed in good times and kicked when she was down in bad ones, yet of all these past things she said not a word. I saw with my own eyes how certain famous figures of the arts turned a full 180 degrees in their attitude around the time of her rehabilitation, and in her book she reproached none of them, taking throughout the stance of writing only of others' virtues and contributions. The various separations and estrangements among the family caused by political pressure she dissolved one by one, until at last they reached harmony. She often said, "When the family is at peace, all things prosper" — and this family was both our small family and, more, the great family, the nation.
My grandmother's strength was more than human. In her life she was imprisoned four times — twice each by the Nationalists and the Communists — and above all, after the founding of New China, the two spells of imprisonment at Qincheng, seventeen years in all, did not shake in the least her faith in following the Chinese Communist revolution, nor change in the least her pursuit of the Communist ideal. A single parting of the living from the dead would be enough to crush a person's will, yet she went through nine blows, and used only nine "scars" to describe them. How hard was the process of rising again after each of those blows; how great the pain each of those scars brought — and all of it she passed over lightly, in a few spare strokes.
My grandmother's autobiography has at last been published. Since the liberation of China she has spent most of her time reviewing her own history with her pen — first for investigation, then for appeal — and only now does it take on real, substantial meaning: to set down a record, the record of the footprints of an early Chinese Communist revolutionary across a century; the record of a woman's struggles, her striving, her successes and setbacks, in a world ruled by men.
I thank Grandpa Mei Yi for his support and the editors of the Encyclopedia of China Publishing House for their work, which brought this book at last to publication. I thank the many elder relatives for all the toil they gave as this autobiography passed from manuscript pages to printed type.
Of course, the one I need to thank most of all is my grandmother. Her rich and colorful life is not only a treasure to us — her descendants of the third and fourth generations — but a precious mirror as well, reflecting one facet of the changing fortunes of Chinese society in the last century and of the course of the Chinese Communist revolution; and it is a reference for generation after generation of those to come in China as they choose their own paths in life. Just for this reason, it may be more fitting to close by borrowing a passage from an American specialist in women's studies, Professor Dena Gutman of Stanford University, in her book A New Culture, a New Stage: "The footprints of China's first generation of women pioneers, who stepped onto the Chinese political stage driven by the hot tide of the May Fourth movement, are without parallel in the history of either East or West. For they threw themselves into politics not in order to liberate women, but to realize an ideal and a conviction, and so became one of the important components of the Chinese revolution" (Stanford University Press, 1997, p. 21). An outstanding Chinese woman, who cast herself into the torrent of the Chinese revolution back in the 1920s of the last century and became an important member of this generation of women pioneers — she is the daughter of the Communist Party whom all revere, and the grandmother I love deeply.
Chen Hongxin
2 August 2003
Sydney, Australia
