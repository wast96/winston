NOTE-ADD p002
ANCHOR: an enemy from five hundred years ago
NOTE: The phrase renders 五百年前的冤家. A 冤家 (<i>yuanjia</i>) is a fated adversary&#8212;an enemy one is karmically bound to&#8212;and the word carries a wry undertone, since it doubles as an affectionate term for a lover or a maddening intimate. &#8216;Five hundred years&#8217; invokes the folk notion that the ties of this life, love and enmity alike, were fixed by a connection in a former one. Corroborated.
WHY: "An enemy from five hundred years ago" is baffling in English without the karmic-foe idea behind 冤家.

NOTE-ADD p018
ANCHOR: Down on your knees
NOTE: To make someone kneel (下跪) was to force from the body its own admission of inferiority: kneeling belonged to the lesser before the greater&#8212;servant before master, child before parent, subject before throne&#8212;and to command it, as Lishui does here and Rong Sheng does moments later, is to press the full weight of that hierarchy onto A-Chu. That a Western-trained doctor still kneels on command measures how completely the bond-servant&#8217;s station has survived his education. Corroborated.
WHY: A Western reader reads kneeling as generic submission and misses that it enacts the specific master/servant hierarchy the chapter is straining against.

NOTE-ADD p073
ANCHOR: a weakened body cannot take tonics
NOTE: In Chinese medicine 补药 (<i>buyao</i>), &#8216;tonics&#8217; or restoratives, are taken to replenish the body&#8217;s vital forces; but a classic maxim holds 虚不受补&#8212;a body too depleted cannot &#8216;receive&#8217; a tonic, so that dosing a ruined constitution only harms it further. Rong Sheng has turned exactly this against himself, pouring tonic after tonic into an already broken body so that his self-willed decline would pass for natural sickness. Corroborated; standard traditional-medicine doctrine.
WHY: The plot point (feigning illness by overdosing on tonics) depends on the traditional-medicine principle 虚不受补, invisible to a Western reader.
