NOTE-ADD p039
ANCHOR: scattered-point framing and flat modelling
NOTE: A-Chu is contrasting the conventions of Chinese brush painting (国画) with Western art. In place of the single fixed vanishing point of European perspective, the Chinese scroll uses a &#8216;scattered&#8217; or moving viewpoint (散点透视), letting the eye travel through a scene from many positions at once, and it builds form through line and ink rather than by Western shading and volume. The pictures are done in ink and mineral color on absorbent xuan paper with a soft animal-hair brush&#8212;the tools laid out on this desk. Corroborated.
WHY: the technical terms name real, distinctive conventions of Chinese painting that set it against the Western art the reader knows.

NOTE-ADD p114
ANCHOR: endured the “family discipline”
NOTE: &#8216;Family discipline&#8217; (家法, <i>jiafa</i>) was the recognized power of a household head to punish his own&#8212;wives, children, servants, bond-servants&#8212;on his own authority, a private penal jurisdiction the state left largely untouched; the word also names the very rod kept for the purpose. Rong Sheng&#8217;s ordering A-Chu to strike his own face is this power in action, and it stands in bitter counterpoint to the character for &#8216;law&#8217; (法), whose just meaning A-Chu expounds later in the same chapter. Corroborated.
WHY: the private penal authority of the family head over its dependents is the exact measure of A-Chu&#8217;s bondage, and a Western reader needs it named.

NOTE-ADD p164
ANCHOR: Where things are not level, they cry out
NOTE: Ronghua is playing on a famous line of the Tang essayist Han Yu (韩愈, 768&#8211;824): <i>it is the general rule of things that they cry out when they cannot find their balance</i> (大凡物不得其平则鸣). Han Yu meant it of writers driven to speak by injustice and hardship; Ronghua turns it, by way of the ink she has just splashed, into a goad&#8212;the wronged should protest, so why does A-Chu not? Corroborated.
WHY: the ink-and-water metaphor is built on a specific classical maxim about injustice provoking outcry that a Western reader will miss.

NOTE-ADD p175
ANCHOR: bowl of bird’s-nest-and-white-fungus sweet soup
NOTE: Bird&#8217;s nest (燕窝)&#8212;the hardened saliva with which swiftlets build their nests, painstakingly cleaned and stewed&#8212;is among the costliest of Chinese delicacies, prized as a nourishing tonic; white (silver) fungus (银耳) is a similarly esteemed restorative. Simmered into a sweet soup, it is at once a luxury and a token of tender care, which is what Chan&#8217;er&#8217;s late-night bowl for A-Chu conveys. Corroborated.
WHY: &#8216;bird&#8217;s-nest soup&#8217; is bewildering without knowing it is a prized, costly delicacy, offered here as a mark of care.

NOTE-ADD p187
ANCHOR: draw a fortune-stick
NOTE: Drawing lots (求签) is a common temple divination: the worshipper shakes a cylinder of numbered bamboo slips until one falls out, then exchanges its number for a printed slip of verse&#8212;here read by the old nun&#8212;whose cryptic lines are taken to foretell his fortune. The quatrain A-Chu draws maps his whole predicament, and its last line furnishes the title of the next chapter. Corroborated.
WHY: explains the temple lot-drawing rite and how the fortune-poem A-Chu receives is meant to be read.
