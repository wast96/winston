NOTE-ADD p003
ANCHOR: pear blossom
NOTE: The pear blossom (梨花) is white, and in Chinese tradition its whiteness reads as purity, transience, and grief&#8212;white, not black, being the color of mourning. Because 梨 (&#8216;pear&#8217;) sounds exactly like 离 (&#8216;to part, to separate&#8217;), pear imagery has long shadowed stories of loss; the old taboo against splitting a pear to share it (分梨, homophone of 分离, &#8216;to part&#8217;) rests on the same pun. The blossom recurs through the novel as the token of the Yang family, and the prologue&#8217;s closing line, in which the Yangs&#8217; pear blossom &#8216;bloomed strangely, bewitchingly bright&#8217; over a night of burial and abduction, turns that beauty ominous. Corroborated.
WHY: A Western reader reads "pear blossom" as a neutral springtime flower and misses its freight of whiteness, mourning, and the pear/parting pun that the book leans on.

NOTE-ADD p003
ANCHOR: embroidered peony
NOTE: The peony (牡丹) is the Chinese emblem of wealth and high rank, traditionally called the &#8216;flower of riches and honor&#8217; (富贵花) and reckoned the king of flowers; worked in embroidery on a woman&#8217;s skirt it marks the wearer as a lady of a prosperous house, and its full spring bloom carries a further note of ripe feminine beauty. Corroborated; a standard motif of Chinese decorative art.
WHY: The peony's specific symbolism (rank, wealth, beauty) is invisible to a Western reader, who sees only a flower.

NOTE-ADD p016
ANCHOR: golden lotus embroidered on its side
NOTE: A bound foot was called a &#8216;golden lotus&#8217; (金莲), and the tiny embroidered silk slipper worn over it&#8212;like this pink, lotus-embroidered shoe&#8212;was its emblem. Foot-binding, practiced among Han Chinese women for roughly a thousand years, bent and crushed the foot from childhood to a length of a few inches; it was a mark of gentility and all but a precondition of a good marriage, and was only beginning to die out in the years this novel opens. Corroborated. The motif returns in the next chapter, where the Rong bride is named Jinlian, &#8216;golden lotus,&#8217; yet has natural, unbound feet.
WHY: The pink embroidered shoe, the "foot soft as fine jade," and the "golden lotus" are all the imagery of a bound foot; a Western reader has no way to know the scene is describing one.
