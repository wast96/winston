NOTE-ADD p009
ANCHOR: the Romance of the Three Kingdoms
NOTE: The <i>Romance of the Three Kingdoms</i> (三国演义) is one of the four great classical Chinese novels, a sprawling historical epic of the wars that followed the fall of the Han, celebrated above all for its ruses, feints, and battles of wits. To call a scheme &#8216;the stalest trick out of the Three Kingdoms&#8217; is to call it a stratagem every Chinese reader has met a hundred times. Corroborated.
WHY: names the classic that stands, for a Chinese reader, as the storehouse of stratagem the sentence is invoking.

NOTE-ADD p044
ANCHOR: robed in sackcloth and mourning-white
NOTE: Chinese deep mourning (披麻戴孝, &#8216;wearing hemp and mourning&#8217;) dressed the bereaved in coarse, undyed sackcloth of hemp or jute and unbleached white, the plainer and rougher the closer the kin&#8212;the opposite of Western black. The white banners, white flowers, and hemp robes here set a full funeral tableau before A-Chu. That it is staged for a father twenty years dead, to bind the son to revenge, is the scene's cold theatricality. Corroborated.
WHY: the specific hemp-and-white mourning dress, and its degrees, are invisible to a reader who pictures mourning as black.

NOTE-ADD p064
ANCHOR: a long queue always trailed down
NOTE: The queue (辫子)&#8212;the front of the head shaved and the rest grown long and braided&#8212;was imposed on Han Chinese men by the conquering Manchus in 1645 as the visible badge of submission to the Qing, on pain of death. By the last years of the dynasty it had become, to the reform-minded, a mark of servility and backwardness, and it was widely cut off after the revolution of 1911. The comedy of eunuchs waltzing in Western coats with queues swinging &#8216;like a tail&#8217; is the collision of two worlds the whole novel watches. Corroborated.
WHY: the joke and its bite depend on knowing the queue was the compulsory Manchu badge of subjecthood, not a quaint hairstyle.

NOTE-ADD p065
ANCHOR: aunt-mother
NOTE: &#8216;Aunt-mother&#8217; renders 姨娘, the name the children of a household used for their father&#8217;s concubine&#8212;a woman ranked below the wife (see the note on the Madams, Chapter 1) but addressed with the affection due a junior mother. The word marks her double place in the house: not the mother, yet a mother of sorts, and close in age here to the daughter who befriends her. Corroborated.
WHY: the coined term &#8216;aunt-mother&#8217; needs its original, 姨娘, and the form-of-address relation it names between a concubine and the master's children.

NOTE-ADD p104
ANCHOR: let the plum wither in the peach’s place
NOTE: The image renders the set phrase 李代桃僵, &#8216;the plum dies in place of the peach.&#8217; It comes from the Han <i>yuefu</i> ballad &#8216;Jiming,&#8217; in which a plum growing beside a peach lets the insects gnaw its own roots and dies so the peach may live; it came to mean one person or thing sacrificed, or substituted, for another, and is the eleventh of the Thirty-Six Stratagems. Here the murdered Yang Yubo&#8217;s very name and place are taken over by the brother who killed him. Corroborated.
WHY: the substitution stratagem that drives the entire plot is a specific classical idiom a Western reader cannot recover from the plum-and-peach image alone.

NOTE-ADD p109
ANCHOR: ask a bride-price
NOTE: The bride-price (彩礼) was a gift of money and goods given by the groom&#8217;s family to the bride&#8217;s at betrothal&#8212;flowing opposite to a Western dowry, from the man&#8217;s side to the woman&#8217;s. Fourth Madam&#8217;s cover story turns on it: a destitute mother marrying off a daughter could raise a needed sum this way, which is exactly the pretext she uses to enter the Rong house. Corroborated.
WHY: a Western reader is apt to read &#8216;bride-price&#8217; as a dowry; the direction of the payment is the point of the ruse.
