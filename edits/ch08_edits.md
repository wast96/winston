NOTE-ADD p004
ANCHOR: in a pink qipao
NOTE: The qipao (旗袍, also &#8216;cheongsam&#8217;) is the close-fitting, high-collared, side-slit dress that became the signature garment of the fashionable modern woman in the Shanghai of the 1920s and 1930s. Recut from the loose Manchu banner-gown to sheathe the figure, it marked its wearer as urban, stylish, and up to date. Corroborated.
WHY: a Western reader may not know the qipao or that it signals a modern, fashionable Shanghai woman.

NOTE-ADD p010
ANCHOR: communication between Shanghai and Yan’an
NOTE: A small anachronism. In 1931 the Communists&#8217; central base was the rural Soviet in Jiangxi (see the note on the Soviet areas, Chapter 4); Yan&#8217;an (延安), in Shaanxi, became the Party&#8217;s headquarters only after the Long March, from 1935&#8211;1937 onward. The novel names the later, more famous capital of the revolution in place of the base that would actually have received Shanghai&#8217;s wireless traffic at this date.
WHY: a reader checking the history would be misled: Yan&#8217;an was not yet the Communist base in 1931.

NOTE-ADD p033
ANCHOR: freshly steeped Longjing
NOTE: Longjing (龙井, &#8216;Dragon Well&#8217;) is a celebrated green tea from the hills above Hangzhou, long the most prized of Chinese teas and a mark of refinement and means. To serve it freshly steeped is to set a table of quiet luxury. Corroborated.
WHY: names a specific prestige tea whose serving signals the household&#8217;s refinement.

NOTE-ADD p082
ANCHOR: Shanghai Garrison Command
NOTE: The Shanghai Garrison Command (淞沪警备司令部) was the Nationalist military authority policing the Chinese-administered parts of the city; through the early 1930s it was a chief instrument of the &#8216;white terror,&#8217; hunting, arresting, and executing Communists. Its cars converging on the newspaper office are the state closing in on Lao Yu. Corroborated.
WHY: identifies the specific security force that pursued the underground, distinct from the intelligence services noted in Chapter 4.

NOTE-ADD p115
ANCHOR: white medicine powder, cotton balls
NOTE: The &#8216;white medicine&#8217; (白药, <i>baiyao</i>) is a traditional Chinese styptic powder&#8212;most famously Yunnan Baiyao, compounded from herbs&#8212;sprinkled on a wound to stanch bleeding and speed healing. That the Western-trained A-Chu reaches for it alongside anesthetic and alcohol is a small emblem of the two medicines meeting in one pair of hands. Corroborated.
WHY: &#8216;white medicine powder&#8217; is opaque; it is a real traditional wound-powder, and its use here blends Chinese and Western medicine.
