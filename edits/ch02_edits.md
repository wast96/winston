NOTE-ADD p004
ANCHOR: household servant of a special sort
NOTE: A 家奴 (<i>jianu</i>), literally a &#8216;house slave,&#8217; was a hereditary bond-servant belonging to a household&#8212;not a hired domestic free to leave, but a person owned by the family, whether through debt, sale, or being taken in as a foundling. That A-Chu is a jianu, however well educated and however loosely called a &#8216;foster son,&#8217; is the fact that governs his whole predicament: he is chattel of the Rong house, bound by ownership as much as by gratitude. Corroborated; such bond-servitude persisted in wealthy Chinese households into the Republican era.
WHY: A Western reader reads "household servant" as an ordinary paid domestic and misses that A-Chu is an owned bond-servant, which is what the chapter's conflict turns on.

NOTE-ADD p005
ANCHOR: private tutor’s
NOTE: The source has A-Chu schooled first at a 私塾 (<i>sishu</i>), the traditional private school where boys learned the Confucian classics by memorization and recitation, and then at a 洋学堂 (<i>yang xuetang</i>), a &#8216;foreign-style school&#8217; teaching a modern Western curriculum. The move from the one to the other traces the great educational divide of the early twentieth century, as classical learning gave way to Western science&#8212;here, the Western medicine A-Chu goes on to take up. Corroborated.
WHY: "Private tutor's" and "foreign-style school" name two specific, opposed institutions of the era (old classics vs. new Western learning) that a Western reader cannot see behind the plain English.

NOTE-ADD p024
ANCHOR: xiao flute
NOTE: The <i>xiao</i> (箫) is a vertical, end-blown bamboo flute with a soft, breathy, melancholy tone, quite distinct from the transverse <i>dizi</i>; it is traditionally the instrument of solitude and grief. Rong Sheng&#8217;s playing of it recurs through the novel as the emblem of his ruined and mournful life. Corroborated.
WHY: A Western reader has no image of a xiao or its plaintive associations, which is why the recurring "flute in the night" reads as mere atmosphere rather than as Rong Sheng's signature.

NOTE-ADD p024
ANCHOR: opium pipe
NOTE: Opium&#8212;smoked through a long pipe over a small lamp&#8212;was epidemic in China from the nineteenth century onward, and a wealthy idler like Rong Sheng sinking into addiction abroad is wholly of its time; the drug ruined countless fortunes, and its suppression ran through the whole Republican period. As a Western-trained doctor, A-Chu manages the craving with measured injections of morphine, then itself used to wean opium addicts. Corroborated.
WHY: The opium pipe, and A-Chu dosing him with morphine to control it, belong to a specific history of addiction in China that a Western reader would not supply on their own.

NOTE-ADD p184
ANCHOR: Confucian doctrine of gratitude and repayment
NOTE: Confucian ethics laid heavy stress on 报恩 (<i>bao&#8217;en</i>), the duty to repay a debt of kindness&#8212;above all the debt a child owes for having been raised, which shades into filial piety itself. It is this obligation, more than any law, that chains A-Chu to the Rong household and to Fourth Madam, who took him in off the street; Cong Feng is charging him with letting that inherited duty devour his freedom and his youth. Corroborated.
WHY: The force of "gratitude and repayment" as a binding moral debt (not a vague sentiment) is the engine of A-Chu's paralysis, and a Western reader needs it named.
