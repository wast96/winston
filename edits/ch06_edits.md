NOTE-ADD p084
ANCHOR: St. Mary’s Girls’ School
NOTE: Shanghai&#8217;s elite girls&#8217; schools&#8212;St. Mary&#8217;s Hall (圣玛利亚女校) above all, the famous Episcopalian school where the writer Eileen Chang would later study&#8212;educated the daughters of the wealthy and Westernized in English, music, and Western manners, and were a badge of the highest social polish. (The &#8216;Hongxia&#8217; school named beside it is the novel&#8217;s invention.) A girl bred at such a school could be expected to waltz, which is exactly what Muci is fishing for. Corroborated for St. Mary&#8217;s.
WHY: A Western reader cannot know that naming these schools instantly marks a girl as a Westernized aristocrat trained to dance, which is the point of Muci's question.

NOTE-ADD p102
ANCHOR: cut out two butterflies
NOTE: The paper butterflies are an unmistakable sign. In the beloved folk legend of the Butterfly Lovers (梁山伯与祝英台), two lovers parted in life are buried together and their souls rise from the grave as a joined pair of butterflies&#8212;China&#8217;s very archetype of 殉情, dying for love. By cutting out two butterflies and laying them on the dead couple, the silent Yu Xiaojiang names Guo Ziqiong and He Yashan lovers united at last in death. Corroborated.
WHY: Without the Butterfly Lovers legend the paper butterflies read as a random gesture; with it, they are an eloquent, wordless elegy.

NOTE-ADD p200
ANCHOR: a deep, long bow
NOTE: A-Chu makes a 长揖 (<i>changyi</i>): the traditional Chinese salute of respect, made standing, hands clasped together and swept from high before the face down to the waist in a deep bow. Deliberately old-fashioned and courteous, it sets his gracious formality against the scrum of flashbulbs and shouted questions. Corroborated.
WHY: "A deep, long bow" hides a specific, formal, hands-clasped gesture whose studied old-world courtesy is part of the scene's meaning.

NOTE-ADD p210
ANCHOR: little earth-god’s temple
NOTE: The 土地庙 is the shrine of the 土地公, the local earth-god&#8212;the humblest and most ubiquitous deity of Chinese folk religion, a minor tutelary spirit with a small shrine in almost every village and neighborhood. A-Fu&#8217;s grumble that the reporters have &#8216;sprung from who knows what little earth-god&#8217;s temple&#8217; casts them as small fry crawling out of every cranny, the way such shrines dot the land. Corroborated.
WHY: The joke depends on knowing the earth-god shrine is the small, everywhere-present folk shrine, so "sprung from an earth-god's temple" means "popped up out of every nook."

NOTE-ADD p217
ANCHOR: the Shanghai Bund
NOTE: The Bund (外滩) is the grand waterfront along the Huangpu River, lined with the banks and trading houses of the foreign concessions; by extension 上海滩, &#8216;the Shanghai shore,&#8217; came to stand for the whole glittering, cut-throat world of the city&#8212;its finance, its glamour, and its gangland alike. Lishui&#8217;s imagined headline casts Rong Sheng as a gallant of that world in the stock role of the &#8216;hero who rescues the beauty&#8217; (英雄救美). Corroborated.
WHY: "Hero of the Shanghai Bund" carries the whole aura of old Shanghai and a stock romantic trope that a Western reader would otherwise miss.
