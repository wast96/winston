ANCHOR: the eldest brother stands as a father
NOTE: A-Chu invokes the old maxim &#38271;&#20804;&#22914;&#29238;&#65292;&#38271;&#23044;&#27604;&#27597; and its like: within the Confucian family the eldest son, once the father is dead, holds a father&#8217;s authority over his younger siblings, who owe him a father&#8217;s obedience. By this rule A-Chu &#8212; the elder twin and lawful heir &#8212; claims the right to command his brother Yang Muci, whatever Muci&#8217;s own standing. The principle ranks brothers strictly by age, and it is the ground of the whole contest of wills between the twins. Corroborated.
WHY: the claim rests on a specific rule of the family hierarchy &#8212; the eldest brother as stand-in father &#8212; that a Western reader will not supply.

NOTE-ADD p086
ANCHOR: When I play at opera, I am furthering the national essence
NOTE: To &#8216;play at opera&#8217; (&#31080;&#25103;) was the pastime of the well-born amateur (&#31080;&#21451;) who studied and performed Peking opera for love of the art rather than for pay; a leisured young gentleman&#8217;s command of the arias was a mark of cultivation. The &#8216;national essence&#8217; (&#22269;&#31929;, <i>guocui</i>) was the period&#8217;s honorific for Peking opera and the other traditional arts held to embody the quintessence of Chinese culture. Young Tang&#8217;s joke is to dignify his idle dilettantism as patriotic guardianship of that heritage &#8212; and then to strain the same excuse to cover his opium. Corroborated.
WHY: the boast bundles two culture-specific ideas &#8212; the dilettante opera-amateur and Peking opera as &#8216;national essence&#8217; &#8212; that a Western reader cannot recover from the English alone.
