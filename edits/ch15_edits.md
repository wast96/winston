NOTE-ADD p057
ANCHOR: the principal mother and the mother who bore her
NOTE: In a polygynous house the principal wife&#8217;s children ranked as 嫡 (of the main line) and a concubine&#8217;s as 庶 (<i>shuchu</i>, &#8216;of the branch&#8217;), lower in standing and inheritance (see the note on the Madams, Chapter 1). Yet every child formally called the principal wife &#8216;mother&#8217;&#8212;the 嫡母 or ritual mother&#8212;while the concubine who actually bore them was the 生母, the birth mother. Ronghua, borne by the Third Madam, is thus torn between the First Madam she must honor and the mother of her own blood; and it is because Rongrong is concubine-born that the First Madam holds her funeral of small account. Corroborated.
WHY: the whole scene turns on the principal-wife/concubine child hierarchy and the two-mothers relation, which a Western reader has no way to supply.

NOTE-ADD p102
ANCHOR: Books of loyalty and filial piety, of honor and righteousness
NOTE: Loyalty, filial piety, honor, and righteousness (忠孝节义) are the four cardinal virtues of Confucian popular morality and the stock themes of the operas and storybooks on which a traditional child was raised. That Rong Chu was schooled on such books frames the fierce sense of filial duty&#8212;and of vengeance owed a parent&#8212;that drives him. Corroborated.
WHY: names the specific virtue-formula of a traditional moral education that shaped Rong Chu, invisible behind the plain English list.

NOTE-ADD p155
ANCHOR: ridden off on the yellow crane
NOTE: The image alludes to Cui Hao&#8217;s (崔颢, d. 754) celebrated Tang poem &#8216;Yellow Crane Tower&#8217; (黄鹤楼), whose opening&#8212;<i>the one of old has ridden off on the yellow crane, and here remains only the empty tower</i>&#8212;became the classic elegy for someone gone beyond recall. The narrator turns it on the dead Fourth Madam: she who is gone has ridden the yellow crane away, and here only an endless grief remains. Corroborated.
WHY: the elegiac force is a specific Tang-poem allusion a Western reader will miss entirely.

NOTE-ADD p169
ANCHOR: the endless, unbroken wars of the warlords
NOTE: After Yuan Shikai&#8217;s death in 1916 China fragmented among rival militarists, and roughly a decade of shifting warlord wars (军阀混战) followed until the Nationalists&#8217; nominal reunification in 1928. Han Zhengqi&#8217;s soldiering years fall in that chaos, the violence he wearied of before drifting to Shanghai. Corroborated.
WHY: places Han Zhengqi&#8217;s biography in the real Warlord Era, a period a Western reader will not know by name.

NOTE-ADD p202
ANCHOR: master-pledge card
NOTE: A &#8216;master-pledge card&#8217; renders 拜师帖, the written pledge by which one performs 拜师&#8212;kneeling and kowtowing to acknowledge a master&#8212;and so binds oneself as his disciple. In the secret-society world such a pledge sealed a lifelong bond of obedience and protection between master and man; Han Zhengqi&#8217;s old card, sworn to the Golden Dragon Society, is what A-Chu&#8212;now its head&#8212;holds out to reclaim his allegiance. Corroborated.
WHY: the scene's whole leverage is the disciple-pledge bond a Western reader cannot read from &#8216;master-pledge card&#8217; unaided.
