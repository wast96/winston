NOTE-ADD p003
ANCHOR: the advertisement girls on a New Year print
NOTE: A New Year print (年画, <i>nianhua</i>) is a brightly colored woodblock picture pasted up at the lunar New Year for luck&#8212;plump children, door-gods, goddesses of plenty. By the 1930s Shanghai&#8217;s printers had wedded the old form to advertising, turning out posters of rosy, near-identical &#8216;New Year girls&#8217; to sell cigarettes and soap. To liken the maids to such girls is to call them uniformly, mass-producedly pretty. Corroborated.
WHY: the nianhua and its stock idealized girls are invisible to a Western reader, and they are the whole point of the simile.

NOTE-ADD p021
ANCHOR: kowtow to First Madam
NOTE: To kowtow (磕头, <i>ketou</i>, &#8216;knock the head&#8217;) is to kneel and touch the forehead to the ground, the deepest of ritual obeisances&#8212;owed by child to parent, servant to master, the living to the ancestors. First Madam waives it (&#8216;no need to hold to the old rules&#8217;) because a foreign-trained doctor now stands above such servility; Fourth Madam insists on it all the same, as the repayment of a debt of kindness that binds the bond-servant. Corroborated.
WHY: &#8216;kowtow&#8217; is a word many readers know only vaguely; the scene turns on its exact weight as submission and gratitude.

NOTE-ADD p021
ANCHOR: burn incense to the ancestors
NOTE: Reporting the family&#8217;s doings to its ancestors&#8212;here a son&#8217;s safe return&#8212;is a central act of Chinese domestic religion. Before the ancestral tablets the family burns incense, bows, and announces births, marriages, homecomings, and deaths, so that the dead share in the fortunes of the living and go on watching over the line. It is Confucian filial piety made visible: the dead are not gone but present, and owed the same duty as the living elders. Corroborated.
WHY: the reader needs to see that burning incense to the ancestors is worship of the family dead, not a decorative flourish.

NOTE-ADD p100
ANCHOR: the Qingming festival
NOTE: Qingming (清明, &#8216;Clear and Bright&#8217;), the tomb-sweeping festival of early April, is the day families visit the ancestral graves to weep, clear the weeds, lay out food and wine, and burn paper money for the dead. It is the great spring rite of remembrance; Rong Sheng&#8217;s promise to come &#8216;after Qingming&#8217; sets the season for the graveside scenes to follow. Corroborated.
WHY: names a specific and central festival of the dead that a Western reader will not recognize.

NOTE-ADD p128
ANCHOR: Bodhisattva Guanyin
NOTE: Guanyin (观音), the Bodhisattva of Compassion, is the most beloved deity of Chinese Buddhism&#8212;the merciful being who hears the cries of the world, popularly worshipped in female form as the goddess of mercy. To &#8216;build bridges and mend roads&#8217; (修桥铺路) is the stock phrase for pious charitable works that earn merit. Rong Sheng is calling A-Chu, half-fondly and half-mockingly, a soft-hearted living saint who can refuse no one in need. Corroborated.
WHY: the joke depends on knowing Guanyin as the merciful bodhisattva and &#8216;building bridges and mending roads&#8217; as charitable merit-making.

NOTE-ADD p129
ANCHOR: “Tongji” hospital
NOTE: The Tongji Hospital (同济医院) grew from the German-founded medical school set up in Shanghai in 1907 by the physician Erich Paulun; by the 1920s it was among China&#8217;s foremost institutions of Western medicine. That A-Chu, trained abroad, practices there places him at the leading edge of the modern, foreign-derived medicine that was displacing the classical Chinese pharmacy of his own adoptive family. Corroborated.
WHY: the name marks a real, prestigious Western-medicine institution and sharpens the old-medicine / new-medicine contrast that runs through the book.
