NOTE-ADD p011
ANCHOR: Amitabha
NOTE: Amitabha (阿弥陀佛, <i>Emituofo</i>) is the Buddha of Boundless Light who presides over the Western Paradise, the Pure Land; reciting his name is the central devotion of Pure Land Buddhism, the most popular form of the faith in China. Spoken aloud as here, it serves as a pious exclamation&#8212;a breath of blessing, resignation, or thanks, roughly &#8216;mercy be&#8217;&#8212;rather than a statement. The scarred old nun studs her fortune-telling with it to mark each utterance as devout. Corroborated.
WHY: a Western reader hears &#8216;Amitabha&#8217; as a nonsense exclamation and misses that it names the Buddha of the Pure Land and works as a breath of blessing.

NOTE-ADD p123
ANCHOR: putting an innocent man to the block
NOTE: A-Chu translates the grim phrase 宰白鸭, &#8216;to slaughter a white duck.&#8217; Under the late Qing it named a real abuse of justice: a poor man was paid to confess to a capital crime he had not committed and go to execution in the true culprit&#8217;s place, so that a wealthy killer went free and the magistrate closed his case. To shoot men merely to fill an arrest quota, A-Chu says, is that same murder of the innocent under another name. Corroborated.
WHY: the line renders a specific Qing term for judicial scapegoating that a Western reader cannot recover from &#8216;innocent man to the block&#8217; alone.

NOTE-ADD p132
ANCHOR: pingtan ballad-singing
NOTE: Pingtan (评弹) is the storytelling-and-ballad art of Suzhou and the lower Yangzi, performed in the soft Wu dialect: one or two performers narrate and sing a long romantic tale to their own accompaniment on the <i>pipa</i>, a pear-shaped lute, and the three-stringed <i>sanxian</i>. The sung portion is <i>tanci</i>. Long prized by the Jiangnan gentry as the most refined of popular entertainments, it is heard in the teahouse &#8216;storytelling houses&#8217; (书场) like the one later in this chapter. Corroborated.
WHY: names the specific genre&#8212;its Wu-dialect song, its pipa and sanxian, its storytelling houses&#8212;that frames the whole second half of the chapter.

NOTE-ADD p195
ANCHOR: the Herd-Boy and the Weaving-Maid stars
NOTE: The Herd-Boy (牵牛, the star Altair) and the Weaving-Maid (织女, Vega) are the lovers of China&#8217;s best-loved star-legend: a mortal cowherd and a heavenly weaving-girl, married and then parted to opposite banks of the Milky Way, allowed to meet only once a year, on the seventh night of the seventh month, over a bridge of magpies. To watch their stars is to sigh over love kept apart&#8212;here the concubine Xu Yuzhen&#8217;s longing for a love she is denied. Corroborated.
WHY: the allusion a Western reader will miss, and it carries the concubine's whole lament in the sung tale.

NOTE-ADD p195
ANCHOR: a top-graduate husband
NOTE: A &#8216;top-graduate&#8217; renders 状元 (<i>zhuangyuan</i>), the scholar who placed first in the palace examination at the summit of the imperial civil service&#8212;the highest honor the old system could bestow, and the storybook ideal of a husband, a poor student raised at a stroke to fame and office. The examinations were abolished in 1905, a few years before the events the singers tell, but 状元及第, &#8216;to pass as top graduate,&#8217; lived on as a set phrase for the perfect match. The concubine sings that she wants no such glory, only love. Corroborated.
WHY: &#8216;top-graduate husband&#8217; hides the imperial-examination ideal (the zhuangyuan) that a Western reader needs to feel the weight of what she renounces.
