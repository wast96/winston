NOTE-ADD p092
ANCHOR: a grown man of seven feet
NOTE: The Chinese 尺 (<i>chi</i>), conventionally Englished &#8216;foot,&#8217; is shorter than the English foot, so 七尺 (&#8216;seven <i>chi</i>&#8217;) describes not a giant but a man of ordinary adult height. 七尺男儿 and 七尺之躯 are stock phrases for an able-bodied grown man, carrying the sense of a person of full stature and the dignity proper to one. A-Ci&#8217;s indignation is that of a grown man told to hold out his hand to his parents for money. Corroborated.
WHY: "a grown man of seven feet" reads to a Western reader as a seven-foot giant; the point is the opposite — a full-grown, self-respecting adult male.

NOTE-ADD p025
ANCHOR: the Dragon King’s own temple
NOTE: The Dragon King (龙王) is the folk water-god who commands rain, rivers, and the sea, his temples a fixture of old Chinese towns. Cong Feng is quoting a two-part folk saying, 大水冲了龙王庙，一家人不认一家人 (&#8216;the flood has washed away the Dragon King&#8217;s own temple: kinfolk no longer knowing their own kin&#8217;), used when people of one side clash by mistake before recognizing that they belong together. Learning that A-Chu and Ronghua are of one household, he means that they have been at cross-purposes like the flood and the god&#8217;s own shrine. Corroborated.
WHY: the Dragon King and the xiehouyu behind the line are invisible to a Western reader, who cannot recover why a flooded temple means "we are all family."

NOTE-ADD p124
ANCHOR: policy dissertation
NOTE: The 策论 (&#8216;policy dissertation&#8217;) was the essay on statecraft a candidate wrote in answer to set questions in the imperial examinations, the graded state tests that from the Sui and Tang dynasties to 1905 were the sole ladder into the scholar-official class. A-Chu&#8217;s 举子 who &#8216;leap the Dragon Gate&#8217; are the successful candidates, likened to the carp of legend that leaps the gorge of the Yellow River and is transformed into a dragon. His point is that a civilization schooled for centuries to declaim fixed positions on command never learned to converse as equals. Corroborated.
WHY: the examination system and its 策论 statecraft-essay (first substantively raised here) are opaque to a Western reader; anchored on 策论 rather than the recurring 鱼跃龙门 image (whose earlier occurrences at ch13/ch16 are left for the book-wide idiom sweep).

NOTE-ADD p156
ANCHOR: Ears-that-hear-on-the-wind
NOTE: &#8216;Ears-that-hear-on-the-wind&#8217; (顺风耳) and its partner &#8216;Thousand-<i>li</i> Eyes&#8217; (千里眼) are folk deities, the sharp-eared and sharp-eyed guardians who attend the sea-goddess Mazu; the pair are proverbial for beings who hear and see everything, however far off. Finding that Rong Chu has somehow overheard the whole conversation, A-Chu dubs him the all-hearing one. Corroborated.
WHY: the epithet is a named folk-mythological figure, not a coinage; the texture is lost on a reader who takes it for an ad-hoc nickname.
