## Thieves and Detectives in the Old Society

In the old society robbery and theft were rampant, and the detective police sheltered and harboured the thieves and sat still to split the spoils; the black curtain over it all is more than the bamboo can hold. Before liberation I was chiefly engaged in Juntong secret-service work, and for a period carried on my activities under cover of the open post of detective-squad captain, so I know a little of the inner tricks of it. A few years ago, on a tram, a pickpocket went for my wallet and I caught him red-handed. Afterwards a friend laughed: "This pickpocket had no eye for it at all — he actually picked the pocket of his own ancestor. Truly a small thief meeting a great one." I set down here, so far as memory serves, an account of these matters in the old society.

Many people know that in the old society robbers, thieves, pickpockets and the like all had their own secret organizations, each with its own "trade rules" and its own argot. As I understand it, before the war against Japan the people who followed these trades were not only organized but generally kept to the rules — as the saying went, "the thief has his thieving mates, the robber his robbing crew." These trades were divided into gangs and lines, each with its own set ways, each minding its own business. Take Shanghai: besides the local gang there were the Subei gang (also called the Jiangbei gang), the Anhui gang, the Zhejiang gang, the Cantonese gang and others. Within each gang there was a further division by what "money" one ate: the bold ones who feared neither heaven nor death, who turned robber, "ate money in the open"; the nimble-fingered "ate matches" (became pickpockets); the sharp-eyed and quick-legged "ran the toppers" (specializing in grabbing hats from outside a tram or bus just as it pulled away) or "ran the wheels" on the trains (stealing luggage and freight).

Besides the division by gang and line there was a division by turf, and one could not just intrude on another's territory to "grab the business." Anyone who ignored a warning got, at the least, a beating, and at the worst punishments such as having a hand or foot chopped off. As for fighting over turf, armed brawls between crews were common enough.

There were, of course, a few driven by hardship to desperate courses, who neither belonged to a gang nor followed a line. But these were the ones most easily caught: not only did the detectives go after them, but the men of the trade would "twig" them out too.

"Green the first time, familiar the second, expert the third" — this was a saying the thieves used of themselves. It meant that a raw beginner was a novice the first time; but once caught and shut up in a lockup or prison, there he could find a skilled master to teach him a "craft." By the time he was released at the end of his term he had become a hand at it. Should he be arrested and jailed again, he got a further chance to study and refine his "technique," so that he came out an old hand who knew the business through and through. Some could even take on a few apprentices, so that they looked on a stretch in jail as a chance to strengthen their skills and make useful mates.

An old hand going into jail had his own way of "making his entrance." The moment he entered the cell, if there was anyone he knew he gave a nod; if not, he would hang whatever he was carrying, or a coat, on a nail in the wall, give the nail a shake, and say: "This nail I drove in this room last time is still good and firm." If someone protested that he had just driven it in himself, he would say: "Then who pulled out the one I drove?" Such talk was simply to show that he was no first-timer, no novice, and not to be bullied.

At that time the Shanghai local gang was very displeased with the outside gangs, holding that they were snatching the local men's rice bowls; the hatred between them ran deep, and fights broke out constantly. When an outside gang came to Shanghai to "seize a wharf," they had shed many lives before they could carve out a place. Even after the gang bosses negotiated and marked out spheres of influence, things could never stay settled: they had constantly to "drink judging-tea" and negotiate afresh over the boundaries. In an old society where "the strong is king," the thieves were no exception — as a rule those with real force came off best.

For one period the gangs' spheres in Shanghai were roughly as follows: the old city district was the local gang's base; within the British and French Concessions there were parts where they could go "guerrilla"; the Hongkou district of the International Settlement was the Cantonese gang's ground; a stretch of the British Concession went to the Zhejiang gang; and the Shiliupu area went to the Anhui gang. There were finer details of division too, which I can no longer recall.

The reactionaries' public-security organs and the Concession patrol-houses' detectives quietly permitted this, because it helped in solving cases. When a person of power and position lost something, a single telephone call could have the very article returned at once. Many know the thieves' rule of "not moving stolen goods for three days"; in fact sometimes they would not move it within a week either. As for those outside the gangs, some would move it the moment it was in hand.

In the Nanshi district of Shanghai there was a pickpocket boss who, though already forty or fifty, was still called by his childhood name, "Ah Gou." Once the Juntong bureau's secretary-general Tang Zong came to Shanghai to inspect the work; getting out of his car near Laoximen, he had gone only half a street before his wallet was gone. He was very anxious, for the wallet held a small notebook with important matters in it. I found Ah Gou, and the next day had the very article back. Tang Zong insisted on seeing this brilliant pickpocket. Ah Gou pleaded again and again, saying the one thing most taboo in his trade was "showing his face"; I applied both soft and hard and would have no refusal. That evening he asked me to his home and introduced his apprentice to me. At the meeting I got a great shock: this brilliant pickpocket turned out to be a girl still at middle school, only a little over ten years old. She begged me bitterly not to take her to see Tang, so I could only report to Tang that the pickpocket had taken fright and fled.

After the war began the situation changed. In Chungking, apart from the local gang's organization still existing as before and keeping in touch with the detectives, the "down-river gangs" that had fled from Shanghai, Nanking, Wuhan and the like often went each their own way, so that the cases they pulled off were not easily traced.

When a thief joined a gang, entered a line and took a master, it was not only in hopes of mates to make the work easier and to look out for one another, but also of learning a skill. The great robber Wang Huzi ("Wang the Bearded" — his original name was Wang He; grown old he wore a long beard, and the whole fraternity called him Wang the Bearded), who had ranged over the great cities of the Southeast before the war, trained no few highly skilled apprentices, specializing in robbing the foreigners and great magnates who lived in the tall mansions. In those days the topmost floors of the ten-odd-storey Western buildings in the British and French Concessions were forever losing valuables and cash to theft, and the cases went long unsolved. One year the abbot's room in Longhua Temple was burgled: a jade Buddha, quantities of gold and silver ritual vessels, and hand-copied rare sutras were taken. This temple stood right beside the Songhu Garrison Headquarters, and the garrison commander Yang Hu was himself a great gang boss; some lay devotees who knew Yang told him of it and asked him to solve the case soon. Hearing it, Yang felt it too great a loss of face, and resolved to break the case, or the blow to his "prestige" would be too great.

I was one of those assigned to handle the case, and on receiving it went, as usual, first to the scene to look it over. Observing, we saw that this temple was not only walled high on all four sides but had several courtyards, so that it was hard to get in from outside. We all judged it an inside job, searched every corner of the whole temple, and questioned no few suspect monks, without getting the least clue. In the end we found on the outer wall the marks of someone's tread on the moss, and only then concluded it was the work of someone from outside — and all felt the case a very thorny one.

Our next step was to fetch several bosses who did "peeling the dew" (stealing before dawn), "gathering the lamp oil" (slipping in door to door at dusk) and "lifting the curtain" (digging through a wall at midnight), and take them to look the place over, asking which line's men had done it. All of them shook their heads and were positive that only an outside expert who did big jobs would have such skill. Unable to produce anyone, and pressed by us again and again, one old man thought a while and then said that by the look of it, it was Wang Huzi's work if not his, then his apprentices'. This old man knew only that Wang himself had already "washed his hands" and was passing his last years in peace by West Lake in Hangzhou; the address he still could not make out.

At my wits' end, I decided on a trip to Hangzhou. When I sought out the Hangzhou public-security chief, the Juntong agent Zhao Longwen, to inquire about Wang Huzi, he told me Wang's circumstances at once. It turned out Zhao Longwen knew Wang Huzi's ins and outs: Wang had guaranteed to Zhao that his apprentices did no business in Hangzhou, so Zhao permitted him to live there. That day Zhao sent an inspector to accompany me to find Wang. In a rather elegant villa-style house I met an old man finely dressed and gentle in manner, whom one would never from his looks have taken for a great robber of the rivers and lakes. When Wang Huzi heard me explain my errand, he said with a smile: "The lads have been too senseless, stirring up trouble; I am truly sorry." He promised to send someone to Shanghai to look into it, and if it was his apprentices' doing, he would certainly have them return the articles, and if not, would gladly help think of some way to find a lead.

Leaving Wang's house, I asked the inspector who had come with me, and learned that Hangzhou had never had a case of this kind. It turned out that the property of the wealthy and powerful who lived in peace by West Lake was under this old man's secret protection. On the fourth day after my return to Shanghai, the things lost from Longhua Temple had been stacked overnight at the door of the abbot's room, not one missing. Half a year later, going to Hangzhou again, I saw Wang once more; he said only that the affair had been the work of three of his apprentices, and never told me anything else.

During the war, when I was deputy head of the inspectorate at Chungking, the inspectorate caught a habitual thief taken in the act of burgling the American embassy. He admitted he was an apprentice of Wang Huzi, and I talked with him several times, learning that all the apprentices Wang taught had a "scale-the-wall, cross-the-roof" skill. Rumour outside had it that these men could "fly along the eaves and walk the walls"; out of curiosity I had him give me a demonstration, and it turned out to be nothing of the sort. Their wall-scaling and roof-crossing depended mainly on tools. One tool they often used was called a "soft pole": a long cord of woven hair or silk thread, a little thicker than a chopstick, with a metal hook at one end, which, thrown up and caught on a wall-top, let a man climb up. This "soft pole" could ordinarily be coiled at the waist, leaving no trace. Another tool was called a "hard pole," shaped outwardly like a pistol, its inner structure like a camera's tripod, extendable to a zhang and more; hooked at one end to a window ledge, one could climb straight up it.

They had a way of breaking glass to push open a door or window: first, with a glazier's diamond, they scored on the glass a frame large enough to put a hand through, then pressed a handkerchief smeared with fresh rubber over it; a moment later, a gentle push, and the cracked shards all stuck fast to the handkerchief and did not fall to the ground and make a sound — so that one could reach a hand in through the hole, draw back the window's catch, and climb in.

In stealing from the tenants of tall ten-odd-storey Western buildings, they always mixed into the building by day first and found a place to hide, then at night let themselves down by rope from the top floor to the tenant they had marked. So the taller the building, the more easily its topmost floors were robbed, for such places did not lend themselves to climbing from below upward but only descending from above; and once the goods were taken they could return the way they came, or open a door and flee from inside. Many who did not understand this passed the tale from mouth to mouth, holding that the thieves could fly from the ground up to a high storey, and made them out to have marvellous powers.

Some thieves who worked the rivers-and-lakes circuit from wharf to wharf, though not belonging to a gang, could still be tracked, for very few could work alone without needing a mate. Skilled "flying thieves" of that sort, when at their work, usually had a man or two "keeping the wind" for them. If a patrolling policeman happened by just then, the wind-keeper would pass word by a prearranged signal — on the one hand alerting the thief at work, on the other drawing off the policeman's attention.

"A distant thief must have a near nest." Wherever he came from, a thief had to find a place to lodge. Having stolen something, he had to find an outlet for it, not keep it for his own use. In the past many great cities had a kind of black market, mostly trading by lamplight before dawn. Much of what thieves stole changed hands in these places, and it was from these places that the detectives sought their leads. Many of the "nest-masters" who harboured thieves both sold the stolen goods and served as the detectives' eyes and ears, for in the ordinary way they needed the detectives' protection to carry on the trade at all. When a case had to be broken, they had to help find the lead too; so by mutual use they were able to go on existing.

In the past I handled many cases myself, and was in those days called a "famous detective" for the supposed speed with which I cracked them. In truth, laid bare, it is not worth a laugh: much of it was by coincidence or a chance encounter. Here I shall give just two or three cases briefly.

Before the war a Nationalist divisional commander, Liang Huasheng, after attending a meeting in Nanking, slipped off alone to Shanghai for pleasure. On the train he met a beautiful young woman, richly dressed and of graceful bearing, with two maidservants and much luggage. At the station a new-model car came to meet this woman, and Liang Huasheng took her for the family of some dignitary. When she invited Liang into the car and offered to see him home first, Liang said he had not yet decided which hotel to stay at, so she asked him to her home and settled him in a guest room. The next day, waking, Liang found the leather case he carried gone, and the master and servants of the house all absent — only then did he know he had walked into a trap. Liang hurriedly sought Wu Naixian, then chief of the police bureau of the Nanking-Shanghai-Hangzhou Railway. Wu had once been the Juntong's Shanghai regional chief and was a first-class Whampoa classmate of Liang's. Wu asked my help, saying that Liang's case held, besides more than seven thousand yuan in cash, many important documents, and it must be recovered for him. Liang and I went together to look over the place where he had spent the night, and learned it was an apartment run by a White Russian; the woman who had rented the rooms had paid only a month's rent, nearly up, all the furniture was hired, and the few trunks she had left were packed with rubbish, nothing of the least value. Liang was frantic and begged my help again and again; though I agreed verbally, I had no confidence at all.

I first questioned several thieves who "ran the wheels" on the Nanking-Shanghai railway. Hearing me describe the woman's features and the events of the day, they all said they did not know her. I then made inquiries of the "local snakes" of that district, and they too knew nothing. In the end I could only use the method of "watching the stump for a hare."

In a small lane by the Old North Gate of Shanghai there was a Wealth God temple, this "Wealth God" being enshrined especially for all the women of Shanghai who plied the flesh trade or did wicked work. The temple keeper, one Wang, was my informer. On the first and fifteenth of each month the prostitutes mostly came to burn incense and pray, and some of the "swindle-women," after taking their spoils, would come to redeem a vow. That day few came, and I had a chance from Wang's room to see clearly every person who came to burn incense. I waited until afternoon and still had not seen the young woman Liang Huasheng described come to burn incense and redeem a vow. Just as I was about to leave, I noticed a very plainly dressed woman drop two silver dollars into the money chest before the shrine, which at once aroused my curiosity. On a pretext I went close to her and found the one feature Liang had mentioned — two black moles by her right ear. So I waited for her to finish and followed her out; two turnings on, I saw the new-model Studebaker sedan Liang had described waiting for her. As she was about to get in I arrested her, and drove her, car and all, back to the detective corps. Under questioning she at once gave up her accomplices. I caught them one after another and recovered Liang's case and the seven thousand-odd yuan. Liang marvelled greatly that a case could be broken and the goods recovered so fast, and I too crowed a while. In fact it cost no effort — the quarry had walked into the net of her own accord.

Another great case that made a stir before the war, which not a few Shanghai tabloids trumpeted for "lightning case-cracking," was this. A wooden boat had loaded local produce at Anqing to sail for Shanghai; the night before it sailed, two deserters from a Nationalist unit joined with three habitual river-robbers, and at midnight, posing as inspectors, killed the boat-owner's whole family of five and the three boatmen and threw them into the river. Having done the deed, the five bandits sailed the boat to Shanghai and moored at the Shiliupu wharf, meaning to unload the goods and flee. I led a squad and arrested the five brutes; from the crime to the cracking of the case was under forty-eight hours.

It was truly a coincidence: I had no informer on the water, and the breaking of this case was but a "blind cat catching a mouse" hitting it by chance. It turned out the boat-owner had a relative, one Xiong, with whom I had some connection. Xiong had taken passage on this boat to come to Shanghai; when the bandits boarded to do the deed he had just gone ashore to find someone, and returning saw people boarding the boat and heard confused noises. Seeing this, he knew something was wrong, and hid in the dark to watch; before long he saw the boat cast off and head for Shanghai, whereupon he took a car and hurried to Shanghai through the night. At first light he found the boat heading for the wharf, and knowing not a soul aboard, knew something had happened. Seeing it moor at the Shiliupu wharf, he at once telephoned me, and so I broke the case easily.

When detectives told of how a case was cracked, they never mentioned which informer had supplied the lead. This was partly to make a show of their own skill, and partly because everyone who served as an informer feared the bandits' revenge and did not want it known. For such people had no protection and could at any time be killed by the bandits' fellows. After this case I gave the informer less than a tenth of the value of the boat's ownerless cargo, and he was well satisfied. By custom the goods on the boat went half to the case's chief officer and the other half to the relevant handling personnel to divide. Nobody would trouble himself over the families of the murdered "victims."

In my years in Shanghai I also met some very thorny great cases, such as when the Shanghai General Post Office was robbed of more than forty thousand yuan in cash. That case stirred the whole country at the time, and I and many detectives were ordered to crack it within a set term. Though it happened on North Sichuan Road in the International Settlement, the General Post Office was not within the range of the Settlement's police authority. There were four or five robbers; taking their chance as the post office was remitting cash to a bank, they snatched several mailbags holding more than forty thousand yuan, struck a doorkeeper dead on the spot, and fled in a stolen taxi. We later found the car abandoned in the western outskirts of Shanghai: the bandits had first hired a car, driven it to the outskirts, then detained and bound the driver in a small thatched hut with one man guarding him, and after the job drove the car back there, told the guard to flee, and abandoned it.

After studying the case with many others, I concluded that the doorkeeper struck dead was an inside man: when he had pointed out to the bandits the mailbags full of cash and was about to board the car and flee with them, the alarm bell and police whistle screamed wildly; the bandits, unwilling to stop the car and afraid that leaving him behind he would give up the others, fired two shots and killed him. We ascertained that this doorkeeper's native place was Yancheng in Subei, and that the cartridge cases the bandits left were from Mauser pistols, and so inferred the case was the work of Subei-gang bandits. For the Shanghai gang's bandits generally favoured small pistols and not the Mauser. This was because in a great city a small pistol was easier to carry and conceal in a job, whereas a job in the provinces called for long range and great power, so the Mauser was mostly preferred.

Ordered to take three veteran detectives to Subei to investigate, I made contact with the gangs from the moment we boarded the boat. The veteran detectives had a whole method: they could find a gang connection the instant they boarded a boat, and it was the same on putting up at an inn in some small place. Their most striking trick was to set a suitcase and the like down on the ground, pull out handcuffs, lock one end to the luggage and the other to something fixed. After locking it thus, they would walk off without a care, and in a moment someone would come to keep an eye on it, and greet them of his own accord on their return. Locking their inn-room door too they used handcuffs, so that all along the way they could make contact with the "local snakes" everywhere. At the time I was quite worried that going about a case so openly would give away our identity, but they were entirely unconcerned. After a month and more, having gone round several Subei counties, we did find a lead and cracked the case, recovering more than thirty thousand yuan. This was mainly through leads supplied by the gang members of the various counties. For these robbers, having snatched a great sum of tens of thousands and returned to their home villages, had spent it wildly and drawn people's notice; and so, told and retold, the leads let us find the bandits concerned. During my time as inspectorate head at the Changde Garrison Headquarters and the Dongting Lake Garrison Headquarters, there were very few great robberies in the Changde city district, while cases large and small kept occurring in the surrounding villages and nearby market towns. Over three years I kept the city district free of big cases only by relying on the head of the inspectorate's first section, Hu Baowen, who was in league with the habitual bandits. This section head Hu Baowen, whom people called "Brother Bao," made a specialty of harbouring bandits and sitting to split the loot. The habitual bandits who robbed the villages and towns often stayed at his house after a job. Once I understood this, I played deaf and dumb and did not expose him, for so long as nothing happened inside the city I could get by. Once the family of a Ministry of Military Administration official, travelling by wooden boat, was robbed near Taoyuan of much gold and valuables, and the Ministry sternly ordered the case cracked within a set term. Hearing that these robbers all stayed at Hu Baowen's, and the case being grave and the victims a Ministry official's family — not to be fobbed off as one might ordinary merchants and townsfolk — I resolved to crack it. One night I went in person with men to Hu's house, and came upon Hu and several others feasting. The moment he saw me he knew word had leaked, and put terms to me, pleading for the bandits: he promised to hand over two men and return part of the loot. After much thought, in hopes of nothing happening inside the city thereafter, I could only agree not to touch the ringleaders, and to have them hand over two accessories to take the fall. The two "bandits" they handed over, on my questioning, turned out to be two peasants who, without clothes or food, had been hired to carry the loot, each getting only ten yuan, and were now made "scapegoats" and put to death; and so the case was reckoned cracked. Cases of this kind, cracked by detectives in partnership with bandits, that I handled myself or heard of, are many more, and I shall not cite further. Detectives also adopted some so-called "scientific methods" from the West, setting up in many detective organs fingerprint, forensic-medicine, police-dog, physico-chemical, testing and other units to help in cracking cases. But these things learned from abroad often changed with every transfer of the man in charge. Those who had studied in Germany urged the German method; those who had studied in Italy, Japan or America urged the set they themselves had learned; and even on whether a single fingerprint should be classed by the Henry-Edward or the Battley system there was never any agreement, each place running its own. Many old detectives had no interest whatever in these newfangled toys. Though I too once championed adopting foreign methods, in fact, doing this work for some ten years and cracking cases by the hundred, I very seldom cracked one by these methods; it was mainly the old set of experience and countless informers I relied on.

In the past detectives, in handling cases, relied very little on fingerprints. This was because ordinary people lacked all sense of protecting a scene: every time a murder or great theft occurred, before ever the detectives arrived, the victim's family and the gawkers had thrown the scene into confusion, often smearing a left-behind weapon with fresh fingerprints and destroying the culprit's. To hunt the culprit by such prints was unreliable nine times in ten. Add that the reactionary government's public-security organs had built up no systematic fingerprint apparatus, nor any large body of data to check against, and it amounted to a mere show.

The most laughable thing was that after the victory, the so-called scientific criminal-investigation equipment America presented to help the reactionaries suppress the Chinese people's revolutionary movement proved, on trial, fit only to be an ornament. I saw with my own eyes the American interrogation "lie-detector" used to question suspected thieves: of a dozen and more, not one was got right. Whereas by the old detectives' method of interrogation, one had only to throw a few words of argot in a habitual thief's face and he would at once pour it all out — I have had such experience myself. The first time, questioning a thief by the ordinary interrogation procedure, I got nothing beyond his name, age and native place, and could get nothing more however I tried. Then an old detective watching by the side went over to him, lifted one of his ears, put a few questions in rivers-and-lakes argot, and he at once changed his manner and confessed honestly.

I once handled some criminal cases jointly with the detective-department head Rao Boze and detective-chief Pulaixi of the administrative-affairs bureau of the Shanghai French Concession, both noted old detectives of Paris. In questioning a suspect they would begin in their old French way, putting questions and rapidly typing the gist of the exchange on a typewriter. In the end, when they could get no further, they would as usual stretch and, impatient, hand the prisoner to their Chinese subordinates for torture. After a beating, once the prisoner had given up the facts, they would carry on questioning.

The reactionary government's detective organs at every level, some long established, still used, right up to the liberation of the whole country, that same decayed old method. In the old society, "he who steals a hook is put to death, he who steals a state is made a lord." Detectives dealt with those they judged to have broken the law and statutes just as they pleased, and no one would question it. The reactionary government had also, with false benevolence and sham righteousness, issued grand and solemn decrees forbidding the use of corporal torture on prisoners, and the courts would not accept a prisoner beaten till his flesh was split. Some who had been tortured, on being sent to court, would charge the detectives with extorting confession by torture, and the court would, as a matter of form, summon the accused detectives for questioning. In Shanghai I was constantly summoned for having injured prisoners; but this was wholly a matter of official paperwork — summon me they might, the torture went on as before, for going to court only meant being told one must not use corporal punishment, and never once was a detective detained in court over such things. On the contrary, when the court met a grave case it could not manage, it would, under the name of "referral to the detective organ for investigation," hand it to the detectives to use torture and force confession. In 1947, when Sun Ke's mansion at No. 22 Columbia Road in Shanghai was burgled, the case was cracked by the seventh brigade captain of the Shanghai Garrison Headquarters inspectorate, Xiao Fuquan, and the thief, once sent to court, retracted his confession. As it touched a burglary at the great president's own mansion, the court could not but handle it seriously, to please the president. Unable to apply torture itself, it referred the case back to the original unit for review. Xiao Fuquan gave the recanting thief another heavy beating, and when he was sent to court again, sure enough he retracted no more. Before liberation, the use of corporal punishment on prisoners by public-security organs was a commonplace, taken for granted, and reckoned an open "official torture." In fact, beyond these official tortures there were many inhuman private tortures the detectives used in secret. Detectives who had never known what human rights meant could, toward bandits whom they reckoned several grades below ordinary people, do all the more as they pleased. Before the war, in Shanghai, I caught a habitual thief who specialized in stealing the luggage of first-class sleeper passengers on the Nanking-Shanghai railway. Each time he did a job he gave us a headache, for the first-class sleeper passengers were mostly dignitaries of the Nanking government, and these people, however small the thing they lost, made a great to-do and insisted the case be cracked. We caught this man and sent him to serve a few months in court; released, he took up his old trade again. Once, when my assistant Chen Jinbiao and I caught him a third time (he had stolen, near the Nanxiang railway station, the leather case of a Central Committee member up in Shanghai for the weekend), I discussed it with Chen Jinbiao and decided this time not to send him to court, nor take him back to the office, but to take him to the outskirts and deal with him properly.

We sat in a small teahouse until after dark, then took him to the edge of a lonely wheat field. He thought we meant to kill him and knelt begging us to spare his life. Chen Jinbiao pinned him to the ground and asked whether he wanted to die or live. Live, of course; whereupon Chen and I put it to him that to live, he must never eat of this trade again. He agreed, thinking he was safe — and the upshot was that we severed one of the tendons of his foot with a sharp little knife. When he had fainted from the pain, we undid the handcuffs and left him there. This man, crippled for life, we never saw again; yet the theft of first-class sleeper passengers' luggage on the Nanking-Shanghai line went on just as often as before, and our use of such savage private torture did not make the dignitaries stop losing their things.

During the war, not long after the Juntong chief Dai Li had a fountain pen picked from him on a Chungking street, the American secret-service chief Bei Leli, SACO's chief of staff, also had a small wallet lifted from his trouser pocket at the door of the Victory Building. By his own account, the wallet was even chained to his waistband, which added to the case's air of mystery. Getting Bei Leli's notice, Dai Li naturally flew into a rage again, feeling he had lost face — that an insolent pickpocket should dare lay hands on his master, how could it be borne! That very evening Dai Li sent for me, wrote out an order to crack the case in three days, and had me deliver it in person to the detective-corps captain Li Lianfu. Getting this order and hearing what I told him, Li, like me, judged it the work of a highly skilled old hand from out of town and no easy lead to find.

Three days passed; nearly a hundred suspects were taken, but still not the least lead. Dai Li, telephoning to scold Li Lianfu, sent me to the detective corps to press them. Half a month later a corps man caught a major suspect at a gambling den, and and found on him five American hundred-dollar bills, close to the six hundred-dollar notes Bei Leli had lost. After a bout of severe torture, this man gave up that the American notes had been bought from another man. Tracing it round, we at last found the man who had sold the six hundred-dollar notes, and recovered the leather case he had thrown into a ditch and the ivory seal carved with a Chinese name that Dai Li had given Bei Leli. Li Lianfu ground his teeth in hatred of this thief, saying he would surely make him know Li's fierceness.

Hearing this, I later asked Li privately what fierceness he had left. He told me that before the war, as detective-squad captain in Peiping, he had a way of subduing such habitual thieves. He said that after these habitual thieves had served their term and been released, he would seize them and apply private torture, or use private torture on them without sending them to court for sentence at all. These private tortures, Li Lianfu told me, in the lighter form were to score one or two cuts on the habitual thief's face with a sharp blade — impossible to hide even when healed, so that the detectives could watch or arrest them at any time; in the heavier form, to maim a limb and make him a cripple. To gather such material I afterwards asked him how he had dealt with the man who stole Bei Leli's wallet. He told me he had taken two men in person and smashed the knuckles of the thief's right middle and index fingers with a stone, so that he could never eat of this trade again. For a pickpocket depends chiefly on these two fingers to steal, and once they are crippled, even the most skilled habitual thief can no longer ply his tricks.

Such savage private torture existed everywhere in those days. I once asked Xu Zhongwu, detective-squad captain at Lanzhou (later Chungking detective-corps captain), and learned that he too had caught outside habitual thieves who belonged to no gang and cut off a piece of the upper or lower part of the ear to mark them. Chen Zhiqiang, as detective-squad captain in Xi'an, had once blinded with lime both eyes of a habitual thief who specialized in stealing from great officials and magnates. Captain Qiu of the Guiyang detective squad and the Kunming criminal-police captain Zhou Baixian, was also fond of private torture and had crippled some disobedient habitual thieves. These men, telling me of such atrocities, were still very pleased with themselves and reckoned it the best of methods. Yet for all that, in that dark old society the valuables of the great and powerful were still constantly stolen, and neither the official nor the secret tortures made men stop taking desperate courses.

To seek wealth by taking life is a crime men deeply loathe and all demand be brought to justice. But detectives would again and again use their authority to kill for gain at will. When they caught bandits whose loot was very valuable, in order to swallow the goods themselves they often used inhuman means to kill privately bandits whose crimes did not warrant death, tagging on a charge of "resisting arrest with a weapon, shot dead on the spot" or "killed by mischance in pursuit" to close the case. Before the war, in Shanghai, I was often with the Shanghai public-security bureau's detective-corps commander Lu Ying and several of his capable subordinates — Dong Deming, Shen Wenda, You Hanwen, Wu Kezhong, Li Dibao and the rest — and they told me of this experience of killing. The Juntong agents, though they did not use these methods on the bandits, did use what they learned of them to kill Communist underground workers and democratic figures (the particulars are written in my piece "The Juntong Agents' Criminal Activities in Shanghai before the War").

The old society's detectives used their authority to prey on the people, and the harm they did was truly appalling. In the ten years and more I did this work as a sideline, the crimes of these men that I saw and heard of are past counting; I select a few to relate briefly.

Before the war, in Shanghai, I knew an old detective of the French Concession named Gu Jisheng. After his death, when his concubine and adopted son went to law over the inheritance, it came out that he had piled up more than four hundred thousand yuan in property. When the newspapers published this, many were astonished. This detective on a monthly salary of under sixty yuan had, in twenty or thirty years, come by so much money.

It turned out that, besides the money-making means detectives commonly used — protecting prostitution, gambling and drugs, sitting to split loot, and blackmail and extortion — he often used framing to ruin many people utterly. He kept a number of thieves; when he had his eye on some rich family or shop, he would first have someone sell them fine goods cheap, and a few days later bring his kept thief, handcuffed, straight to that house to "recover the loot." Since these families or shops, greedy for a bargain, had bought the thief's goods, the detective on arrival could of course find these things, and would then flatly charge the family with being a "nest-master" harbouring thieves' loot. In the old society cases were tried without regard to right and wrong — "the thief's one bite sinks three tenths into the bone" — and with witnesses and material evidence besides, one had a mouth but could not clear oneself.

In his blackmail and extortion, Gu Jisheng chose especially as targets those with few social ties in Shanghai. For instance, when the owner of a grocery on Rue du Consulat had just died and his widow carried on the business, Gu got a shop assistant to help, and had the habitual thieves he kept sell her black goods several times. When all was arranged, Gu brought men to search, took the widow away, and in the end drove her to madness and slowly made the shop his own. Or again: an old couple who had earlier been a county magistrate in some province, squeezing the people's blood and sweat, and had later come to Shanghai to live as idle rentiers, bought a small Western-style house at the end of Rue Lafayette. Gu took advantage of their furnishing the house to have someone sell the old man some cheap things, among them a small safe. Gu then, in league with a French detective-chief, arrested the old man, flatly claiming the safe had been stolen by him from somewhere, that it had held so many valuables which he had embezzled, and that he must pay them back before he could be released. This old man, without kith or kin in Shanghai, was in the end squeezed until all the ill-gotten money he had scraped together was cleaned out of him, and he left Shanghai helpless. Gu, in league with the French Concession patrol-house, did this kind of thing exclusively, and in under ten years owned a dozen and more pieces of real estate, going on later to trade in land. Had his adopted son and concubine not taken the inheritance dispute to court, this inside story would not have come to light. In fact, in the old society, fierce and venomous men like Gu Jisheng were more than one.

As for the detectives' contempt for human rights, beating innocent people at will and applying torture as they pleased to those they judged suspects — that was reckoned all the more "lawful." Because I long served as an instructor in action technique at the Juntong's agent training classes, I gathered material of every kind of corporal torture in order to teach the interrogation part of action technique. Later the Juntong agents took many robust "criminals" as test subjects and carried out all sorts of experiments, to see just how many kinds of torture a man could bear and how long each could be applied to the body. The agents observed closely and recorded it in detail as teaching material. This inhuman atrocity was worse than the beasts: beasts eat men, but do not know how to torment them.

While I was deputy head and chief inspector of the Chungking inspectorate, I went one day to inspect the work at the Chaotianmen water-inspection station, and saw the inspectors had caught a so-called suspect charged with stealing army rice. He was a very robust dock porter, and only some army rice swept up off the ground was found in his pocket; in the ordinary way it would have been no more than a scolding or a few slaps and having him return the rice he had picked up, and that would have been the end of it. The inspectors put him on a car and took him back to the inspectorate, and under the name of thoroughly tracing the cause of the army rice long gone missing, had several inspectors who specialized in torture put him to the question, while I watched from the side. The inspectors first bound his hands behind him and hung him up by the two thumbs, so that his body left the ground and his whole weight hung on the two fingers. After four hours and more the man fainted, and was brought round with cold water. After that the detectives went on to use the tiger bench, the plank-treading and seven or eight kinds of torture in all, each duly recorded. This was mainly to "experiment," not to trace anything, so however he cried out and confessed wildly they kept applying one kind of torture after another. Later, seeing he truly could bear no more, from the things he had confessed at random they arrested three men, and went on using these three for three days of experiment before ending the atrocity.

When I was Chungking detective-corps captain, the detectives constantly used many petty thieves as subjects for torture experiments. The students of the Juntong agent class too took these people as "living teaching material," practising the methods of torture and observing the effects, so as to use them later on captured Communists and revolutionaries. These thieves or suspects used as experiments, after suffering every kind of inhuman punishment, could only count themselves unlucky even if crippled; and if they wished to lodge a charge there was no door for it, for officials shielded officials. Should some rare one, unafraid, dare to do so, it would only bring a fatal disaster on him: the detectives could on any pretext arrest him again and apply still more venomous torture.

Before the war, in Shanghai, I knew a Chinese detective named Lang Zuyin at the Laozha patrol-house in the International Settlement, an exceedingly cruel man who beat people at the slightest thing. He habitually rode roughshod over the Concession on the strength of the foreigners' power, and once kicked a rickshaw puller half to death. This rickshaw puller went home and before long really died; before dying he told his wife she must avenge him. After his death his wife went to the Special District Court of the International Settlement to make a charge, and before the court had even accepted her plaint, Lang Zuyin with several toughs beat her. This unbending woman went to charge him again the next day and was again stopped. That evening Lang took men and threw her into the Huangpu River, meaning to drown her, but she was saved by a fishing boat. A third time she went to charge him, and this at last drew the court's notice: it summoned her to ascertain the case and summoned Lang to court to answer. The court summoned him three times, and each time Lang defied the summons and did not appear. Some people of conscience in society backed her, and in the end the court could only report it up to the Ministry of Justice to pass to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs to negotiate with the International Settlement. But when would the reactionary government's Foreign Ministry trouble the foreigners over such a small matter? In the end it was dragged out with no result.

During the war the inspectorate head of the Yuanling Garrison Headquarters, Huang Jiachi, was in league with the western-Hunan bandits, and a bandit chief gave him a saddle made of human skin. The skin, it was said, was that of an enemy of Huang's, whom Huang had had the bandits kill; to please Huang the bandits flayed the man and made the saddle. Had Huang not brought the thing out to show me, I would not have believed such cruelty could be.

As for the detectives who at will seized others' wives, daughters and property and framed them as thieves — these are more than can be listed. The Chungking inspectorate detective corps' Wu ××, taking a fancy to a driver's wife, arrested and framed the driver on a trumped-up charge and so achieved his aim of seizing the wife. The inspectorate's social-investigation section member Che Zhongguang, after killing the owner of a small biscuit factory in Ranjiaxiang, Chungking, seized his wife and property together. The detective-corps man Li Huchen, after killing the owner of a bathhouse, took the bathhouse for his own. Facts of this kind are truly too many, and I shall not list more. In sum, the reactionary government of the past, and the Concessions under imperialist rule, set up detective organs of every kind, and countless people suffered their poison; what I have written here is but a limited part.
