## An Outline of the Juntong

I have already given a fairly full account of the Juntong's origins and development, its structure and its personnel, in an article called "What I Know of Dai Li." That article first appeared in the twenty-second collection of *Selected Historical Materials*, published by the Committee for the Study of Historical Materials of the National Committee of the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference. In 1962 the Masses Press in Peking combined it with another piece of mine, "Inside the Bureau of Secrets Preservation," and issued the two together as a single volume for internal circulation; it went through two printings. In 1980 the Historical Materials Press combined "What I Know of Dai Li" with Comrade Wen Qiang's "Dai Li the Man" into one book, *Dai Li the Man*, which was put on public sale and also went through two printings. I had originally meant to include "What I Know of Dai Li" here as well, but since it has now been printed so many times I decided against adding it to *Inside the Juntong*. So that readers who have not seen that article may still have some idea of what sort of organization the Juntong was, I have added this chapter instead, in the hope that it will make the other chapters hang together when they come to them.

The full name of the Juntong was the Kuomintang's Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Military Affairs Commission of the National Government, abbreviated in Chinese to Juntong.

Its predecessor was the Special Services Department of the Chinese Renaissance Society — the Fuxingshe, which some people also called the Blue Shirts — the organization of Chiang Kai-shek's fascist ruling clique.

The Special Services Department in its turn grew out of the Confidential Investigation Group of the Military Affairs Commission. That group was got going by ten men: Dai Li, Zhang Yanyuan, Xu Liang, Ma Ce, Hu Tianqiu, Zheng Xilin and others. Inside the Juntong it was known as the Ten-Man Team.

The Renaissance Society was founded in the first days of March 1932; internally it was also called the Lixingshe. Chiang Kai-shek held the post of director concurrently. Under him was a central standing council of nine members: He Zhonghan, Feng Ti, Teng Jie, Zhou Fu, Kang Ze, Gui Yongqing, Pan Youqiang, Zheng Jiemin and Qiu Kaiji. There were three alternates: Hou Zhiming, Zhao Fansheng and Dai Li.

The society had four departments — organization, propaganda, training and special services. Dai Li was head of the Special Services Department and Zheng Jiemin his deputy. The department was established on 1 April 1932. It had a secretariat, with Tang Zong as secretary, later styled secretary-general, and below that four sections, headed at various times by Zhang Yanyuan, Xu Liang, Liang Ganqiao, Yang Jirong and Xu Yedao. By the time the department was set up the dozen-odd men of the Confidential Investigation Group had already grown to more than a hundred, and the number rose steadily afterwards to close on a thousand.

Because the Renaissance Society's Special Services Department was a secret organization, it was awkward for it to arrest people or kill them openly. So in September 1932 Chiang Kai-shek also appointed Dai Li to serve concurrently as head of the Second Department of the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Military Affairs Commission, which was run by the boss of the CC clique, Chen Lifu; this gave him a way of carrying on secret-service work in the open. That bureau of Chen Lifu's was a thorough hotchpotch. The head of its First Department, Xu Enzeng, was at the same time head of the Kuomintang's Party Affairs Investigation Department, and was likewise using the Military Affairs Commission's signboard as cover. It was not the same body as the Juntong that Dai Li later ran.

In July 1934, after Chiang Kai-shek had removed Deng Wenyi from the post of head of the Investigation Section of the Military Affairs Commission's Nanchang Field Headquarters, he ordered Dai Li to take that section over as well, and its agents, headquarters and field alike, were absorbed into Dai Li's Special Services Department.

After the war against Japan began, on 29 March 1938, the Kuomintang held an extraordinary national congress at Luojia Hill in Wuchang. By then the armies under Kuomintang command had already been driven back from Shanghai and Nanking, but Chiang Kai-shek still wanted to tighten his secret-police rule over the people. At that congress he proposed setting up an additional Bureau of Investigation and Statistics under the Kuomintang Central Executive Committee — the Zhongtong for short — with Xu Enzeng as its director, while the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Military Affairs Commission, which Chen Lifu had headed, was handed over to Dai Li entire. Dai Li's standing in the Kuomintang was too junior, however, and his Whampoa class was a late one — the sixth. Chiang wanted to promote him over the heads of others but was afraid his other subordinates would resent it, so he could only make him deputy director, carrying the real responsibility, while the head of the First Bureau of Chiang's own Personal Staff Office held the directorship concurrently. That is why He Yaozu, Qian Dajun and Lin Wei, each of whom served in turn as head of the First Bureau, were each also director of the Juntong. These men understood what Chiang Kai-shek intended, and they understood Dai Li's character even better, so they never once enquired into the Juntong's work, its personnel or its money. Dai Li handled the whole of it himself and answered directly to Chiang Kai-shek. On 1 April each year the Juntong held its "April the First Assembly," a celebration and working conference marking the founding of the Special Services Department in 1932; that was the one occasion on which the concurrent director came to make a speech and listen to Dai Li's report on the year's work. In his own mind Dai Li attached no importance whatever to this director, and the ordinary field agents did not so much as know that a director existed. They knew only that Dai Li was their one and only boss.

When Dai Li first took the Juntong over, Zheng Jiemin, who had been his deputy in the Special Services Department days, had to be found a place as the bureau's chief secretary, which came to the same thing as the secretary-general of the earlier period. The existing sections — intelligence, operations, judicial, telecommunications, general affairs and the rest — were turned into departments. After the withdrawal from Wuhan to Chungking the organization grew larger and larger. By 1942, when I was transferred to head the General Affairs Department, the one intelligence department had become two: the First Department handled military intelligence and the Second party and government intelligence; the Third remained the operations department and the Fourth telecommunications; the Fifth had been expanded out of the judicial section, the Sixth converted from the personnel office, the accounting office had become the Seventh, and the general affairs section had been enlarged into the Eighth.

Besides these eight great departments, the training section soon became a training department as well, the police section of the operations department turned into a police affairs department, and several units responsible for planting work in the occupied areas were merged into a deployment department.

On top of that there was a planning committee, a disciplinary committee, an assessment committee and a committee for inducing defections; and just after the victory over Japan a committee for the liquidation of property was set up too.

Bodies ranking equal with the departments were these: the Special Political Problems Research Office, run by Zhang Guotao; a Special Technical Research Office, which existed to study killing, arson, sabotage, the manufacture of poisons and the like; an economic research office expanded out of the economic intelligence section; and a Zhongshan Room in charge of recreation.

Later on even the cipher section was raised to the status of a confidential group and the documents section became a documents group, while the armed agents went from being a brigade to a corps and then to a regiment, and the motor pool became a brigade.

The long and short of it is that Dai Li believed the more agents the better and the bigger the organization the better. There were more than forty training classes for agents under one name or another. The headquarters staff alone numbered over fourteen hundred, all of them concentrated at the Juntong's main headquarters at Luojiawan in Chungking and at the country office at Ciqikou. At its largest the service came to nearly fifty thousand men, headquarters and field together. Besides the districts, stations and groups inside China, and the demolition, sabotage and action corps and brigades, it maintained stations, groups and correspondents in a great many places abroad.

Every field agent in those days had anywhere from a few to a few dozen people he was running, and once all of those are counted in the number becomes very large indeed.

On 17 March 1946 the aircraft Dai Li was travelling in flew into Dai Hill, at Banqiao in Jiangning county near Nanking, and every person aboard was killed in the crash. Chiang Kai-shek then appointed Zheng Jiemin director of the Juntong and Mao Renfeng deputy director. Since the number of Juntong agents had grown far beyond the authorized establishment, and its outgoings exceeded the funds allotted to it by heaven knows how many times over, both Zheng Jiemin and Mao Renfeng took the view that an organization of that size could not be kept up, and decided to cut it back and lay men off. It was then that Dai Li's old rule — men might come in but never go out — was finally broken.

In the summer of 1946 the Kuomintang's Military Affairs Commission was reorganized into the Ministry of National Defence, and the Juntong's stinking signboard was changed with it to the Bureau of Secrets Preservation of the Ministry of National Defence. There is a chapter in this book devoted to that bureau, so I shall say no more about it here.
