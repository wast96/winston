## The Inside Story of Chiang Kai-shek's Plot to Assassinate Li Zongren

In November 1948 I was serving in Kunming as chief of the Yunnan provincial station of the Ministry of National Defence's Bureau of Secrets Preservation, when I suddenly received an urgent wire from the director, Mao Renfeng, telling me to come to Nanking at once; I flew there the next afternoon. I had scarcely moved into the Bureau's senior guest house when Mao Renfeng came to find me. He told me that this time I had been called to Nanking because Chiang Kai-shek had personally designated me to take charge of the work of assassinating Li Zongren. He said the task had originally been assigned to Ye Xiangzhi, head of the Bureau's action department, which specialized in assassinations and the like, who had already made some of the arrangements; but when Chiang Kai-shek heard that Ye had come up as a man of letters, able only to wield a pen and not to kill with his own hands, he said several times over that this would not do, and specified that the job must be led by me — an old hand who had done action work in the Juntong for many years. Mao Renfeng enjoined me that when I saw Chiang I must give my assurance that I could carry out the mission.

When Mao Renfeng had finished, he rode with me to Chiang's residence inside the Central Military Officers' Academy to request an audience. We sat in silence in the reception room for better than two hours before Chiang found a moment to receive us in his office. This time I saw that his manner was exceedingly gentle, and he even put on a show of great warmth, asking after conditions in Yunnan and after my family. At the last he asked whether Director Mao had already told me the task that was bringing me to Nanking. I said he had. He then said that his decision to have me take charge of this work was because it bore on the whole general situation, and he declared his trust in me. He boasted, too, that the Communists could be beaten sooner or later, but that the disorder within was harder to deal with than the Communists, so that this was the only method left, a way to unify the inside and turn it as one against the outside. He said as well that the Communists had only one enemy, which was why they could win their battles, whereas we had two enemies, or three, several quarters to cope with at once, so that our difficulties were far the greater. He said again and again that this operation was a great matter bearing on the safety of the party and the state, that I must on no account let it leak, and must make my arrangements with all speed and only wait on his final decision, whereupon I was to carry out the mission without fail. He cited certain assassins out of history to spur me on, and praised me for having risked my life for him over so many long years and, after the victory, for having volunteered to go into the liberated areas at my own peril to search for Dai Li's crashed plane. From his tone, and from his detailed questions about my family, he told me quite plainly that to carry out this task he was now handing me, he would not grudge even the sacrifice of my life.

In the better part of an hour we talked, Chiang Kai-shek never once named the target he wanted me to kill: this was a device he always used. The Juntong bureau had killed so many people for him, and yet we had never once seen him put a single line in writing to the Juntong specifying whom to kill, so that all our talk went by tacit understanding. As I took my leave, to please him, I gave my firm assurance that I would never fail his expectations, that I would count no sacrifice too great, that I would think of every possible means to carry out the task he had personally given me. He was very pleased to hear it, and gripping my hand said to Mao Renfeng: "This is the most loyal and brave of our good comrades. If he meets any difficulty in his work or his life, you must do everything you can to help him solve it."

The next morning I went to the Bureau, where Mao Renfeng gathered just four of us for a secret consultation — the director's office chief, Pan Qiwu, Ye Xiangzhi, and myself — not even letting the deputy director, Xu Zhidao, take part. It was decided that the unit charged with the task should be called the "Special Action Group," and that the method should fall into two parts: one body of men to carry out the assassination, another to keep Li Zongren under watch and prevent his leaving Nanking. We also worked out how to deal with several other Guangxi-clique chiefs, the dispositions to be arranged by Ye Xiangzhi and me in consultation. Mao Renfeng further gave personal orders to the management department and the personnel department that whenever the Special Action Group wanted money or men, its needs were to be met as fully as possible.

A week later, we had settled on the following concrete dispositions. The work of assassinating Li Zongren would be led by me, with Qin Jingchuan and Wang ×× chosen by Mao Renfeng as my assistants. Qin had always done killing work in the Juntong; his marksmanship was accurate and he was very cool. Wang was a hardened bandit from the Northeast who had killed and robbed since boyhood and could bring down a bird on the wing with a pistol. I too had long been a noted crack shot in the Juntong. Li Zongren was then living behind Fuhougang; his car took the turn very slowly going in and out, and could be fired on from both sides at once. To watch his movements, we opened a second-hand bookstall at a corner of the road near his residence, which not only gave cover for surveillance but let us keep several men loitering there, using the pretext of browsing to stay longer and observe without easily being noticed. This post I filled with Wu Dehou, an agent I picked and transferred, a graduate of the Juntong's Linli special training class in Hunan, who had always been in action work.

To guard against Li Zongren's leaving Nanking suddenly, we prepared to shoot his plane down in the air, so that we could accomplish the killing under cover of an "air crash." This was the best plan; so we opened a small general store as cover on a lane outside Guanghua Gate leading to the airfield, and specially installed a telephone, so that the moment Li Zongren was found heading for the field, we could report it by phone in a prearranged code, and Mao Renfeng would notify the two fighter planes kept ready at all times to shadow Li's aircraft: the instant it left the sky over Nanking, they would fire and bring it down with all aboard. The Guangxi armies were then concentrated for the most part around Anhui, so we also bought a wooden house near the railway station and set out a cigarette stand there, ready, should Li Zongren flee by train, to rush out and ambush him at one of the small stations where the train stopped along the way. We reckoned Li might also go to Hangchow for a little pleasure, so we opened a small eating house on the highway near Tangshan; if Li left the capital by car, we would give chase with the two high-speed cars Mao Renfeng had allotted the Special Action Group and ambush him on the road. According to what Mao Renfeng told me, Chiang held that if Li Zongren left Nanking suddenly behind his back, it must be to muster troops and carry out an "armed forcing of the palace"; so an assassination anywhere outside Nanking need not wait on any further order from him — only if we acted inside the city of Nanking must we wait on his final decision. In Chiang's own words: if Li left without taking leave, he was seeking his own death.

The most tense period for this Special Action Group was the middle of January 1949, when Chiang Kai-shek was weighing whether to retire for the time being and let Li Zongren act in his place while he waited on American aid, or to have Zhang Qun go to Japan to recruit mercenaries, or else simply to finish Li Zongren off and hold on by leaning on the natural barrier of the Yangtze. Through that stretch of time Mao Renfeng went to see Chiang Kai-shek every day, and every day enjoined me not to leave, to keep everything ready at all times, and to wait only on a single word from Chiang, upon which, day or night, I was to act at once. If Li did not go out, we would go to his residence to ambush him there. Mao Renfeng also had two agents in the Nanking Electric Light Company assist: on the pretext of overhauling a transformer, they would stand on the transformer and fire a hand-held machine gun from outside the wall into Li's bedroom, dining room and the like, and make ready as well to climb the wall and ambush him inside the grounds. The bullets the marksmen used had the most violent poison packed inside the heads, so that a hit anywhere on the body would bring on blood poisoning past all saving. In the second-hand bookstall we had set up, a Thompson submachine gun and several grenades were also kept ready. The Special Action Group brought in several more agents habituated to the killing trade, arranging a double layer, ready after killing Li to finish off in one stroke the several Guangxi-clique chiefs in Nanking. So through those one or two months we were kept the whole time in a state of tension.

On January 20, 1949, Mao Renfeng sent for me and told me to wind up the group's work at once, hand all the men over to the personnel department to be placed elsewhere, and bring only three — Wu Dehou, Qin Jingchuan and Wang ×× — with me to Kunming, to be ready to assassinate for Chiang whomever he next wanted killed. In September of that year, when Mao Renfeng stayed at my house in Kunming, he brought this affair up more than once. He said Chiang Kai-shek often regretted not having acted at that time, leaving alive this root of the trouble that plagued him. This inside story of Chiang Kai-shek's plot to assassinate Li Zongren I once set down in a brief manuscript, published in the *Selected Historical Materials*, thirty-second collection. In 1965, when Mr. and Mrs. Li Zongren came back from America and saw this piece, Comrade Yin Bingyan of the Central Committee of the Revolutionary Committee of the Kuomintang invited me to lunch at their residence in Shijia Hutong. The moment Mr. Li saw me he said that it was only on reading the inside story I had written that the truth had dawned on him: so that was how it had been. At the time he had merely felt that someone was paying close attention to his movements in Nanking; never in his wildest thoughts had it occurred to him that Chiang Kai-shek would want to assassinate him. He said that he and Chiang Kai-shek were sworn brothers, who met with the greatest warmth. Mrs. Li, Guo Dejie, added one thing in particular. She said that as soon as Li Zongren reached Nanking, Chiang Kai-shek and his wife invited the two of them to dinner, and Chiang Kai-shek made a point of asking whether Li's aircraft was well fitted out. Li answered that it was an ordinary passenger plane kept for his private use. Then Song Meiling and Chiang Kai-shek played off against each other: Song asked, as if idly, whether the "Meiling" — Chiang's own plane — might not be given to the Lis, and Chiang said with a smile, "If you hadn't brought it up, I'd have forgotten. We have the 'Zhongmei,' which serves well enough." Song then described to Guo Dejie how comfortable the fittings of the "Meiling" were, and pressed the plane on them for their use. Guo took a great interest in the "Meiling" then, and several times urged Li Zongren to fly it to Shanghai or Hangchow for a little pleasure, but Li was too busy and never went. "We truly never guessed," Guo Dejie said, "that the plane they were giving us was meant to take our very lives. Had we boarded it, the fighters you had ready would have chased us down and fired, and we should never have known what was happening! The two of them are beasts in human form." And Li Zongren said too: "Coming back to Peking this time, I could never get used to hearing people call Chiang Kai-shek 'Chiang the bandit,' 'Chiang the brigand'; I still called him Mr. Chiang. But from this business I see that this sworn brother of mine is indeed no better than a beast, and I too am willing now to call him Chiang the bandit."
