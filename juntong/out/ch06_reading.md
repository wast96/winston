## General Zhang Xueliang under Detention

After the Xi'an Incident, Chiang Kai-shek was released and returned to Nanking, where he broke faith at once and repudiated on the spot every promise he had made at Xi'an. He hated the two generals who had launched the incident, Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng, to the marrow of his bones, and would have liked nothing better than to see them dead; but before the eyes of the whole nation it was awkward to move against them immediately, and he had no choice but to swallow his hatred for the time being. Beyond forcing General Yang Hucheng to go abroad, which removed that thorn from his eye for the moment, he had General Zhang Xueliang publicly sentenced to ten years' imprisonment on the charge of "coercing the supreme commander," and turned him over to the Juntong secret-service chief Dai Li to be kept under guard.

Of the first period after Zhang Xueliang was imprisoned I know little in detail; I know only that before the War of Resistance broke out, General Zhang was held at Xuedou Temple in Fenghua county, Chiang Kai-shek's home district. Dai Li sent Liu Yiguang, captain of a Juntong secret-agent detachment, with his deputy captain Xu Jianye and more than thirty agents, to handle the inner guard, while Chiang Kai-shek assigned a company of military police besides to the outer cordon; the two forces worked in concert and also checked each other, as insurance against anything unforeseen.

Only on the eve of the war did General Zhang leave Fenghua, to be held for a time at Nanchang, then at Taohuaping in Yiyang, Hunan, and at Fenghuangshan in Yuanling, Hunan, until in 1939 he was moved to Yangming Cave in Xiuwen county, Guizhou. The place is not far from Xuantian Cave at Xifeng, where General Yang Hucheng was imprisoned; the counties adjoin. To ease the guard work, Dai Li went so far as to recommend the senior Juntong agent Li Zhongzhen for the post of Xiuwen county magistrate. Dai himself always found time to visit once a year or so, personally directing the disposition of the inner and outer cordons. He laid it down that by day the agents of the inner guard should stand posted in a ring some ten zhang from General Zhang's quarters, moving at night to the bedroom window and the door; the military police outside stood farther off by day, and at night moved up to the line the agents had held in daylight. On the roads leading toward Yangming Cave he posted further sentries, and passers-by were not allowed near the place.

In those days Dai Li looked after General Zhang far better than General Yang where daily life was concerned. For one thing, he remembered that at the time of the Xi'an Incident, when he had gone to Xi'an with T. V. Soong and the others, Zhang had not made things difficult for him, and had released him together with Chiang Kai-shek; for another, Zhang and T. V. Soong were close personal friends, and in many matters Soong's connection was taken into account, so that in his daily needs Zhang was treated with comparative indulgence.

Around 1942 the Nationalist Ordnance Administration built an arsenal inside a great natural cavern in Tongzi county, Guizhou. The works covered a wide area, and behind them lay a reservoir of nearly a hundred mu that fed the arsenal's power generation. In imitation of West Lake the arsenal had laid out its own Three Pools Mirroring the Moon in the water, with peach trees and willows alternating along the banks. The country around the cavern was naturally very fine; more to the point, security there was exceptionally strict, and communications were easier than at Yangming Cave in Xiuwen — a car could drive straight in, with no long stretch of mountain path to walk after leaving it, as there was at Xiuwen. Dai Li judged this the most suitable place of all to confine General Zhang, and with Chiang Kai-shek's approval obtained, he asked Yu Dawei, the director of the Ordnance Administration, for a section of the reservoir grounds and built there a single-storey house of seven rooms in a row, as quarters for General Zhang and for the captain of the agent detachment. The three rooms on the left were General Zhang's: entered from the middle, three connecting rooms, of which the southernmost was the bedroom, the middle one the study, and the outermost the sitting room. The three rooms on the right were Liu Yiguang's office and his family's lodging, and the middle room of the row served for meals. To the left, the right and the rear of this house were built quarters for the plain-clothes guards and the military police, and a number of sentry posts.

After General Zhang moved there he stayed until the second half of 1946. During those years, since I was director of the General Affairs Department at the Juntong bureau, Dai Li regularly sent me to deliver things to General Zhang, so I know this period rather better. His companions at that time were his wife Yu Fengzhi and Zhao Yidi — that is, Miss Zhao the Fourth, whom for years everyone simply called Fourth Miss. The two women took turns, relieving each other once a year, the one going off duty leaving for America to rest. After Yu Fengzhi went to the United States in 1943 she did not come back, and Fourth Miss stayed on with him from then on.

Originally there had also been an aide who kept General Zhang company, whose name I have forgotten. The man had been with Zhang for many years, and from the moment Zhang was imprisoned his own freedom of movement went too. Once he insisted on leaving, and Zhang consented. He picked up his luggage and genuinely meant to go, but he had not got far before Liu Yiguang, the detachment captain, went after him with agents and placed him under arrest, wiring Dai Li at once for instructions; the upshot was that he was sent to the Juntong prison at Xifeng and locked up, and for fear he would leak what he knew, he was never released. Later, when the Xifeng prison was wound up, he was sent with a group of political prisoners to Baigongguan in Chungking and held there. Just before the liberation of Chungking, in the massacre at the SACO site of November 27 — the case, conducted by Mao Renfeng in Chungking, that shocked the world — this man was killed along with the rest.

There was besides a woman servant of General Zhang's who followed him everywhere and could not bear to leave him. This old grandmother often told us that the Old Marshal himself had charged her to take good care of the Young Marshal, and that wherever Zhang went, she had to go too. There was also an old family retainer who had looked after General Zhang's daily life for decades, and who loved to sit with the old woman telling us stories of General Zhang's boyhood. Once Zhang stole up quietly behind him and burst out laughing as he listened; the old man instantly stood to respectful attention and dared not go on. At such moments Zhang would often squeeze in beside us on the bench, sit down, and press him to continue: "You like hearing it, and I like hearing it too. Go on!"

The captain guarding General Zhang, Liu Yiguang, was never once replaced. A Hunanese, a graduate of the sixth Whampoa class, he watched over Zhang for more than ten years, and while remaining captain of the detachment rose in rank from major all the way to major general. The deputy captain, Xu Jianye, was from Jiangxi, and was transferred away not long after the war began; no deputy captain was ever appointed again. Apart from its captain the detachment had one business clerk, one accountant, and a student of Tongji Medical College who had never finished his course and served as Zhang's doctor. The detachment was equipped with a small wireless station, whose single operator doubled on cipher work, and with one small motor-car and a driver. Though the agents themselves were often rotated, their number was always kept at about thirty, on lieutenant-grade terms, with only the three squad leaders ranking as field-grade officers. Guard duty ran in three shifts of eight hours each, turning over day and night.

Throughout General Zhang's imprisonment, Dai Li made a point of visiting him once or twice a year, always bringing daily necessities and food to give him. In addition, Chiang Kai-shek had Mo Dehui, a Manchurian-born member of the People's Political Council, visit Zhang two or three times. Whenever Mo went, it was always Chiang Kai-shek who notified Dai Li, and Dai sent his personnel director Li Xiaobai (Li was later transferred to head the Special Inspection Department of the Military Affairs Commission Secretariat) to accompany him; each visit lasted two or three days before they returned.

With the agents who guarded him General Zhang never once spoke of the Xi'an Incident, and no one felt it proper to ask him. Nor did he ever grumble in front of the agents or say anything against Chiang Kai-shek, for he was intelligent and alert, and understood that the men guarding him carried a double commission: to guard him, and to learn what was moving in his mind. He knew that a careless word to such men could only deepen Chiang Kai-shek's hatred of him and make his release still less likely. But whenever Chiang Kai-shek sent Mo Dehui down to see him, then he would pour out some of what he kept hidden, often talking on past midnight, always in the hope that he might be allowed, while the War of Resistance lasted, to do some small service for his country. Each time, Mo could do no more than comfort him a little, for Chiang Kai-shek had never given the slightest sign of being ready to let him go. In that period, when his mood turned restless he would often lose his temper, and Liu Yiguang would wire a report to Dai Li at once. On learning of it Dai Li sometimes found time to go himself, and sometimes wrote a letter in his own hand and sent me or someone else down with a few things for him. When I went to see him he likewise never spoke of politics. He was a man of wide general knowledge and a great talker; once he opened his mouth the flow never stopped, and no one else could get a word in. He understood a great many things — driving a car, flying an aeroplane, he was expert at all of it; what he often said he regretted was that he could drive a car but not repair one.

We all understood his state of mind in those days, and never asked him about his past in the Northeast or the Northwest; about everything else we could chat with him freely, without the least constraint, and he always had an answer for every question. For years I had believed that Zhang and Hu Die had once been involved with each other. To satisfy my curiosity on this point I asked Hu Die to her face, and, separately, asked Zhang. I remember that once, before Dai Li sent me down with things for Zhang, he first called me to lunch at the Yangjiashan mansion, where the only company was Dai Li and Hu Die. After the meal, while Dai Li was out taking a telephone call, I asked Hu Die whether she had a letter or anything else she would like me to carry to Zhang. She seemed to want to speak and yet not want to, and only shook her head a couple of times. I said that people outside had long claimed the two of them were very close — why not send something along? She pressed me: who said so? I said, "There is a poem to prove it." At that she only pursed her lips and smiled. Just then Dai Li walked back in, and Hu told him what had passed. Dai Li was not at all pleased, and said to me, "You do love believing such stuff." But I did not stop believing it on that account. After I reached Tongzi and saw General Zhang, I found another opening and asked him what the truth of the matter really was. General Zhang flatly denied that anything had ever existed between him and Hu. He said that in those days he had truly been far too busy; he had seen Hu Die once at a dance, and beyond that had never had any dealings with her at all, let alone anything more. He repeated that rumors from outside were not to be lightly believed, and told me to ask Fourth Miss, who could confirm it. I believed that General Zhang was telling the truth, and did not go on to ask Fourth Miss. Hu Die, for her part, would neither deny it nor admit it; I am afraid she deliberately kept people guessing, the better to raise her own standing.

What consumed most of General Zhang's time in prison was the concentrated study of Ming history. In this field he not only collected a great deal of material but arrived at insights of his own, and made a great many notes, which Fourth Miss put in order and copied out for him. Whenever he needed relevant materials, Dai Li did whatever he could to supply them. In 1945 General Zhang told me that he had gathered a considerable stock of unofficial histories, manuscript copies of popular traditions and the like, and that he thought he could become a specialist in Ming history. He hoped a few historians who had worked on the Ming might come and talk with him from time to time. Liu Yiguang, on a hint from Dai Li, put him off with the excuse that it would be difficult to arrange, so he never made the request formally, and said only to me that if ever the chance came, he certainly meant to see a great deal of the specialists in that field.

During his imprisonment General Zhang grew very stout, so that walking cost him some effort, and he suffered from high blood pressure. The area in which he could move freely measured only two or three hundred metres, and that only in daylight; after dusk he could not go out at all. Beyond the perimeter held by the Juntong agents, the military-police company ringed the place with a post every three paces and a sentry every five, each within sight of the next. He could not cross that line unless the detachment captain accompanied him in person. His one pleasure then was to go fishing every day in the reservoir opposite his quarters. As long as it was not raining he would be up early, invite Liu Yiguang along, and pole out in a small square wooden punt to a sandbar that rose in the middle of the reservoir, surrounded on every side by water, where ordinarily no one could land. On the bar stood two small A-frame shelters of cornstalk; he and Liu took one apiece and sat angling over the stream, sometimes the whole day, sometimes half of it, and never tired of the sport. Among the rods he used was an American-made reel rod that T. V. Soong had given him, which could bring up a fish of several catties, and there were small rods he had made himself out of bamboo, for crucian carp and for fish that fed at the surface. Behind the house where he lived, one could always see lengths of old bamboo that had grown none too straight, one end tied down with a big stone and the other hung from the eaves, being slowly drawn straight. But though he loved catching fish, in eating them he took no great interest.

In those days he greatly hoped for callers, and at the sound of a motor-car driving into his part of the grounds he would be in a hurry to go out and welcome it. He and Fourth Miss were both fond of turtledove. Since I was keen on hunting, I never went to see him without bringing some game and turtledoves, and in autumn and winter I would join Liu Yiguang in inviting him out to shoot nearby. He was delighted to be out with us, always carrying his own water-bottle, taking a walking stick, and trotting along with us for a stretch. He knew his position and never asked to fire a gun himself; he only made a point of carrying whatever was shot, and would walk back with the bag in high spirits.

In food and daily things General Zhang Xueliang then lacked for nothing, for his wife had brought back great quantities of things from America, and T. V. Soong and Dai Li were forever sending him more. He had money of his own, and any expense that could not be put on the books he paid himself. His cook not only made excellent Chinese food but could turn out several Western dishes, Western pastries and bread. After meals there was usually fruit and coffee. In this respect he was far better off than General Yang Hucheng, who was often driven to anger because he could not even get steamed bread of white flour, while Zhang had more than he could eat; nor did Zhang ever once show dissatisfaction on that score. In the evenings General Zhang's way of passing the time was to play cards for small stakes with the agents on guard. He understood the agents' psychology very well: to lose too much might invite misunderstanding, as though he were buying them under another name, so each evening's winnings or losses were held to a matter of two or three silver dollars either way.

After the victory in the War of Resistance, General Zhang began to show a certain restlessness. Word went round outside at the time that when he had been shut up a full ten years, he sent Chiang Kai-shek the centenary commemorative watch, made by the Omega works, that he had bought in Switzerland — a hint that the time had come, and a hope that Chiang Kai-shek would keep faith and release him now that the ten-year term was served. But Chiang Kai-shek took no notice, and kept him imprisoned as before.

In the summer of 1946, when I went to Tongzi to see him for the last time, he did grumble to me a little. I had come to see him in passing, and told him the circumstances of Dai Li's death. When he had heard me out he said: everyone else is going home, even the arsenal has closed its doors, and here he still was in this kingdom of Yelang, with no telling when he would ever leave. He said too that through the eight years of the War of Resistance he, a soldier, had not been able to contribute the least effort against Japan, which shamed him deeply, and that by now people had forgotten him. Talk of this kind he was usually most unwilling to let out; for whatever reason, that day he said a great deal of it. I knew that Chiang Kai-shek had by no means forgotten him, and was even less inclined to release him merely because his ten years were served. Quite the contrary: a place for his continued imprisonment was at that moment being prepared. Later Wang Lingji, who had served as governor of Jiangxi, told me that Chiang Kai-shek had ordered him to put up a number of buildings at Yangming Cave in Xingguo county, Jiangxi, which were given out at the time as being for Chiang Kai-shek himself. After the houses were finished, Chiang Kai-shek once brought Wang Lingji along to inspect them in person, and only then did Wang learn that they were for the confinement of Zhang Xueliang. If Zhang went on living at Tongzi after the victory, then, it was because the houses in Jiangxi were not yet ready. And if Chiang Kai-shek had fixed on Yangming Cave in Xingguo, it was not only because the place was remote and easily guarded; above all he hoped that Zhang Xueliang would settle down quietly in that desolate little town and take Wang Yangming for his model — devote himself to study, and let the affairs of the court alone. That was why in Guizhou he had him live at the Yangming Cave of Xiuwen, and after the victory would have had him live at the Yangming Cave of Xingguo: the reason was the same.

Afterwards Chiang Kai-shek went to Taiwan, and saw that the Caoshan hot-spring district outside Taipei was more ideal still than Xingguo; so in the autumn of 1946 he had Mao Renfeng bring General Zhang from Tongzi to Chungking, where he stayed about half a month, first at the Songlinpo mansion inside the SACO compound at Ciqikou, the house Dai Li had built during the war on the pretext of sheltering Chiang Kai-shek from air raids. Li Jue, then director of a branch corps of the Central Training Corps at Chungking, Zhang Yanfo, head of the Juntong winding-up office, Xu Yuanju, head of the Second Department of the Chungking Pacification Headquarters, and others went often to see him, and kept him company over Hunanese card games to pass the time. I had hurried from Nanking to Chungking on Mao Renfeng's orders to look after Zhang, and to arrange the special plane for the flight to Taiwan. At first we kept from General Zhang that Taiwan was the destination, telling him only that he was to wait in Chungking for news. He was overjoyed, convinced that this time his release could not miscarry: not only had the ten years run their course, but the war had been won, and there could not possibly be any further difficulty.

I remember his first meal at the Songlinpo mansion, when Liu Yiguang's whole family joined us at table to keep him company. Liu Yiguang's two small children spat the bones they had finished with onto the floor, and seeing it he said quickly, laughing: "That will not do. This is not like the old days when we lived in the country. From now on you must take more care. Where we are going to live, the floors will all have beautiful carpets like these, and you cannot go spitting things onto them any more!" One can see how optimistic his reckoning was. He never imagined that when every preparation was complete, it would suddenly be announced that he was to be taken not to Nanking but to Taiwan. At the news he not only slammed his hand down hard on the table in anger; his distress showed too, and he clenched his teeth tight.

We had always addressed General Zhang as "Vice-Commander," since he had held such posts as vice-chairman under Chiang Kai-shek and deputy commander-in-chief of the Bandit Suppression Headquarters for the three provinces of Hubei, Henan and Anhui. He had never objected to the title before; but that time, when Liu Yiguang answered some question of his with "Reporting, Vice-Commander," he said at once: "What is all this Vice-Commander? Have done with it and treat me as a convict!" He was in a towering rage, but he knew that to quarrel and lose his temper with his keepers was useless and would settle nothing. Having said it, he sat for a while staring wide-eyed like a man stunned, forcing the surge of feeling down. Then he signified his consent to go to Taiwan, and told Liu Yiguang to wire the answer to Mao Renfeng in Nanking. When he went back to his own room and spoke of it to Zhao Yidi, his voice still trembled a little with anger. When we saw him onto the aircraft, he and Zhao Yidi forced smiling faces to shake our hands in farewell, and then, full of grief and indignation, walked silently onto the plane.

On February 28, 1947, when the people of Taiwan, unable to bear the Nationalist government's crushing exactions, rose in armed revolt and all Taiwan was in confusion, Chiang Kai-shek at once instructed Mao Renfeng that if anyone attempted to carry off Zhang Xueliang, Liu Yiguang was on the one hand to resist with all his strength and prevent the abduction, and on the other to shoot Zhang Xueliang first, making certain at whatever cost that Zhang was neither carried off nor able to escape in the confusion. The wireless station attached to the guard detachment had always reported once or twice a week; when the Taiwan rising broke out, Mao Renfeng ordered that besides reporting to Nanking three times a day, it could call through at any hour in an emergency. The Nanking head station assigned a dedicated set to listen day and night for Taiwan's call, so that contact could be made at any moment, and Mao Renfeng reported constantly to Chiang Kai-shek. From what I learned afterwards, Chiang Kai-shek at the time wanted nothing better than for someone to go up to the Caoshan hot springs and fire a few shots, so that he could seize the chance to have Zhang Xueliang killed without bearing the responsibility himself. But the Gaoshan people nearby had not yet found time to move against that district, and so General Zhang's life was preserved. In October 1949, when Mao Renfeng reached Kunming after having General Yang Hucheng murdered in Chungking on Chiang Kai-shek's orders, I asked him how things stood with General Zhang. Mao Renfeng told me that Zhang was still living at Caoshan. Since then I have never again heard any news of General Zhang.
