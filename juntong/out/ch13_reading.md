## Inside the Sino-American Special Technical Cooperative Organization

The Sino-American Special Technical Cooperative Organization (SACO for short) had the English name Sino-American Co-operation Office (abbreviated SACO). It was a product of the open merger of the American and Chiang secret services during the war against Japan, organized by cooperation between the Nationalist secret-service organ, the Military Affairs Commission Bureau of Investigation and Statistics (the Juntong), and the American navy department's intelligence office. I was then head of the General Affairs Department of the Juntong bureau, and as I had constant close dealings with this organization, I understand fairly well its founding and winding-up and its organization, personnel and activities. I write here what I know and saw of it, and where it falls short I hope the gentlemen who once knew the organization's affairs can instruct and supplement me, to bring out fully the various doings of this notorious criminal group, so that more people may understand the true face of the American and Chiang secret services.

### How It Came to Be Set Up

During the war against Japan, after the Chiang Kai-shek clique fled to Chungking, almost every organ had an American adviser presiding and manipulating within it. At the time only the secret-service branch had not yet won the masters' favour, and the Juntong chief Dai Li felt it a great loss of face that the unit he led got no notice from the masters. When he raised this with Chiang Kai-shek, Chiang too said it was a most regrettable thing. From around 1940, Dai Li repeatedly signalled to Xiao Bo, then the Juntong's chief of the American station (his open post being assistant military attaché at the National Government's embassy in the United States), hoping he would make contact on the American side and approach the relevant secret-service departments. Despite a fairly long stretch of effort, it never drew the Americans' notice.

A few months before the Pearl Harbor affair in the winter of 1941, the technical research office in charge of Japanese cipher work under the Juntong's signals department, from the movements and various dispositions of the Japanese air force, deciphered and learned that the Japanese air force intended to undertake operations in the Pacific region. Dai Li reckoned this the best chance to court the Americans, and after Chiang Kai-shek's approval passed the information to Xiao Bo. Xiao Bo relayed it to the attaché in Washington, Guo Dequan, and told the relevant people in the American defence department separately, telling them to watch the Japanese air force's movements. It is said that the responsible American generals who heard this not only roared with laughter and disbelieved it, but held it a deliberate Nationalist attempt to sow discord between America and Japan. Afterwards the Juntong kept getting intelligence of this kind, and after telling Xiao Bo to relay it to Guo Dequan, they were too embarrassed to notify the American defence department formally again, and only talked with individual American officers with whom they had some personal connection — who likewise only laughed. When on December 8 the Japanese air force successfully raided Pearl Harbor and the American Pacific fleet took a heavy blow, many American commanders recalled that the things Guo Dequan had told them months before were not fabricated out of thin air, and only then sought him out to ask the source of the news, learning it had been deciphered by the Juntong. They began on the one hand to make contact with Xiao Bo, and on the other told the attaché at the Chungking embassy, Colonel Depass, to deal directly with the Juntong close at hand, so that in the war against Japan they could use this organization for the intelligence work they needed.

At his first meeting with Dai Li, Depass praised the work Dai led at length. Dai felt it a great honour, and besides wiring Xiao Bo to seize the moment and press his activity in America, laid on a grand banquet at his Zengjiayan residence for Depass, inviting the Ministry of Military Operations Second Bureau head Yang Xuancheng, the deputy head Zheng Jiemin, the Aviation Committee chief Zhou Zhirou, and his friends He Shili and Wu Renshuo to attend, doing his utmost to show off before Depass his "achievements" in the war and his adoration of the Americans.

The result of Xiao Bo's activity in America was that he obtained the agreement of the naval staff's intelligence office, which ran America's overseas intelligence work, to send Lieutenant Commander Miles (Mary Miles), a former Far East fleet captain, who came to Chungking in the spring of 1942 accompanied by Xiao Bo, negotiated with Dai Li in person, and toured some of the Juntong's units. After Miles returned home and reported the negotiations to the naval intelligence office, he strongly urged first undertaking technical cooperation with the Juntong in signals decipherment, hoping in the course of it to learn thoroughly for himself the experience the Juntong signals department's technical research office had in deciphering Japanese air-force ciphers. Once the American navy department approved this plan, in the summer and autumn of 1942 Miles led a dozen and more American agents specializing in signals work to Chungking to make preparations. Dai Li, besides hoping the Americans would supply more of the wireless equipment the Juntong then urgently needed, hoped still more that the Americans would send a part of their cipher-breaking organ and equipment — a thing called the "Black Chamber" — to Chungking, so that he could learn the American methods on the sly while keeping his own bit of experience in deciphering Japanese air-force ciphers as capital, not letting the American agents learn it.

In the earliest stage of cooperation, since each side had its own selfish calculations, they were forever at odds in spirit while nominally together, and could find no way to cooperate; the work made no progress. Though the personnel each side sent kept increasing, they drifted ever farther from their real aims, and it nearly halted once, unable to go on. But by this time the American secret-service department gradually came to understand that the Juntong organization under Dai Li was spread into every corner of the Chiang-controlled areas, and that if secret-service work of all kinds could be carried on in China through this unit, it was the most ideal instrument, hard to come by. Dai Li, for his part, saw that the Americans were far more generous than the British he was then cooperating with, and to meet the Juntong's future material needs, this was the best of backers. Since each side saw what could be used in the other, though neither was satisfied with the signals-technology cooperation, neither would lightly give up the chance, and they sought to change the direction of the cooperation and widen its scope.

In the winter of 1942, in a talk between Dai Li and Miles at Dai Li's villa at Yangjiashan by the silk filature in Ciqikou, they raised the question of further strengthening cooperation. What the Americans most lacked then was information on Japanese military activity in China and along the Pacific and Chinese coasts, together with data on the weather and hydrography of these regions. The Juntong, besides the signals equipment it most needed, hoped to obtain American weapons and transport. After that exchange of views, the cooperation at once entered a new experimental phase, and the projects originally planned were shelved. With the approval of both sides' responsible departments, American weather personnel and equipment came to Chungking one after another, and some of the light weapons and explosives Dai Li needed were shipped in too. Though these weapons were few — the first batch, as I recall, was no more than several hundred revolvers, automatic pistols and carbines and Thompson submachine guns — they came very fast, and the ammunition allotted was fairly plentiful too. Unlike the British, who dragged their feet, forever haggling over quantities and allotting very little besides, the Americans' "generosity" pleased Dai Li greatly. During the trial period of changing the direction and widening the scope of cooperation, Dai Li kept raising requests — for American help in training and equipping the armed secret-service forces the Juntong led, and for American supply of transport, medical equipment and the like — and all got satisfactory answers. The SACO that had originally been organized quite simply, its scope of business widening by the day and personnel on both sides constantly increasing, gradually took on an internal structure. Where Miles and a batch of American agents had at first only worked and lodged around Zhongjiashan behind Yangjiashan by the silk filature in Ciqikou, by 1943 it slowly spread out east, west and south.

From the spring of 1943 new cooperative work was constantly added; whatever Dai Li asked for he soon got a satisfactory answer to, and great numbers of American agents and equipment poured into Chungking without cease. From early March both sides decided to fix in writing the various works already under trial and the requests each had raised, and to sign a formal treaty. Over what to call this treaty Dai Li thought again and again. Fearing others would one day accuse him of signing an unequal, country-selling treaty with imperialism, he sought a fitting term. In the end he decided on the form of a "contract" (the Americans sometimes called it an "agreement"), to signify that it was signed wholly on a basis of equal cooperation. In drafting this contract Dai often gathered the senior agents — Juntong department heads and above, SACO section heads and above — to study it, often not breaking up until deep in the night. The first contract, after more than a month's deliberation and seven or eight rounds of revision and addition, besides having its content repeatedly sounded out with Miles verbally by Dai Li to get his agreement first, was made as careful and thorough as possible in its wording. In April 1943 the first draft contract to be signed with the Americans was drawn up, and Dai went at once to consult T. V. Soong. Once Soong judged there was no problem, Dai reported to Chiang Kai-shek. Chiang read it and was well satisfied, and only then was the American side formally notified to set a date to sign. After the first contract was signed, SACO's preparatory work ended, and the various trial cooperative works were formally launched.

### A Summary of the Three Contracts Signed

The first contract was signed on about April 15, 1943, in the great hall of the Juntong country office at Yangjiashan by the silk filature in Ciqikou, Chungking (also the hall of the Juntong's Chungking training class).

Presiding over this signing: on the American side, the then Navy Secretary Knox and President Roosevelt's personal representative, the Life magazine proprietor Luce, together with Lieutenant Commander Miles of the navy's intelligence office; on the Chinese side, Chiang Kai-shek had originally appointed Foreign Minister T. V. Soong, but Soong had business and did not come, so the standing vice-minister of foreign affairs, Hu Shize, presided in his place, jointly with the Juntong deputy director Dai Li.

Those attending this signing ceremony were: on the American side, SACO's chief of staff Bei Leli, chief secretary Smith, and the American deputy heads of several SACO sections; on the Chinese side, SACO's Juntong chief of staff Li Chongshi, chief secretary Pan Qiwu, and the several section heads the Juntong sent. In addition, Dai Li specified that the Juntong's "three great heads" Zheng Jiemin, Tang Zong and Mao Renfeng, and the first-department head Bao Zhihong, second-department head He Zhiyuan, third-department head Xu Yedao, fourth-department head Wei Daming, fifth-department head Shen Weihan, sixth-department head Gong Xianfang, seventh-department head Xu Renji, eighth-department head Shen Zui all attend. This was because the two sides needed to work closely together in business and affairs, and he wished SACO and the Juntong to be as one, so at important gatherings of SACO and the Juntong the section- and department-head-level personnel of both units had to attend.

That afternoon the hall was newly decorated, the two national flags hung in the centre. The ceremony was held before the banquet, the attendants first seated at the horseshoe-shaped tables to wait. At five sharp Dai Li led Luce and the others and Hu Shize and the others into the hall amid warm applause, and after they were seated at the head, Dai Li first briefly expressed thanks to Luce and the others for coming so far to preside over the signing, and introduced those present to meet Luce and the others. By calling the roll, he spoke each attendant's name; each stood, and he added a word on the man's post, and Luce smiled and nodded and sat down again. Only Zheng Jiemin, Tang Zong and Mao Renfeng shook hands with Luce. When the introductions were done, Pan Qiwu read out the Chinese text of the contract, then Smith read out the English text, and the two sides' presiders signed each of the two Chinese and two English copies. Then Knox spoke. He first said that the cooperation, held so as to defeat the common enemy — Japanese imperialism — the sooner, accorded with the two countries' common interests and aims. He said the American president placed very earnest hopes in this work, believing the two sides would henceforth faithfully carry out everything the contract laid down and produce outstanding, astonishing results. He expressed satisfaction with the points listed in the contract, and proposed of his own accord that henceforth, if during cooperation a matter arose not written into the contract but needed for the work, the American side would do all it could to help China. Finally he praised Dai Li and the Juntong he led especially, which made every one of this crowd of secret-service heads very happy.

After Knox spoke, Hu Shize spoke. On behalf of the Chiang Kai-shek government he expressed the greatest thanks and respect to the American side, extreme satisfaction that the American representatives Knox and Luce had come so far to preside, and high praise of Luce as an outstanding figure of America. Hu Shize said the Chinese side would surely faithfully perform the contract, and henceforth hoped, with America's constant help, to smash Japanese imperialism. Dai Li and Miles also said at the last: they would faithfully perform everything the contract laid down, guaranteeing that the two sides would achieve loyal cooperation and the closest intimacy, not failing the two heads of state's hopes, and so on. When the ceremony ended a banquet was held. The two sides drank toast after toast to the smooth cooperation of the American and Chiang secret services and to each other's leaders' health. The banquet went on until about nine at night. Knox and Luce stayed five days in SACO before returning home.

The first contract's content was much and very involved. Though I took part many times before the drafting in studying this contract, and afterwards during its execution personally carried out some of the matters it stipulated, and after liberation pieced it together in memory many times with some of the people once concerned, I can still recall only a part; and since two more supplementary contracts were signed afterwards, I may also have the order confused. What I know I set out as follows:

The contract opened by stating that this was a cooperation between China's Military Affairs Commission Bureau of Investigation and Statistics and the American navy staff's intelligence office, so as to defeat the two sides' common enemy the sooner, willing to undertake exchange of intelligence bearing on the war against Japan, psychological-warfare propaganda, and guerrilla sabotage in enemy-occupied areas and the like, and establishing the Sino-American Special Technical Cooperative Organization in China's wartime capital Chungking. It went on to state that SACO was established for the war against Japan, and should upon victory in that war be declared at an end, all matters agreed in the contract, whether executed or not or under execution, to cease at once; but that while the war against Japan continued, if either side no longer wished to carry on the contract and asked to end the cooperation, it must, besides formally notifying the other side half a year in advance, take responsibility to keep all secrets bearing on the organization, and the other side had the right to demand compensation for all losses suffered from the ending of the contract.

SACO's personnel should be jointly formed as needed by both sides; the head and deputy of each department chosen by both sides, in principle the Juntong sending the head and the American side the deputy, with other arrangements possible by consultation when necessary. In choosing the responsible persons of each department both sides should first seek the other's consent before sending anyone. If during the work one side had objections to the other's personnel, it could ask for a replacement, and neither side might delay or refuse on any pretext. Henceforth, when the work required it, questions of increasing or reducing the organization should be agreed by both sides' responsible persons in advance, and neither side might act alone.

All of SACO's external correspondence, to be valid, must be jointly signed by the head and deputy head; internally the two sides' highest responsible persons were, on the Chinese side, the SACO head, and on the American side, the deputy head the Americans sent. When the head or deputy head needed for work reasons to direct the other side's personnel, the order must be jointly signed by head and deputy head to take effect. When the head or deputy head left his post on business, he could designate someone to act, notifying the other side in advance and seeking its consent.

Both sides' personnel's salaries and treatment were each to be borne by their own side. For all American personnel coming to China to work, matters of food, lodging, transport and other daily living were to be handled by the Juntong. All expenses first advanced by the Juntong were to be repaid by the Americans on schedule. Building works for SACO's offices, dormitories and other public housing were all to be managed by Juntong personnel, the costs borne jointly.

The main business of cooperation was the exchange of information on the deployment and activity of Japanese naval, land and air forces along China's coast and on the mainland, and related materials; both sides should offer for exchange and study all intelligence they gathered, without reservation, to benefit the war against Japan (in reality it was the Juntong that supplied such intelligence, and though nominally an exchange, the Americans never supplied the Juntong any intelligence). Where the Americans laid mines or made surveys along the Chinese coast, the Chinese side could send personnel to take part.

Though the American secret-service organ then attached great importance to the Chinese Communist Party and the Eighth Route Army, New Fourth Army and local people's armed guerrillas it led, the activity of the various democratic parties, and the situation of the Soviet Union, the contract did not mention these formally, saying only vaguely that in intelligence exchange, besides the materials on Japan spoken of above, "other relevant intelligence" was also within the scope of exchange and study. So nowhere in the whole contract can words of opposing the Communists, the Soviet Union and the people be found; but in actual execution the Juntong constantly had to supply such materials to satisfy the Americans. The Juntong's Xinjiang provincial station then gathered information on the Soviet Union along the Sino-Soviet border especially, to supply the Americans. Materials on the Communists and their armed forces, and on the democratic parties, the Juntong's responsible departments supplied whole sets at a time.

The armed secret-service forces the Juntong led numbered fifty or sixty thousand in all, and Dai Li's request for American help to train them and issue American equipment also got the Americans' agreement. The first contract proposed first training and equipping fifty thousand, and laid down that all secret-service forces SACO trained and equipped must take SACO's direction, and must report their operations against Japan to SACO too. The contract said much about American training for the Juntong which I can no longer recall; I know only that afterwards, besides setting up many "Sino-American special technical training classes" in various places, it set up an instructors' training class and a weather-personnel training class in Chungking, a medical-personnel (military-surgeon) training class at Dong'an in Hunan, and demolition classes and the like.

The contract mentioned too that, to facilitate the two sides' naval and air operations against Japan, SACO should set up hydrographic stations, weather stations and wireless stations along the Chinese coast, in important rear cities, and where its armed secret-service forces were based. As for the signals and weather equipment, weapons and ammunition, transport, medical equipment and other materials SACO needed for its work, all were to be supplied free by the Americans in the full quantities actually needed, and transported at their charge.

The contract also stated that all American personnel in SACO, on coming to China, enjoyed the treatment of diplomatic personnel. It also stated that if American personnel went missing, were injured or killed, the Juntong should make every effort to help search and take responsibility for their safety. The Americans specially proposed that personnel from Burma, Thailand, Korea, Taiwan, Annam and the like, who had been trained in America, judged absolutely reliable, and had sworn loyalty to the Allies, might, on American proposal and Chinese approval, work under the organization's direction.

The contract closed by stating that it took effect from the day of signing. All measures during SACO's preparatory period before signing that did not conflict with its agreed provisions should continue, and otherwise cease at once, to be settled by separate consultation. It also stipulated that during execution, if both sides felt a need to amend or add matters, they could, after prior consultation and agreement, sign a supplementary contract separately. The contract was written in two Chinese and two English copies, the texts of equal validity.

The second, supplementary contract was signed in the autumn of 1944, at the Chen family compound inside SACO. The American presider was the head of the Office of Strategic Services, Donovan (W. J. Donovan); for the Chiang Kai-shek clique, Dai Li. Those attending, besides SACO section heads and above, included the department heads and office chiefs of the Juntong bureau — over thirty in all.

The second contract's main content was the American agents' training of American-style secret-service personnel for the Chiang Kai-shek clique and their supply to the Juntong of American torture instruments to strengthen the Juntong's suppression of the Chinese people.

The contract stipulated that SACO would set up in Chungking a special-police training class specializing in training criminal-police cadres. The instructors needed were to be chosen by the Americans, the technical training wholly the Americans' charge, and the teaching equipment and practice equipment all American-supplied. The trainees were to be selected by the Juntong, the housing and training funds borne by the Juntong. In this contract the Americans also agreed to train twenty senior Juntong agents, sent to America for a year. Besides travel costs borne by the Juntong, the trainees' expenses in America were paid by the Americans — about ten thousand US dollars a head for the year. In 1945, when Admiral Cooke, commander of the American Seventh Fleet, came to inspect SACO, he doubled the number of senior agents sent to America to forty on his own initiative. This contract also raised increasing the American supply of transport and medical equipment to SACO. The ten-wheel trucks were raised from the agreed five hundred to two thousand; the light and medium jeeps from fifty to two hundred, with a full set of maintenance equipment for all vehicles and three years' worth of spare parts of every kind supplied. Medical equipment was raised to a full set of equipment and medicine for a thousand hospital beds.

The third contract was signed in the winter of 1945. By then Dai Li and Miles had both gone to inspect the Southeast, and according to the principles the two had settled, it was signed in SACO in Chungking by the American chief of staff Bei Leli and the Juntong chief secretary Pan Qiwu, its content mainly the administrative matters of winding up.

By the first contract, upon victory in the war against Japan SACO should be declared at an end. All SACO's winding-up work would be managed by the Juntong. All American personnel in China should return home at once. Of the weapons, ammunition and daily necessities the Americans held in SACO, all already shipped to China, whatever the quantity, would be handed to the Juntong to receive and dispose of at no charge. As for the materials the Juntong had asked to have increased and the materials the two contracts stipulated the Americans should supply, apart from the full medical equipment and instruments for a thousand beds, still to be supplied in full by the Americans but delivered at Shanghai instead, other materials already shipped for China would be supplied in the quantities shipped, and those not yet shipped would no longer be supplied. Of SACO's data and documents during the cooperation, the Americans could take one copy as needed, the rest to be handed to the Juntong. After the American personnel left China, all housing they had built in China and all equipment and furnishings would be given to the Juntong to receive at no charge, the Americans retaining no ownership or right of use.

### Organization, Personnel and Activities

SACO had one head, held concurrently by Dai Li; one deputy head, filled by the American agent chief Miles. All work, internal and external, was the joint responsibility of head and deputy head.

Each side set up one chief of staff: on the Juntong side Li Chongshi, on the American side Bei Leli.

Each side set up one chief secretary: on the Juntong side Pan Qiwu, on the American side Smith. The internal departments were divided into the following sections:

Military Operations Section (also called the Staff Section or, for short, the Military Section): head Shang Wang, styled Weifu, the chief of staff of the Special Operations Army command the Juntong led. Deputy head Yi Wei, the Special Operations Army's staff-department head. Both held the posts concurrently, and in fact Yi Wei was in charge. Under the section were four senior staff officers, all senior Juntong agents. The Americans sent a deputy head and several staff officers, names unknown. This section's main work was nominally to direct the armed secret-service forces the Juntong led, including the action corps and demolition corps of the various places; in fact these armed secret-service heads did not obey SACO's direction but were still controlled by the Juntong. All operational plans this section drew up had first to be agreed with the Juntong before being issued. Even so, the armed secret-service heads of various places still would not much accept them, and followed their own set of ways, asking the Juntong directly for instructions. But to get American weapons, ammunition and equipment, they had to report each operation's casualties, results and expenditure to SACO and ask for replenishment. These figures were mostly repeatedly inflated or even fabricated, and often differed from what they reported to the Juntong. The Americans, however, attached great importance to these statistics and constantly replenished these units' weapons and ammunition, for on the basis of these falsely reported "results" they could go to the American government to ask for things.

Intelligence Section: head Lu Suichu, American deputy head unknown.

This section was SACO's key work section, mainly classifying and editing the various intelligence the Juntong supplied to the Americans and passing it to them, while passing to the Juntong the questions the Americans needed understood in depth, for supplement or re-check. In the earliest period the intelligence the Juntong supplied could not, in quantity or quality, satisfy the American agents, and Miles repeatedly asked for improvement in written memoranda or verbally to Dai Li. Later, as the Juntong merged with the traitors, a great number of Juntong agents were placed in Shanghai, Nanking and the various Southeast areas, with wireless stations everywhere, and more was gathered on the Japanese situation in China. These materials were what the American secret-service organ had most lacked, and now they were both plentiful and of fairly high quality, so they drew great American notice — and set off infighting within the American secret-service organ from envy. The American army staff's strategic intelligence bureau demanded to send personnel to join SACO's work and required Miles to supply intelligence to the American forces headquarters and embassy in China. It is said that because Miles would not agree, Donovan detained him at the American embassy for a day, so vexing Miles that he nearly went mad, and he returned to America to recuperate for a few months. Afterwards American army and FBI agents also joined this section's work in 1944.

This key section's work developed and strengthened constantly over the years. Before 1943 it had only some twenty American and Chiang agents; by 1944 it had increased to over forty. What had at first relied wholly on some intelligence the Juntong supplied could no longer satisfy the Americans. After 1944 several intelligence stations were added in the Southeast, directly gathering intelligence of the Southeast coastal region. As the business kept widening, and the Americans laid special stress on Southeast work, in the summer of 1944 the Juntong added another deputy head, Wang Yixin. This man had for years done undercover-placement work for the Shanghai area at the Juntong headquarters, and knew the Southeast well, so he was moved to fill this post.

Psychological Warfare Section: the Juntong had never much stressed propaganda and rumour-mongering, but after merging with the American agents it set up a psychological warfare section within SACO in imitation of the American secret-service organ. This section was headed in succession by Xie ×× and Wu Lizza. Xie was a Trotskyite renegade who had been Chen Duxiu's secretary and joined the Juntong by selling out Chen. SACO set up this section only after the first contract was signed in the summer of 1943. When Dai Li introduced Xie to Miles, he mentioned Xie's history, and Miles was very pleased, holding that such a man was most suited to psychological-warfare work.

This section had over twenty members. From the conscripted personnel the American agents specially picked a batch suited to SACO work to come. Among those sent to this section were two cartoonists once of great renown in America (names now forgotten). I recall that when I went to the section to find Xie ××, he introduced them specially, saying these two men got tens of US dollars for a single cartoon in American papers and magazines, and on being conscripted were posted to SACO to do anti-enemy propaganda, to make use of their talent. There were besides a man who had done broadcasting in America and several who did news work, all assigned to this section. Dai Li, not to fall behind, also engaged the famous Chinese cartoonists of the day as specialists in this section. Wei Jingmeng of the Nationalist international propaganda office was also engaged to join. Wang Pengsheng, head of the Institute for International Affairs, constantly supplied this section with propaganda material on Japan.

This section, besides constantly broadcasting to the Japanese-occupied areas and air-dropping propaganda, in 1944 set up a mobile propaganda corps, led by He Yuan, active constantly on the Southeast front. This corps was allotted a dozen and more propaganda vehicles fitted with broadcasting equipment, specially propagandizing to Japanese soldiers near their garrisons. This section's most tense and active period was in 1945, when the American forces attacked Japan island by island and dropped atomic bombs on Nagasaki and Hiroshima; they constantly propagandized the American forces' results in fighting on Japanese soil, exaggerated the atomic bomb's power, and worked to shake the morale of the Japanese forces. After the victory, Miles, eager to learn what effect this section's work had had among the Japanese forces, sent many American agents to question some Japanese soldiers, and got very satisfactory answers. For some blockaded news had kept reaching the Japanese forces' ears, and they spread it secretly among themselves, with, it is said, a considerable effect on front-line morale. During the American-Chiang secret-service merger, the Juntong, taught by the American agents, learned this psychological-warfare set too, and Dai Li and Chiang Kai-shek rated it highly. So when the Juntong wound up and the Bureau of Secrets Preservation was set up, during the anti-Communist, anti-people civil war, the Bureau too set up a psychological-warfare section specializing in such propaganda and rumour-mongering.

Weather Section: this section was set up entirely at American proposal. At the time it was sometimes said to be for the two sides' naval and air activity, needing to understand the mainland's weather; in fact the Nationalist navy's personnel had fled into the mountains of western Hunan, the air force's few battered planes spent all day fleeing air-raid warnings, and the ones truly in urgent need were the Americans. It is said the Americans had many times asked the Nationalist government for weather materials on the mainland. The Chinese side truly could not answer, for it had never valued this work at all, and even for a plane to take off it had to make contact with the destination by wireless on the spot. After fleeing to the interior, even the little equipment it had before was broken and incomplete, forming a state of paralysis. The Americans understood this well, so from the outset SACO stressed this department's work first, and great quantities of weather equipment kept arriving. They had, through SACO, to gather the materials they needed by their own hands on the Chinese mainland.

This section had at first a Juntong head surnamed Wang, later succeeded by Cheng Jun, but the one actually in charge was the American deputy head and their experts. Before the first contract was signed, over 150 weather stations and wireless stations had already been built in Chungking, Chengtu, Kunming, Guiyang, Xi'an, Lanzhou and so on. After the first contract, the American weather expert Kotrla (R. Kotrla) came to run the training, and great numbers of weather personnel were trained. Almost everywhere a SACO- or Juntong-related force, training class, office or other field unit was based, weather stations and wireless stations sprang up like bamboo shoots after rain. By the end of 1944, this section's work units were everywhere in the Chiang-controlled areas. According to an American deputy head of SACO's general-affairs section, apart from the weapons and materials America spent equipping the Juntong's armed agents — the most of all — the weather side came second. After the victory, this unit's Juntong personnel and all the weather stations' equipment everywhere were handed to the Nationalist Executive Yuan's Civil Aviation Bureau to receive.

Action Section: head Zhou Zhisheng, deputy head Jiao Jinyan; American deputy head unknown. This section was set up only after the first contract, its work much like the Military Section's. Since SACO itself had no field action organization, it relied wholly on the roughly eighty-odd action corps, action squads, action groups and demolition squads and groups of various names the Juntong led. Dai Li had these units' "results" reported by the Juntong to Chiang Kai-shek for merit and reward, and at the same time reported to the Americans by SACO for more weapons and explosives. Miles's repeated rewards and steady promotions were not unconnected with this section's work "results." Some of the Juntong's action units then working behind enemy lines and at the front, besides constantly doing a little sabotage of railway and highway traffic, also constantly carried out activities such as assassinating traitors like the Shanghai puppet mayor Fu Xiao'an, likewise listed as SACO's results. SACO sometimes even inflated the results to over two thousand enemy killed and wounded a month.

Transport Section: head Huang Ronghua, an overseas Chinese long resident in America, concurrently transport-department head of the Ministry of Finance's Wartime Goods Transport Bureau. SACO's most tense transport work then was going to Kunming to fetch the materials shipped by sea from America to India and then flown to China. The heaviest tonnage of these was weapons, ammunition and equipment, next the American agents' daily necessities. After 1944 SACO had two thousand ten-wheel trucks, but in fact only about a thousand were regularly out. Of the goods shipped from Kunming, part was stored at Guiyang for transshipment to the Southeast; part at Lantianba, Luzhou, for transshipment to the Northwest; and most was carried to Chungking. This section had a receiving station at Kunming, and transshipment stations at Luzhou, Guiyang, Xi'an, Jianyang and so on. The most weapons then went to the Southeast, and on the return the trucks carried goods the Goods Transport Bureau bought from the occupied areas, so the Bureau's and SACO's transport work nearly meshed. This section too was set up only after the first contract. Before it was set up, SACO's materials were all carried by the Juntong's motor-transport corps. From SACO's founding until over a year after its winding-up, the weapons, ammunition, explosives, torture instruments, signals equipment and so on the Americans supplied had still not all been transported, and a batch was disposed of on the spot at the last.

Management Section: before 1943 this was the Accounts Section, renamed the Management Section after the organization was enlarged; headed in succession by Mao Zongliang and Liu Junshi, with Zhou Haoliang as deputy head. Most of its personnel were Juntong agents; besides financial and accounting work, it later also handled the materials accounts. The Americans had their own separate management organ.

Medical Section: this section was set up only after 1943, when SACO was enlarged. The head was at first Zhang Yuehan, introduced to Dai Li by the Ministry of Military Administration's surgeon-general Robert Lim. But finding SACO a secret-service organ jointly formed by the American and Chiang secret services, Zhang left without notice, and the section was later headed concurrently by the Juntong medical-office head Dai Xiamin. This section's main work was medical administration, specially receiving the American-supplied medical equipment and planning the requests to the Americans for such materials. It ran a Four-One Hospital jointly formed by SACO and the Juntong, its director also Dai Xiamin. This hospital treated only the two units' agents and their families. The Americans had besides their own clinic and a well-equipped dental room. These American clinics then would not treat Juntong agents; even a porter who carried the American agents' daily necessities and collapsed of heatstroke before these clinics, the American agents had carried to the Four-One Hospital, and only a very few senior agents, with Miles's or Bei Leli's permission and prior notice, could go there to see a doctor and be examined. I once got their permission to have my teeth examined, and the American doctors' arrogant air kept me from going again.

General Affairs Section: this section was set up from SACO's very start, and was busy from the start until the winding-up, and was throughout the section the Americans had the most objections to and were least satisfied with. Dai Li sent Guo Bin, the Juntong's most experienced hand at general affairs, to be head. This affairs specialist, who had run the Carlton Hotel in Hong Kong and been the Juntong's general-affairs section chief for years, in under two years as SACO general-affairs head had over fifty objections raised against him by the Americans, verbal and written. Dai Li could only move Yang Longhu, twice the Juntong's general-affairs section chief, to replace Guo Bin. In 1943, after SACO was enlarged, Dai even set up a management-personnel training class specially to do SACO's general-affairs work well, selecting over forty of the best affairs men from the Juntong and the open units it led for three months' special training before posting them to this section, hoping to satisfy the American masters in living arrangements. Though he wore himself out currying favour, the American agents were still not satisfied. By 1945 Yang Longhu was replaced again by the American-returned student Luo Jie. Had the victory not ended SACO, there is no telling how many more would have been changed.

This section's main work was serving the American agents' daily life; from laundry and meals to housing, there was almost nothing that did not deal directly with the American agents. With the difficulty of material supply in wartime, the American agents always held that they were paying and merely having Chinese handle it, and it was handled badly; when what they wished to buy could not be bought at once, or the food did not suit their taste, they flew into rages. Though a fine dining hall was built specially for the American agents, and over twenty waiters who knew a little English were rounded up from several big Chungking restaurants, and several cars went out daily to buy chicken, meat, fish, duck, vegetables and fruit, the American agents still had objections daily. At its peak SACO had four or five hundred American agents; besides the food problem, what displeased them more was that the housing was too little, and the hastily built temporary housing did not suit their living habits. Though Dai Li ordered great quantities of flush toilets and other sanitary fittings bought up in Chungking, Chengtu, Kunming and elsewhere, nearly to the point of scraping the barrel dry, it still could not meet the need. Any of the better houses in and near SACO the American agents took a fancy to were vacated at once; even the Juntong's Baigongguan prison, which the Americans found good, they wanted for a dormitory. Dai Li could only build a new prison at Zhazidong and turn Baigongguan into the Third Guest House. This section, besides affairs work, was also responsible for keeping weapons, ammunition and materials. But the disposal of every bullet, every inch of telephone wire, was not for the section head to decide, but had to be approved by the American deputy head or American management personnel before it could be issued from the warehouse. Besides the nine great sections handling business and affairs, SACO's internal organization had a general office, headed concurrently by the chief secretary Pan Qiwu. This general office had several sections under it. Liaison Section: before SACO was enlarged this section was directly under the head and deputy head. After the general office was set up in 1943 it came under the general office. The head, Liu Zhenfang, was Dai Li's and Miles's interpreter and the bridge between them. Understanding Dai's and Miles's intentions, he signalled to each side in advance, so in discussions there was rarely a deadlock, and both Dai and Miles valued him, making him the most favoured man in SACO. This section's main work was oral interpretation and liaison between the two sides' personnel. In units where American personnel worked, several liaison men (also called interpreting officers) were posted. These liaison men were mostly graduates of the foreign-affairs and foreign-language training classes the Juntong ran, all classmates of Liu Zhenfang, and among those Dai and Miles trusted were Pan Jingxiang, Zhou Guanxi and Pan Fushuo. Whenever Liu Zhenfang was away from SACO, only these few were qualified to act as interpreter between Dai and Miles.

Under the general office were three further sections — documents, personnel and cipher — of which, apart from the personnel section, which had American personnel handling the Americans' personnel administration and card registration, the cipher and documents sections had no American personnel. For these two works the Americans had their own separate organization, whose situation is unknown to me.

The general office had two secretaries, one Li Yiyun, one Wen Wansheng, both intimates of Pan Qiwu and his fellow townsmen from Fujian. The general office could not in authority directly lead the nine great sections' work, but since the Juntong side's responsible person was the chief secretary Pan Qiwu, and Pan was concurrently general-office head, the overall coordination of the other units invisibly fell to the general office.

In the summer of 1944, as the business widened by the day, the document-translation work originally done by the liaison section and the liaison officers posted to each unit was often more than they could manage, with much backlog, so a general translation office was set up, headed concurrently by Liu Zhenfang, responsible solely for written translation. The interpreting personnel the Juntong trained itself could not keep up with the need, especially after a dozen and more Sino-American special technical training classes were set up in various places, which needed great numbers of interpreters for the American instructors' lectures and liaison, so Dai Li asked the Military Affairs Commission's foreign-affairs bureau head He Haoruo for a hundred students from the interpreter training class the foreign-affairs bureau ran at the Central Training Corps. These trainees were all that year's graduating university students, conscripted and to be given short training before posting to units with American advisers to interpret. When these hundred were sent to SACO, Dai Li set up a temporary training class.

This class was then called the Juntong Chungking Training Class Interpreter Detachment; it spent a month explaining SACO's business and unifying translated terms to the students. For in the Central Training Corps interpreter class they had had only some unified translation of military terms, and SACO being a joint American-Chiang secret-service organ, they were unclear on some secret-service common knowledge and terms. At the same time they were required first to complete all the formalities of joining the Juntong secret-service organization before being formally set to work. Among these hundred were several women students. Not long after they worked in the general translation office they were spotted by the American agents. Taking a great interest in these English-speaking women, the agents lined up dozens-strong on the road from the offices to the dormitory to wait for them going to and from work, and pestered them. When Miles and Dai Li learned of this, they at once moved the women away, and laid down that no women be employed in SACO, for fear the American agents would cause trouble from jealousy. Once these trainees were posted to SACO it was felt there were too many, so Dai Li sent some who had studied chemistry to work at the Juntong's plant at Zunyi, Guizhou, refining vegetable oil into petrol. By the end of 1944 some were uneasy working in this secret-service organ, and Dai Li, fearing trouble, sent a batch back to the foreign-affairs bureau. When SACO's work ended at the end of 1945, they were all sent back. SACO also had an engineering department, set up in 1944, headed by Shen Diankang. As housing was short and the buildings Dai Li cleverly threw up in haste kept having design and quality problems, Miles repeatedly gave Dai Li written memoranda telling him to mind the American personnel's safety, and only then was this department set up, responsible solely for building work. Before this, all SACO's building had been designed by Dai himself, with Hou Zhenxiang, concurrent chief of the engineering subsection of the Juntong silk-filature office, responsible for hiring labour and building. After 1943, as great numbers of American personnel kept pouring in and Dai himself was very busy, he often only pointed and gestured a few words, and Hou did exactly as directed, with the result that buildings kept collapsing and stone embankments subsiding. In 1944, needing to set up the Chungking Sino-American special-police training class, Dai wanted to show his strength before the Americans and to build a hall finer than any in Chungking. So it was decided he would no longer design it himself but only state the requirement — able to hold four thousand people, and as handsome and serviceable as possible. For these reasons this department was set up.

After the engineering department was set up, Dai also decided that SACO's future building would no longer be the engineering subsection's charge but wholly contracted to the Shanghai Lu Genji Construction Company. This company's proprietor Lu Genquan was a friend Dai had newly made in Yunnan, and Dai specially invited him to Chungking to help build SACO's housing. Through Lu's introduction, the then first-rate Chungking Kwan Chu and Yang engineering firm sent experts to this department for design and supervision. From the spring of 1944, as SACO's scope widened by the day with Dai Li's and Miles's needs, mountains were opened and stone quarried everywhere, fields wasted and ditches filled, and thousands of earth, wood and stone workers laboured through the nights. In the fierce summer, every few days one or two workers died of heatstroke or overwork in the rush, and injuries costing hands and feet happened daily; but Dai Li and Miles still complained the work was too slow.

To build SACO's housing, nearby dwellings were forcibly demolished. When they marked a dwelling for demolition, they had the people inside move out at once, tore the old house down at once, and gave only a little removal fee. Paying and ordering the move at the same time, they never asked where these residents could move in a single day, so deaths kept occurring from the forced eviction. I recall that when nearly a hundred households at Wulingguan were forced to move, some who were a step slow were, besides being beaten by the agents and having their luggage thrown out, and at the least sign of dissatisfaction, seized and locked up at once. One woman who had just borne a child did not move at once, and the agents tore off her roof. That night, in a raging storm, the next day this new mother and her newborn both died. Hearing of it, Dai Li said: "This is a good example; let us see who else dares hang on in their house from now on." Later, in demolishing dozens of dwellings around Buyunqiao, some old people who could not bear to leave the ancestral homes they had lived in for generations were pushed to the ground by agents, and on the spot too an old man of about seventy died of a stroke, and in the end only a little burial money was given, and that was that.

In those days the residents near SACO were in daily dread, not knowing when their house might be taken and leave them with nowhere to live. I recall that once, when I accompanied Dai Li nearby to look over a site for building a warehouse to receive the American explosives the Juntong took over, hoping for somewhere a bit far off, we had gone beyond SACO's bounds. On the way back, passing a peasant's thatched house, Dai Li saw the family kept a fine pair of little geese and went over to look. At this the whole family was terror-stricken, for at the sight of that retinue, and with their daily fear of losing their house, the moment Dai came near the whole family of six or seven ran and knelt down before him, saying over and over that their family was large and could find no house, begging his mercy to let them stay. Dai was baffled, and explained with a smile that he had not come for the house but to look at the little birds, and only then did they dare to rise. As Dai left, being a step slow I saw with my own eyes this family's old man at once dash the pair of little geese to death — perhaps because he hated them for nearly bringing on disaster. The people's dread and hatred of these agents in those days has floated up in my mind for years.

Over the years SACO's scope kept widening, stretching from the silk filature in the north south to Chadianzi on Little Geleshan and up to the huangbo trees at its summit. There had been a shortcut here from Geleshan to Ciqikou, which was also blocked off, and passers-by made to go round by Yanggongqiao. SACO's west side stretched to the coal pit at Zhazidong, up against the arsenal. To take this coal pit, the little pit's owner was pressed to death before it was taken. The east side reached the arsenal's ballistics research institute. The whole scope was near thirty li around. All the houses, fields and forests within it were forcibly requisitioned, and anyone who strayed in was, at the least, beaten and cursed, and at the worst imprisoned or killed. Four middle-school students who strayed into the forbidden zone on a holiday outing were charged as reconnaissance men sent by the Communists and imprisoned for more than ten years in the concentration camps at Chungking and Xifeng; before liberation three were still killed at Baigongguan.

In its year and more, the engineering department's main work was the great hall of the special-police class and its housing and parade ground, erected on the south side of Buyunqiao east of Zhongjiashan. In the spring of 1945 it also built for Miles a splendid little residence on a hilltop south of Zhongjiashan (Miles had lived in a villa of Dai Li's at Zhongjiashan). While it was under way, Dai took me one day to see the progress and told me smugly: "This house backs on the hills and faces water; the scenery looks fine, but in fact the front gate faces straight down a great gully, and the master who lives here is bound to lose great wealth." He very much hoped his master would move in and lose great wealth, the better to supply the Juntong more weapons and materials. To please Miles, he named the house the "Plum Garden," and meant to plant several hundred plum trees around it after it was built. In the autumn of 1945, when the house was finished, victory was near; the furniture had just been arranged when Miles and Dai Li went off to the Southeast together, and he never lived in it a single day. The special-police-class hall, newer and more spacious than even the National Government's great hall, was rushed to completion before Christmas 1944, and apart from a few grand gatherings and banquets, was never used again because of the victory.

Motor Transport Corps: set up in the winter of 1943. The post of corps commander long went unsettled, and the Juntong motor-transport corps commander Zhang Chengwu long served as deputy commander in charge. Only in the spring of 1945 was Xu Jianye, then inspectorate head of the Zi(gong)-Nei(jiang) Garrison Headquarters, moved to be commander. The corps had a business section, headed concurrently by the Juntong motor-transport deputy commander Wang Ji; a maintenance section and a general-affairs section, the maintenance section headed concurrently by the Juntong motor-repair office head Zeng Timing. Under the corps were three battalions and a repair shop. Chungking had two battalions, over a thousand ten-wheel trucks. Guiyang had one battalion, stationed at Kunming and Hengshan. In the Southeast and Northwest a company each was stationed. When this corps was set up it had at first some American drivers, and later all overseas-Chinese drivers allotted by the Southwest Transport Office head Song Ziliang. Most of these were overseas Chinese returned from Southeast Asia to join the war work, over a thousand in all, who went back one after another after the victory. Only over a year after SACO's winding-up did this corps end. SACO's field units had originally only one Southeast office, set up at Jianyang, Fujian, in 1943. As SACO's key work was then in the Southeast, it was headed concurrently by the Juntong chief of staff Li Chongshi, long stationed in the Southeast to direct. This office had a large organization, with military, intelligence, training-personnel, and general-affairs sections, and secretary, accounts and inspectorate offices. SACO's and the Juntong's Southeast work was then nearly all led by this office. The four intelligence stations and one forward command post SACO set up in the Southeast in 1944 were also directly directed by this office. Of these four stations' work, the Shanghai station was most valued by the Americans. Its head Zhuang Xintian, through the connections of some traitors going to Japan, could gather information on Japan itself. Especially were the various surface phenomena some traitors saw with their own eyes — Japanese naval vessels dragged back for repair after being damaged in battle with the American navy — materials the American navy was eager to learn, so this station's work was constantly rewarded. There were also the Minhou station (head Wang Diaoxun), Dinghai station (head Zhang Yuan) and Zhangzhou station (head Wang Deyuan), also responsible for gathering the Japanese activity in coastal areas (mainly the navy) and the military deployment along the coast. According to Miles, this was because the American navy meant to help China fight Japan, meaning to attack Japan from the sea and then land troops in a pincer against the Japanese forces. Many then believed it and hoped they would soon open a front along the Chinese coast. Especially in the winter of 1944, when the Japanese vanguard had crossed Guangxi into Guizhou and Chungking was gravely threatened, Chiang Kai-shek, He Yingqin and others eagerly hoped the American navy could launch an offensive along the Chinese coast to save the Chungking crisis. But it was not until after the victory, to help the Nationalists set up the civil war against the Communists, that this American navy calmly landed on the Chinese mainland.

The forward command post led by the Southeast office, headed by Mao Sen, was at Yinzhufang in Fenshui county, Zhejiang. Though listed within SACO's establishment, this unit was in fact an outpost of the Juntong bureau. Its main work was directing the Loyal and Patriotic Army and other assorted secret-service armed forces the Juntong led to make forays into puppet-garrisoned areas and then report their battle merits to the Juntong and SACO separately. Because this post was fairly close to the Japanese-occupied areas, the American agents never once dared go there. Mao Sen used these American-armed secret-service forces to set up checkpoints nearby and levy taxes by force, becoming the local overlord.

There was besides a Southeast transport station belonging to the Ministry of Finance's Wartime Goods Transport Bureau, in touch with SACO and led close at hand by the Southeast office. The transport station head, Sou Shouquan, had to ask Li Chongshi for instructions on everything. As Li was Southeast office head concurrently with being SACO's Chinese chief of staff, even the Sino-American special technical training classes SACO set up at Xiongcun, Yuhu, Zhangzhou, Jianyang, Gangkou and so on in the Southeast were under Li's supervision and direction. This office took nearly half of SACO's work.

The Southeast office also had a large American forces guest house at Huilong Temple in Jianyang; besides hosting American agents passing through the Southeast, it held several grand gatherings a year to entertain the American agents working there. It is said that once at a Christmas Eve party several American agents, dead drunk, groped Li Chongshi's wife, so enraging Li that he drew his pistol to have it out with them, and only after others' hard persuasion did it break up unpleasantly.

SACO's training classes: from its founding to its winding-up SACO ran over twenty training classes of every kind. In helping the Juntong train agents and armed secret-service forces the Americans spent the most manpower and materials, and among these the Chungking special-police training class was the most complete, the best-equipped, the longest-running, and best illustrates the matter.

The Chungking special-police class was set up somewhat later than the others, partly because it was only formally raised in the second contract, partly because housing, equipment and student selection took much time, so training began only in the autumn of 1944. The special-police class was headed concurrently by Dai Li, with Miles as concurrent deputy head; the one actually in charge of administration was, for the first course, the deputy head and Juntong agent Le Gan, and for the second, Liu Renkui. The one running the training was the American chief instructor White; all power lay with the chief instructor's office. The class had over fifty American instructors, and all the technical courses were taught by American instructors, entirely on the American secret-service model, none of the Juntong's old set of secret-service courses being used. At the time even Dai Li felt that not mixing in some courses suited to Chinese conditions would make the work inconvenient later, since it was not training to work in America; but the Americans still would not agree, holding the Juntong set backward and unscientific. Dai Li, unwilling to press the point, left it wholly to their arrangement. This class, like the Juntong's other training classes, had teaching-affairs, political-training and general-affairs sections. The first course's teaching-affairs head was Li Hanyuan, later Tianjin police chief before liberation; the second's was Zeng Xiankai. The class had a battalion headquarters, the first course's battalion commander Li Hanting, the second's Yang Yuansen. Under the battalion were three companies. The first course had eight hundred students, the second 1200. These students all came selected from the Juntong's several basic agent training classes, such as the Lanzhou, Xifeng and Chungking classes. This class's training was divided into two departments, criminal police and security police. The criminal-police department's courses were the most complex — besides reconnaissance, interrogation, fingerprints, traces, criminal psychology, disguise, arrest, police-dog use and so on, it stressed practical work such as criminal experiment, pistol shooting, motor-vehicle driving and demolition. What the American agents were proudest of then was the whole set of criminal-experiment equipment brought from America, among it a newest "lie detector," specially used to detect whether an interrogated person's words were true.

I went many times to watch how they used this "lie detector." This machine was very complex, taking up half a room, all electrically operated. The interrogated person sat in a special chair, electric wires bound to chest and pulse. It was said that once the switch was thrown, whether each sentence the person spoke reflected a normal state of mind, what physiological change occurred, the machine's instruments would indicate; the interrogator had only to watch these and could judge whether the person was lying or confessing honestly. The American agents treated this machine as a treasure. After watching many times, I felt then it was too unreliable! For an experienced man who kept his composure it became a saviour: so long as he was not tense when lying and his heart beat quite normally, the instruments showed a record beyond doubt. And a raw beginner or someone with heart trouble might, even speaking honestly, be muddled for life. Some, once seated in that chair, were already tense past measuring, not knowing what it was about, and the pointers and automatic recording showed a wild, disorderly high-and-low — which meant he was not telling "the honest truth"!

I recall that just after this machine arrived, they wanted to show off American scientific progress before Dai Li, and after installing it invited Dai Li to watch, and I went along. It happened those days that the Juntong general-affairs department's Honglu warehouse within SACO had a theft; the warehouse guards suspected several hired porters and detained them all, but it could not be settled, no one admitting to the theft. I suggested to Dai handing these men over for them to experiment on, to perform for everyone on the spot. Dai agreed at once and had me send a car to fetch these men from the guard-corps headquarters to the criminal-experiment room. The first dragged to the chair, thinking he was to be electrocuted, at once cried out to heaven and kicked up a fuss; after questioning for over half an hour, the several American agents working the machine were dripping with sweat, and in the end said only "this man's testimony is not honest." Dai Li, watching impatiently, made an excuse and left. As I saw him to the car he said to me with a smile: "So much trouble — not nearly as effective as our unscientific methods!" Eager to clear up this theft, I went back to the experiment room and patiently waited for them to test each man. The result was that every one was "not honest, gravely suspect," and in the end all had to be detained for further investigation. Half a month later this warehouse theft was wholly cleared up: it was the warehouse-managing agents stealing from their own charge, and infighting over the uneven split led them to denounce one another, and only so were these innocents — who told the honest truth but were judged by this machine to be dishonest and gravely suspect — released. After this first test showed the "lie detector" itself lying, Dai Li took no interest in it, though he much praised the Americans' other torture instruments.

There was also a "strong-light interrogation device" shipped from America, made of several extremely bright electric lamps. After a fair time under its glare, the interrogated person's nerves were so stimulated that he lost control and talked wildly, and the interrogator could find contradictions in his many words and press on. There were besides several sets of new electric-torture equipment; these were not only handier than the electric-torture instruments the Juntong had always made from hand-crank telephones but very handsome, like a neat little radio, able to control the current's strength, using different levels on people of different builds, so that however long it was used the victim would not faint but only suffer more and more, and though the pain was such that sweat poured and even semen ran out, he could still speak, which was most useful for interrogation. These American torture instruments had the further great feature that though used many times, the victim's nervous system and heart function were gravely injured while not the least mark showed on the surface. For these reasons, afterwards, apart from a few students specially trained in using, working and repairing the "lie detector," most of the special-police students learned other American interrogation methods. The American agents also gave the special-police students many American handcuffs and leg-irons for practice. Dai Li was delighted to see them, holding them lighter, more reliable and more portable than the Juntong's own. Hearing this, the American agents at once presented five thousand pairs to the Juntong. Afterwards these American handcuffs and leg-irons, apart from a batch the Juntong kept, were mostly distributed to the open secret-service organs everywhere. In the great massacre at SACO before liberation, many of the martyrs shot down still wore these on their hands and feet.

The special-police class had over a hundred police dogs for practice, all shipped to Chungking by sea, air and land from America. Dai Li had thought these "living teaching materials" could be bred by the Juntong's kennel to save shipping tonnage for other materials, but the American instructors would not agree. Having looked at the Juntong's police dogs, they judged even the number-one dog Dai Li most praised, called Xiaolin, impure in breed and unfit, and decided to ship them all from America. Only the horses for training mounted police in the security-police department, too inconvenient to ship from America, were bought in a batch from Xinjiang, but the American agents felt even these unfit and used them reluctantly. They held that mounted police made of the finest horses were far cleverer at dispersing demonstrating crowds than the Nationalist secret-service organs' use of fire-engine hoses to spray the crowd. Dai Li valued this new method too, and the security-police students in practice often drilled the techniques of riding tall horses to charge into crowds and beat people's heads with leather-covered truncheons. The special-police class's first course had just finished its year's training when the victory came, and Dai Li at once assigned them to police bureaus and inspectorates in big cities across the country, and had me prepare a rich gift for each of the American instructors who had devoted themselves to the training, to thank them for their pains. The second course, because SACO ended and the American instructors all went home, kept the original methods but retained the best of the first course's students as assistant instructors. But as this course's students had not been personally taught by the American instructors, on graduating they were not welcomed everywhere as the first had been. SACO's earliest training class was the weather-personnel class. Because the American naval and air forces were eager to grasp the mainland's weather, it was set up in the winter of 1942 before the first contract was signed. Of this class I know little, only that it ran four courses in all, specially training weather-survey technicians. Besides the American experts, the Chinese side engaged two weather experts to help. Each course had thirty to fifty students, mostly selected from the Juntong wireless training class, all posted to weather units everywhere on graduating. Another early class, set up in the spring of 1943, was the "assistant-instructor training class." This class was to train the American instructors' assistants for the Sino-American special technical classes everywhere, wholly the American agents' charge. Headed concurrently by Miles, the students called it the "Miles training class," to show they were trained wholly by the American agents and set apart from the Juntong's other classes. This class ran only one course of over fifty students, selected from the Juntong's other classes' graduates, and on graduating Miles presented each a dagger engraved "Presented by Miles." The students were mostly assigned as assistant instructors in the Sino-American classes.

What SACO ran most of were the "Sino-American special technical training classes." These were all headed concurrently by Dai Li, with a Juntong deputy head, the Americans sending only a chief instructor and instructors. Each class had the same organization: under the head and deputy head, an American chief instructor's office, a dean, and teaching-affairs, political-training and general-affairs sections. The students came drawn from the armed secret-service forces the Juntong led, and on finishing training the Americans issued American weapons and equipment in full to the actual number trained. The weapons issued were all carbines, Thompson submachine guns or UD submachine guns, with a few rocket launchers, automatic pistols and revolvers. The training's main content was the use of American weapons, plus demolition, reconnaissance, guerrilla tactics and tactics for coordinating with American landings. These were all the American instructors' charge; only political training was the Juntong agents' charge. Each course was mostly about three months. Where there were many people, a second course was begun before the first ended, generally one course after another. From 1943 to 1945 these classes trained and equipped over fifty thousand armed agents, all reorganized into the Traffic Police Corps during the war of liberation.

These classes were numbered in order of founding, but were usually named after their location. The first, at Xiongcun in Shexian, Anhui, near where the Juntong's Loyal and Patriotic Army was based, so the First Sino-American special technical training class was generally called the Xiongcun class. Its deputy head was Guo Lüzhou, dean Yu Wanxuan, teaching-affairs head Wu Weisheng, general-affairs head Zhang Dongsheng, political-training head unknown. The class had over thirty American instructors. The forces trained were all members of the Loyal and Patriotic Army's columns. This class ran longest, ending only at the victory, and trained the most, about eight thousand. In the autumn of 1945 Dai Li accompanied Miles to inspect it, and was well pleased. At the first course's opening ceremony, the Nationalist Third War Zone commander Gu Zhutong went in person to speak. The secret-service forces that raced into Shanghai first after the victory were trained by this class, then called by the Juntong the best-equipped, strongest basic force.

The Second Sino-American special technical training class was set up at Nanyue, Hunan, in the autumn of 1943; in 1944, as the Japanese attacked northern Hunan and the Nationalist forces gave way step by step, this class fled at once to Hongjiang in western Hunan, but was generally still called the Nanyue class, not the Hongjiang class. Its deputy head was Tao Yishan, dean Guo Zongfen, political-training head Su Yeguang, general-affairs head Du Cangzhou, teaching-affairs head unknown. The forces then trained were mainly the Military Affairs Commission Special Operations Army sixth column of He Jiyuan and the bandit forces of Chen Shihu the Juntong had recruited in Hunan. After training Chen Shihu's forces, Tao Yishan, seeing these bandits were no few, wanted to swallow them himself and fell out badly with Chen. On the surface they still called each other brother, but secretly each played his tricks: first they denounced each other to Dai Li, and Dai fobbed both off. It came at last to Chen Shihu preparing to send men to assassinate Tao Yishan, and Tao, on hearing it, hurried to Chungking to report Chen's various illegal doings in Hunan, saying Chen was too ambitious and would, once he had American equipment, set up as a mountain king. When Chen Shihu's forces were trained he had bribed the American instructors, and the weapons equipped were not only better than He Jiyuan's but had more ammunition. Dai Li believed Tao's words, and fearing Chen Shihu would defect and cost him face, agreed to Tao's proposal to have Chen Shihu secretly killed and his forces reorganized.

The Third Sino-American class was set up at Fengxue Temple in Linru county, Henan, in 1943; in 1944, because of the war, it moved to Niudong near Xi'an, and was generally called the Niudong class. Its deputy head was in succession Wen Qiang and Yang Wei; the dean in succession Jin Shuyun and Zhang Shuxun; the teaching-affairs head Chang Huier, political-training head Liu Kedi, general-affairs head Huang Jubai. The forces trained and equipped were the Military Affairs Commission Special Operations Army columns of Liao Zongze, Yue Zhuyuan, Yang Wei and others. In 1944 Dai Li and Miles inspected this class together. It then had about forty American instructors. Both Dai Li and Miles valued this class greatly, for it lay in the Nationalists' key anti-Communist strategic region, so it was decided to make it the base for training anti-Communist secret-service armed forces in the Northwest.

The Fourth Sino-American class was at Shanba in Suiyuan, generally called the Shanba class. Its deputy head was Gao Rong, dean Huang Kangyong, the other section heads unknown. Not long after this class was set up, Gao fell out with the American chief instructor, and Dai Li repeatedly rebuked Gao Rong and told him to heed the Americans; Gao accepted on the surface but secretly still ran his own set, and in the end Dai dismissed Gao and replaced him with Qiao Jiacai. This class ran only a few courses before ending.

The Fifth Sino-American class was at the old site of the Juntong Xifeng special training class at Xifeng, Guizhou. Its deputy head was in succession Deng Kuangyuan and He Efang, training the Special Operations Army column of Xu Guangying. Dai Li and Miles inspected this class too.

The Sixth Sino-American class was near the headquarters of the Nationalist Thirtieth Army Group of Wang Lingji at Xiushui county, Jiangxi. Its deputy head was Tang Xin. It specially trained the Special Operations Army columns of Yang Yuchun, Sheng Yu and others, and some Juntong action corps and demolition corps. At each course's opening Wang Lingji went to speak. This class did not end until the victory. When the Japanese surrendered and Wang Lingji led his forces into the Wuhan city area, Tang Xin followed with his action corps and over thirty American instructors, driving to Wuhan. The Japanese forces then in Wuhan, finding American officers with Tang's forces, refused to let them into the city, and these American instructors returned crestfallen to Chungking.

The Seventh Sino-American class was at Zhangzhou, Fujian, its deputy head in succession Lei Zhenzhong and Chen Dayuan, the rest unknown.

The Eighth Sino-American class was at Yuhu in Rui'an county, Zhejiang, its deputy head Zhao Shirui, training the Loyal and Patriotic Army force of Zhang Weibang and Juntong action corps, the rest unknown.

The Ninth Sino-American class was at Jian'ou, Fujian; only its deputy head, Lin Chao, is known, the rest unknown.

The Tenth Sino-American class was at Linquan, Anhui. Only this class's head was not Dai Li but Tang Enbo concurrently. The deputy head Zhou Linxiang, dean Liu Qingsheng, political-training head Qian Mengqi and so on were still Juntong men. It mainly trained the guerrilla forces led by Tang Enbo's Su-Lu-Yu-Wan Border Region command and Zhou Linxiang's Juntong action corps. This class was set up at Tang's proposal to Dai Li; Tang and Dai had always been on good terms, and to respect Tang, Dai did not hold the headship himself, but not long after it was set up the victory ended it, and it ran only a few courses.

Besides these ten Sino-American special technical training classes specializing in training armed secret-service forces, a Sino-American demolition-personnel training class was also set up at Gangkou in Chun'an, Zhejiang. Its deputy head was Mao Sen. This class too had the Americans send special instructors and supply training equipment to help train.

There were also two courses of a Sino-American medical-personnel training class run at Dong'an, Hunan. Its deputy head was Fu Rong. This class was the American agents' training of military surgeons for these armed secret-service forces.

After the victory, by the first contract SACO should have ended. But to keep a batch of traitor agents experienced in anti-Communism, Dai Li asked the Americans to set up, still in SACO's name, a Sino-American special-police-personnel training class each in Shanghai and Peiping. This request at once got American agreement: besides keeping a batch of American instructors to run the training, they agreed to supply the equipment the two classes needed, to gild this batch of northern and southern traitor agents into American-Chiang agents. After Dai Li took over the great buildings of the Japanese navy club at Jiangwan, Shanghai, and the Japanese secret-service organ in Peiping, feeling the scale still not grand enough, he enlarged them further. As the Shanghai building work was too great, before it was finished Dai Li had died, and only Zhao Zhixi being decided as deputy head, it was stopped while still in preparation. The Peiping special-police class, headed in succession by Lou Zhaoyuan and Qiao Jiacai as deputy head, had recruited over three hundred students and was about to train when it too was hastily ended by Dai's death. After Zheng Jiemin took over as Juntong director, fearing attacks from all sides and much troubled, these two classes for training traitor agents were not carried on.

### The American Agents' Activities

The American agents posted to SACO, besides their open SACO posts, all had another task; the plainest was using SACO as a base to develop intelligence work in Asia and strengthen their secret-service ties with the Southeast Asian countries. Some of this work they carried on directly, some through the Juntong's connections.

I recall that the first to have dealings with SACO's American agents was the British secret-service organization operating in Burma and India. The particulars I do not know, only that once I happened to hear the agents doing affairs work say three British guests from afar had stayed a few days and left. I went to ask Dai Li if he knew of it. He answered that he only learned of it after the British had left, because the Americans had not told him beforehand. Over this Dai Li was a little put out, feeling the Americans should not have failed to consult him.

Before long a French agent operating in Indochina wanted to come to SACO to make contact with the Americans, and the American agents notified Dai Li first, and Dai was pleased to hear it. The French agent's name I have forgotten, only that his wife was called Maria, the daughter of the Vietnamese emperor. To entertain this Vietnamese princess and her consort, Dai Li held a grand banquet and prepared a rich gift for the princess, who was short, only two-thirds her husband's height. But Dai Li met this French agent only at the banquet and chatted a while; when the American and French sides formally discussed matters, he was not invited. Though much pained, he had to put on an air of indifference, and from his losing his temper and cursing people that day on a pretext, one could see his mood. Later a Thai delegation led by Wan Sha came to Chungking, and made contact first with the Juntong; Dai Li found a small Western-style house at Huangjiayakou for the six or seven of them to live in. Dai took me there many times to arrange things, and they were entertained very attentively. When the Americans learned of it, they quickly pulled these people over, on the surface having them make contact with SACO, in fact meaning to use them directly. Once the Thai delegation had linked up with the American secret-service organization, feeling it more advantageous than dealing with the Juntong, they threw themselves into the American embrace. Dai Li was the more displeased, but as he still had to rely on the Americans in everything, he could only bear it.

The American agents used the convenience of the Sino-American secret-service cooperation to carry on secret-service work in China, at first still secretly, later openly. For instance, when American drivers carried weapons and materials to the Southeast and Northwest, according to some Juntong drivers who went along, these Americans not only paid close attention to the highway traffic but at every rest inquired into local customs, mountains and rivers, and products. The American instructors posted to the Sino-American classes carried on espionage anytime, anywhere, sending reports directly to the Americans in Chungking by wireless sets they brought themselves. One most concrete activity I took part in myself: the disappearance of the Zhejiang University professor Fei Gong in the spring of 1945, over which the American agents searched for nearly two months, which I know best. From this one thing the American agents' various activities in China can be shown.

The affair went thus: the progressive Zhejiang University professor Fei Gong came to Chungking at Fudan University's invitation to lecture in the spring of 1944. One morning Fei Gong was at the Qiansimen dock in Chungking about to take a boat to Beibei, with a Fudan student along. As neither had breakfasted, the student asked Fei Gong to stay at the dock and watch the luggage while he went ashore to buy breakfast. When the student returned with the breakfast, Fei Gong was nowhere to be found, and still no shadow of him at sailing time, so he had to go back alone to report at the school. After a day's wait, Fei Gong still not at the school, Fudan reported to the Chungking Garrison Headquarters and asked it to search. The Garrison Headquarters' way of handling it was to question this student who had waited for the boat with Fei, and asking round and round got no result.

By this time Fei Gong's disappearance drew the notice of many in education. Judging he must have been secretly seized by those lawless agents, they undertook rescue efforts, hoping for an early release. A week passed without any news. As it grew, many professors, feeling personal safety so unguaranteed, feared for themselves. Later, when Chiang Kai-shek learned of it he asked Dai Li. Dai answered that the Juntong had not seized the man. That evening Dai gathered the Central Statistics Bureau director Ye Xiufeng and the military-police commander Zhang Zhen at the Juntong bureau's Jifang office to meet, and these two units' secret-service heads also flatly denied secretly arresting Fei Gong. Chiang Kai-shek was ready to let it drop and ignore it.

But a university professor vanishing suddenly with no cause — besides Fei Gong's family and friends' extreme concern, some progressive opinion in society also raised reproaches, and still the reactionaries ignored it. Then forty university professors who had studied in America with Fei Gong jointly petitioned the American Far East theatre chief of staff Wedemeyer in China, asking him to come forward to rescue this American-educated professor. This indeed drew Wedemeyer's notice, and he went in person to ask Chiang Kai-shek. Chiang Kai-shek, though answering that he had not seized the man, was still uneasy and once more had Dai Li investigate closely, again without result, only torturing the accompanying student severely once more.

When Chiang Kai-shek, having got Dai Li's answer again, told Wedemeyer, Wedemeyer, wishing to establish American prestige in China, decided to hand the affair to Miles, hoping the American agents could find Fei Gong. Miles felt it very thorny then and sought Dai Li's counsel, hoping to accomplish it with Dai's help. Once Dai agreed, Miles sent out a New York detective in SACO, Major Clark, to take charge of the work. Dai Li had me assist, and praised me to Miles as a former famous Shanghai detective — for before the war, doing secret-service work in Shanghai, I had used detective work as cover. In sending me, Dai Li enjoined again and again that if a reliable lead were found I must first get Fei Gong into our hands, not let Clark get him, lest Dai lose face before Chiang Kai-shek. Dai also told me not to offer many ideas but to watch how this American detective went about it. So when Clark and I first met to discuss, I asked how he meant to proceed. He proposed first investigating at Fudan and Zhejiang University; I agreed to go first to Zhejiang University to learn about Fei Gong's past.

The next day he and I, with a SACO interpreter Pan Jingxiang, set out from Chungking for Zunyi, Guizhou, first to see the Zhejiang University president Zhu Kezhen. I recall that day Mr. Zhu received us very impatiently in the president's office, and after I explained our errand he spoke directly in English with Clark, answering the American detective's questions about Fei Gong. I recall Mr. Zhu told us most definitely that Fei could absolutely not be found at Zunyi, and that to find the man it were best to return to Chungking and inquire of the government organs specializing in arresting and imprisoning political prisoners, where a satisfactory answer might be had. But we were not satisfied with so definite an answer, and asked him to introduce Fei's connections at Zunyi and elsewhere, that we might learn more from many sides. Mr. Zhu thought a long while, and after asking others present, had us go to a relative of Fei's at nearby Meitan county to learn something. We also asked him for a recent photo of Fei Gong. The next day we drove to Meitan and saw a relative of Fei's and several who knew him, and they said much as Mr. Zhu had — that Fei's thinking was very progressive, that he often spoke against the government, and that the Zhejiang University students all respected him.

Returning from Meitan, I sought out Chen, the head of the Zunyi group of the Juntong's Guizhou station, to ask. He told me Fei had always shown himself very radical among the Zhejiang University professors, that besides the Juntong the Central Statistics Bureau watched him closely too and had posted agents on him, and that this time going to Chungking he might have had Central Statistics agents along. He judged that if the Juntong had not arrested him, he might well have been secretly arrested by the Central Statistics Bureau.

We stayed nearly a week at the Juntong's plant at Maocaopu, Zunyi, refining vegetable oil into petrol, then returned to Chungking. Clark held Zhu's advice to inquire of the government organs well worth heeding. When we reported together to Dai Li and Miles on the Zunyi investigation and Zhu's view, Miles too held that an inquiry of the Chungking public-security organs might clear it up; and if not, Wedemeyer could answer the forty American-educated professors who had petitioned him. Dai Li could only agree to have me again accompany Clark to inspect the arrest records since Fei Gong's disappearance at the Chungking inspectorate, the police bureau's criminal-police department and so on, and where necessary to take Fei's photo to check against the prisoners arrested in that period.

On our way out I quietly asked Dai Li what to do if Clark wanted to see the Juntong's detention house within SACO. Hearing this he pulled a long face and answered sternly: "They want to please these dozens of American-educated professors, and we can go along with everything else; but if they mention seeing our detention house, you just answer flatly that these are all people locked up long ago, none arrested recently." After a pause he added: "We didn't seize Fei Gong — you know that well; how would the question come up?"

When we had turned over all the records of the inspectorate, the criminal-police department and so on and found not the least lead, these units' heads suggested that he might have slipped into the water and drowned, so he could not be found anywhere. Clark thought this likely too, and went with me to the dock to investigate, then to a place at Tangjiatuo downstream on the Yangtze that specialized in recovering corpses, and even dug up over a dozen recently recovered unclaimed bodies to check. The weather was very hot, and we spent two days in the graveyard near Tangjiatuo carefully checking those dozen and more bodies rotten and stinking, none of which could even barely be linked to Fei Gong, and returned disappointed.

Hearing there was no result, Wedemeyer was displeased and had Miles keep investigating. He held that whatever unit had seized him, once it was confirmed, he had the power to get him back. Just then the Chungking Garrison Headquarters suddenly received an anonymous letter signed by a Zhejiang University student ××, saying he had himself seen the missing professor Fei Gong crossing at Wushan county, dressed as a monk, and that on being recognized Fei had enjoined him to tell no one, for he had seen through the vanity of the world and resolved to leave it, and made the student swear secrecy. The Garrison Headquarters, anxious and without a way to answer — for a university professor gone missing could not be found, and the American masters had come forward to inquire, truly leaving no way to excuse itself — on getting this letter sent men overnight from the inspectorate to search. Before those men returned, Miles too got the news and wanted to send men, and Dai Li again had me accompany Clark to Wushan county. When the county government heard the foreign gentleman wanted a monk, it was ready to order every township and town to send all the monks of every temple in Wushan county to the county for us to check in person. Clark and I both disapproved of this beating-the-grass-to-startle-the-snake method and decided to go to the temples ourselves. We spent over half a month, going through the twelve peaks of Wushan, visiting dozens of temples large and small, and still could not find a monk who might be Fei Gong. We stayed two days at the Wushan crossing, watching the crossing traffic, and did not see this professor come by either, and returned disappointed.

In the end, thanks to victory in the war against Japan, when the news came and the Americans made ready to go home, the affair too was let drop. Right up to after liberation I never heard of Fei Gong's whereabouts, and this university professor's disappearance remained a mystery throughout.

Here, finally, a word on the American agents' misdeeds in SACO. The American agents working in SACO, forever running wild everywhere, were often surrounded by the people who cried to beat them, and fled in disorder. Only inside SACO's bounds did they dare swagger, for their pursuers dared not step past the line, fearing to be harmed for nothing. These agents later found that many American airmen carried on their backs a talisman the Nationalist government issued, bearing twelve large Chinese characters: "This foreigner has come to China to help in the war; give him every protection." From these lettered-back airmen they learned this talisman's benefit and wished to get one. Hearing the interpreters' report, Dai Li considered it, and held that SACO being a special organ this method was not used, and changed to an armband bearing both countries' insignia, and had a batch of light-blue badges made bearing "SACO" in white letters for them to wear. The Chungking police and open secret-service Juntong men, seeing American troops wearing such badges and armbands, knew them for SACO men, and had to defer to and protect them in any illegal act, so they grew ever more brazen and unrestrained.

Just before the war's end, the American agents busied themselves with business. Some who ran timber, paper and sundries businesses in America sought people to sign contracts, hoping to find someone in China to sell their goods. Some China hands who had been pastors and priests in China, no few moved to SACO during the war, went back to their old posts after the victory. Among them was one American agent, Ren Fugen, who had operated for years in western Sichuan; after liberation he was arrested for espionage and not released home until 1956, and he too had once worked in SACO.

### The Criminal Activities of Miles

Miles, the American agent chief who came to China in the name of helping China fight Japan, this executioner whose hands were steeped in the Chinese people's blood, the Chinese people will not forget their hatred of. In his few years in China he not only did his utmost to help the Juntong train great numbers of American-style agents and select senior Juntong agents to study in America, to strengthen the Juntong's suppression of the Chinese people's revolution, and supplied great quantities of every kind of killing weapon to slaughter the Chinese people, training and equipping tens of thousands of armed secret-service forces for the Juntong, meaning to strengthen the reactionaries' obstruction of the Chinese people's liberation. Many undercover agent organizations uncovered after liberation had close ties with Miles's past criminal activities in China. He was a very sinister and cunning man, and a China hand. In his few years "cooperating" with Dai Li, the master-servant relationship was managed very well: the master doted on the servant, and the servant was extremely deferential. He understood Dai Li's character well: on the surface Dai loved to win and show his strength, at bottom he had another set. So whenever he and Dai Li were at a meeting with senior Juntong agents present, he never gave Dai Li a setback directly. When a matter could not be settled and he had to insist, he always put it off with "let me think it over," and back at his office at once notified Dai Li by written memorandum. On getting such a notice wholly against his view, Dai, though he might lose his temper a while, in a moment had to do exactly as the notice said, not daring to insist on his own view. Because Miles never embarrassed Dai Li before his subordinates, Dai could often boast to them that between him and Miles all was equal, that sometimes Miles even had to heed him. But heaven knows, in those years I never once saw a matter of any size that Dai Li could decide himself without heeding Miles. As I understand it, Dai Li once wished to set up a Sino-American class at Liuzhou or Kweilin in Guangxi; Miles thought it needless, and Dai could only defer to him and send the Guangxi secret-service forces to Xifeng, Guizhou, for training. Again, Dai Li wished the SACO weapons more concentrated at Chungking, but Miles wanted more allotted to the Southeast coast to support an American landing there. Miles insisted on a great weapons warehouse at Guiyang, so that the weapons shipped to Kunming could be split to Guizhou and thence more quickly to the Southwest. Again, Dai Li had never liked being photographed, and Miles made him change and let the American agents photograph him at will.

Miles understood Dai Li's psychology well. To be able to use the Juntong forever for secret-service work in China, besides spreading in America various myth-like tales of Dai Li and his role at Chiang Kai-shek's side, which made Dai feel wholly at ease, he did his utmost to urge Dai Li to travel to America. And the boon that made Dai Li most grateful and unforgettable for life was that during the Cairo Conference, President Roosevelt asked Chiang Kai-shek in person to see this Chinese Himmler. Hearing this, Dai Li knew it was Miles's puffing him in America that made the master of his master value him so, and felt Miles bore all the more on his future prospects, and grew the more submissive.

Miles took pains not only over Dai Li but also over the senior agents in SACO and the department-head-level Juntong agents, doing his utmost to play the host. No few senior agents got watches, clothes and the like as his gifts, and all could get some cigarettes and chocolate. A few got pistols, carbines and Thompson submachine guns as gifts. As I dealt with him often in affairs work, he even gave me a jeep engine I could assemble into a car myself. In giving these things he always had a certificate typed up and signed to avoid misunderstanding.

From the day SACO was formally founded, Miles raised his seven-star flag on the open ground before his office at Zhongjiashan, until SACO ended. Except while home recuperating and out inspecting, he had it raised early each morning and taken down only at dusk. I was very curious about this narrow little pennant, less than two ma long and a few cun wide. It was white with a red border, seven red stars in a row, resembling neither the American flag nor China's old marshal's-character flag. After much inquiry I learned its origin. It turned out that when Miles was a naval captain, he once went to suppress a fleet of Filipinos resisting America in arms; when his warship's guns had sunk the anti-American armed vessels fleeing on the sea, the Filipinos crowded onto many little boats to flee, and these were soon sunk one by one too. On his triumphant return he met one boat carrying the most people struggling to flee on the sea, and he ordered the ship not to fire but to follow close, and only when within machine-gun range did he sweep it with a machine gun himself. When all aboard were killed, he swept the boat's bottom too, and blood welled from seven bullet holes in the little white boat. Delighted at this scene, the next day he added this seven-star flag to his warship to mark this victory. Thereafter this flag became his personal emblem too. In dealing with subordinates, Miles constantly urged Dai Li to learn his set of sham-democratic methods. For Dai always stressed personal prestige and wished his subordinates to fear him, so he constantly used beating, cursing, imprisonment and even execution to show his power and make others dare not defy his will. Miles differed from this; even in rebuking subordinates he often did not use the crudest way. Two things I recall most clearly. One happened in the Geleshan area: a couple just out of university, unable to find work in Chungking and unable to live, and reckoning they could speak a few words of English, borrowed money from relatives to open a small tavern on Geleshan behind SACO, hoping to earn these foreigners' money, dealing with the American agents all day. One night, while the man went to fetch goods, several American agents gang-raped the woman proprietor; the man, returning, would not stand for it and complained to SACO. Learning of it, Miles only found the rapists and scolded them, punishing them with a month's ban on leave; and had the interpreter ask me to tell the local police to come forward, pay a little money and tell them not to do business nearby again, and if they would not heed, then in future when something happened it would not be seen to. As the local police substation and post were then in the Juntong agents' hands, a word settled such a gang-rape case easily. Another time, just after SACO was set up and it was preparing to requisition nearby dwellings and land by force, Dai Li one day accompanied Miles with me and two interpreters looking over the terrain on the hillside behind, when we suddenly heard from behind a small hill a woman's frightened cries and a man's wild laughter. Dai Li, anxious that the Juntong agents were running wild and would cost him face before the American master, quickly signalled me with his eyes to go and see. Miles too, hearing the sounds, was eager to learn what it was and walked toward the spot with me. The moment we entered the little hollow, a middle-aged woman in tattered clothes came running wildly, two American agents in army fatigues chasing close. Seeing our group, the woman stopped, one hand smoothing her tousled hair, the other clutching the torn front of her dress over her chest. Dai Li was rather angry then, for he always allowed himself to run wild but demanded discipline of subordinates; yet toward the Americans he dared show nothing, and only watched to see how Miles would deal with these two who had insulted a woman. When the interpreter told Miles the poor bullied woman's words, he burst out laughing and asked the two agents in a very warm voice: "Molesting a woman like this — aren't you afraid she's dirty?" Then he too said only a few light words, and at the last patted them on the back and sent them off. Dai Li, seeing this handling, much disapproved. Miles saw his mind at a glance. When I had given the woman a little money and sent her off, they sat on some loose stones and talked. Miles took the chance to urge Dai Li to learn America's way with subordinates, telling him not to beat and curse at every turn, but to make subordinates submit not only with their mouths but, more important, with their hearts.

Dai nodded at the time and said he was willing to accept it, but afterwards I rarely saw him truly do so. Only before Miles was Dai's wild shouting and cursing of subordinates somewhat restrained, and he often changed instead to the harshest words, only a little milder in manner, so that people could not easily see he was in a towering rage; but these words often frightened the hearer the more. Once, to entertain the Vietnamese princess who had come with the French agent, he had a SACO affairs subsection chief arrange a great reception room, wanting sixty rattan sofas (because it was hot and he had heard Vietnamese liked rattan seats), but the man prepared ordinary soft sofas. When he walked into this temporary reception room with me and saw it, he flew into a rage at once, and was about to kick that affairs subsection chief when I saw Miles come in by another door and quickly told him. He at once changed his manner and said fiercely: "If you don't get all the rattan sofas ready for me tonight, you won't be alive tomorrow." After greeting Miles, fearing he had not said enough, he added: "Fail and I'll have your life!" This subsection chief, trembling all over, after Dai and Miles walked off begged me privately to think of a way, for so many things at once truly could not be scraped together. In the end I asked the inspectorate and detective-corps agents' help, and forcibly borrowed the rattan sofas from several furniture shops for one use. When Miles walked in with the guest and saw the whole arrangement changed in two hours, he praised Dai Li especially.

Where Miles most satisfied Dai Li was in supplying what SACO and the Juntong needed. Dai Li, in stating a figure, often feared it too much and was embarrassed to open his mouth, and Miles always added some on his own, sometimes giving extra besides, especially weapons, ammunition and explosives, always leaving Dai Li satisfied beyond expectation. And the American secret-service organ's scheme of using Chinese to kill Chinese was further realized through Miles. That Miles rose three grades from lieutenant commander in a few years shows how satisfied the Americans were with this agent chief's results.

### The US Navy and Army Struggle for Control of SACO

Within America, over control of SACO, the contradiction between navy and army was never resolved from first to last.

After Miles came to China and made contact with Dai Li, learning that the Juntong, this huge secret-service organization, had not only every strong privilege but organs spread everywhere, and that if work could be carried on through it America could effortlessly get all it needed, this discovery of Miles's at once drew the army's notice. The navy, fearing the army would step in, as soon as SACO was negotiated declared by open order: the navy organs in China were a working unit of the American Pacific fleet and should operate under the fleet commander-in-chief's direct direction. The aim of this declaration was plain: SACO should be controlled by the navy alone, no other unit to interfere.

The navy's declaration produced the opposite result — "no three hundred taels of silver buried here." The army, which had not much noticed before, now, through the American military attaché and others, learned in depth that this was a fine chance to reach deep into every part of China, on no account to be let the navy monopolize, and at once issued its own statement: all American forces organs in China should be under the American China-Burma-India theatre command and subject to the American chief of staff's control. The chief of staff then was Marshall, who from the outset opposed SACO being an independent organ directed straight by the navy.

Another who wished to control SACO was the head of the Office of Strategic Services, Donovan. This office had originally been the American forces' intelligence coordination office, reorganized and enlarged into two organs — the military intelligence bureau and the OSS. Donovan had always been fairly close to the army, so as to the army's wish to control SACO he not only strongly favoured it but hoped to seize the chance to place SACO in the OSS's grasp. As soon as SACO was set up he made Miles the OSS's Far East representative besides, to control Miles through and through.

This scheme of Donovan's at once met the opposition of the American navy department and Dai Li. Donovan then had the army come forward and, through Miles, proposed sending a batch of instructors to China to help SACO train the secret-service armed forces the Juntong controlled, and also sought to set up a base in the Northwest, sending out Barnes and Dr. John Hayden and others to China. Miles at once told Dai Li that as Donovan's people came without first seeking SACO's consent, they should be firmly refused, and the army's ready instructors all had to turn back halfway. Donovan did not give up on that account, so when SACO signed its second supplementary contract in 1944 he acted as the American presider, seeking to have the OSS replace naval intelligence in controlling SACO, and recommended the OSS's Colonel Coughlin as SACO's second American deputy head, which Miles and Dai Li likewise refused.

After Wedemeyer succeeded Stilwell as American commander of the China-Burma-India theatre, he not only slighted SACO and the Juntong but held them in deep prejudice. He strongly urged placing SACO under the OSS, making the Chinese subordinate, and had long been displeased that a Chinese held the SACO headship. Some American "white supremacists" fanned the flames, while Miles insisted the OSS personnel coming to China be subject to SACO. Wedemeyer went further and demanded SACO be under his command, or he would resign. The dispute went to Washington, where Marshall, representing the army, first favoured Wedemeyer's view, the air force chimed in, and though the navy fought hard it got no result. The American Joint Chiefs of Staff issued an order that the American forces personnel working in SACO, from Miles down, be under Wedemeyer's direct command.

As Miles only acknowledged Wedemeyer's command on the surface and in fact ignored it, Wedemeyer's headquarters personnel took the chance to strike at him. Of the about 150 tons of materials SACO needed monthly, in fact not even half was transported. When the navy offered to provide its own planes and vehicles to transport SACO's materials, Wedemeyer blocked it too. The navy admiral Nimitz, in his anger, said he would use warships to ship SACO's materials to India, then over the Ledo Road to Chungking.

The American navy had originally prepared to send three thousand personnel to SACO, but for want of army support about two thousand were stranded in India, unable to fly the Hump. Unwilling to endure the hard highway to China, they dragged on until victory and were sent back from India to America.

At the time even many Americans could not stand SACO's activities in China, for the Juntong and Dai Li were of ill repute, long resented by many enlightened Americans. When the navy department wished to publish in domestic and foreign papers some inflated and fabricated "results," it met the opposition of the American forces' news-releasing organ. Miles, furious over this, prepared to hold a press conference in Chungking to expose publicly the many ugly inner workings of the CBI theatre command and the OSS. In the end, before the press conference could be held, the CBI theatre command and the OSS found out, at once declared Miles deranged, and sent men to escort him home. So in the autumn of 1945, when Chiang Kai-shek inspected SACO, Miles had already been sent off, and Bei Leli, as acting deputy head, presided over the welcome for Chiang.

After Miles returned home, the Pentagon heads declared him stripped of office and reduced from major general to colonel, though the navy still supported him. In late March 1946, when the American admiral Nimitz learned of Dai Li's death in a crash, he prepared to send Miles as representative to China to offer sacrifice. This was heard by Marshall, just back from China, who hurried to the office of Navy Secretary Forrestal to object. His reason was that Dai Li was China's notorious anti-Communist chief, and if America sent an official to Dai's funeral it would affect his mediation of Nationalist-Communist relations. Not only that, even the "postwar navy aid-to-China bill" the American navy had drawn up met the opposition of Marshall and others and could not be realized.

In early 1947 Miles was reappointed by the navy department as colonel captain of the cruiser Columbus, and his request granted to patrol the China coast for a year, so when Dai Li was buried he could hurry from Shanghai to Nanking on hearing the news. When Mao Renfeng took me to the airport to meet him, the moment he saw us his face was streaked with tears. At Dai Li's grave he wept past speech. Plainly he wept not only for his dead friend but also for his own misfortune.

### The Grand Banquets Held to Please the American Agents

Of the grand gatherings SACO held, the Christmas of December 25, 1944, must be reckoned the liveliest. For this Christmas Eve party, a great hall able to hold four thousand was rushed to completion day and night. This hall had not a pillar in the middle nor fixed seats, for at ordinary gatherings people mostly stood to hear the speeches, and only for the party were tables and chairs set up temporarily. In building it, all the electrical materials — amplification, lighting, and the electric stage and so on — were shipped from America. Miles even had dozens of flags of all nations made to order in America to fit the hall. These handsome flags were over a zhang long, of the finest silk, edged with gold fringe. Among them were no German, Italian or Japanese flags, and no Soviet flag either. For this gathering the Juntong flew over sixty musical instruments from India, and the band members were all dressed in uniforms made from the general's-serge the Ministry of Military Administration issued, with new leather shoes and white gloves. The hall's festive decoration began in mid-December. The gifts Dai Li meant to give the American princes were all ready by the end of November. In early December Miles had shipped from America great quantities of dress material for women, tablecloths, sweets, children's toys, and Christmas paper napkins and so on. These things were packed in separate portions in little bamboo baskets. A Christmas tree was brought by special truck from dozens of kilometres away. All the arrangements followed American custom, directed by some ladies who had been to America, wholly in the American way, so that the American agents felt at every turn as if in their own home.

From December 20, Dai Li spent some time daily going to look himself, sometimes inviting Miles too, to see if it suited the master. For the Christmas dinner they had to eat turkey, which Chungking had never had, so they had to fetch the few turkeys kept for show in the zoo and park for the honoured guests, using goose for the rest. Dai Li had wanted a grand ball after the banquet, but as two hundred women guests could not be mustered, it was changed to a variety show after.

That night, at about six, the banquet was held; all three hundred and more American agents in Chungking attended, and the SACO deputy section heads and above and the Juntong deputy department heads and deputy office chiefs and above had to bring their own wives and daughters or daughters-in-law over eighteen, and forty young and pretty women agents were chosen besides as company. Fearing some wives would not dare go, Dai Li had me notify them beforehand that any woman guest who attended, even without dancing, would get a small basket of American gifts, and any invited woman who did not go would be looked into. Once the women entered the hall, Dai Li had them all seated separately among the male agents. Some wives who had never dealt with Americans, jammed among these American ruffians, sat as on pins and needles. After the banquet some wives, so angry they dashed the little basket of gifts to the ground, quarrelled with me, saying I should not have urged them to go and suffer, that it shamed three generations of ancestors. A few, though, felt it a rare chance to attend so grand a banquet.

Over eight hundred were invited to this, seated at five long rows of tables in the hall with a cross-row at the head. Over thirty senior Nationalist military and civil officials were also invited. The band played many popular American tunes, drawing strange whoops of approval from the American agents. When the banquet ended, gifts were exchanged. Miles, in a novel touch, had each woman guest walk up onto the hall's stage to him to receive her gift. Dai Li stood beside him, urging them again and again to show the host warmth and respect. All the women only shook Miles's hand and took the gift and walked down, save one woman agent, Liu Meimei, who had been a taxi-dancer in Hong Kong: at Dai Li's urging she publicly embraced and kissed Miles, at once drawing wild laughter and applause from the American agents. Dai Li had her do it again, and Miles gave her an extra gift. Dai Li hoped the other women would follow Liu Meimei, but in the end no second would do so, leaving him much disappointed, holding these people too out of step with the times. After the gifts, the variety show began; the Peking opera had run half through when some dead-drunk American agents ran onto the stage and grabbed the performers' broadswords and spears and cavorted about. Dai Li went up too, hung on a string of false beard backstage, and shouted and thrashed with them, carrying on till deep in the night. At the last, urging the affairs men to clear the hall, the agent in charge of tableware reported to me with a long face that scores of pairs of chopsticks and silver spoons had been stolen by the American agents as souvenirs!

The Christmas of 1945 was held at the Juntong office on Route Doumer in Shanghai; though fewer attended than at Chungking, it was more lavish, for after the victory Shanghai's supplies were far easier than Chungking's. What especially pleased Dai Li was that the women guests invited were the so-called society beauties, socialites, film stars, famous taxi-dancers, and famous Peking-opera, Shanghai-opera and Yue-opera actresses. They all had a way with American ruffians, so the American agents present were all delighted.

Before the Sino-American special-police class hall was built, the 1943 and 1942 Christmas parties were held in the American personnel's great dining hall in SACO. As the road was not yet through then, part of the way had to be by sedan chair (Sichuanese call it a huagan), so each such party, besides mobilizing all the Juntong's and SACO's huagan to fetch and carry guests, had to send out large numbers of armed agents and police to seize dozens of huagan from Geleshan, Ciqikou and so on for duty. Over a hundred huagan-bearing coolies, once seized, had to carry until the party broke up before being let go. Some, from carrying to and fro without rest, were worn to exhaustion, and if on a wet, slippery road one carelessly jolted a finely dressed woman guest or honoured guest or soiled their clothes, they would fawn and complain to Dai Li, and in a rage Dai would often have the huagan-bearers beaten half to death.

To please these American masters, besides Christmas, at Spring Festival and the Juntong's founding anniversary Four-One meeting, Dai Li held a banquet and party to entertain. When the American presidential representative Hurley and the Seventh Fleet commander Admiral Cooke and others came to inspect SACO, a grand banquet was of course held. Besides, when some American condolence delegation or the like came to SACO to perform or offer comfort, it had to be grandly entertained. Especially, whenever it was heard Miles had been promoted, a celebration had to be held. Every such grand, lavish banquet cost a fearful sum. As I handled general affairs I know this best: such great banquets, held several times a year, cost by weight of gold several hundred taels at most a time, and at least several dozen — one table's cost more than a poor man's grain for a hundred years!

### Chiang Kai-shek Most Admired the American Torture Instruments

In the autumn of 1945 Chiang Kai-shek came to inspect SACO, bringing his second son Chiang Wei-kuo, his secretary Cao Shengfen, the aide-de-camp Pi Zongkan and a dozen or so attendants, and looked over several key work departments in SACO. What Chiang Kai-shek found most interesting was the Sino-American special-police class's American criminal-experiment room. For here were America's newest torture instruments of every kind for interrogating prisoners, and great numbers of fine breed police dogs shipped from America. These things all had great use in suppressing the people and consolidating his rule, so at each new torture instrument, having asked its use and effect, he nodded again and again and praised it without stop.

The main programme Dai Li and Bei Leli (Miles had by then been escorted home, and the American chief of staff Bei Leli was acting deputy head) arranged for Chiang Kai-shek was to show him the various brilliant performances of the special-police class's first eight hundred students. On the newly built great parade ground, a reviewing stand had just been rushed to completion; after Chiang Kai-shek mounted it, on the left the SACO and Juntong middle-and-senior agents were ranged in a line, on the right nearly three hundred neatly uniformed American agents of SACO. These American agents all wore American navy uniform that day; in my years dealing with them I had never once seen them assembled so neatly and correctly. In the centre of the ground stood eight hundred students and over forty American instructors. The moment Chiang Kai-shek appeared, still over a hundred metres from the reviewing stand, Dai Li signalled me to direct eight buglers to sound "attention," holding it until Chiang Kai-shek slowly mounted the stand and returned the salute, then ordered "at ease." As the students began some drill-formation and American-style movements, Chiang's interest was not great. But when the criminal-police items were performed, and Chiang Kai-shek saw the American instructors directing these agent-students in how to pursue and arrest prisoners, mounted police wielding American rubber truncheons charging into a crowd and beating people, and police-dog searches and so on, he was so delighted he danced for joy and cried approval aloud. Seeing this, Dai Li and Bei Leli put their heads together, regretting they had not arranged more such brilliant items to interest Chiang most. The performance ended after nearly an hour, and Chiang Kai-shek, without waiting for Dai Li to invite him, went to the amplifier and gave a speech, Pi Zongkan translating it into English. Beaming, he thanked again and again the American agents who had come from afar to devote themselves to raising these claws and fangs for him, and did his utmost to praise their years of achievement. To these American-trained agents Chiang gave much encouragement, saying over and over that they were not only Director Dai's best students but his own most beloved students, that their responsibility henceforth was very heavy, and they should resolve firmly to accomplish the tasks he gave them. He spoke on for nearly half an hour, then left amid cheers.

### Task Unfinished, Ended Early

By the first contract, upon victory in the war against Japan SACO should end. But Dai Li and Miles held that this intimate cooperation's task was not yet done. For the work outside the contract had only partly been carried out — helping the Juntong race into the occupied areas to take them over first, and blocking the people's anti-Japanese armed forces the Communists led from entering the big cities. Besides, the tasks of training anti-Communist-experienced traitor agents and continuing to help the Juntong fight the Communists with all its strength were not done; and though both sides were reluctant to part, they could not for a time find sufficient reason to carry on.

Only after getting Chiang Kai-shek's permission did Dai Li, in the winter of 1945, first publish SACO's so-called years of results openly in the Nationalist party organ. Miles had assured Dai Li again and again that, to help the Nationalists in future defeat and destroy another enemy fiercer than Japan — the Chinese Communist Party — they would return anytime for still more intimate cooperation with the Juntong. And in helping the Nationalists build a navy to suppress the Chinese people, commanded by Dai Li, they would do their utmost. After Dai Li's death, when Miles hurried from America to offer condolences, he expressed this "generous" wish again and again to Mao Renfeng and Zheng Jiemin. But the Chinese people, under the Communist Party and Chairman Mao, won too fast, and with the deep contradictions between the American navy and army, they forever lost the chance to make a comeback.

As for SACO's winding-up work, it was handled wholly by the Juntong on its behalf, which I have recorded in several other pieces of material, and shall not repeat here.
