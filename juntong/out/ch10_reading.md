## The Juntong's Criminal Activities against the Shaan-Gan-Ning Border Region

The Juntong bureau had always made its secret-service work against the Shaan-Gan-Ning Border Region a priority, and Dai Li often directed this work in person and attached the greatest importance to it. I did not myself have charge of this side of things; only because I had worked in the Juntong for many years, and above all because during the war against Japan I served as head of the General Affairs Department at the bureau's own headquarters and regularly attended the bureau's business meetings and all manner of confidential meetings, I heard something of it from the side, and now and then, in handling some routine matter connected with this work, touched on it a little. What I know is therefore very limited. I did not pay close attention at the time, and it is now many years past, so I cannot give any very detailed account. I can only set down, so far as memory reaches, the scraps I once understood, for reference.

### Some of the Early Activities

Before the war against Japan, the secret-service work against the Border Region by the Special Services Department (the Juntong's predecessor) was in the charge of the Department's Shaanxi provincial station. Before the Xi'an Incident the station chief, Ma Zhichao, regularly sent agents into the Border Region; the station had a Northern Shaanxi group responsible for gathering intelligence on the region. Around 1936, when Ma Zhichao concurrently held the post of chief of the Shaanxi provincial capital police bureau, Dai Li directed him to train a batch of agents especially, so that they could penetrate the Border Region and build up a secret-service organization there. On receiving the order Ma set up a "special police training class" inside the Xi'an police bureau, heading it himself; fifty or sixty men were selected and sent up by the Shaanxi and Gansu provincial stations, and given more than half a year's secret-service training. Dai Li sent Lou Jianru from Nanking to serve as detachment leader, with the Shaanxi Juntong agents Shu Xiang, Zhang Ye, Xu Kai, Chen Ziqiang, Li Yousan and others as instructors. When the training was over, only a few agents, Xue Zhixiang among them, are said to have slipped into Yenan and stayed a short while. Dai Li's original scheme of establishing a "Yenan group" in Yenan came to nothing, because the agents sent in could get no foothold there.

During the Xi'an Incident the Special Services Department's provincial station in Xi'an was broken up, and its agents elsewhere all fled. After the incident the Juntong re-established the provincial station and made fresh dispositions; and though the plan still provided for finding suitable people to penetrate the Border Region and build a group in Yenan, this could not be managed. Only when the war began and the Juntong bureau was formally set up did activity of this kind proceed on a large scale.

### Some Activities Centred on Zhang Guotao

During the war the Juntong hoped that the Communist defector Zhang Guotao could produce results on the anti-Communist side. At first Dai Li believed that, working through the connection Zhang Guotao gave him, "worming in and pulling out" from within the Communist Party itself was bound to be a fairly sure thing, and he pinned on Zhang the whole set of schemes he had for years been unable to realize.

After Chiang Kai-shek assigned Zhang to the Juntong, Dai Li at once set up for him a "Special Political Problems Research Office," with Zhang as its head, to develop and study secret-service work against the Communist Party. Dai did not, however, entirely trust Zhang from the outset, and the Juntong's existing "Communist Section," which dealt specifically with the Communists, remained under the party-and-government intelligence department and was not subordinated to the Special Political Problems Research Office.

Zhang's first proposal after coming to the Juntong was to run a "training class for special political-work personnel," to train a batch of agents for work in the Border Region and for anti-Communist prevention work within the Chiang-controlled areas. Dai very much approved this proposal, and around 1940 set up the class at the Xibufang in Tongjiaqiao, Ciqikou, Chungking, with Dai as concurrent head and Zhang as concurrent deputy head, drawing in for specialized training the agents who had graduated from other secret-service training classes and those in the Juntong who worked specifically in this field. The class ran two courses in all, each with about two hundred men. Zhang Guotao often lectured the students: besides analyses of "Communist problems" and the like, meant to slander and defame the Communist Party, he lectured on how to enter the Border Region and how, once inside, one should carry on.

Zhang had once written a document of some tens of thousands of characters on conditions inside the Communist Party and in the Border Region; besides violently attacking and defaming the Communist leaders, it dealt with a good many questions concerning the region. The Juntong, besides copying it to Chiang Kai-shek, He Yingqin and others, printed it as a booklet for the reference of the Juntong's senior agents (department heads at headquarters, provincial-station and regional chiefs in the field), with orders that it not be circulated and must be returned after reading.

Dai Li was not entirely satisfied with this document of Zhang's, chiefly because he judged it of little use in the matter of how the Juntong might send agents into the Border Region, since Zhang mainly supplied more on conditions at the Communist upper levels. To carry the study of how one could penetrate the region deeper, Dai laid it down that every worker who came out of the Border Region, or anyone who had ever been to Yenan, whom the Juntong's inspection stations arrested, must write a report on "how to penetrate the Border Region," setting out clearly the course of his own journey, the points to watch, and the methods of getting in. The special detention house the Juntong kept at No. 13 Zhishanli in Xi'an was constantly sending material of this kind up to the bureau. Besides hearing the detention house's chief, Ding Minzhi, describe this work, I heard it mentioned by Huang Yuzhong, chief of the transport section of the General Affairs Department, which handled the bureau's central registry, and by Xie Xiao of the receiving-and-dispatching subsection and others. But I never saw what these people had written; I only knew there was such a thing.

At the bureau's business briefings Dai Li often said: "The most effective way to deal with the Communist Party is to have Communists" — meaning defectors — "deal with the Communist Party." So the Juntong used no small number of defectors in its criminal anti-Communist work. Once a man named Lai Guomin was brought from the Xi'an detention house to serve as a judge at the bureau. This man had been a cadre of the Border Region government; unable to bear the hardship, he had fled, been arrested by the Juntong, and after turning traitor had worked in the Xi'an detention house specifically on gathering material about penetrating the Border Region. He was later transferred to the bureau's judicial section, where he specialized in interrogating captured Communists and suspects. The Juntong gathered and worked up a good deal of material on "how to penetrate the Border Region," and in the class Zhang Guotao ran it became an important course.

The students of this class were picked by Dai Li himself and trained for half a year under Zhang Guotao's own direction; but when the course was over, and after examination by Zhang and the others, it was judged that even so, not many were fit to be sent into the Border Region to penetrate Yenan — the exact number I do not know. It is said that after selection only a very few were sent to work at the several ""winning-over" stations" Zhang led, while the great majority were placed on "Communist prevention" duty at some of the important arsenals within the Chiang-controlled areas.

Of the ""winning-over" stations" set up near the Border Region at the time, I remember only three — Hanchung, Luochuan and Yaoxian — and cannot recall the rest. Just who penetrated Yenan afterwards, I do not know either. While I was at the bureau I handled, two or three times, the reception of defectors Zhang claimed to have "won over." Dai Li had me receive and fete these people at the "Shulu," the guest house he used for open receptions and ordinary entertaining (near the main gate of the bureau headquarters at Zaozilanya in Chungking). Of these people's names I remember only one, a man called Zhu Chunrong; the rest I cannot recall. It was always around dinnertime, only two or three men each time, brought along by Zhang in person, with Zheng Jiemin, Tang Zong, Mao Renfeng and others attending as a matter of course.

Because Zhang's training class produced no results, Dai Li was much disappointed with it, and so it ran only two courses before it was stopped.

### Fragments of the Juntong's Activities against the Border Region

Though Dai Li hoped Zhang Guotao would produce some results in anti-Communist work, he did not entirely trust him. The Juntong itself carried on many other schemes besides; and although what I know is not detailed, these scraps may still have some reference value.

Of the Juntong's core agents, the only ones I know to have been to Yenan are Shen Zhiyue and, among those who joined the Juntong later, Qin ×× and Lai Guomin, a few men in all. Shen Zhiyue is said to have gone to Yenan two or three times, and he worked for a while in the Special Political Problems Research Office that Zhang Guotao ran. He was then much valued in the Juntong by Dai Li, Zheng Jiemin, Mao Renfeng and others. He often claimed kinship with me as a fellow member of the "same clan," and though there was some personal warmth between us, he said very little about his time in Yenan. Around 1943, when the Juntong set up a Southeast Special Reconnaissance Station specifically to deal with the New Fourth Army and other anti-Japanese armed forces, Shen was posted there as secretary, and after that we saw little of each other. He had in the past told me only a little, and simply, about life in Yenan and the like, and I only listened out of curiosity, without touching on any concrete work. As for Qin ×× and Lai Guomin, I was often in contact with them, but as there was no deep friendship between us I never questioned them.

The Juntong's activity of this kind relied mainly on the Northern Shaanxi station at Yulin (its chief being Huang Yigong). Dai Li tried to build a "Yenan group" through the Northern Shaanxi station, but never succeeded. I remember one such episode: a batch of agents the Northern Shaanxi station sent into Yenan on secret-service work were discovered and arrested by the Border Region government, released before long, and the station formally notified. Later, at his daily noon work briefing, Dai Li raised this, saying Huang Yigong had no ability and had done a thoroughly disgraceful job; the particulars I can no longer recall.

Another was the Northwest Special Reconnaissance Station at Hanchung, whose chief, Cheng Muyi, was an old colleague of mine from my Shanghai days. This man had always done work against the Communists: before the war he had penetrated a district-committee organization of the Jiangsu province set-up in Shanghai and later destroyed it. Dai Li trusted him greatly, and during the war sent him to Hanchung to run the Northwest Special Reconnaissance Station. Besides sending agents repeatedly to penetrate Yenan and "build a group" — without success — he ran a small training class. Patriotic and progressive young people going to Yenan by the highway, once seized by the Juntong's inspection stations (posts), were in part handed over to him for training; after a spell of poisoning education, these people were sent on to slip into Yenan. Most of them, it is said, gave themselves up to the Border Region government, and only a very few gathered any intelligence for him.

Some of those who went to Yenan, unable to bear it, came out again, and some of these were detained by the inspection stations and sent to the Northwest Special Reconnaissance Station. The main aim was to learn about conditions in the region from their own mouths and to use their Yenan connections, forcing them to supply methods of penetrating the region. When Cheng Muyi was transferred to the Southeast around 1943, changing cars at Chungking, I entertained him. He had a woman and a child with him at the time, and as he told me, the woman (in fact his concubine) was a Communist suspect who had been detained and was later forced to live with him.

Dai Li set great store by the traffic-inspection work in the Northwest, and had once set up a Xi'an sub-office of the Surveillance Department of the Transport Control Bureau, appointing the defector Wang Banglian as its head to run the inspection work in the Northwest region. When Wang was later arrested for embezzlement, Dai, finding the place beyond his reach, abolished the sub-office. The various inspection stations of that time detained patriotic and progressive people bound for Yenan by the thousand over the years. The Juntong's method of sending agents into Yenan was to have them slip in disguised as progressives. As I understand it, many people going to Yenan carried letters of introduction, or documents showing what school they had studied at or what unit they had worked in; when such people were detained, the Juntong confiscated these documents that would serve to get one in, and handed them to disguised agents to impersonate the holders and slip in. By this method the Juntong had upwards of a hundred people go to Yenan. A graduate of the Juntong's Linli special training class, Zhong ××, told me that he had gone to Yenan under someone else's name and studied at the Anti-Japanese University, leaving later because of illness.

To get as clear a picture of Yenan as he could, Dai Li once gave a banquet for the liaison staff officers posted at the Eighth Route Army headquarters, Xu Foguan and Guo Zhongrong, at which I and several other Juntong department heads attended. Before this I had seen a booklet Xu Foguan wrote on conditions in Yenan. Meeting him that time, the impression he gave me was of a man full of big talk and self-important discourse. Before and after the meal he used fabricated material specially to slander and jeer at the Communist leaders as a source of jokes. Later, when Dai Li asked how he had gone about secret-service work in Yenan, he gave off a lot of nonsense to the effect that "it is hard if you call it hard and easy if you call it easy." Dai Li was much taken with this talk of his and had the party-and-government intelligence chief Wang Xinheng and the personnel chief Gong Xianfang write it down. As I dimly recall, he said the hard part was that inspection was not strict but the vetting of one's history was, so that a foothold was hard to gain; the easy part was that anyone in no hurry for results, prepared to lie hidden for the long term, with credentials that would win trust, who once inside disguised himself as a progressive to the utmost and did not act at once, could still fairly easily gain confidence.

The next day Dai again asked Xu to his home for a long talk. That time I did not take part; only Mao Renfeng, Wang Xinheng, Gong Xianfang and others were present. Gong Xianfang and I lodged in rooms across from each other then, and often chatted after work. He told me that Dai Li was always eager to set up a group in Yenan and put in a radio station, but never managed it, and had taken no little scolding over it. After hearing Xu Foguan's views that time, he was thinking of working both ends at once and preparing a batch of people expressly to lie buried inside the Communist Party — time no object, a year or two or even ten would do, so long as they could gain a foothold and inch their way in step by step, and were sure to produce results in the end. For in ordinary times one could neither find the real underground Communist organizations nor easily approach Communists who had shown themselves openly, whereas just now Yenan was recruiting people in great numbers and turning no one away — a chance not to be met with in a thousand years. Hearing all this from Gong Xianfang, I thought it very sound. But just whom he sent afterwards, and how they disguised themselves to get in, I never asked.

Dai Li racked his brains constantly to set up a group in Yenan. As I understand it, the nearest group and radio station the Juntong then had to the Border Region were at Luochuan; agents did slip into Yenan, but no radio station could be established. Such intelligence on Yenan as there was all went to Chungking through the Luochuan radio station and the Northern Shaanxi station at Yulin.

When Liang Ganqiao was commissioner at Yaoxian, that place became a centre of the Juntong agents' activity against the Border Region. Around 1942, going to Xi'an on official business, and being on fairly good terms with Liang, I went specially to Yaoxian to see him. He had by then left the Juntong organization and was doing anti-Communist work for Hu Zongnan. He told me that every year some dozens of Juntong agents passed through his area to slip into Yenan — some for whom he found connections, mainly through the merchants who then went to trade in the Border Region, slipping in together disguised as merchants to gather a little information. He also had a unit specifically for collecting intelligence on the region. I asked him why he did not build an organization inside the region. He said that was rather difficult; one could only go in and operate case by case, and to try to stay there for the long term was very hard.

The chiefs of the Juntong's Northwest region at Lanzhou, Cheng Yiming, Wu Jingzhong and Huo Liren in succession, were all on close personal terms with me. The region's main task at the time was likewise secret-service activity aimed at the Border Region. Huo, who was later transferred back to Chungking, also talked with me about some of the region's activities against the Border Region. He said that outside Yenan itself they had built secret-service organizations in some of the counties inside the region, only without radio stations. No small number of the agents sent in to penetrate the region were graduates of the Juntong's Lanzhou special training class. The students the class drew in for training, or who were sent in for training on an agent's recommendation, were mostly of Northwest origin, and among them were men whose old homes or relatives and friends lived within the bounds of the Border Region; the Northwest region used these connections to send people in and set up secret-service organizations.

Huo Liren also told me that at the time it was not very difficult to send agents into the region to gather intelligence; it was sabotage and the building of organizations that were very hard. Several of the agents he sent in to carry out sabotage were arrested by the Border Region government.

The Juntong's methods of sending people into the Border Region to gather intelligence left no crack unentered. I once, on Dai Li's instructions, went with the senior agent Li Xiaobai, who had charge of Juntong personnel, to see the Catholic bishop Yu Bin. As far back as before the war, when Yu Bin was bishop of the Nanking district, he had had dealings with the Juntong chiefs; after the war began he fled to Sichuan and became bishop of the Leshan district in Sichuan, but he lived for long stretches near Dai Li's residence on Zhongsi Road at Zengjiayan in Chungking, and had regular dealings with Dai. Dai sent Li Xiaobai and me to see him that time because a batch of Catholics wanted to go to the Northwest and hoped to ride free on the Juntong's vehicles. I was not at all pleased, thinking I was going to make trouble for myself. But Li Xiaobai told me Yu Bin was very warm in helping the Juntong's work, and said the man could set up a working network for the Juntong in the Border Region through the Catholics. When we saw the bishop he analysed conditions in the region for us, seeming to understand its problems very well. I remember his saying that because the Communists forbade people to hold "superstitions," the believers were greatly offended, and that in particular many people in the Border Region counties who had originally professed Catholicism were dissatisfied with the Communists. He said, too, that if these people were used for some work, the task could certainly be accomplished. That time Li and he did not, in fact, discuss anything concrete about using the region's believers; they spoke only of how I was to find free transport as quickly as possible for the believers going to the Northwest. From then on several dozen people rode free on Juntong vehicles in succession, and the personnel department did send someone to keep in touch with them, though what it came to I do not know.

### The Juntong's Plot to Assassinate the Communist Leaders

From 1942 on, I came to know that Chiang Kai-shek had several times directed Dai Li to pick agents in action work and slip them into Yenan to assassinate the Communist leaders. Around the spring and summer of 1942, Dai Li himself picked out three agents who had always done assassination work in the Juntong; one of them, Jiang Gengsheng, was a student I had trained. Before he set out Jiang Gengsheng came to see me, saying this mission was very heavy and very difficult, and also most secret, and begging me on no account to speak of it. Afraid of trouble, I did not question him closely at the time; I heard only that the other two were fairly familiar with conditions in the Northwest. I did not meet those two and had no wish to.

Several months later, Dai Li summoned me, together with Liu Shaofu, head of the Juntong's Special Technical Research Office, the personnel chief Gong Xianfang and the action chief Xu Yedao, to his office. Dai, very angry, handed a telegram to Gong Xianfang to read, saying the several men sent last time had all been arrested. From their talk it seemed all of them had taken part in planning this scheme before, and only I was new to it. It was not for me to ask about the earlier business just then; I only listened. Dai Li's view was that in future they should not be sent in group by group but should go one by one. The action chief of the day, Xu Yedao, had always had charge of judicial work in the Juntong and was a layman at action; he asked me whether one man alone could carry out an assassination. I said that for one man to assassinate in "enemy"-controlled territory, if he had an inside line and used poison or a time bomb, the task could be done, but that if it was to be a shooting there was no certainty. Dai Li was much displeased to hear this. He said that so long as a man was resolved to sacrifice himself, one man could carry out a shooting just as well. I held there was no certainty; he still insisted, saying more men only made mishaps likelier, and it was not for me to say more. He then directed Gong Xianfang to pick two men and send them separately, letting no lateral connection form between them; and so it was decided then and there.

A few days later Gong Xianfang picked, from the plain-clothes squad of the Juntong's Special Service Corps, a Henan man (whose name I have forgotten), and from among the students posted to the Inspectorate of the Chungking Garrison Command after graduating from the Lanzhou special training class, a Shaanxi man, Li Tian, whom he handed to me to test. The Henan man was a hardened bandit by origin, bold, a good shot, and Dai Li had him sent off first. Li Tian was young and inexperienced, and I gave him two weeks' remedial coaching in pistol shooting and some of the technique proper to a shooting. His marksmanship was still far from ideal, but Dai pressed for him to be sent all the same. Dai Li did not receive them himself; Gong Xianfang and Xu Yedao merely talked with each separately and handed over the mission. According to Gong Xianfang, getting a man into Yenan involved a great many formalities; he could not necessarily slip in at once, but had to work his way in through many connections. As for waiting for a chance to accomplish the mission, that might take a year or two.

Though I too was anti-Communist at the time and much hoped the agents could bring off one or two big assassinations in Yenan, I did not approve of sending individual agents to carry out assassinations, and shootings above all, but held rather to the set of methods that had proved effective before. Since Dai Li held that the old methods did not suit Yenan, I lost interest. The Juntong went on to pick two or three more men and send them in, and not a word was ever heard of any of them.

I once asked Dai Li why he did not fix on one or two Communist leaders at Chungking and other such places to assassinate. He heard me out and then cursed me for having no political head: if an assassination of that sort occurred at Chungking or the like, anyone would know it was the work of agents; but if it happened in Yenan, it could not be laid to agents, and we could put it about that this was infighting within the Communist Party, killing one another.

Once I happened to see a document that shows well enough how the Juntong racked its brains to assassinate the Communist leaders. Doing general-affairs work at the bureau, I had a great many callers and could get no rest at midday. Once I took my nap in the small room across from Mao Renfeng's office. This was the quarters-cum-office of Yuan Jibin, a secretary of the Juntong secretariat, who was a fellow townsman of mine from Xiangtan, my own age, and on the closest personal terms with me. I had him work in the larger outer office while I rested in his room. He was then greatly trusted by Mao Renfeng, and many highly confidential documents were in his keeping; but they did not keep things from me either. After sleeping a while and not wishing to go out yet, I reached into the document cabinet at the head of his bed and took out a document to read. To my great surprise, this document turned out to be a survey table of the bodyguards of many Communist leaders. I glanced through it hastily, and to this day still remember that Zhou Enlai had a bodyguard named Long Feihu — because the man's name was rather unusual — the rest I cannot recall.

From this survey table one can see how the Juntong was scheming to assassinate these leaders. In the ordinary way I had never heard anyone at the bureau speak of this, and since I had seen it in passing it was not for me to ask how it had been obtained. I knew the Juntong's invariable practice: whenever it meant to assassinate someone, it would first, as far as possible, get clear the target's bodyguard situation. This survey table was certainly a preliminary step toward assassinating these leaders; unfortunately I did not at the time get clear the source of these things, and cannot give a concrete account.

The above is what I know of some of the Juntong's activities against the Shaan-Gan-Ning Border Region.
