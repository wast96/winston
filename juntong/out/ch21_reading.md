## What I Know of Zheng Jiemin

Chiang Kai-shek's trust in Zheng Jiemin, one of the three great chiefs within the Juntong secret service, was on a par with his trust in Dai Li, though he used him rather differently. Besides having him assist Dai Li in running the Juntong's secret-service work over a long period, Chiang also had him carry on open activity and some international-liaison work, which is why Zheng often said he did not do underhand, furtive business.

Within Chiang Kai-shek's clique the reactionaries had always praised Zheng Jiemin as some sort of "military strategist" with a "political brain" and "scientific training," and a "theorist of peace"; in fact he was a secret-service chief through and through.

He was a native of Hainan Island, yet was built like a burly northerner, his swarthy face wearing a smile all day. He liked to wear a military uniform hung with medals, full of self-importance; at a glance he seemed a regular officer.

Zheng Jiemin was known for being so-called "seasoned and weighty" and "learned and gifted" and "willing to study problems closely." In talking with people he loved to hold forth without pause, mixing in humorous, light remarks from time to time, so that one could not easily make out his true mind. I was his subordinate for over a decade and had long contact with him, yet what I understood of him is still very limited, so to set him down fully and deeply is no easy thing.

### Family Background and Early Life

Zheng Jiemin was born on the fifteenth of the eighth lunar month of 1897, in Xiashui village, Wenchang county, on Hainan Island in Guangdong, into a declining landlord family; there were four brothers, and he was the eldest.

Zheng Jiemin's original name was Tingbing, his style-name Yaoquan; he changed his name to Jiemin when he sat the Whampoa entrance examination, and the alias he used as a Juntong agent was Jiefu.

Around 1930, when Zheng Jiemin was doing secret-service work in Wuhan, he married a Hankou woman, Ke Shufen, and they had eight children one after another. Ke was an extremely shrewish woman, and Zheng was much afraid of her.

Losing his father young, with many brothers and no family property, Zheng barely finished lower primary school, and in his teens went with fellow townsmen to Kuala Lumpur in Malaya to make a living. At first he was an apprentice in a rubber plantation, his income so meagre it barely fed him. Since few of the overseas Chinese doing hard labour in the South Seas then had any schooling, and he could write and reckon, before long he became a bookkeeper at a fish farm, his monthly income a little more, enough to send some money home now and then. He worked at this fish farm several years; at first content, since it was a little better than being a child labourer, before long he grew dissatisfied, often feeling there was no future in living under another's roof, and having no capital himself to go into business, he began to look for a way out.

When the news reached the South Seas in 1924 that Sun Yat-sen had founded the Army Officers' Academy at Whampoa in Guangzhou, Zheng resolved to return home and take up soldiering, and with his fellow townsman Huang Zhenwu went back to Guangzhou to sit the first Whampoa class's entrance examination. In the end Huang Zhenwu was admitted and he was not. In great distress, at an impasse, he thought of going back to the South Seas but feared being laughed at, while staying on he could not keep himself; later, with the help of his fellow townsman Xing Senzhou, he lodged for a time at the Xing home. After waiting over a month and finding no work, he joined the noncommissioned-officers' corps run by the guard-brigade commander Wu Tiecheng as a cadet soldier, and diligently brushed up his lessons in preparation for another try at Whampoa.

When Whampoa's second class enrolled, he sat the examination again, and this time was at last admitted. Overjoyed, he resolved thenceforth to study hard and train diligently. It is said that at Whampoa he read whenever he had a spare moment and seldom went out to play. Later he often told people: "For a man to have no chance to study is a very unhappy thing." The bitterness of failing the first Whampoa examination he never forgot his whole life, and thereafter, however busy, he kept up the habit of constant reading.

After his return Zheng often styled himself of the "overseas-Chinese working class." At the Whampoa academy, through Huang Zhenwu's introduction, he joined the "Sun Yat-senism Study Society." He took a keen interest in this organization and introduced Xing Senzhou into it too. They then used Xing for outside liaison; Xing is said to have been the first non-Whampoa student to join. Zheng, with Huang Zhenwu, Zhou Fu and seven others made up a small group that, under the name of studying the Three People's Principles and uniting the revolutionary forces, joined with He Zhonghan, Pan Youqiang, Yang Yinzhi and others to oppose the revolutionary "Young Soldiers' Union" and carry on anti-Marxist, anti-Communist activity.

After graduating from Whampoa's second class, Zheng sat the examination for the Chinese Labour University (the Sun Yat-sen University for short) that the Soviet Union ran in Moscow in memory of Sun Yat-sen, and was a classmate of Kang Ze, Li Shouyi and others in the same intake. At the Sun Yat-sen University he was most interested in a few of the main courses — the history of the Chinese revolution, the history of the Russian revolution, political economy, dialectical materialism — and spent no little time studying them closely. But his aim was not to seek revolutionary truth; it was to lay up counter-revolutionary political capital for later advancement and wealth.

Zheng Jiemin had only a primary-school education, but while studying in Moscow he set to writing a book, <i>National Struggle and Class Struggle</i>, whose content merely repeated the stale clichés of the reactionary chiefs — that China had no classes, that the Marxist doctrine of class struggle did not suit China's conditions, that China's problem was a national one, and so on. Later even he thought the book too clumsily written and would not mention it again. Still, the book is said to have won the appreciation of Chiang Kai-shek and some reactionary chiefs at the time, and in thought and word it played a supporting and accessory part in Chiang Kai-shek's "April Twelfth" party purge and anti-Communism.

In August 1927 Zheng graduated and returned from the Soviet Union, and to his great disappointment and grief arrived just as Chiang Kai-shek had stepped down and gone to Japan. Unable to find a master, with even his meals in doubt, uneasy at heart, he had no choice but to attach himself to He Zhonghan. He was then commandant of the sixth class of the Central Military Academy, and placed Zheng in the commandant's office as a political instructor. Zheng used the post to preach his set of anti-Communist theories to the students, winning Pan Youqiang's praise. Before long Pan recommended him to be a secretary in the political department of the Fourth Army, whose director then was Du Congrong.

In January 1928 Chiang Kai-shek returned from Japan and resumed the post of commander-in-chief of the National Revolutionary Army's land, sea and air forces, and Zheng did all he could, through others, to reach Chiang directly. When Chiang received him, he reported to Chiang on the thinking of the Whampoa students who had studied in the Soviet Union, and declared himself firmly anti-Communist. Chiang Kai-shek was much taken with all this and kept Zheng at his side as an aide-de-camp. From then on Zheng's official fortunes rose without check and he climbed rapidly.

His early secret-service activity. As Chiang Kai-shek's aide-de-camp, Zheng Jiemin in fact did secret-service work for Chiang. The one running this work then was Wang Bailing, and the one leading Zheng directly was Cai Jinjun. Zheng and Cai were not only Whampoa classmates of the same intake but Hainan fellow townsmen, and got on very well. At first, doing this work in Nanking, Zheng showed nothing remarkable, only gathering a little intelligence among classmates and fellow townsmen. But before long, in Chiang Kai-shek's struggle with the Guangxi-clique warlords, he showed his skill and made a name at a stroke, winning Chiang's special trust and laying the foundation for his later secret-service career. The affair went roughly as follows.

In the winter of 1928, after Zhang Xueliang "changed his colours" in the Northeast, Chiang Kai-shek's empire was on the surface "unified," but the New Guangxi clique's power stretched from Guangxi through Wuhan to North China, taking no small share of his territory. Chiang was much unwilling to bear this and constantly thought to pull out this thorn in his eye.

Learning that Chiang Kai-shek was resolved to deal with the Guangxi clique, Zheng Jiemin volunteered, asking to go work in Wuhan. What he counted on most was that he and Li Zongren's brother Li Zongyi had been on very close terms as Sun Yat-sen University classmates in the Soviet Union, and that Li had long wanted Zheng to work for the Guangxi clique, so that Zheng could use this tie to penetrate the clique. Wang Bailing thought this tie very usable and formally put it to Chiang Kai-shek. Chiang at once summoned Zheng, briefed him in person, and ordered him to set off for Wuhan immediately.

Zheng Jiemin always regarded this operation as a masterpiece and often boasted of it afterward, so I know it in fair detail.

Pretending to be unemployed, Zheng Jiemin slipped quietly from Nanking to Wuhan, deliberately putting up at a very small inn and acting utterly down-and-out. When he had looked over the situation in Wuhan, he went to the Fourth Army Group headquarters to find Li Zongyi. At the Fourth Army Group headquarters people generally called Li Zongren "the old chief" and Li Zongyi "the second chief." The "second chief" was respected on all sides at headquarters, and no one imagined he would bring in a secret agent.

When Zheng Jiemin met Li Zongyi, he said he had long been jobless and could not even keep himself, that he had come specially to offer his service and hoped to be taken in. He spoke very earnestly, and Li Zongyi, knowing nothing of his real circumstances, believed him and promised at once to see to it.

A day later Li Zongyi went to the inn to look in on Zheng, and seeing his poverty had no doubts and at once had him move into headquarters to share his room. Li even had clothes made for him and often took him out. Seeing how good the "second chief" was to Zheng, the senior officers working at headquarters were glad to get close to him too, and he used every chance to draw in the headquarters' confidential staff. First he stole and photographed the codebooks Li Zongren used to keep in touch with his units and various parties, then copied out one by one the tables of Li's troop strengths and stations, numbers, equipment, and officers' names, and secretly sent them to Chiang Kai-shek, giving Chiang a full grasp of the Fourth Army Group's whole situation and every activity.

At the same time Zheng sowed discord among the Guangxi generals. Earlier, on reaching Wuhan, Li Zongren, to draw in the Hubei men, had put forward the slogan "Hubei men to govern Hubei," so that Hubei-born generals of the clique such as Hu Zongduo and Tao Jun were for a time riding high, which stirred the resentment of some Guangxi-born generals such as Xia Wei, Li Mingrui, Yang Tenghui and Huang Quan. Zheng seized the chance to widen the rift between them, often spreading talk like "the Hubei men hold power and grow rich, but it's the Guangxi men who fight and die."

Zheng Jiemin reported the discord among the Guangxi generals to Chiang Kai-shek as it arose, and Chiang sent men to buy them over, draw them in, split and dissolve them. Zheng did little of this work himself, for fear that once exposed he could not easily find another such man to penetrate the clique. At the time few were alert to Chiang Kai-shek's secret-service activity, and Zheng seemed so honest on the surface, always addressing Li Zongren as "Sir De" at every turn, that even Li Zongren never suspected him.

As Chiang Kai-shek's plot gradually came to fruition, Li Zongren still had not perceived that his basic forces had changed from within. By 1929 he still meant to expand his power, joining the clique's forces from Guangxi through central China to North China into one. In grand talk of world affairs with Bai Chongxi in Beiping, he often said: "Since ancient times, unifying China has succeeded only from north to south" — hinting he was preparing, when the time came, to march south from the north. But Chiang Kai-shek gave no sign, secretly making every preparation and only awaiting the chance to strike.

Li Zongren was bent on taking Hunan, meaning to plant He Jian, who leaned toward the clique, in place of the pro-Chiang Lu Diping as Hunan provincial chairman; without the approval of the Central Political Council, he overstepped his authority and dismissed Lu from the chairmanship in the name of the Wuhan Political Sub-council. Just then He Jian secretly professed loyalty to Chiang Kai-shek, which pleased Chiang the more; judging the moment had come, he detained Li Jishen at Tangshan near Nanking and, on the pretext that Li Zongren had disobeyed the centre, dismissed him from his posts. Li Zongren and Bai Chongxi telegraphed their opposition to Chiang, and Chiang, having a pretext for war, launched it in April 1929.

At this the Guangxi generals Chiang Kai-shek had already bought over — Li Mingrui, Yang Tenghui, Huang Quan and their brigades — withdrew toward Huayuan on the Beiping-Hankou railway without firing a shot. Hu Zongduo, Tao Jun and the rest, left isolated and unable to hold on, withdrew toward Shashi and Yichang. So the more than a hundred thousand Guangxi men entrenched in Hubei collapsed at once, and Chiang Kai-shek took Wuhan without effort. Li Zongren, then in Shanghai treating an eye ailment, had not at all expected so swift a defeat, and on hearing the news fled in haste from Shanghai to Hong Kong.

The Guangxi problem quickly settled, Zheng Jiemin returned to Nanking to report to Chiang Kai-shek. Receiving him, Chiang gripped his hand and praised his work again and again. Zheng seized the moment to hand back on the spot a passbook with the balance of the special activity funds Chiang had given him, winning still more of Chiang's praise.

Still uneasy about some of the generals who had defected from the Guangxi clique and come over, Chiang Kai-shek sent Zheng Jiemin to work on them, appointing him director of the political department of Li Mingrui's Fifteenth Division and ordering him to Guangxi with the unit. When Yu Zuobai became Guangxi provincial chairman, Chiang further made Zheng a member of the Guangxi provincial government, still concurrently political-department director of the Fifteenth Division, stationed at Nanning. His task was to keep watch on Yu Zuobai and the Guangxi units returned home.

Before long, since Chiang stationed Yang Tenghui's Fifty-Seventh Division at Liuzhou, and Zheng at Nanning could not see to both, Chiang removed Zheng's concurrent Fifteenth-Division post and had him instead concurrently head the political department of the Fifty-Seventh Division. Zheng now travelled constantly between Nanning and Liuzhou. On the one hand he secretly planted his henchmen within the Guangxi provincial government, on the other he used the political-department political workers in the units for his secret-service activity.

Quite a few others had gone to Guangxi with Zheng to do secret-service work for Chiang Kai-shek, and the one Zheng kept in constant touch with was the Wuzhou customs supervisor Rao Yuchen, who controlled Guangxi's revenues. The Wuzhou customs supervisorship was then the fattest post in Guangxi. This customs' revenue was Guangxi's main source of funds, and once Chiang's man held it, the Guangxi units that returned to this poor land had to look to Chiang for their military and administrative costs. Yu Zuobai's difficulties grew by the day and he was at his wits' end. Zheng Jiemin and Rao Yuchen worked hand in glove, secretly buying over and drawing in Yu's subordinates, playing the Wuhan trick again. Though Yu knew by now they were up to mischief against him, since Chiang Kai-shek had sent them he still had to defer to them on the surface. Though Zheng was but an empty provincial-government member, he insisted on a say in every provincial measure and reported everything to Chiang Kai-shek as it happened.

In the autumn of 1929, when Wang Jingwei, Chen Gongbo and others joined with Yan Xishan, Feng Yuxiang, Tang Shengzhi and Zhang Fakui to brew opposition to Chiang, they sent men to canvass Yu Zuobai and his subordinate generals. Yu and the rest felt that rather than be held down by Chiang and left to die in Guangxi, they had better rise against him and "fight their way out" to find another road. Getting wind of it in Nanning, Zheng Jiemin on the one hand telegraphed Chiang urgently, on the other hurried overnight to Liuzhou, meaning to grasp and split Yang Tenghui's forces.

In early October that year, when Yu Zuobai announced at Nanning that he was taking the post of commander-in-chief of the anti-Chiang army, in response to Zhang Fakui's anti-Chiang telegram from Yichang, Zheng Jiemin was still at work in Liuzhou; not daring to return to Nanning, he fled overnight from Liuzhou to Guangzhou alone, in disguise.

After Yu Zuobai raised his anti-Chiang banner, the response on all sides fell far short of his hopes. Among his subordinates, Lü Huanyan, Huang Quan and others whom Zheng and Rao had bought over rose against him; Zhang Yunyi and Li Mingrui led their troops to raise the Red flag on the Right River; and part of the army simply scattered. Yu Zuobai, reduced to sorry straits, collapsed before he could even take the field.

In December that year Zheng returned from Guangzhou via Hong Kong to Nanking. This time, when he saw Chiang Kai-shek, he was not valued as he had been on his return from Wuhan. Chiang reproached him to his face, for not only had an anti-Chiang movement broken out in Guangxi, showing Zheng had achieved little in controlling and splitting the Guangxi units; what Chiang hated most was that no few Guangxi units had risen and become Red Army, the thing that gave him the worst headache.

On this return Chiang Kai-shek left him out in the cold for a time before sending him to the General Staff Headquarters as a colonel staff officer. With nothing to do all day, he applied himself to reading books on military affairs, politics, philosophy and the like; Zheng often said later that in this period he had learned a good deal that helped him greatly.

In this period Chiang Kai-shek did not seek him out, and he dared not go to see Chiang. But before others he never uttered a word of complaint, often instead saying he had done his Guangxi work poorly, had failed Chiang's hopes, and could not share the principal's burdens, and blaming himself. These words slowly reached Chiang's ears, and so Chiang slowly gave him important work again.

The Renaissance Society Special Services Department period. In November 1931 Chiang Kai-shek gathered the Whampoa students in Nanking for a talk and announced the founding of the Renaissance Society; Zheng Jiemin at once asked to join and was elected an officer of the society's executive committee. In this fascist body Zheng always played an important part. When Liu Jianqun became secretary in 1936, then going to Beiping to work, Chiang Kai-shek designated Zheng Jiemin acting secretary, until Kang Ze took over the secretaryship that winter.

In March 1932 Chiang Kai-shek gathered the Renaissance Society's senior members for a meeting and decided to set up a Special Services Department. Before the meeting Chiang first talked with Kang Ze and Gui Yongqing, sounding out their views. Chiang wanted Kang to be department head; Kang said at the time it would not be fitting, and Chiang said nothing and did not ask Gui either. At the meeting Chiang proposed that the Renaissance Society should set up a Special Services Department, and that Dai Li be prepared as head and Zheng Jiemin as deputy head, and asked those present for their views. Hearing that the candidates had already been named by him, they could only agree. Zheng said nothing at the time, not daring to show he was unwilling, though at heart he was much displeased. For he understood Dai Li's character, knew the man was not easy to work with, and was somewhat unwilling to be under this Whampoa sixth-class junior.

On the eve of the Special Services Department's founding, when Dai Li and Zheng Jiemin came forward to feast the guests at a great banquet in Nanking, the former was elated while the latter seemed most reluctant. In fixing the department's organization and personnel, though Dai asked Zheng to bring in some relatives and friends, Zheng pleaded he had no suitable men, and the whole thing was based by Dai Li on his "Ten-Man Group." Only later did Zheng bring in Xing Senzhou, Wang Changyu, Zheng Gongxuan and other fellow townsmen, and a nephew, to work in the department.

After Zheng became deputy head of the Special Services Department, his General Staff Headquarters post was not cancelled. At first he seldom went to the department office and wanted to shake off the work and find another road. Each time Dai left Nanking and asked him to run the department, he was unwilling to handle personnel, finances, or any weightier matter, often leaving them to Tang Zong, whom Chiang Kai-shek had placed in the department as secretary, and doing all he could to avoid friction with Dai Li on such matters. Twice he asked Kang Ze to secure him other work from Chiang Kai-shek. Kang once recommended him to head the Central Military Academy's Northwest training class, and later to be head of the political department's Second Department, but Chiang did not approve either, telling Kang that this man lacked drive, that he should stay in the department, and not to recommend him again.

Chiang Kai-shek set extraordinary store by the Special Services Department's work. Knowing Zheng was unsettled, he once talked with him, telling him to help Dai Li do the work well. Knowing Chiang would not transfer him, Zheng had no choice but to go on getting along with Dai Li, and Dai, understanding his mind well, was very courteous to him on the surface.

Zheng was cautious in manner yet loved to talk. Before a piece of work was done he would not casually speak of it, but once he had a result he loved to boast of it. In this period the things he was proudest of were these several.

After the "January Twenty-Eighth," when the Nineteenth Route Army moved to Fujian, Chiang Kai-shek instructed the Special Services Department to plant many men in this force as junior and middle officers. Apart from a few placed by planned arrangement through the department's organization, most were sent through Zheng's personal ties. These men ordinarily had no connection with the department, keeping in touch only with him personally, so their identities were not easily exposed, their activity more convenient, and the part they later played greater. For instance, Yun Dayi, a fellow townsman from Wenchang county on Hainan whom Zheng sent, induced the Seventy-Eighth Division commander Yun Yinglin to come over to Chiang Kai-shek. Yun Dayi also supplied much intelligence, often more than the department's Fujian provincial station obtained on the Nineteenth Route Army.

When the Nineteenth Route Army rose against Chiang in Fujian, Zheng Jiemin was in Beiping, and Dai urgently telegraphed him back to Nanking. Chiang Kai-shek had meant to send Zheng to Fujian to run the work of breaking up the Nineteenth Route Army, but Zheng had many misgivings and hesitated. He reckoned that many Guangdong men knew him, and that of those planted into the Nineteenth Route Army through his ties some years back, some had never contacted him again and their attitude was unclear, so he feared being recognized and betrayed by them. Knowing Zheng was timid, Dai Li would not force him, and had him hand over some reliable ties, whereupon Dai took a few men himself and went secretly to Gulangyu to direct operations.

Through the agents Zheng had planted in the Nineteenth Route Army and the activity of the Fujian provincial station, Dai Li not only had a full grasp of the Fujian situation, which he reported to Chiang Kai-shek as it came in, but even had the codebooks the Nineteenth Route Army headquarters used with its units and outside contacts all stolen by the agents, so that after the affair broke out the headquarters could not issue orders to its units. Telegrams sent back by the units could not be deciphered either, and both sides lost contact.

The "Fujian Incident" ended quickly. After Dai Li returned to Nanking, though at first somewhat displeased that Zheng Jiemin had refused to go, since the matter had been resolved so unexpectedly smoothly and he could count the whole merit his own, he turned round and showed Zheng goodwill. Because the Guangdong fellow townsmen and Hainan fellow townsmen Zheng had planted beforehand played a large wrecking part, Dai Li praised Zheng before many agents for his foresight in "getting the jump on the enemy." Hearing Dai Li's report, Chiang Kai-shek too praised Zheng warmly.

In the spring of 1933, with the North China situation very tense and He Yingqin hard put to cope, Chiang Kai-shek told Dai Li to send an important agent to run the work in North China. Dai then proposed to Chiang that Zheng go and concurrently head the North China district, with the cover of being a colonel staff officer of the Military Affairs Commission's Beiping branch, and gave the department's work in North China a fresh reorganization.

When the fighting at Gubeikou had just broken out, Chiang Kai-shek, dreading a major Japanese offensive, was anxious to know the situation as it developed at any moment. The North China district then held little such material, and no agent organization had been planted around Gubeikou; Chiang cursed Dai Li for not knowing the priorities of the work. Dai urgently telegraphed Zheng to go in person to arrange things and find out the situation, and only then, at the last minute, was a small group with a radio formed and rushed with him to Gubeikou. Zheng stayed there only two or three days, left the group and radio, and hurried back to Beiping. Afterward he forever boasted to the uninformed that he had taken part in "the anti-Japanese battle of Gubeikou" — when in fact this was all it amounted to.

Zheng Jiemin's proudest deed as North China district head was to have Zhang Jingyao shot dead at the Liuguo Hotel in Beiping on 7 May 1933. This he undertook in person, disguised as an overseas-Chinese capitalist returned from the South Seas to carry out reconnaissance; once he had Zhang's movements clear, he sent the North China district's action-group head Bai Shiwei, leading the action man Huang Youqin and others, to shoot Zhang dead.

After running the assassination of Zhang Jingyao, Zheng Jiemin returned to Nanking and would no longer go to Beiping, fearing revenge from Zhang's old subordinates and the Japanese secret service. In the autumn of 1933 Dai Li replaced him with Wang Tianmu as North China district head; Zheng dared not even go to hand over, but since he had held the district head post only concurrently as deputy department head, no one made trouble for him.

In the spring of 1934, to imitate the fascist dictatorships of Hitler and Mussolini, Chiang Kai-shek resolved to pick a batch of men to go and study. Zheng Jiemin was chosen too. That summer Zheng Jiemin, Pan Youqiang, Du Xinru, Teng Jie and three others, seven in all, went to Europe under the name of a military inspection mission, in fact mainly to Germany and Italy. The task Chiang Kai-shek gave them was, first, to learn the fascist methods of ruling the people and of opposing and guarding against Communism, and second, to develop the Renaissance Society's organization in Europe. They went to Germany, Italy, Britain, France, Austria, Switzerland and other countries, spending longer in Germany and Italy. They studied every side of these two dictatorial cliques. Zheng spent the most time studying their secret-service organization and activity, gathering much material, which Dai Li put in order and sent to Chiang Kai-shek. Referring to this report, Dai made no few adjustments to the department he led.

At first Dai could put forward no complete method for how the Special Services Department's work should be done. This inspection of Zheng's was a great help to him. Much of the activity of the department, of the later Second Department of the Military Affairs Commission's Bureau of Investigation and Statistics into which it was reorganized, and of the still later Juntong, was set up through this inspection of Zheng's and the set Tang Zong learned as deputy military attaché at the Nationalist Government's embassy in Germany. Measures such as controlling transport and communications through the secret-service organization, monitoring wireless stations and radios and jamming broadcasts, planting agents into organs, factories and schools, and strengthening the police apparatus, all later played a part in anti-Communist, anti-people work.

While in Europe they would gather the overseas students at every stop to publicize the Renaissance Society's organization and role, telling them the society was headed by Chiang Kai-shek himself and that all students must join it. Zheng was quite blunt in speaking to the students, often saying without reserve that Chiang Kai-shek was resolved to rely on the Kuomintang no longer, and so had formed the Renaissance Society to replace it. He praised Chiang to the skies, saying how resolved Chiang was to make China rich, strong and happy, while the Kuomintang was already old and decayed, without vigour, and only the Renaissance Society could revive China. By his own account these words often met a warm welcome from the students. For these people had long been dissatisfied with the decrepit, incompetent grandees of the Kuomintang, daring to be angry but not to speak, and Zheng's words hit their fancy exactly.

While in Germany Zheng and the others repeatedly asked for an audience to pay their respects to Hitler, and after some trouble and a long wait were at last allowed to be received. When this Hitler devil saw these disciples come from ten thousand li to pay their respects, his manner was most arrogant, but they, satisfied to have seen the "Führer" once, all felt the trip had not been in vain.

In Italy, it is said, Mussolini's manner in receiving them was far better than Hitler's, and he encouraged them. Feeling overwhelmed by the favour and overjoyed, after their return Zheng Jiemin often counted it a supreme honour to have seen these "fascist forefathers." But some also said that in Germany their request for an audience with Hitler was not granted, and that they bragged after their return for face's sake.

In the spring and summer of 1935, after Zheng and the others returned from Europe, Chiang Kai-shek spent much time hearing their oral reports and read their long written reports with keen interest. Dai Li too kept asking Zheng about it all and had him report to the department, and Zheng at once became the most sought-after busy man.

Not long after his return Zheng was promoted a grade by Chiang Kai-shek and made major-general head of the Fifth Section of the Second Department of the General Staff Headquarters.

In 1933 the Special Services Department merged with the Second Department of the Military Affairs Commission's Bureau of Investigation and Statistics led by Chen Lifu; Dai Li was head, Zheng still concurrently deputy head. Chen Lifu then kept Dai on a very tight rein, giving him no facility at every turn. The Second Department's agents met many difficulties in outside activity, and Chen would not even issue them many Military Affairs Commission badges. So Dai Li had Zheng Jiemin get many badges and travel-and-leave passes from the General Staff Headquarters, and at the time many agents carried on their outside activity all in the name of the Headquarters' Fifth Section.

From the spring of 1936 Dai Li kept receiving intelligence that Hu Hanmin was gathering the Guangdong-Guangxi military and political chiefs in Guangzhou to plot against Chiang in secret. Chiang Kai-shek several times telegraphed urging Hu Hanmin to come to Nanking, without result, and ordered Dai Li to strengthen the secret-service activity in South China. Dai transferred large numbers of Guangdong-born agents from Shanghai, Nanking, Wuhan and elsewhere.

Before long, when the Japanese Third Fleet commander Oikawa visited Guangzhou, the agents got intelligence that Chen Jitang had accepted Japanese support and was preparing to join Guangxi in marching on Nanking to overthrow Chiang Kai-shek's regime. Chiang then sent Zheng Jiemin to Hong Kong as well, to direct the South China district in assassinating Chen Jitang and in splitting and buying over the Guangdong units, and in March or April Zheng went secretly from Nanking via Shanghai to Hong Kong.

The Special Services Department's South China district head then was Xing Senzhou, a man Zheng had recommended. Xing Senzhou had knocked about the South Seas a good while and had some standing among the overseas Chinese, with good personnel ties, but at assassination and splitting he was far less experienced than Zheng.

After Zheng reached Hong Kong, on the one hand he actively arranged the assassination of Chen Jitang, on the other he sought by every means to buy over Chen's subordinates to betray Chen for Chiang. An assassination group Zheng sent to Guangzhou rented a house on the road from the city to Chen Jitang's residence at Meihua Village in the suburbs, meaning to snipe at Chen on his daily journeys, but was discovered by Chen's agents, who seized the several agents Zheng had sent. This stiffened Chen's resolve against Chiang the more. Both Chiang Kai-shek and Dai Li were furious over it and berated Zheng by letter and telegram, ordering him to rearrange things quickly and not rest until Chen was killed.

In early June, Chen Jitang, on the pretext that Chiang Kai-shek would not resist Japan, telegraphed his opposition to Chiang and made himself chairman of the Military Affairs Commission. Chiang Kai-shek, besides sending a great army to suppress him, added agents to intensify the secret buying-over of Chen Jitang's subordinates. The proudest masterpiece of Zheng Jiemin and Xing Senzhou in this affair was buying over the Guangdong-Guangxi air forces. Many in these air forces were overseas Chinese, and Kai had ties with no few of them. Zheng first bought over Chen's air-force pilot Chen Zhenxing and others for 120000 Hong Kong dollars, then bought over the air-squadron commander Huang Guangrui for 400000. They also bought over the Guangxi air force, and induced over forty general- and field-rank officers such as the division commanders Li Hanhun and Li Zhenliang to betray Chen for Chiang.

In early July, all arrangements ready, Huang Guangrui and the others led their planes, with all their flight personnel, to fly to Nanking. The Guangxi air force fled the same day and reached Nanking together. Huang Guangrui also issued a telegram cataloguing Chen Jitang's crimes over years in Guangdong of squeezing the people's wealth and colluding with the Japanese.

Once Chiang Kai-shek and Dai Li got Zheng's secret telegram and knew he was buying over the two air forces, they allotted millions of Hong Kong dollars for him to press it actively, longing day and night for early success. When these planes reached Nanking, Chiang and Dai were overjoyed, sent telegram after telegram of commendation, and urged him to press on faster with breaking up Chen's forces.

Within about a month of issuing his anti-Chiang telegram, Chen Jitang was so beset by defection and desertion that he had to telegraph his own resignation. This affair ended quickly, and Zheng played no small part in it. Just as he was happily preparing to return to Nanking to claim credit and reward, Dai Li had him go to Guangzhou to arrange Chiang Kai-shek's protection.

In August, Chiang Kai-shek flew to Guangzhou. Escorting Chiang this time, Zheng naturally won further reward. Only after Chiang left Guangzhou did he return to Nanking, where Dai Li held another grand celebration banquet for him and secured him a huge bonus.

In 1937, after the "July Seventh Incident," Zheng Jiemin was transferred to be head of the Third Section of the Second Department of the General Staff Headquarters, in charge of intelligence for the war against Japan.

### Activities During the War of Resistance

After the "July Seventh Incident," though Chiang Kai-shek on the surface accepted the Communist proposal to unite together to resist Japan, in secret he pressed his anti-Communist plots the harder. When the Nationalist government fled from Nanking to Wuhan, at the extraordinary national congress of the Kuomintang held at Luojia Hill in Wuchang Chiang Kai-shek proposed adding a huge secret-service organ, handing the former Military Affairs Commission Bureau of Investigation and Statistics led by Chen Lifu to Dai Li and setting up another identical Bureau of Investigation and Statistics within the Central Party Headquarters, to strengthen his anti-Communist, anti-people secret-service power.

Though Chiang meant then to promote Dai Li, his seniority being insufficient, he had to designate the head of his Attendants' Office First Department, He Yaozu, as director, with Dai as deputy director bearing the real responsibility. But the arrangement for Zheng Jiemin became a problem: Chiang would not let Zheng leave this secret-service organization, yet found it awkward to have two deputy directors, and at last decided to make Zheng concurrently chief secretary of the Juntong, assisting Dai Li. Zheng was much displeased with the title and at first was very unwilling to go to the office, always pleading press of business, and the chief secretaryship became an empty post in name only. Dai, understanding Zheng's mind, would not force him, but the inside work still needed leadership, so the Juntong kept the post of secretary-general to run the office business. Tang Zong, Zhang Yanfo, Zhou Weilong and Wu Qingshu served successively as secretary-general.

Among the Juntong agents then, not to speak of the field agents, who did not know of this chief secretary, even some of the office staff did not know there was such a leader above the secretary-general. Feeling this could not go on — Zheng might use it as a pretext not to go to the Juntong, and above all Chiang Kai-shek might ask and there be no answer — Dai Li in 1940 abolished the secretary-general title and changed it to acting chief secretary, filled by his trusted fellow townsman Mao Renfeng. Right up to Dai Li's death in March 1946, Zheng's chief secretaryship was held by Mao as acting. Around 1942, when SACO's work was getting under way and Mao Renfeng was overstretched, a deputy chief secretary was added, filled by Zhang Yanfo until the victory.

Though Zheng Jiemin took little interest in the substance of his Juntong chief secretaryship, his wife dreaded losing the post. For the Juntong had much money and many things, a steady supply; not only were Zheng's car and petrol furnished by the Juntong, but his household's steward, menservants and maidservants, cook and the rest were all on the Juntong's payroll, and even many of the household's expenses and the costs of entertaining were borne by the Juntong. When the open organs the Juntong controlled — the anti-smuggling office, the freight bureau, the transport-inspection office and the like — "tributed" the great Juntong agents with goods got by smuggling and anti-smuggling, there was always a share for Zheng Jiemin. For all this, Zheng's wife went to the Juntong head office even more often than Zheng. Whenever Dai Li left Chungking and Mao Renfeng wanted Zheng to preside over the memorial week at the Juntong, Zheng would often plead press of business. Mao Renfeng later found a knack: he had only to telephone the house and tell Zheng's wife, and the next day Zheng would arrive on time without fail, never late.

Not long after the war began, Chiang Kai-shek asked Britain to help set up a commando force. Getting wind of it, Zheng took a keen interest and asked, through others, to be given the task. Chiang held that Zheng had no experience of commanding troops in battle and was unsuited to such work, and sent Li Mo'an instead to prepare a British-armed commando force. Zheng felt keenly disappointed once more.

After the General Staff Headquarters was reorganized into the Ministry of Military Operations, Zheng was unexpectedly promoted by Chiang Kai-shek to lieutenant-general deputy head of its Second Department. Though this set his mind at rest, he still could not give up the wish to command troops. When the War College set up a general-officers' class in 1939, specially to train senior Nationalist officers, he asked to attend while keeping his post. Asked why he still wanted to enter the War College, he said happily: "Nowadays the chiefs of staff of military organs and units are all required to be War College graduates. If I am not, I not only have no chance to command troops but do not understand the business and cannot work. And since I am not a classmate of the War College students, they are not easy to control, so I must go."

Zheng Jiemin entered the War College general-officers' class about September 1939 and graduated in April 1940. The War College was then still at Zunyi in Guizhou; some senior officers on training kept their posts and had even their map-drawing done by staff, while they themselves often went off to Chungking and Guiyang to amuse themselves. Even at Zunyi they lived amid wine and song, sometimes gambling the night through, attending lectures only perfunctorily, since they were only there to pass through and get a diploma. In this period Zheng Jiemin was different: he studied most earnestly, and in his spare time did some writing. On the rare occasions he went back to Chungking for a few days, it was to get material from the Juntong and the Second Department for reference in his writing. His wife often wrote scolding him, saying others often came home while he seemed not to miss home, and suspecting he was up to some tricks at Zunyi; twice she went secretly to Zunyi, and seeing that he really was buried in study and writing, set her mind at rest.

On the one hand Zheng regretted never having led troops in battle, feeling he lacked something of the "martial" flavour; on the other he was set on becoming a "scholar-general." Many reactionary generals then held the thought that "a military man regrets not being able to write," and he was a typical case of it. Over a few years, gathering much material from the Juntong and Second Department archives, he wrote one after another several books — <i>The Science of Military Intelligence</i>, <i>Draft Regulations for Espionage Service</i>, <i>A Study of Guerrilla Tactics</i> and others. The Nationalist government publicly commended his spirit of close study and writing and awarded him a "diligent-study medal." He set great store by this medal, counting it the rarest "honour," and loved to bring it out to show off, to display that he was a "scholar-general" with "both pen and sword."

While deputy head of the Ministry of Military Operations' Second Department, from the second half of 1940 Zheng also concurrently held the deputy directorship of the "Sino-Soviet Intelligence Cooperative Institute," the director being the Second Department head Yang Xuancheng concurrently. The Soviet side sent a deputy director too; this was the Soviet Union helping China gather intelligence on Japan. The institute was at No. 1 Qingde Lane, Lailongbu, Fuzichi, Chungking, and on the south bank near the Niuping teahouse at Huangshan a wireless-detection main station was set up, headed by the Juntong agent Xiao Jianbai. The Soviet side sent a deputy station head and several dozen technicians, and built two dormitories at Wangshan on the south bank for the Soviet personnel. After the institute was wound up, the site was turned into the Juntong's Wangshan farm.

Though this institute was fronted by the Ministry of Military Operations, in fact it was run by the Juntong in cooperation with the Soviet Union, specializing in intercepting and studying and translating the wireless codes of the Japanese army and air force. The Soviet side provided a batch of interception equipment. Cooperation in name, in fact each side wanted to learn the other's technique for intercepting and deciphering Japanese codes while keeping its own from being learned. Dai Li sent the Communist turncoats Xie Ligong, Wu Jingzhong and others who had studied in the Soviet Union to be section heads there (the Soviet side sent deputy section heads), and the other staff were mostly Juntong agents Dai Li chose.

On the one hand Dai Li directed the agents in the institute's wireless section to steal the Soviet personnel's techniques and methods, and on the other instructed them to contrive by every means to get close to the Soviet personnel, hoping to buy over individual Soviet workers and vainly hoping to plant intelligence work inside the Soviet Union. Dai set great hopes on Xie Ligong. Xie's Russian was fluent and he had always been good at socializing, and Dai specially assigned him a small villa near Naohuo Street for contacting the Soviet personnel. Xie's ways were lavish, all his social expenses borne by the Juntong, and he spent no little money, but no result was ever heard of. Zheng Jiemin's wife often grumbled over this, saying that though Zheng was concurrently deputy director of the institute he was far less lavish than its section head.

While concurrently deputy director, Zheng ordinarily asked little about the institute's work, leaving it all to the Juntong. When anyone chanced to ask him about the institute, he always said mysteriously: "This is a secret and cannot yet be made public; when the war against Japan is finally won, there will surely be astonishing results published in the papers." In fact there was much he did not clearly understand either; apart from attending the odd banquet for the Soviet personnel, he never once went to see the detection main station on the south bank. When Xie Ligong had business he went straight to Dai Li for instructions; some things he told Zheng, some he did not even mention afterward, and Zheng never pressed.

### Collusion with Imperialism

In the summer of 1940, Chiang Kai-shek told Dai Li to strengthen the Juntong's secret-service activity throughout the South Seas, and since Zheng was fairly familiar with the region, Dai had him go to inspect the South Seas and open up the Juntong's overseas organization. Zheng held that these places were all within Britain's colonial sphere and that he must keep in close touch with the British imperialists to avoid their displeasure; when he went to inspect, the British specially sent two agents along with him.

That autumn, through Zheng's dealings with the British, it was decided to set up a "Sino-British Intelligence Cooperative Institute," and Britain sent the agent Anderson to Chungking to discuss the matter formally. Chiang Kai-shek decided to have the Juntong handle the cooperation with Britain, and Dai Li recommended the Juntong armed-agent-troops chief and Military Affairs Commission commando commander Zhou Weilong as concurrent head of the institute, with Anderson as deputy head.

When the Sino-British Intelligence Cooperative Institute was founded, Dai Li at a memorial week held at the Juntong specially praised Zheng for "making a new contribution to the development of the Juntong's international intelligence work."

The institute was set up beside the commando headquarters at Zhoujiawan, Hongqifang, Xiaolongkan, Chungking. The cooperation consisted mainly of the Juntong providing the British side with intelligence on the Japanese army, navy and air force along China's coast and mainland, with emphasis on the Japanese air force. At the British side's request the Juntong sent a special work group to work in Hong Kong and India (going to India before the Japanese took Hong Kong), specializing in monitoring Japanese air-force activity and deciphering Japanese air-force codes. This group was much valued by the British, who several times wanted to send men to learn the Juntong's technique for deciphering Japanese air-force codes, but the Juntong always refused to give this to the British.

Around 1942 Dai again had Zheng, in the name of the Ministry of Military Operations' Second Department, send the Juntong agents Li Hanyuan, Wu Wenzi and a dozen others to work in Burma, with several agents sent along by the British side.

Zheng's proudest deed was that in January 1942 he put forward his own view on the Allied battle plan at Singapore, later proved correct. When Roosevelt and Churchill decided to hold Singapore, the Allied military base in the Far East, the Southwest Pacific Allied commander-in-chief, the British general Wavell, convened an Allied military conference at Singapore to discuss the plan for defending Singapore; besides sending Du Yuming and others, Chiang Kai-shek sent Zheng Jiemin to handle liaison on the situation. After Zheng arrived, he was not valued by Wavell, and even the British commander-in-chief at Singapore then treated him coldly. He had contact only with some senior staff of Wavell's headquarters and did not formally attend the conference. The ever-arrogant Wavell declared firmly at the conference that, so long as the line was shortened and forces concentrated, holding Singapore was beyond doubt. Zheng Jiemin put his different view to Wavell's staff. He held that Singapore was absolutely indefensible, and that it were better to concentrate strength on holding Indonesia and make it the Southwest Pacific Allies' counter-attack base, holding Indonesia being surer than holding Singapore. His words then went wholly unnoticed by Wavell.

Zheng returned from Singapore to Chungking and put this view to Chiang Kai-shek too. Chiang, trusting his words fairly, formally passed them to Wavell, but again they were not adopted.

In less than half a month the Japanese massed great land, sea and air forces to storm Singapore. This so-called first great port of the Far East, which Britain had built up over years at a cost of sixty million pounds, with more than sixty thousand British troops defending it, was taken by the Japanese in just over ten days, in February 1942, and Wavell fled in panic with his headquarters staff to Java in the Dutch Indies.

Now not only Chiang Kai-shek and those in Chungking who had heard Zheng's view thought Zheng had indeed "foretold like a god," but even Wavell, telegraphing Chiang Kai-shek, expressed regret at not heeding Zheng and suffering this crushing defeat. Chiang again sent Zheng to Java to liaise with Wavell. This time Wavell's manner was wholly unlike before. By Zheng's account on his return, even Wavell went in person to the airfield to meet him. But this time Zheng did not judge rightly: just as he had returned from Java to Chungking and was preparing to go again, the Japanese pressed their advantage into Indonesia in a great offensive. Within a month, in early March, Java too fell to the Japanese. Wavell fled alone by plane to India, and the tens of thousands of British troops defending Indonesia and Wavell's headquarters officers all became Japanese prisoners.

After defeat upon defeat, the Southwest Pacific Allied command was for a time dissolved, and Wavell went back to his old post as British commander-in-chief in India. Chiang Kai-shek still often sent Zheng to India to liaise with Wavell. Later, when Britain sent Mountbatten to Ceylon as South Pacific Allied commander-in-chief, Zheng went to Ceylon to liaise with Mountbatten too. In coordinating the war against Japan, Zheng put forward no few views.

Chiang Kai-shek held that Zheng had a knack for dealing with the Allies, and afterward designated him to take part in the Sino-American joint chiefs-of-staff meetings held in Chungking. Wedemeyer, then chief of staff of the China theatre and commander of the U.S. forces in China, had a fairly good impression of Zheng too.

Zheng always loved to put forward his analyses and judgments on military questions; though sometimes his estimates were wrong, now and then he hit a few right. The thing he loved most to boast of was that he guessed right the date the Allies opened the second front. In the summer of 1944, talking with some U.S. generals of Wedemeyer's headquarters, everyone forecast the timing of the second front. Many U.S. generals talked wide of the mark; only Zheng pointed to early June as very likely. At the time everyone took it as idle talk to be idly heard, and no one believed him.

Not long after, the Allies did indeed land at Normandy in France on 6 June. When the news reached Chungking, many U.S. officers who had heard Zheng's talk at once recalled that the date he had named beforehand was exactly right, and on seeing him all praised his uncanny reckoning, that he could figure the Allied landing date so precisely. At this he peddled his wares grandly and boasted a round. Hearing this, Wedemeyer even praised him to his face. In fact the basis of this guess was mainly that, from the whole war situation, he judged the opening of the second front could no longer be delayed and that all preparations were ready, so it would not go beyond that year; as for the exact date, he reckoned by the English Channel's largest pre-dawn high tide in early June each year, those days being the most suitable for a landing. On the one hand he boasted of his wide learning, on the other he hinted he was well-informed.

Though these predictions of Zheng's won some people's regard and pleased him, his wife often exposed him before many people. Just as he was relishing holding forth on this and that, his wife would cut in from the side, telling others not to believe his wild talk. Once she said: "At the start of the war he thought Hong Kong safest and stored our valuables and luggage there for safekeeping — result, Hong Kong soon fell and it was all lost." The more she said the angrier she grew, pushing and pulling at him and demanding he give her things back. At this he only smiled and went on talking to the others.

When SACO was first founded, it was said that besides Dai Li as concurrent head, Zheng too would be named, along with the American secret-service chief Miles, as deputy head. Later, for reasons unknown, his concurrent deputy directorship was never announced.

Though Zheng held no concurrent post at SACO, he was most keen on its various activities and often had dealings with Miles. He took part in both the first and second signings of the SACO contract. During his time in Chungking, at every SACO gathering and grand banquet he and Dai Li acted as "hosts" attending on the American agents. At table he was always all smiles, using droll, humorous words to amuse the Americans. Whenever Dai Li was away from Chungking, questions concerning SACO were handled by Zheng in dealings with Miles. Many American agents liked to chat with him and praised him to his face as "a most amusing character."

After Dai Li's death, when Miles came from America to Nanking to mourn, Zheng hurried specially from Beiping to Nanking and had several long talks with Miles. He hoped to restore Sino-American secret-service cooperation once more, and Miles too said he was willing to make a little more effort against the Chinese Communists. Later, for reasons unknown, right up to the nationwide liberation the Americans had not restored this organization, which greatly disappointed him. In the end the Americans agreed only to set up a "Sino-American Office" within the Bureau of Secrets Preservation, to go on supporting the Juntong's anti-Communist activity.

Zheng Jiemin flattered the American "special envoy" Marshall to the utmost. When Marshall went up Mount Lu nine times, Zheng took part in the send-offs and welcomes several times. Once when Marshall and his wife went to see Chiang Kai-shek, Zheng walked more than ten li behind their two sedan chairs. That time he wore a trim, pressed uniform, even his coat soaked through with sweat. He had a chronic bronchitis that flared worst in winter. That time he was left gasping for breath, his bronchitis broke out badly, and back in Nanking he was long under treatment. His wife reproached him, but he held that acting as Marshal Marshall's attendant this time was of great political significance. In his dealings with Marshall, though Zheng did all he could to trumpet this disguised "peace envoy" everywhere, he later thought Marshall not active enough in helping Chiang Kai-shek and privately complained a little. Once at the Bureau of Secrets Preservation he grumbled to a few department-head-rank great agents that many things had not been done to plan, causing the Kuomintang much loss.

Though Zheng Jiemin had known the American cultural-secret-service chief John Leighton Stuart for a long time, relations were not close. In 1933, when Zheng was North China district head of the Special Services Department at Beiping, his cover post was colonel staff officer of the Military Affairs Commission's Beiping branch. Stuart was then president of Yenching University and consorted only with the Kuomintang's grandees, taking no notice of this colonel staff officer. Much as Zheng wanted to curry favour, he could not.

This "China hand" John Leighton Stuart was imprisoned by the Japanese for a few years, and in 1946 succeeded Hurley as U.S. ambassador to China. By then Zheng's standing was much different from before. Both felt the need to draw closer, but each found it awkward to approach the other directly. When Zheng spoke of this to the Bureau's director's-office chief Huang Tianmai, Huang told him Stuart too had long wished to talk with Zheng. Huang was a Yenching University graduate who had been the Nationalist government's consul-general in Paris, and after joining the Juntong during the war had been deputy head of the Juntong's First Department and overseas-district head, in charge of overseas intelligence. After the victory Huang Tianmai worked on international intelligence in Beiping and Tianjin, mainly liaising with the U.S. personnel there. Huang and Stuart's secretary Fu Jingbo were Yenching classmates and close friends. When Fu took Huang to see the old president and mentioned Huang's working under Zheng Jiemin, Stuart praised Zheng before Huang as "one of the few military strategists in the Kuomintang." Hearing himself so praised, Zheng was the more anxious to meet this new ambassador whom Chiang Kai-shek honoured as an esteemed guest. By Huang Tianmai's account, when Zheng first went to see Stuart, once Huang had arranged it with Fu Jingbo, the two talked for several hours, warming to each other the more they talked.

When Zheng was in Beiping, whenever Stuart came to Beiping or Tianjin on business Zheng would hurry to meet him. When Stuart held his seventieth birthday in Nanking, Zheng could not get there in time and had someone buy a fine birthday scroll to send, with a telegram of birthday wishes.

Zheng Jiemin was good at ingratiating himself with the imperialists, for which Chiang Kai-shek highly commended him, and Chiang knew how to use this strength of his for such work. Zheng too styled himself a "military diplomat." What he counted proudest were his activities around the "Cairo Conference" of 1943.

Before the conference Chiang Kai-shek first sent Hu Shize, Zhu Shiming, Li Weiguo and Zheng Jiemin to Cairo to liaise with the U.S. and British sides. On his orders Zheng at once consulted Dai Li, the two talking almost the whole night. Zheng's task, besides going ahead to discuss the various preparations, was above all to arrange Chiang Kai-shek's security. Before the conference the Juntong hastily sent a batch of agents to India, Pakistan, Egypt and elsewhere; Dai Li saw them off in person and issued them large funds. Though the Juntong had organizations in these places, the numbers were few and could not bear this new, temporary, weighty task.

After Zheng went to Cairo, he soon returned to Chungking with Li Weiguo to report, and the moment he stepped off the plane went straight to report the preparations to Chiang Kai-shek. When Li Weiguo went to see Chiang Kai-shek, Chiang said at once: "Director Zheng has already reported everything," and Li Weiguo had to withdraw.

The route of Chiang Kai-shek's special plane to Cairo was not known even to many who went with him; only Dai Li and Zheng Jiemin knew beforehand, this arranged in advance by Zheng. For fear the Japanese would intercept him, Chiang had his attendants fly to Calcutta while his own plane went by way of Karachi.

That night Chiang Kai-shek and Soong Mei-ling stayed at Karachi, and Zheng Jiemin was busier still, hardly sleeping the night through, directing the Juntong agents sent ahead in close secret protection, so that the next day he dozed the whole flight.

Chiang Kai-shek took few attendants that time, but Zheng Jiemin never appeared openly at the conference. Chiang stayed at a large hotel beside the pyramids, and Zheng was seldom seen coming or going; in fact he was busy every day with security and could not rest. The moment the conference ended, Zheng went ahead to Calcutta to make arrangements. While Chiang Kai-shek stopped in India, Zheng was in charge of his security too.

In September 1949, after John Leighton Stuart returned from Nanking to America, he telegraphed Guangzhou inviting Zheng Jiemin to America to discuss matters. Zheng told people: "The U.S. ambassador Stuart did not leave when Nanking was taken by the Communist army, and only returned to America at the very end; a few days ago he telegraphed inviting me to America. I reported to the Generalissimo [meaning Chiang Kai-shek], who has approved my making the trip. I am now rushing the procedures for the trip, and as soon as they are done I will go to Hong Kong and fly to America." As he spoke he wore a most pleased look, not at all downcast that the whole country was about to be liberated. He then pinned all his hopes on American help.

A few days before Guangzhou's liberation, Zheng Jiemin went to Hong Kong and on to America. Later it was heard that on his return from America to Taiwan, Chiang Kai-shek made him head of the National Security Bureau under the National Defence Council, to strengthen Taiwan's secret-service rule. His taking this post had an important bearing on this trip to America.

In his official dealings with Britain and America, the Americans gave Zheng Jiemin two medals and the British one. He also had a French medal, whose origin is unclear, and which he never spoke of in casual talk.

### Secret-Service Activities During the KMT-CCP Peace Talks

At the victory, the Kuomintang military and political men's greed for spoils broke out, and all prepared to "seize the takings." Zheng Jiemin too hoped to get a takeover post and make a fortune, and his wife hoped all the more that he would be put in charge of some fat takeover task. Unexpectedly Chiang Kai-shek made him a member of the "Reparations Committee" run by Jiang Menglin, which greatly deflated him.

Just as he was moping, afraid Chiang Kai-shek had forgotten him, an unexpected new task fell to him. Chiang Kai-shek decided to make him the Nationalist representative at the Beiping office of the Executive Headquarters of the Truce Mission, having him use the chance of the "peace talks" between the Kuomintang and the Communists to carry on secret-service activity in the liberated areas. This secret-service chief, the most active anti-Communist ordinarily, understood the Communist situation fairly well and felt the task most thorny, not easy to accomplish. He hurriedly consulted Dai Li, talking two or three days running. Dai declared to the great agents heading the various Juntong units that they must help Zheng accomplish the task with all their strength — men when men were wanted, money when money was wanted, radios, weapons, transport all supplied first and fully, no pretext for delay allowed. Dai held that Chiang Kai-shek's choosing Zheng for this task was a mark of trust not only in Zheng but in the Juntong, and that they must therefore seize the chance to reverse the years of repeated failure at planting work in the liberated areas. On the one hand Dai praised the wisdom of Chiang Kai-shek's decision, on the other he cursed roundly those who had opposed using such an open secret-service chief as the Nationalist representative, saying these dissenters had no brains, no common sense, and did not understand that the sole enemy at home after the victory over Japan was the Communist Party.

At the emergency meeting Dai Li convened, Zheng Jiemin's mood seemed very heavy, not full of cheer and gesturing as usual. He said simply that the task was weighty, bearing on the party and state's future, that his own strength was limited, that all must help him with a will, and that with Chiang Kai-shek's "wise leadership" and Dai Li's constant instruction he had the confidence and resolve to do the work well. Breaking his usual habit, he did not hold forth on his analyses. Everyone understood: with Dai Li present, for fear he would be impatient to hear it, no one put any question to Zheng.

Two or three days later Zheng went to the Juntong to find Mao Renfeng, and several department-head-rank agents surrounded him at once, asking about everything, and he talked a while. He held that the greatest and hardest task was how to restore transport in a short time. He said the several railways occupied by the Communists were the Kuomintang's arteries, and taking them back would be difficult. For over the years the Kuomintang and the Communists had fought and stopped and fought again, making it hard to convince the other side of one's "sincerity"; relying on political maneuvering alone to negotiate, without a strong army as backing, would not readily bring a satisfactory result. He held that relying on a few dozen planes to move troops was like scattering pepper — of little use — and that rail transport must be restored.

When someone asked whether the Soviet Union would openly block the Kuomintang's takeover of the Northeast and help the Communists, this Zheng Jiemin, who styled himself the "expert who understood the Soviet situation best," opened up and held forth without pause. He held the Soviet Union would not block openly but only strengthen aid to the Communists in secret, and cited many reasons to prove his view.

As for gathering intelligence in the liberated areas, he thought the chance excellent: areas that could not be entered ordinarily could now be entered openly. He said jokingly: "In the past you always said intelligence here was too scarce; in future, when you can't even get through all of it, don't blame me!"

On leaving, he gravely warned these great agents not to grow slack, not to see the problem too simply, not to pin everything on the two sides' negotiations, but to strengthen work against the Communists more than in any past period. He held the "peace talks" were only one side of solving the problem. He said the Americans would surely stand on the Kuomintang's side in this matter, and that the U.S.-appointed representative Robertson [later Robertson] had good relations with him. He said: "When three men hold a meeting and a matter comes to a vote, you can always make it two votes to one."

Before going to Beiping he picked, from the agents of the Juntong and the Ministry of Military Operations' Second Department, over a hundred men — Hu Pinghan, Zou Lufu, Wen Tianhe, Huang Weiqin, Huang Changxin, Pan Zhimin, Tu Xuwu, Xiao Fengqi, Guo Ziqi, Dong Chenglie, Huang Jiexin and others — to staff the truce teams in various places, and transferred the Juntong Communist-affairs section head Guo Ziming to be his adviser.

When the papers announced his appointment as Nationalist representative, many reporters went to interview him. Receiving reporters in Chungking and Beiping, he always said: "We won't settle old scores any more — they can't be settled anyway. We've talked eight or nine years; talk on, and we'll all be old." From the outset he meant to shift the whole blame for the failed negotiations onto the Communist side, as though the Kuomintang were most sincere. Yet before the talks even began, he and Kai were already secretly and actively planting secret-service activity in the liberated areas.

After Dai Li was killed in a plane crash on 17 March 1946, Chiang Kai-shek on the twentieth decided to make Zheng Jiemin succeed Dai Li as Juntong director. Mao Renfeng urgently telegraphed Zheng to return to Chungking at once. Zheng had planned to set off from Beiping on the twenty-second, but on the morning of the twenty-second another telegram came from him, saying urgent work with the Truce Mission kept him, and he changed it to noon on the twenty-fourth.

On the afternoon of the twenty-fourth Mao Renfeng took several department heads to the airfield to meet him. The plane was over two hours late; the agents and his wife were anxious, and kept sending the airfield-inspection agents to ask the airline, and though they knew there was no trouble were still uneasy. The weather that day was bad and the plane flew against a headwind, so it was over two hours late. Stepping off the plane, Zheng joked to those who had come to meet him: "The weather's terrible today — I was afraid Mr. Dai was calling me to dinner!" That evening Zheng Jiemin went to see Chiang Kai-shek, and the next morning went to the Juntong to gather the heads of the various units. Mao Renfeng first announced that Chiang Kai-shek had designated Zheng to replace Dai Li as director, and hoped all would, under Zheng's leadership, carry on Dai's will and do the Juntong's work well. Then Mao asked Zheng to give a talk. Zheng spoke very briefly, saying only that his present work was extremely important, so that he could not run the Juntong in person and it would still be led by Mao Renfeng, and that except for major matters to be studied with him, ordinary matters would be handled as before. He also said he had asked little about the Juntong in the past and was still out of touch with much of it.

When someone asked about the Truce Mission's work, his answer was also very brief, saying only that the problems were hard and the opponent not easy to deal with. He said the other side [meaning the Communists] worked most meticulously, not crudely like the Kuomintang, and that in some respects it was worth learning from.

In the afternoon he talked with Mao Renfeng for several hours. Afterward, by Mao's account, Zheng mainly wanted to learn about the Juntong's finances and personnel. Because he had avoided these before for fear of friction with Dai, he had never inquired; now that he was to be responsible he had to learn. Hearing that the Juntong's funds fell short of outlay month after month, always eating into next month's grain, deep in debt, kept going in the past only by Dai Li scraping and shifting from here to there, with personnel several times over the establishment, he was aghast, and declared at once he would carry out retrenchment and cut personnel, holding that the whole thing had been spread too large in the past, that no one afterward would have the drive to keep it up, that the work was heavier and harder than before, and that culling the weak and keeping the strong was the only way.

Though Zheng announced orally that during his work in Beiping the Juntong would still be run by Mao Renfeng, he had long since made up his mind: over years Dai Li had single-mindedly cultivated Mao Renfeng, who already monopolized power. In the past Zheng could leave it be, but now that Chiang Kai-shek had put this burden on him, he feared trouble, and so before leaving Chungking he went to see Chiang Kai-shek. He asked Chiang to send Tang Zong to act for him in running the Juntong. No sooner had he left than Chiang Kai-shek's order making Tang act for Zheng arrived too. Seeing this, Mao Renfeng knew Zheng must have said something to Chiang, making Chiang uneasy, so that Tang was sent to act. Tang too saw Zheng's intent — to make him the awkward man — and simply acted without acting, everything still run by Mao. From this time the seed of discord between Zheng and Mao was sown.

Zheng stayed in Chungking only four days and then went back to Beiping. Just then I had returned to Chungking after collecting Dai Li's remains, and went to Zheng's home to report the circumstances of Dai's death. When Zheng heard that after Dai's death his corpse had lain exposed three days on a hill in Jiangning county near Nanking, washed by heavy rain, he could not help a fellow-feeling of the fox mourning the hare, and said angrily: "How disgraceful! Our organization not only covers the whole country but is spread over the globe, yet a leader lies dead near Nanking three days before being found — how can that be? If others learn of it, it'll be a great joke!" Zheng's wife cut in from the side to demand of me the houses, cars and other things Dai Li had prepared in Shanghai, Nanking and Hankou. Zheng neither stopped her nor showed agreement, only pretending not to hear. Only after his wife had demanded this and that to her fill did he go on instructing me to hurry and clear up the enemy-and-puppet properties taken over in various places, lest the local agents, seizing on Dai Li's death, swallow the seized property.

Not long after Zheng returned to Beiping, the Kuomintang papers around the end of March carried news that after Dai Li's death Zheng Jiemin had succeeded as Juntong director, with Tang Zong and Mao Renfeng as deputy directors. Zheng was furious to see this, and in early April gave an interview to a Central News Agency reporter in Beiping, publicly denying the fact and saying he had not succeeded as Juntong director.

In early May, when I and the Juntong supervision-room head Liao Huaping went to Beiping to check over the property the Juntong had taken over, Zheng, still not over his anger on seeing me, said someone in Chungking was talking nonsense, and why had they announced he was director, adding to the difficulties of his work. I learned the news had come from a Central News Agency reporter through the Military Affairs Commission office, and explained it to him. Though Zheng had by then received Mao Renfeng's reply telegram, he was still so angry, which for him was a most rare display. Eating with me that day, Zheng was still saying: "If the government sends a Juntong director to hold peace talks with the Communists, what impression does that give people?" — as if before this news came out the Communists had not known he was a Juntong secret-service chief.

Zheng used every trick in the talks and fancied himself very clever. Besides publishing, in the Kuomintang papers, pages of material slandering and defaming the Communists, he directed agents to wreck the Communist office of the Truce Mission.

On 21 February 1946 a body called the "South Henan Refugees' Homecoming Petition Corps" went to petition the Executive Headquarters of the Truce Mission at the Peking Union Medical College. This "petition corps" was based on the landlords and local despots who had fled the Hebei liberated areas, organized and stirred up by the Juntong's Beiping station and the Beiping police criminal-police brigade on Zheng's orders, with a large batch of agents and gang riffraff mixed in among them to make trouble by plan. The agents incited these fugitive landlords and despots, saying the Communists would not withdraw their troops from those places (the liberated areas), leaving everyone homeless, and that they should go and settle scores with the Communists. Seeing agents backing them, these fugitive landlords and despots gathered and swarmed to the gate of the Truce Mission, shouting wildly and demanding by name that the Communist heads come out and answer. When the cadres of the Communist office explained the situation to them, the agents led the cry of "Communists withdraw from Hebei at once" and "Down with the Communist Party," not letting the Communist personnel finish, and at the agents' instigation rushed the Communist office all at once and beat it up. The agents meant to seize Communist documents in the confusion, but got only some unimportant things. The gendarmes and police present, notified beforehand by the agents, all looked on with folded arms, letting the mob smash the Communist office's window glass, desks and the rest before talking them into leaving.

That day the three sides' heads of the Truce Mission lodged a serious protest with the Beiping mayor Xiong Bin, demanding he arrest the culprits and guarantee no such outrage recur. Xiong Bin grumbled loudly over it: plainly it was the agents who did it, the secret-service chief Zheng Jiemin took no notice, so where was he, the mayor, to arrest these culprits from? Even the Americans then felt the tactic too crude and were much displeased. Knowing it had to do with Zheng, they privately told him not to use such methods again. Before his American master Zheng flatly denied any connection, insisting the common people had done it themselves.

One day in May, when I and Liao Huaping were dining at Zheng's home, the group head of the Beiping Garrison Command's inspection office, Luo Riming, came to see Zheng, and Zheng asked Luo to dine with them. During the meal Zheng suddenly asked Luo: "Still haven't found a single one?" Luo answered: "Still haven't." After these two lines they said no more of it.

The next day I saw Luo and learned that Zheng had had Luo send agents to tail the staff lodged at the Peking Hotel, to see whether any went at night to brothels in the Badahutong or took rooms at other hotels. Luo was in charge of inspecting hotels and brothels, and Zheng told him that if he found Communist personnel going to such places to arrest them, so that when the Communist side asked for their return he could hand them back and shame the Communists. In July or August I went to Beiping again and heard that still not a single Communist had been found at such places, which disappointed Zheng greatly. He was then most anxious to catch the Communist personnel in Beiping at some fault, adopting all sorts of surveillance methods, but never found a thing.

The Juntong's Beiping station had a special group solely for watching and tailing the Communist personnel in Beiping, and the station chief regularly reported to Zheng. Zheng paid close attention to this, and the group did not cease activity until the Truce Mission ended.

During his work with the Truce Mission, Zheng joined the Americans in many plots against the Communists, the details of which I do not know. I know only that each time he returned to Nanking and spoke at the Bureau of Secrets Preservation to some great agents about the problem of restoring transport, he said it gave him a great headache. Chiang Kai-shek too often pressed and reproached him over it.

Zheng Jiemin held that the Communists were "wrong" on the "big questions," since China's conditions did not suit Communism. But on the "small questions" of the Communist personnel's working spirit and style he thought them worth learning from. He held that the Communist personnel's general traits were plain living, thrift and hardship, earnest, solid work, strong organizational discipline, and a mild manner toward others. What gave him the worst headache was that many Kuomintang men worked sloppily, took no responsibility, and reported things untruthfully. This meant that in negotiating with the Communists he often, not having the matter clear, ran hard into a wall and could not get down from it, while the Communists' rebuttals were each grounded in fact. He often said that luckily he had a set of countermeasures (that is, of quibbling and playing the rogue) and could just manage to carry off the scene.

He complained the agents were useless, would not work well, and lived corruptly, his most capable important assistant, his chief of staff, included. Zheng put the blame for many failures on others, as though only he were most able and resourceful. He dreamed of taking the train from Beiping back to Nanking, but it never came true.

When the Truce Mission's work ended, Zheng published an account of his negotiations with the Communists, spinning slander and defamation and turning black into white to the utmost, laying the whole blame on the Communists' "lack of sincerity" to confuse the public. At the time many field units of the Bureau of Secrets Preservation gathered much intelligence on the many grievances the people of the whole country had against the Kuomintang, all of which Mao Renfeng sent Zheng to see. Apart from sending part of it to Chiang Kai-shek, Zheng kept most as files, not daring to let Chiang Kai-shek know the full extent of the popular dissatisfaction with the Kuomintang.

### Actively Opposing the Communists Yet Most Fearing Them

On 1 October 1946 the Juntong was reorganized into the Defence Ministry's Bureau of Secrets Preservation, with Chiang Kai-shek making Zheng director and Mao Renfeng deputy director. Once the formal establishment was issued, the organization, personnel and funds were all much reduced from the Juntong period, to less than a third. The Military Affairs Commission was wound up and reorganized into the Defence Ministry, whose departments and bureaus were set up one after another in July, Zheng Jiemin's Second-Department headship being announced then too, while the Bureau of Secrets Preservation was dragged out over three months before being settled. Zheng Jiemin strongly favoured retrenching this reorganized, renamed secret-service organ. He had always opposed Dai Li's reckless style of ignoring the establishment and budget; while Dai lived he could do nothing about it, but after Dai's death, made to shoulder this broken burden, he felt it could not be kept up and had to take it in hand.

Zheng returned from Beiping to Chungking, and before the Juntong prepared to move to Nanking gathered the heads of the various Juntong sections for a meeting. He stressed that the Juntong's work henceforth was struggle with the Communists, which required able, capable men to shoulder this weighty task, so it were better to have fewer but abler men, the target not too large. He also mentioned that commander-in-chief Zhu De had long telegraphed Chiang Kai-shek to abolish all secret-service organs, and that to avoid adding to Chiang Kai-shek's difficulties they should of their own accord put forward the method of "culling the weak and keeping the strong" and "retrenching outlay," and must do the work well. At the time the heads of the various Juntong units, led by Mao Renfeng, all hoped to keep the huge apparatus and numerous personnel, which is why he raised these views against the tendency.

During Zheng Jiemin's time in Beiping, his Defence Ministry Second-Department headship was held in turn by Hou Teng and She Dian as acting, and the Bureau directorship by Mao Renfeng as acting. From June 1946 on, Zheng often returned to Nanking, as a rule only gathering the heads of the two units for a meeting and briefly recounting his work with the Truce Mission. He consistently slandered the Communists, seeking to cover up Chiang Kai-shek's plot of "false peace, real war preparation." He several times cited the case of a Kuomintang team member shot dead by a stray bullet at Yuncheng as an example of the Communists breaking faith and shooting Kuomintang truce-team personnel, to defame the Communists.

When Chiang Kai-shek's dispositions for large-scale civil war were complete and the Truce Mission's work halted, Zheng left his chief of staff Huo Wenzhi in Beiping and flew back to Nanking himself. Having failed the "restore rail transport" task Chiang Kai-shek had given him, and taken a severe reproach from Chiang, he hated the Communists the more. In 1947 Zheng ran the Bureau of Secrets Preservation's persecution of the underground Communist parties everywhere to an unprecedented degree. He put almost all his energy into this, to vent his fury at the Communists and to coordinate with Chiang Kai-shek's military offensive.

In early September that year the Bureau's Beiping station found leads to the Communist Beiping underground organization and radio; after Zheng Jiemin and Mao Renfeng studied it, they at once sent the Bureau's Operations Department (in charge of counter-espionage, action, defection-inducement, psychological warfare and so on) head Ye Xiangzhi to Beiping to run the wrecking. After Ye arrived, since the case was grave and implicated many senior personnel of the Baoding Pacification Headquarters, Zheng at once flew to Beiping in person to direct the work.

Through the development of this case, radios were later wrecked in Xi'an, Lanzhou and elsewhere, over ten Communist underground workers arrested, and an underground organization wrecked in the Northeast too.

Zheng was much excited by this wrecking, and besides reporting to Chiang Kai-shek from time to time, repeatedly gathered the great agents at the Bureau to study it, counting it a proudest achievement. He stressed that henceforth, to coordinate with the front's military offensive, the Communist party organizations active in the rear must be wrecked with all their strength, to stabilize the rear order. What most interested him were the Communist underground radios seized. Before he saw them he had judged these underground radios must be new-type sets supplied by the Soviet Union to the Communists for secret work, hard to detect. When the agents sent the seized documents and radios to Nanking, he was anxious to look, and to his surprise these radios were not Soviet-made new-type secret sets at all, but very crudely made. Having seen them, he first said mockingly: "We threw these things into the rubbish bin long ago, and they still treasure them." But after leafing through some archive material, he changed his tune: "To do so much work with such radios is really no small thing — this is where they are stronger than us."

In November that year the Juntong wrecked a Communist supply body in Shanghai, again with Zheng hurrying to direct in person.

The affair went roughly thus. The agents the Juntong had planted in the Second Department of the First Pacification Zone headquarters, inspecting passing boats at Sheyangkou in northern Jiangsu, found a suspicious wooden boat loaded with goods forbidden to be shipped to the northern-Jiangsu liberated area. The agents detained the whole crew, and by close search and cruel torture learned it was linked to a Communist underground supply unit; besides pursuing more leads, they reported to the Bureau of Secrets Preservation. Mao Renfeng at once told Zheng Jiemin, and Zheng personally drafted a telegram instructing the department head Jiang Jianmin to track down the personnel involved and widen the wrecking. After Jiang Jianmin threatened and enticed the arrested in many ways, one wavered and defected, revealing that the higher leading body was in Shanghai but not the exact place, knowing only the contact method. On getting the report Zheng told Jiang Jianmin to take the turncoat to Shanghai. He himself hurried there overnight, gathered the great agents in Shanghai — the Songhu Garrison Command inspection-office head Tao Yishan, the Bureau's Shanghai station chief Liu Fangxiong, and Zhang Shi working in the Shanghai police bureau — and secretly arranged to pick able agents to join Jiang Jianmin in hunting Communist underground workers in Shanghai. The next day, when Jiang Jianmin brought the turncoat and a few agents to Shanghai, Zheng received him at once and had him coordinate closely with Tao Yishan and the others. Under Zheng's personal direction, they first wrecked the Communist rear-supply main station in Shanghai, arresting the station head Feng Bochang, Liu Ming and over twenty others, and seizing large numbers of documents and, stored in a warehouse, over a thousand bolts of cloth, dozens of barrels of dye, and much other goods, and finding a small steam launch and goods off Wusongkou.

Just as Tao Yishan, Jiang Jianmin and the others were delightedly reckoning the haul no small thing, Zheng thought it not enough and that they must "strike while the iron is hot" and keep hitting hard in pursuit. As a result they wrecked, on a certain road in Shanghai, the joint office of the Communist Central China Bureau, the Jiangsu-Anhui Border Region government, the Central China Bank and other units, and arrested a dozen more.

Zheng Jiemin's style differed somewhat from Dai Li's. He never presided in person over the interrogation of arrested Communist personnel, still less, like Dai Li, loved to direct agents in person to use all sorts of cruel torture on them. He always had the agents do this, occasionally going during an interrogation to listen. Before going, he first had someone tell the interrogating agent not to rise when he came in. When he went he only stood to one side and listened, unlike Dai Li, who either broke in to ask questions or drove off the interrogating agent and sat down to interrogate himself. Zheng disliked showing himself and was unwilling to receive a turncoat at once, fearing a sudden attack. Apart from instructing the agents behind the scenes how to proceed, what most interested him was studying the various materials seized; he could read them for hours at a stretch without letting go, and could find fresh leads in them to widen the wrecking. From the many materials taken in the wrecking of the Communist underground rear-supply body in Shanghai, he found leads connected with the Shanghai Communist supply main station, and one after another wrecked some Communist underground rear-supply bodies in Qingdao, Ningbo, Dinghai, Yantai and elsewhere. At a Bureau meeting he specially spoke on how to find leads in seized materials, telling everyone not to let a scrap of paper or a single word slip lightly.

As Bureau director Zheng went in only an hour or two each morning. The drawers of his office desk were full of Communist-published books, periodicals and documents gathered from all over. The documents he endorsed were few; apart from major matters such as the wrecking of Communist organizations and the arrest of Communist personnel, which he endorsed, ordinary matters were decided by Mao Renfeng. After going through a few dossiers, he would always find some books and periodicals in the drawer, leaf through them, and pick a few to take home and read closely. The Bureau's wireless main station then had two sets solely for listening to Communist broadcasts, and each day copied out two copies of what was received, one sent to him, the other read by Mao Renfeng and passed to the Intelligence Department (the Bureau's First Department) to be filed. He read these transmissions very carefully and took them away after reading.

In anti-Communist activity Zheng always racked his brains and threw in his all. He constantly studied Communist documents; besides carrying on slander propaganda, he sought loopholes in Communist policy for secret-service activity. Using "freedom of religious belief," he directed some Catholic and Protestant Juntong agents to carry on secret-service activity in the liberated areas. The Shanxi Catholic Li Guanghe (Tianjin inspection-office head before liberation) had many ties in the Shanxi diocese, and Zheng had Li make arrangements among the believers. There was also the Belgian priest de Jaegher, whom Zheng always used, under the cloak of religion, for secret-service activity in North China.

Besides, he used the Communist minority-nationalities policy, ordering the field provincial stations several times to develop their organizations among the minorities as far as they could. He often said: "In future, in some areas where the Han can't get a footing, these comrades among the minorities [meaning the agents who had joined the Juntong] will be of great use."

Before liberation Mao Renfeng in person drew in capitalists in Shanghai, Chungking, Kunming and elsewhere, hoping they would help the Bureau's work in exchange for guarantees of their interests abroad. By Mao Renfeng's account this too was a fine method Zheng Jiemin had thought up from studying Communist policy. At the time many thought that when the Communists came the capitalists would be settled with, but Zheng Jiemin said these men would have no trouble, and held that those who did not leave and could be drawn in should be drawn in as far as possible.

During Zheng Jiemin's directorship many agents thought planting work in the liberated areas too hard, and he always said everyone was unwilling to use his head to study Communist documents, and so was forever at a loss. He found no few loophole-methods in Communist documents. He often said: "The Communists cannot do without the common people. So long as they want them, we have a way. Whatever kind of people they want, we plant among that kind." Later, when Mao Renfeng succeeded as director, he planted undercover agents specially among doctors, nurses, teachers, shop clerks, small traders and the like, reversing the past method of recruiting agents from those with complex social ties, the active sort, and local despots, gentry and gangster bosses. This too was decided in Zheng Jiemin's time as director.

Zheng was the most active anti-Communist all his life, and at the same time most feared the Communists. Before liberation he always urged others to oppose the Communists while himself in mortal dread. In open settings he held forth as usual, saying the Communists' military victories were accidental, that if the Kuomintang would correct its mistakes and truly obey Chiang Kai-shek's command it could turn defeat into victory. He also held the Americans would surely not let the Kuomintang collapse and would back it with all their strength. But behind the scenes he showed his trusted men a pessimistic, fearful mind.

In 1948 Zheng Jiemin's younger brother Zheng Tingfeng was commander of the Ninety-Fourth Army, stationed in Beiping under Fu Zuoyi, charged with defending the city. No sooner had the Liaoning-Shenyang campaign ended, with Chiang Kai-shek's crack forces in the Northeast wholly wiped out, than Zheng Jiemin hurried from Nanking to Beiping and asked Fu Zuoyi in person to let Zheng Tingfeng leave his post and return south. Fu, for the sake of face, had to let Zheng Tingfeng hand over the army command for a short time to the deputy commander Zhu Jingmin as acting.

After bringing Zheng Tingfeng back to Nanking, Zheng Jiemin would not let him return to Beiping. He held that once the Northeast PLA entered the pass, to stay on in North China meant, if not death, then capture. He had no confidence at all whether North China could be held. Later he got Zheng Tingfeng a post as deputy commander-in-chief of the Twenty-First Corps on Hainan Island; and before Hainan was liberated, he summoned his brother to Taiwan, dreading this brother be taken alive by the PLA.

When the Huai-Hai campaign had just begun, Zheng's wife was already packing to flee to Taiwan. Zheng himself said to people: "The Communists can't fight a war on their own initiative. This decisive battle at Xuzhou is the starting point of our turning defeat into victory; recovering North China and the Northeast in one stroke rests on this." Many senior Kuomintang military and political officials in Nanking then counted on the same. In fact Zheng knew better: before the Huai-Hai campaign even ended, all his movable property in Nanking had been transferred, and only the immovable houses and furniture were left.

Zheng Jiemin fancied he understood the international situation fairly well and several times predicted the Americans would surely make war on the Soviet Union before it recovered from its crushing losses in the Second World War. Such assertions suited Chiang Kai-shek's taste well, and Chiang had him lecture on it at the Central Training Corps, the Central Military Academy and many other training organs, to steady people's minds.

He held that if the Americans made war on the Soviet Union, America would surely defeat it, and only then would the Kuomintang have hope of wiping out the Chinese Communists. He was not as insanely boastful about the Communists as Chen Cheng, who said things like "wipe out the Communists in three months," but was constantly on edge. Talking with some intimates, he several times showed the dread in his heart of war with the Communists. He once said: "Best of all would be a Third World War; relying on our anti-Communism alone, the difficulties are many, and fighting on it is hard to be sure of winning."

### His Time as Vice-Minister of National Defence

One evening in late November 1947 Chiang Kai-shek summoned Zheng Jiemin for a long talk. The next day Zheng's wife happily told people Zheng was to be promoted again. Those days, going to work at the Bureau, Zheng too showed his joy. On 5 December Chiang Kai-shek did indeed publicly appoint Zheng Jiemin vice-minister of national defence. The Defence Ministry then had three vice-ministers: Qin Dechun in charge of general business, Liu Shiyi in charge of defence personnel, and Zheng Jiemin succeeding Huang Zhenqiu in charge of defence materials. After Huang Zhenqiu went to Guangdong with T.V. Soong, the post fell vacant. To do all he could to secure American arms and materials, Chiang Kai-shek judged Zheng most suited to the post, and a few days before had asked Zheng's view, which Zheng accepted gladly. When the order was published, department-head-rank personnel of the Bureau and the Second Department went to his home to congratulate him, and many reporters swarmed there to ask for interviews and photographs. With guests thronging his door, he found it too much trouble and that very evening went to Shanghai to rest, only going formally to the Defence Ministry to take up the post two days later.

After a week or so Zheng went to work at the Bureau, and his mood was not as happy as those first days. When someone asked about his new post, he kept shaking his head: "Hard to manage, hard to manage." Later he slowly let out some of the situation, saying now that all-out war had opened, the hardest thing was that ammunition could not keep up, especially for the wholly U.S.-equipped units, which used up much ammunition fighting the PLA, the stores dwindling by the day, while the things the Americans had promised were slow to come and could not be made at home, so that fighting on they would end with guns but no bullets and have to fight with bayonets and grenades.

The Bureau then controlled the Traffic Police Directorate, which had formed the old Juntong armed agents and traitor units into some twenty traffic-police corps, all equipped by the American side through SACO. Zheng always favoured throwing these units into the anti-Communist civil war to win more of Chiang Kai-shek's trust, while Mao Renfeng hoped to keep some under his own control. Zheng had at first meant to hand all the stored arms and ammunition to the Traffic Police Directorate to equip these units, but Mao Renfeng kept back no little. After Zheng became vice-minister, knowing U.S.-made ammunition was scarce, he agreed with Mao's view that, apart from what had been issued to the Directorate, the stores should be kept well in hand, and had the General Affairs Department copy him a table of the U.S.-made ammunition in the three ordnance depots at Chungking, Guiyang and Nanking. Seeing the stored quantities still large, Zheng said they would be used at the crucial point when needed, to greater effect. He always regarded these traffic-police corps as a strong anti-Communist force, calling them a "pocket commando." Since this agent force was all equipped with new-type light American weapons and mostly American-trained, light in movement, its officers mostly Juntong agents, Chiang Kai-shek valued it too. In the War of Liberation, though savage, this force was likewise could not stand a single blow, and apart from a few who fled to Taiwan, the rest were all wiped out by the PLA.

After becoming vice-minister of national defence, Zheng was busier than before, and could not attend well to his concurrent Second-Department headship and Bureau directorship, both of whose work was extremely important. Chiang Kai-shek, hoping he would put more energy into securing American aid and unit supply, hinted he should resign the Second-Department headship. At first Zheng clung to it a little; later, when Chiang asked him to his face who should succeed as department head, Zheng had meant to recommend his trusted man Zhang Yanyuan, and Chiang agreed to this old agent of Dai Li's "Ten-Man Group" too. But Zheng did not put in the request in time, mainly because after T.V. Soong became Guangdong chairman he transferred Zhang Yanyuan from Second-Department deputy head to be Guangdong deputy security commander. Fearing to offend T.V. Soong, Zheng dared not recommend Zhang, and though there were others in the Juntong who could fill the post, they were not his trusted men, so he was unwilling to recommend them, and dragged it out for a time.

The Second-Department deputy head Hou Teng had long harboured the ambition to be department head. Hou had been Second-Department deputy head before, then military attaché at the embassy in America. After Hou resumed the deputy headship, while Zheng was working with the Truce Mission in Beiping, Hou acted as department head for a long time. Hou had made all sorts of arrangements; after Zheng was promoted vice-minister, he lobbied hard, on the one hand through the deputy chief of staff Lin Wei, on the other having his wife work on Zheng's wife, and at last Lin Wei came forward to join Zheng in a joint request recommending Hou; fearing to offend Lin, Zheng agreed. Seeing Zheng and Lin's joint signature, Chiang Kai-shek approved it. Once Hou became department head he flew his own flag, not only refusing the Bureau's leadership but no longer taking Zheng's command either. This huge open secret-service unit, always held by the Juntong, thus slipped from the Bureau's control. At the time Mao Renfeng and many Juntong agents cursed Zheng roundly for being irresolute and too selfish, giving this unit away for nothing.

After becoming vice-minister, Zheng Jiemin did not mean to give up the concurrent Bureau directorship, and so was always in friction with Mao Renfeng. Mao drew in Juntong men by inheriting Dai Li's "family estate," and his strength in the Bureau far surpassed Zheng's faction. Zheng and Mao each had their own set of ways of drawing in agents. Mao strongly favoured restoring the Juntong to its scale under Dai Li, which held great appeal for the over seventy percent of the Bureau's agents who were graduates of the special-training classes and the backbone men who had done secret-service work long without schooling or seniority. Zheng favoured expanding outward, doing all he could to encourage the school graduates and senior agents to develop into the open sphere and hold public posts. With his support, no few Juntong agents ran in the 1947 elections for National Assembly delegates and Legislative Yuan members. In the elections in various places, agents elected National Assembly delegates came to over thirty; those whose names I can still recall are: Ji Zhangjian, Ma Zhichao, Ma Hansan, Lou Zhaoyuan, Zheng Xiuyuan, Ke Jian'an, Zhang Jiaquan, Li Guanghe, Wang Fuzhou, Liu Yizhou, Wang Kong'an, Tu Shoumei, Luo Yi, Jiang Zhiyun, Luo Guoxi, Gong Shaoxia, Wu Jingqun, Wang Zhaohuai, Wang Zuhua, Chen Xudong, Chen Shixian, Jian Pu.

When the Juntong agent Wang Xinheng ran for the Legislative Yuan in Shanghai, Zheng Jiemin went to Shanghai in person to work for him. Wang Xinheng was Zheng's Sun Yat-sen University classmate in Moscow, had been a Juntong department head for years, and was then investigation-office head and adviser of the Shanghai municipal government. When Wang Xinheng ran, those who openly backed him were a few Juntong-controlled peripheral bodies, insignificant societies like the Shanghai Local Association, the Shanghai Social Security Promotion Association, and the Blue-and-White Athletic Association. Compared with Yan Huiqing, one of the Shanghai local gentry and diplomatic elders whom the Shanghai mayor Wu Guozhen recommended, Wang was far inferior in seniority and social ties; but because Zheng Jiemin directed the Shanghai agents all to back Wang, Wang's votes actually exceeded Yan Huiqing's and he was elected first. Though Yan had the full backing of Wu Guozhen and others, he was elected only third.

Those Zheng Jiemin drew in within the Bureau were only a few great agents, and in his "Guangdong faction" those who stayed to work in the Bureau were all of thin seniority. He promoted Zhang Jixun, who had headed the Juntong's Communist-affairs section, to be director's-office chief, equivalent to chief secretary, who by rights should have led the inside department heads. But the First Department head He Zhiyuan and the Second Department head Ye Xiangzhi, backbone men of Mao Renfeng's "Zhejiang faction," had been Zhang Jixun's department heads or deputy heads when he was a section head, and naturally would not take orders from Zhang. Add to that Mao's covert direction, making trouble for Zhang at every turn, and Zhang could not work. In the end Zheng had to transfer Zhang out and give the post to another trusted man, Huang Tianmai.

The struggle between the Zheng and Mao factions in the Bureau grew ever sharper, and once Zheng became vice-minister and lost the concurrent Second-Department headship, Mao concentrated all his strength on seizing the directorship. Besides the strength he had planted in the Bureau, more than enough to crush Zheng, Mao had the full backing of Chiang Kai-shek's intimate Yu Jishi, and both sides did all they could to find the other's faults and report them to Chiang Kai-shek. To make the agents work themselves to the bone for him, Chiang Kai-shek too hoped Zheng would give his mind to the Defence Ministry's work. Add to that the Mao faction constantly informing on Zheng through Yu Jishi — even the matter of Zheng's wife charging the pearl powder she took to the Bureau reached Chiang Kai-shek. Chiang once asked Zheng to his face: "Why does your wife eat pearl powder?" — reducing Zheng to sorry straits with no answer. But Chiang still cherished him greatly, and apart from a light reproach never pursued it. At last, when it truly could go on no longer, Chiang had Zheng resign the concurrent Bureau directorship, and in early 1948 Mao Renfeng succeeded as director.

Though Zheng was left a bare vice-minister, in fact Chiang Kai-shek's trust in him was not lessened. Zheng too felt he could henceforth develop in new directions elsewhere.

Hearing that T.V. Soong no longer wished to be Guangdong provincial chairman, he had others lobby for him to succeed. Chiang Kai-shek summoned and consoled him, telling him to work with an easy mind and do the work of begging arms aid from the Americans well, which was more important than anything.

Before nationwide liberation, he proposed to Chiang Kai-shek forming a U.S.-style mechanized corps to fight the PLA in positional warfare. He grumbled to people that this was the only way to win, since the PLA had changed its old strategy and dared fight the Kuomintang in pitched battle. If several mechanized-equipped armies were concentrated to attack the PLA's main force, the past defeats could be retrieved. Though he boasted loudly, Chiang Kai-shek knew in his heart that, first, there was no time, and second, there was no longer that strength. Zheng's dream of becoming commander-in-chief of a mechanized corps came to nothing.

### His Shirking Style and His Methods of Corruption and Graft

Zheng Jiemin was called "seasoned and weighty," but in the old society this phrase was almost the equal of "old, crafty and cunning." He always feared responsibility, was vague about everything, and gave no definite answer. When friends asked his help or subordinates sought instructions, he always answered in equivocal terms, dreading to bear the least responsibility for a weighty matter. Fearing to offend the grandees in power, he yielded at every turn, and one seldom saw him stand firm against anyone. In his struggle for power and profit with Mao Renfeng too, he always secretly directed his trusted men to deal with Mao, while himself all smiles when he met Mao, never showing displeasure.

While Dai Li lived, this set of Zheng's had not yet been seen through. After Dai's death, many matters needing his decision, his slippery ways were soon seen through by all. Below are a few examples to stand for the rest.

On the question of retrenching and cutting Juntong personnel, Zheng never dared say a word. At the time this secret-service group was loathed not only by the whole nation but by some within the reactionary camp, so that Dai Li's death set all sides attacking it. Zheng several times came from Beiping to Chungking to preside over Juntong business meetings. The great agents attending hoped he would smooth things over on all sides. For fear the attack would turn on him, he never gave a straight answer to the question, saying only things like "in the past we were not fierce enough with the enemy and not mild enough within, so it came to this." He proposed that henceforth, in dealing with the Communists, numbers did not matter, but abler men were needed. In fact he agreed with cutting personnel and retrenching. By Mao Renfeng's account, had Chiang Kai-shek not single-mindedly protected the Juntong and done all he could to keep the organization, even the later state of the Bureau of Secrets Preservation could not have existed. Mao said Zheng only stepped into a ready-made directorship and never put out an ounce of effort.

When the Military Affairs Commission was changed into the Defence Ministry, it was decided to set up a Military Justice Bureau. Had Dai Li lived, he would surely have contrived to grab this body. Mao Renfeng suggested to Zheng that they take it in hand, which would greatly facilitate the Juntong's work, but Zheng gave no sign. The great agent Xu Yedao went to Zheng in person, asking Zheng to recommend him to Chiang Kai-shek to be its head. Zheng only promised to inquire first, without saying whether he would recommend. Seeing Zheng lacked sincerity, Xu went to Yu Jishi. Seeing Xu Yedao was an old Juntong agent, Chiang Kai-shek at once approved his becoming Military Justice Bureau head. Thereafter Xu was much displeased with Zheng, often calling him behind his back an "old slyboots."

Dai Li had run gang activity in a big way during the war, using the gang organizations that reached deep among the people throughout the country for secret-service work, and after the victory used a body called the "New Society Undertakings Construction Association" to unify the country's gangs. This association's directors and supervisors were all big gang bosses. The directors included Du Yuesheng, Yang Hu, Yang Qingshan, Xiang Haiqian, Fan Shaozeng, Zhang Zilian, Tian Desheng, Zhang Fang, Xu Weibin and others; the supervisors included Huang Jinrong, Zhang Shusheng, Pan Zixin, Li Fulin, Wang Muyi and others. After Dai Li's death the old Juntong agent Xu Weibin ran it as secretary-general; the head office was in Shanghai, the organization very large, and it became a powerful peripheral body of the Bureau of Secrets Preservation. On the eve of the 1947 National Assembly elections in various places, the CC Clique, much displeased with this body and afraid the Juntong would use it to contend for votes, had the Ministry of Social Affairs come forward to ban it, forbidding its activity anywhere. Both Mao Renfeng and Xu Weibin went to Zheng Jiemin, asking him to report to Chiang Kai-shek and request the association be kept. Zheng not only did not go to Chiang, but to curry favour with Chen Lifu even decided to abolish it.

The great traitor Zhou Fohai not only worked for the Juntong during the war, striking at the Communists and the New Fourth Army in the occupied areas, but after the victory arranged takeovers for the Juntong and colluded with the Japanese army to block the New Fourth Army from entering Shanghai and Nanking, doing the Juntong no little service. After the victory Dai Li sent Zhou Fohai, Ding Mocun, Luo Junqiang, Yang Hanhua and others to Chungking to be protected from legal punishment. At the time all sides were displeased and opinion was in an uproar, and Dai ignored it all. After Zheng took over he was unwilling to go on carrying Zhou and the rest, and sent Zhou and the others back from Chungking to Nanking to be tried by the court. The Tang so-and-so whom Chiang Kai-shek had sent to Nanking to liaise with the puppets, knowing Zhou Fohai had made no few "contributions" to the reactionaries in the past, went to Zheng to speak for Zhou. Zheng put him off: "It's for the leader [meaning Chiang Kai-shek] to decide; I can't handle it." At the time some Bureau agents pointed out to Zheng that if Zhou were shot, who would work hard for them afterward? His answer was simple: "That's a matter for later; we'll speak of it later."

In February 1947 the "gold panic case" that shook the whole country broke out in Shanghai; since the Control Yuan had sent four censors to investigate thoroughly, Chiang Kai-shek specially sent Zheng Jiemin to take part in the investigation too. Zheng had long had the Shanghai station's report on the case: it arose mainly from long-standing malpractice and corruption by T.V. Soong's trusted man Bei Zuyi in collusion with the Shanghai gold-trade association's chairman Zhan Liansheng. He knew clearly the case would implicate T.V. Soong. Fearing to offend Soong, he was most unwilling to go, but the Shanghai and Nanking papers had carried the news that Chiang Kai-shek had sent him, so he had to. After the Shanghai station reported the whole situation to him in detail, he went to see Du Yuesheng, then at once flew back to Nanking to report the inside story to Chiang in person, advising that the matter not be widened and the case be closed early to avoid too much implication. Chiang agreeing with his view, he went to Shanghai again and told the several great Juntong agents there — Wang Xinheng, Liu Fangxiong, Tao Yishan and others — of Chiang's attitude to the case. Many were implicated, over forty found in the investigation, one very important figure being the Xinda cotton-firm boss Sun Zixin, who fled to Hong Kong under the agents' protection. From first to last Zheng held to the attitude of making a big matter small and a small matter nothing, and in the end only Bei Zuyi was removed from the central-bank governorship, and the Central Bank's business-bureau head Lin Fengbao, deputy head Yang Anren, and Zhan Liansheng, three men, handed to the Shanghai district court for prison terms. This great corruption-and-malpractice case that shook the times was thus settled; Zheng offended no one and secretly gained no little himself.

Zheng Jiemin's methods of corruption and graft were quite "clever." He always feigned "incorruptibility," and none of his subordinates dared bribe or give gifts to him directly. In fact he was a master of corruption, but never handled it himself, letting his wife Ke Shufen come forward. He put on a show of fearing his wife to the utmost, to make people believe he could do nothing about her; and should a matter blow up, he could feign complete ignorance and bear no responsibility. So when people said to his face that he feared his wife, he always admitted it with a smile, sometimes adding by way of self-mockery: "Fearing the wife has its uses — it saves trouble."

Though he played this trick on the matter, the sharp-eyed saw it clearly. Each day he came home to see the household's things growing more by the day and a new house going up — could he not know where it all came from?

During the war his wife often had the military and deputy military attachés the Second Department sent abroad buy things for her, and as a rule did not pay, in effect having people give her gifts. The then consul at Calcutta in India, Chen Zhiping (a Juntong agent), bought luxury daily goods specially for Dai Li; she too often had Chen buy this and that, and as a rule the Juntong paid for it after. Of the things bought abroad she kept what she liked and sent the rest to an auction house near Chuanyan First Lane on Linjiang Road in Chungking where she lived, to be sold on commission; much of the foreign goods on show at this auction house was sent by her.

Those subordinates she squeezed always hoped to let Zheng know they had given gifts, and often brought things to his home in person. Whenever Zheng saw someone come to see him carrying a gift, he always made an excuse to slip away and let his wife take it. Such small dealings Zheng really did not handle, all done by his wife coming forward. After Dai Li's death, with nothing to fear, he did it in a big way, and no longer feigned propriety on many matters but set his own hand to them.

In May 1946 the Juntong supervision-room head Liao Huaping and I, as chairman and deputy chairman of the Juntong property-clearance committee, went to Beiping to check over the enemy-and-puppet goods the Beiping office head Ma Hansan had taken over. Ma handed over a great stack of inventories. When Liao asked him for the original lists to check against, Ma first said he was sorting them out, and a day later said they were lost. Liao insisted on having them, and the two quarrelled. At last Ma said shamelessly: "I've given them to Mr. Zheng — go and ask him for them." That evening Liao and I went to see Zheng and asked whether he had received the original lists Ma gave him. With things come to this pass, Zheng had to say haltingly: "I've seen these things; basically there's no discrepancy — take over as much as they hand over." No sooner were we out the door than Liao grumbled loudly: "What's there to check? Let's just go back." This muddled account of several hundred thousand silver dollars was thus carelessly closed.

In the summer of 1946, just after I returned to Hunan, I got an urgent telegram from Zheng in Beiping telling me to hurry back to Chungking at once, because a wooden boat carrying Juntong goods from Chungking to Nanking had sunk near Tangjiatuo outside Chungking. On this boat was the coffin of Zheng's mother-in-law, which had drifted off no one knew where, and he hoped it could be recovered by every possible means. That night Zheng's wife telephoned me long-distance from Nanking, weeping and shouting on the line, insisting I find her mother's coffin. On the one hand I urgently telegraphed the relevant Chungking authorities to send men to search and salvage along the way, on the other I hurried back to Chungking overnight.

I reached Chungking three days later; Zheng's mother-in-law's coffin had already been recovered by the inspection-office's water-inspection station and brought back to Tangjiatuo. Fearing water had got into the coffin, I had the tightly-wound thick hemp ropes untied and the lid lifted for a look. On opening it I took one look and at once had it nailed and bound up again as before: around that old woman's corpse was packed opium wrapped in oil-paper. I at once warned the three orderlies who had opened the coffin not to talk, threatening them with a charge of disobeying orders, gave each a hundred yuan to buy their silence, and picked a General Affairs Department clerk to escort the coffin to Nanking.

Back in Nanking I saw Zheng's wife and told her that, fearing water after the coffin was salvaged, I had opened the lid and seen what was inside. Her face changed at once, and she cursed her brother Ke Xinwu, saying he must have been up to mischief. Then she feigned earnestness in charging me on no account to let Zheng Jiemin know. When the coffin reached Nanking, she and her brother knelt at the riverside at Xiaguan to receive this opium-laden coffin, without a trace of reproach for her brother, and even gave the escorting agent cloth, leather shoes and the like.

That autumn the Juntong decided to ship part of the two thousand ten-wheel trucks in Chungking to Nanking, to go into the transport business jointly with the Jiangnan Bus Company, and I went to Shanghai to arrange the purchase of five thousand large drums of petrol. One day the boss of the Shanghai Lu Genji Construction Works, Lu Genquan, came to see me. Lu Genquan had been on good terms with Dai Li, had helped the Juntong with takeovers in Shanghai after the victory and done business for the great agents, and was then building the Juntong's office block in Nanking, so we were on good terms too. Lu asked me, when I bought and shipped petrol to Chungking, to add one or two large drums to go along. I said the purchase documents and the papers arranging shipping with the Merchants' Steam Navigation Company had already stated the quantity and could not be changed, and refused his request.

At the time petrol was extremely scarce everywhere in the southwest and hard to ship from Shanghai. The Merchants' Steam Navigation Company, fearing accidents in shipping petrol, refused the many organs that asked it to allot boats for oil. But the Juntong not only had an agent body in the company — a guard-inspection group — but knew many of its dark secrets, so arranging boats was easy. Buying a large drum of American petrol in Shanghai, one paid the official price for only fifty gallons though it in fact held fifty-three, and shipping it at the official rate came to less than a tael of gold in all; shipped to Chungking, Chengtu and such places, a drum of petrol sold for over two taels of gold — a business that returned its capital over again in profit, though without privilege one could not make this money.

Two days later Zheng's wife invited me to dinner at her home, and Lu Genquan was there too. No sooner were we seated than Zheng Jiemin telephoned long-distance from Beiping; she talked a few words first, then had me take the call. Zheng said on the phone that Lu Genquan needed to ship things from Chungking to Shanghai, and asked me to help Lu buy a thousand large drums of petrol to ship to Chungking along with the Juntong's purchase. Of course I agreed. The next day I explained the situation to Mao Renfeng and changed the purchase figure in the documents to six thousand five hundred drums. I used the chance to add five hundred drums for myself, and had Lu acknowledge these as the increase he wanted.

After the petrol was sold in Chungking, in settling accounts Lu said to me: "This time I acted purely as a favour for others." With one long-distance telephone call and one dinner given by his wife, Zheng Jiemin had scooped over a thousand taels of gold.

In 1947, heedless of others' talk, Zheng Jiemin approved Lu Genquan's purchase, at a very cheap price, of a fairly large sawmill together with its land that the Juntong had taken over at Yangshupu in Shanghai. The works covered several dozen mu and had its own timber wharf and warehouses. The exchange between them was that Lu would build Zheng a three-storey garden villa on Beiping Road in Nanking.

The above are only a few concrete examples I know of. As for Zheng Jiemin's fiftieth birthday, when his wife collected birthday tribute in quantity, and his everyday brokering of business through others, pleading favours, taking bribes and the like — there was really too much to recount in detail. Every time the couple came to Shanghai, the agent chiefs there had to make their offerings of "filial respect." Not even his wife could say just what was in Zheng Jiemin's Shanghai house. In the spring of 1947, while the couple were away from Shanghai, a few thieves who feared nothing made off with a good deal of what was in this great agent chief's house. Zheng's wife hurried back to Shanghai at the news and made a violent scene with the agents of the Inspection Office and the police bureau, insisting that the case must be solved, and for a time the whole town rang with it. But when she was asked just what she had lost, she could draw up no list herself; she only said: a very great deal. The Shanghai tabloids all ran the story as a gossip item — "General Zheng Jiemin's Shanghai Residence Burgled." Zheng, afraid of the trouble it might stir up, had the arrested suspects released and put a correction in the papers, saying that all that had been lost was a few car tyres and that the case was already solved. In fact the stolen things were never recovered, and not one of the thieves who did it was ever caught.

When he fled to Taiwan, Zheng Jiemin used every kind of privilege to take away everything that could be taken. But the house could not be taken. His wife asked people to sell it off cheap, and in the end nobody would have it. Seeing his wife onto the aircraft, Zheng still said, in a tone of studied calm, before the agents who had come to see her off: "You go to Taiwan for the winter; when the flowers in the courtyard open next year, you can come back." But within a few days Zheng too, loath as he was to leave it, abandoned that villa and fled Nanking.

After going to Taiwan Zheng served for years as head of the National Security Bureau, and in 1960 suddenly fell ill and died in Taiwan.
