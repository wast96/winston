## General Ye Ting in Confinement

In January 1941 Chiang Kai-shek launched the second anti-Communist offensive, directing Gu Zhutong, commander-in-chief of the Kuomintang's Third War Zone, to assemble seven divisions in southern Anhwei and surround the New Fourth Army, which had fought the Japanese with great courage. After the army commander, General Ye Ting, was taken, he was sent to Shangrao in Kiangsi, where the Third War Zone headquarters then were, and held there a long while. Chiang Kai-shek afterwards ordered Gu Zhutong to transfer General Ye Ting to the Military Affairs Commission's Kweilin office, which meant in practice handing him to the Juntong bureau to guard. The head of the Juntong's Kweilin office at that time was Yang Jirong, whose open post was director of the popular intelligence department of the commission's Kweilin office. As soon as General Ye reached Kweilin, Yang Jirong had operatives of the office's guard group confine him in a cave near the Seven Star Crag, the interior guard being the Juntong's responsibility while a platoon of military police was posted outside.

In the spring of 1943 Chiang Kai-shek ordered Dai Li, the Juntong's chief, to have General Ye brought to Chungking. I was then head of the general affairs department of the Juntong bureau. A brother of mine named Shen Mo was head of the operations group of the Juntong's Kweilin office and concurrently head of the guard group of the popular intelligence department, and it was he who was responsible for guarding General Ye. One day I had a long-distance call from him in Kweilin to say that he would be bringing General Ye to Chungking by scheduled aircraft the next day and wanted me to meet them. I got in touch at once with Shen Weihan, head of the Juntong's judicial department, who told me he had already notified Yang Qingzhi, commander of the Juntong's operatives' regiment, also called the operatives' corps, to make ready, and hoped that Yang and I would go to the airfield together to see to it.

The next morning Yang Qingzhi and I went to the Shanhuba airfield in Chungking. Only after all the other passengers had come off the aircraft did I see Shen Mo come down last, accompanying a man whose hair and beard had grown very long and who carried a vegetable-oil lamp in his hand. This kind of lamp is made of a few lengths of bamboo for a frame with an iron dish set on top; fill it with vegetable oil, add a wick, and it can be lit.

When Shen Mo introduced him to Yang Qingzhi and me as General Ye, we first saluted him, and he nodded civilly. I said, "You have had a hard time of it, General." He answered without expression, "It was nothing." As we walked out of the airfield he looked up and around, and said as though to himself, "Ah, so this is Chungking." I asked him out of curiosity why he was carrying such a lamp. He smiled and held it up high. "This has kept me company these two years, and I mean to keep it by me as a permanent memento."

Yang Qingzhi had a sedan chair got ready specially for General Ye, its door and windows covered over with cloth, because the road from the Shanhuba airfield up to the highway climbs several hundred stone steps and he was afraid of the general being recognized. General Ye looked at the chair with evident displeasure and did not want to get in, but Yang Qingzhi pressed him and in the end he reluctantly agreed. We followed close behind, with more than twenty plain-clothes guards and a dozen or so operatives of the Chungking air censorship post, which the Juntong controlled, scattered before and behind. Yang Qingzhi, afraid something would go wrong and that someone might snatch him away, had the chair carried right up to the side of the car. When the general got down, Shen Mo, Yang Qingzhi and I rode with him in one car, with a guard car before and another behind, all the way to number 22 Wanglongmen on Linsen Road, the residence of the commander of the Juntong's operatives' regiment, where he was installed. The house stood very close to the regiment's headquarters in the Hunan-Hupeh guild hall at Wanglongmen and had always been the commander's residence; it had been vacated temporarily for General Ye's use.

On the way back I complained to Shen Mo that he ought to have had the general's hair cut instead of letting him go about like that and attracting notice. He then told me something of General Ye's circumstances since his capture and of his life at Kweilin. From the day he was taken, he said, General Ye had refused to have his hair cut or his face shaved, declaring firmly that he was an officer who had fought the Japanese at the front, that he had done nothing wrong, and asking why he should be arrested and confined. He therefore would not cut his hair or shave, to show that he would not compromise until he had his freedom, and no one's persuasion made any difference. At Kweilin General Ye had got hold of a milk goat, and every day he pastured it or cut grass for it himself and drew its milk for his own use. He had meant to bring the goat with him this time, and had left it only because it was awkward on an aircraft. At dusk, being forbidden to leave the cave, he could only read by the oil lamp and keep a diary. He was always civil in manner to the junior operatives guarding him and never asked for anything outside the regulations; but when senior officials came to see him, the higher their rank the more resolute and severe his manner became, and he always protested at being confined.

Back at the Juntong bureau I saw Dai Li and reported that General Ye had reached Chungking, and how he had lived at Kweilin. Dai instructed me to look after him well in the matter of his living, and told me repeatedly that General Ye must be got to have his hair cut, because Chiang Kai-shek might want to see him. Not long afterwards Dai Li told me one day that Chen Cheng, commander-in-chief of the Sixth War Zone, was going to see General Ye, and that I was to prepare beforehand, above all to get the general's hair properly cut, put him into a better suit of clothes and have the room tidied. I went at once with a barber and an aide to see him. I spoke first of matters of daily comfort, and he said he wanted nothing. At the end I went a long way round, saying how oppressive the Chungking heat was, how many people got lice in their hair and so forth, urging him to have his hair cut a little shorter. The words were hardly out before he saw what I had come for; he opened his eyes wide and told me gravely that until he was released unconditionally he would not cut his hair, that he could bear the heat however hot it grew, and that I need not trouble myself on his account. Of what General Ye said, the sentence that impressed me most and that I remember most exactly is this: "I will certainly not trim my hair and beard in order to see anyone I have no wish to see." He would not change his clothes either. Having run into a wall, I could only go back and report to Dai Li. Dai said the man had always been of an obstinate character, that he should be left to do as he liked, and that it must be taken slowly and not forced.

Two or three days later Chen Cheng went to see General Ye. Dai Li particularly enjoined Yang Qingzhi to be watchful, since Chen's manner was overbearing and General Ye's character obstinate, and he was afraid of trouble. Yang Qingzhi posted himself with several guards outside the window. The two men had known each other before; after they had met and exchanged some talk of no consequence, Chen Cheng proposed that General Ye might for the time being condescend to serve as deputy commander-in-chief of the Sixth War Zone, and asked his consent. General Ye refused outright there and then, declaring firmly that unless the New Fourth Army were restored with himself continuing as its commander, and those responsible for attacking it punished, he would take no work of any kind. He demanded of Chen Cheng again and again why the New Fourth Army, then fighting the Japanese invader, had been attacked; what crime there was in resisting Japan; why he had been arrested and confined. He pointed out repeatedly that conduct of this sort on the Kuomintang's part served only the enemy and injured the state and the nation. Chen Cheng was left with nothing to say, and could only express a hope at the end that General Ye would think it over and that they would speak again. He had gone in with his head high and came out crestfallen, and went off without another word.

After a further interval Chen Cheng went to see General Ye again, and again without result. It is said that General Ye's attitude was harder still, and that Chen Cheng was again reduced to disarray and went off.

Within a month of General Ye's flat refusal of the deputy command of the Sixth War Zone, Dai Li, on Chiang Kai-shek's orders, withdrew the privileges he had been allowed and moved him from number 22 Wanglongmen into confinement at Baigongguan inside SACO. General Ye was held for more than a year in the main upstairs room on the western side of the Xiangshan villa, built by the Sichuan warlord Bai Ju, which stands at the northern foot of Little Geleshan at Ciqikou in Chungking. Later, when the American and Chiang operatives of SACO took a fancy to the house, Dai Li moved the other prisoners held at Baigongguan to the Zhazidong detention house at the far western end of the compound, and moved General Ye alone to a small single-storey building on the hillside near the Honglu factory at the southeastern corner, not far from where Mr. Liao Chengzhi was confined. Before long Chen Cheng had General Ye brought to Enshi in Hupeh, still hoping to persuade him to take the deputy command of the Sixth War Zone. General Ye stayed a while and still would not agree. Seeing no way of shaking his firm will, Chen Cheng had to send him back to Dai Li's keeping. Until his release in the spring of 1946 he was never moved again. Dai Li had by then marked this area off as a "forbidden zone" within SACO, reserved for senior political prisoners, with a particularly heavy guard; ordinary Juntong operatives could not pass through it, still less go near these detached single-storey buildings of three or five small rooms in a row.

During General Ye's confinement by the Juntong I went to see him three or four times. Whenever I asked what he lacked, he always said he needed nothing. He kept to a strict routine, rising very early, taking a cold bath first and then doing exercises for a while, and reading after breakfast. He disliked the Kuomintang newspapers greatly, and would pick one up, glance at it and throw it aside. But as he was held alone with no one to talk to and nothing else to read, he would often pick the paper up off the floor again after a while and go on reading it. Hou Zichuan, the superintendent of the Baigongguan detention house at that time, was a savage creature who beat and cursed prisoners at every turn; yet with General Ye he did not dare take liberties, and when he went in to see him the general would even advise him not to go too far with people.

Not long after General Ye was moved to Baigongguan, Dai Li went one day to look at the construction work on the SACO buildings nearby and took the opportunity to look in on him. Hou Zhenxiang, a section head in the general affairs department who managed construction, and I went with him. The superintendent Hou Zichuan immediately sent word in. When we entered, General Ye was still in shorts and a singlet, sitting cross-legged on the floor and waving a big palm-leaf fan to keep cool. On my ordinary visits he would talk and laugh, but on this occasion, when Dai Li came, he was barely civil. Hou Zichuan, afraid he did not know who Dai was, told him, "Mr. Dai has come to see the commander." Ye did not stir, and answered only, "I knew that long ago. Did you not send someone to tell me?" Dai Li greeted him; he pursed his lips and said, "Sit down." Seeing this, Dai knew there was nothing to be got from talk and merely asked after his circumstances, to which he again said he needed nothing. Dai Li, seeing him so cold, was greatly displeased, did not even sit, and after standing a moment went away.

After victory in the war, during the negotiations between the Nationalists and the Communists, Chairman Mao raised with Chiang Kai-shek the question of releasing Liao Chengzhi, Ye Ting and others. I went specially to see General Ye once more. I was at the time extremely curious to know what a senior Communist commander would want to do first on being released. Juntong prisons regularly held senior Kuomintang officers and big Juntong operatives too, and the first thing such men wanted on getting out was to see their families, or to eat some dish they had wanted for years and could not get, or to have a photograph taken, by way of making up for what confinement had cost them. When I put the question to General Ye, the answer was quite contrary to my expectation. Having heard me out, he answered without a moment's thought and in a tone of certainty: "The first thing I shall do when I get out is to ask the Party to restore my membership." His answer astonished me. It had never occurred to me that since his capture he had been a Communist out of contact with his organization; and I understood nothing then of the high ideals of Communists, or of what it is to be a loyal Communist willing to sacrifice everything to realize a great ideal. I very much wanted to ask further, but seeing that when he had said it he raised his head and gazed out of the window for a long time without a word, I was afraid of another rebuff and hurriedly took my leave, saying insincerely, "That is very good, very good." In truth I had all along been acting on Dai Li's instructions, hoping that by making him ask for more in the way of comforts we might gradually change his attitude and bring him over to Chiang Kai-shek. So when I heard that firm and unmistakable answer I was greatly disappointed, and I reported it to Dai Li. He said nothing for a while, and only after a long silence remarked: "That is exactly where the Communists are formidable."

Chiang Kai-shek was afterwards obliged to agree to Chairman Mao's request, and General Ye Ting was exchanged for Ma Fawu. On 4 March 1946 Dai Li sent Ye Xiangzhi, deputy head of the Juntong's Second Department, to take General Ye Ting to the Eighth Route Army's Chungking office on Linsen Road, and sent me at the same time to take Mr. Liao Chengzhi to the People's Political Council and hand him over to Mr. Shao Lizi. When General Ye Ting heard the news he took down at once from the wall that small oil lamp he had carried from Kweilin to Chungking, and held it in his hand ready to leave the prison. By then his hair had grown down to his shoulders and his beard reached his chest. As he took leave of me before getting into the car, he smiled the smile of a man who has won, and stroking the long hair and beard with a cheerful air he said to me: "You urged me several times before to cut them off, and I would not. Now I shall cut them off of my own accord; but I shall keep them carefully all the same."
