## Preface

I worked for eighteen years in the Kuomintang's Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Military Affairs Commission of the National Government, the Juntong for short. Because I had the confidence of the Juntong's chief, Dai Li, I was promoted by degrees from a minor agent to a department head at the bureau's own headquarters; I also served as the officer responsible for a field region, held a long concurrent appointment as technical instructor to a good many of the Juntong's training classes for agents, and ran the open, above-board agencies that the Juntong controlled. Above all, in the seven long years I spent as head of the bureau's General Affairs Department, I had a great deal of contact in the course of work with units both at headquarters and in the field, so I know something of the inner workings of every side of the Juntong, and of Dai Li's activities, official and private alike. Answering Premier Zhou Enlai's call, I began in 1961 to set down what I had experienced myself and what I had seen and heard, and over time wrote something like a million characters of material, of which about three hundred thousand appeared in *Selected Historical Materials* and in internal publications. After that material was published, some of it was reprinted elsewhere without the consent either of the organization or of myself and without any further checking or verification, and a good many errors crept in. I have now revised and added to what I wrote before and compiled it as *Inside the Juntong*, and I take the opportunity to say something about how these materials came to be written.

On 21 February 1961 Premier Zhou Enlai, with Vice-Premiers Chen Yi and Luo Ruiqing, received us at the Xihua Hall in Zhongnanhai. The Premier spoke to each of us in turn, and when my turn came the first thing I did was to confess my guilt to him. I did so because before the liberation of Shanghai, Chungking and Nanking I had personally led agents, or directed agents, in reconnaissance, shadowing and surveillance against the Premier himself. When he heard this the Premier laughed out loud, and told me with great good humour that all that business of ours had never had the slightest effect on him: we had merely served as his unpaid escort. He spoke in particular of the occasion when he stayed at the Xinya Hotel in Shanghai. He knew perfectly well, he said, that there were men watching him in the rooms to either side of his and in the room opposite, and that even the hotel staff had been replaced by agents; yet every single day he met comrades who were working in Shanghai, talked with them and exchanged documents with them. Had we ever discovered any of it, he asked. I said we had not, and that if we had he would have been seized in secret. He said that he had been in Shanghai working for the Chinese Communist Party, which was a different thing from the Kuomintang grandees who went to Shanghai to eat, drink and amuse themselves; but he too went often to the cinema, and the cinema was where he met his comrades.

The Premier told me he had noticed that a whole crowd of agents followed him every time he went out, which made it awkward to go looking for the comrades working in Shanghai, so he arranged to meet them at the cinema instead. The moment he walked in, he said, the agents would rush to cover the doors front and back, for fear he would go in at the front and come out somewhere else. But the seats in front of him, behind him and on either side held the very people he had arranged to see. Once the film started and the hall was pitch dark the agents could not find him, and all they could do was stand at the doors and wait for the audience to come out. When it ended he always came out first, the agents fastened onto him again at once, and the other comrades scattered and went their separate ways. Having told me all this the Premier said to me, very gravely, that Communists have only class hatred and national hatred and have never troubled themselves over private grudges; that in everything they proceed from the interests of the Party and the people; that pardoning us had likewise been in the interests of the Party and the people; and that he therefore hoped we would from now on do something of use to the people.

Listening to the Premier I felt both ashamed and stirred, and I asked him: "What can I do that would be of use to the people?" You spent all those years in the Juntong, he said, and all that time at Dai Li's side. Write out the inner workings of the Juntong as they really were. Whatever you did yourself, and whatever you saw and heard — obstructing the revolution, butchering revolutionaries, all of it, everything that will not bear the light — bring it into the open, so that those who come after know how hard the revolution was and how savage the counter-revolution, and so that everyone understands that victory did not come cheap: that men gave their heads and shed their blood, that one rank pressed forward as another fell, that they struggled bitterly for decades and that a great many were sacrificed, and that this is what bought the red country we have today. Write it out as it really was, the Premier said, and you will have served those who come after as a teacher by negative example, and that is work of use to the people. He then urged me again and again: whatever you write must be true, and you must not hold anything back. Anything you never confessed before, writing it now counts as a further confession, and the government will certainly not call you to account for it. Neither should you be afraid, nor keep anything back out of delicacy. Write what you know. And in the past there were comrades who, for the sake of the revolutionary work, risked their lives to have dealings with your side; that too is worth putting on record.

At this point Vice-Premier Tan Zhenlin came in to say that something urgent required the Premier, and the Premier left us. Vice-Premier Chen Yi then said to me: in writing historical material what is asked of you is truth, not completeness; write what you know, however little that is. This is not like writing a novel, where you may invent as you please, and still less may you put down what you have only heard at second hand. What you write must be true material from your own experience, seen and heard by you. Chen Yi added that he had read material written after liberation by various people about his own guerrilla days in Jiangxi, and that in his view a good deal of it magnified his personal part in things without bringing out the leadership of the Party, so he had struck all of it out.

The reception ran past twelve o'clock, and the Premier invited us through to the dining room at the back to eat dumplings. He said, with great good humour, that there were a good many southerners present today, but that he was going to follow the northern custom all the same and give everyone New Year dumplings, since it was the seventh day of the lunar year and the countryside was still keeping the festival. Only, he said, nobody here is to make the old mistake. In the past when a northerner asked southerners to eat dumplings, the southerners took the dumplings for a snack and assumed the good dishes were still to come, so they ate a few and waited for the rest, and went home with empty stomachs. Today there are dumplings and nothing after them, so everyone is to eat his fill and nobody is to go home hungry. That made us all laugh. The Premier told us to make ourselves comfortable and said he had something to see to. Vice-Premier Chen Yi led us through, and no place was kept at the head of the table for the Premier: whoever got there first sat down.

The dining room was not large, and a dozen-odd of us were divided between two round tables. Coming in last, I found a corner against the wall and sat down there. When the waiters brought in the four cold dishes, Vice-Premier Chen Yi did not wait for the Premier but told us to begin. None of us liked to start first, so Chen Yi led the way himself. When the Premier came in, the only empty seat left was the one on my left, and he came over and sat down in it. What I felt at that moment was warmth beyond anything I can describe. In the past I had worn myself out for Chiang Kai-shek and Dai Li doing anti-Communist and anti-people work, and they had treated me like a household servant; Chiang Kai-shek in particular would sometimes not so much as let me sit down when I went to see him. That the Premier should receive us today with such complete lack of ceremony was more than I had looked for, and I was very nearly moved to tears.

The Premier went on talking about that stay in Shanghai as he ate. He had known all along, he said, that the agents were on him from morning to night, and he had still managed to meet several dozen comrades working in Shanghai, one after another. He laughed and asked me again: "You never spotted a single one, did you?" "No," I said, "not one." I asked him, out of sheer curiosity, how it was that we had never found out whom he was notifying, or where the meetings were to be. He laughed and said: why do you suppose he had chosen to stay at the Xinya Hotel? Precisely because your people were there and so were ours. Your side would put men in as waiters to watch me, and our comrades would come in as waiters too, to protect me and act as my couriers. You would listen in on the telephone, so I did not use the telephone. Every day when I went out — taking a taxi, settling the bill in a restaurant, paying for something in a shop — I could find one of our own people and get the list of those I wanted to see passed out that way. The Premier also told me that he knew a good deal about the tricks the Juntong and the Zhongtong and the rest of the secret-service organs got up to, but that he could not string it all together, and that some of the key questions were still not at all clear to him; and that this was why he hoped I would write out these inner workings as they really were.

When we took our leave, the Premier shook each of us by the hand, and as he held mine he said once more: "I'll be waiting to read what you write."

After that reception I began, slowly and with great difficulty, to teach myself to write. As I had never written anything in my life, and had not a scrap of reference material to hand, and was working entirely from memory, gaps and mistakes were bound to occur. From 1962 onwards several pieces of mine appeared in *Selected Historical Materials*, and I received a good many letters from readers correcting and adding to what I had written. Some of it, after I had gone over my recollections again and again and checked them against the circumstances of the time, I concluded I really had got wrong, and I have put all of that right in rewriting the material for this book. Other points, after the same repeated going-over, I judged I had not got wrong, and those I have let stand — for each of us stood in a different position at the time, and naturally learned different things. Kuomintang newspapers seldom told the truth; they turned black into white and confounded right with wrong, and a good deal of what they printed was invented outright, while some accounts deliberately put an event later or earlier than it happened. So that press material is by no means all of it true or to be relied on.

Kuomintang archives are not all to be relied on either, and least of all the archives of secret-service organs like the Juntong. Chiang Kai-shek had Dai Li murder a great many revolutionaries and a great many people who opposed Chiang; and when I was compiling the teaching material on "operational technique" for the Juntong's special training classes — the subject that covers arrest, kidnapping, assassination, sabotage and the rest of it — I went through a good deal of the archive on assassinations and other such cases, and never once saw a file containing a letter in Chiang Kai-shek's own hand or a written instruction from him. There was only Dai Li's wording: "By instruction, execute at once," or "By instruction, act accordingly." Whose instruction it was that had been received cannot be discovered from the files at all. In years to come people will have to do a great deal of patient sifting before they can work it out. That men who lived through it can now write down what actually happened is something that can make good what the archives lack.

There are several matters I should explain here in passing.

In the ten-odd years since my materials began to appear I have received more than thirty letters from the families of revolutionary martyrs, telling me that Juntong agents had killed their relatives and asking me why, in writing this history, I had not so much as set down the names of the martyrs who were murdered. That failure on my part has weighed on me for years, and I feel deeply that I have wronged the dead. Besides offering the families my apologies, I have taken the chance this book gives me to put in, so far as I am able, some part of the honoured names of the many who stood firm to the end and were killed for it. The Juntong did its evil work over so long a period, its executive organs were scattered across the country, its butchers were many and those they killed were many, and it is not possible that I should know them all. In the great massacre on the eve of the liberation of Chungking, for instance, more than a hundred martyrs were cruelly put to death, and I cannot possibly list every name; and there are many martyrs whose names I honestly cannot now recall. For all this I must ask the families' pardon. There have also been individual family members who have reproached me to my face and demanded to know what became of their relatives after the Juntong took them away, asking for the living man or else the body. Every time this happens I am filled with the bitterest remorse, and beyond bowing my head, admitting my guilt and begging their forgiveness there is nothing whatever I can do to meet a demand so entirely just and so entirely natural. Every man has parents, every man has children. I understand what these families feel in remembering their own, and beyond the sense that I have wronged the martyrs and their families, there is my own mother, whom I also failed. She used to tell me: "Child, you may go without office, but you must be a decent human being." And for the sake of office I did a great many things, behind her back, that were not the acts of a decent human being; and now, when regret comes far too late, all that is left me is to expose the shameful crimes I committed, and so make what little amends I can.

A number of readers have also written to say that what I wrote before did not set out the vile conduct and the crimes of certain traitors in enough detail to rouse people's hatred and disgust for traitors. When a traitor appears within the Party the harm he does the Party is very great, and people, and above all the younger generation, ought to be brought to understand something of how hard the revolution was and how little its success can be taken for granted. So in the piece "Inside the Bureau of Secrets Preservation" I have added a passage on the traitors. Since I did not want their relatives to suffer for it, I have left out the names of some of them, clearly as I remember those names, and have set down only the crimes by which they harmed the Party and the vile facts of the selling of their souls, choosing a few cases of a representative kind.

In order to expose as many of the Juntong's crimes as I truthfully could, I have over these ten-odd years sought out a good many friends and relatives who knew about the old days and asked them to check and add to what I had written. Some points, though checked and taken to be sound, later drew objections from others, so before this book went to press I checked them once more. The roster of the "Ten-Man Team," the little group with which Dai Li first began his secret-service work, is one such point, and I have verified it yet again. In 1942, at the "April the First" assembly the Juntong held in Chungking to mark the tenth anniversary of its founding, I heard Dai Li read out those ten names with my own ears. In 1961, when I began to write "What I Know of Dai Li," I went to Mr. Huang Yong, at that time a member of the National Committee of the CPPCC, and checked it with him. In 1975, when Zheng Xilin came to Peking on a study visit, I went and checked it with him a second time. Both of them were members of the Ten-Man Team. Another such point is the "class song" of the Juntong's special training classes, which later became the "bureau song" of the Juntong itself. I could not remember the whole of it, but letters from a number of people have supplied the missing parts and it is now substantially complete. There was no time to print it in *Dai Li the Man*, so it will have to appear in material published later.

There is one thing more. A number of friends, and other people who knew about the old days, have written a good deal of material for me. Some of it I judge to fit the circumstances of the time and mean to use; other things I have no recollection of whatever, or judge not to fit those circumstances, and these I do not mean to use. I am most grateful for such warm concern for the collecting of historical material, and I record my thanks for it here. If there are omissions and errors in what this book sets down, I hope readers on all sides will correct and supplement them, and I shall welcome it most sincerely.
