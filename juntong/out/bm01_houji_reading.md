## Afterword

As this book goes to press, let me add a few words.

The pieces in this collection were all written by me between 1962 and 1966. Several of them appeared in the <i>Selected Historical Materials</i> (internal circulation) compiled by the Historical Materials Research Committee of the National Committee of the CPPCC; readers who saw them pointed out some errors and shortcomings in the drafts, which I have corrected as far as possible this time. Most of the pieces in this collection have not been published before, and I still hope readers will set me right.

I put these materials together from memory, one after another, so they cannot be complete and may contain mistakes; where the same matter appears in different pieces, not only does it vary in fullness, but even the tone and wording are not consistent. This time I had meant to put it all in order, but being near seventy and unwell, far weaker in body and spirit than before, I found my strength unequal to my wish. My daughter Meijuan helped me add to and cut some of the pieces, but it is still not satisfactory, and I can only wait until I have time later to revise it.

When I began writing these materials, I thought that if I could follow Premier Zhou's instruction — that people of an age write of their own age, setting down their own experience and what they saw and heard — it would always be easier, and truer, than for later generations to write it. But in fact I found it harder and harder to write, at times could not go on at all.

What, after all, was the reason?

Writing of my own circumstances was sometimes easy: I could often set my pen racing and write several thousand words at a stretch, since most of it was what I myself had done, and at times I even worried it was not detailed enough and feared others would not understand. But when it came to my own historical crimes and shameful deeds, it went much slower; sometimes, remorse welling up and my eyes brimming with tears, I could write no further, and only after a struggle in my mind took up the pen again. I thought: the various crimes of the Juntong agents, though done "under orders," and though I had no personal grudge against the other side, were still my responsibility; moreover these things were not done by one man alone, nor known to one man alone, and if I did not write them others would expose them, so I had better confess them myself. After the struggle in my mind, my historical crimes came out smoothly.

Beyond that, what I found hardest to set down were the things few people knew, and whose knowers were either dead or off the mainland, so that the thought arose of not writing them if I could, or writing as little as I could. To write these things I did indeed go through no few fierce struggles in my mind, often unable to sleep at night. But then again I thought: it is precisely these things that cannot bear the light which may better show how the old society turned men into demons, and how hard-won the new society was — and then my courage came.

Let me give a few simple examples to show why I had this thinking, contradictory in the extreme. After Dai Li's death, for instance, the Juntong split into the Zheng, Tang and Mao factions; I belonged to both the Mao and the Tang faction, and at the same time, holding large amounts of public property in my hands, I even divided up property with Zheng Jiemin's wife. So when I wrote of my siding with Mao to topple Zheng, and the various means I used, I wrote and wiped away sweat as I went, for even I felt such things too shameful to show. To please Mao Renfeng, I went and stirred up a great crowd of Juntong agents' bereaved families to make a scene at Zheng Jiemin's fiftieth-birthday celebration given by his wife, and at the same time went to curry favour with Zheng's wife, telling her that over a hundred agents' widows and orphans, children in tow, were coming to Zheng's home to eat birthday noodles, so that Zheng's wife, frightened, begged me again and again to stop them, and only then did I hurry to turn the crowd back — the affair set in motion by me so as to curry favour with both sides.

Again, I once helped Mao Renfeng squeeze Zheng Jiemin out of the Bureau of Secrets Preservation, so that Mao became director. Mao Renfeng then kicked away the ladder and squeezed me out of the head office, banishing me to Yunnan as though into exile. In the end, when he came to Kunming and stayed at my home over a month, I several times prepared to do away with him. For me this was then not only easy but something I could have done without anyone finding out it was mine. But because I stood then on the reactionary side, and considered that avenging a private grudge would harm the common interest, and that no suitable man could replace him, I did not act.

Again, in 1945, at the election of central committee members for the Kuomintang's Sixth Central Committee, Dai Li, unwilling to be a member himself, put forward Tang Zong and Zheng Jiemin. After Tang Zong was elected I went to congratulate him. Perhaps because in speaking I stressed too much that Dai Li had been unwilling to be a member himself and yielded it to others, Tang was somewhat displeased to hear it, and let slip: "In the end, without the Generalissimo's word, no one could have helped." No sooner were the words out than, seeing my face was not quite natural, he knew he had misspoken, and at once added: "Had Mr. Dai not put it forward, it would have been awkward for the Generalissimo to promote me even had he meant to, since he could hardly hand down a note out of nowhere, this not being an assignment of work." I showed displeasure at his attitude, and he noticed it, and enjoined me again and again not to tell others of the talk. Though I promised readily not to speak, the moment I got back I told Dai Li every word of it, only to run, unexpectedly, into a rebuff. When Dai had heard me out, he said fiercely: "In future don't meddle in these things. I don't want to hear this either." This was plainly sowing discord between Dai and Tang, and I was rather pleased with myself at the time, for since Dai Li had shown himself very pleased with me on everything else that day, I took it that he liked to hear such things.

These shameful, vile doings, done for personal advancement and gain, reflect the scheming, the mutual deceit, the two-facedness and the like among the reactionaries; set down here, they may perhaps serve as teaching by negative example.

Of course, I do not say I now write without misgivings. For instance, in 1943 I saw with my own eyes Lin Biao having dealings with Dai Li in Chungking, the two very warm with each other, addressing each other as "senior fellow-student" and so on. But in writing of Dai Li, though I mentioned no few people, I dared not write Lin Biao's name. Only after Lin Biao died and his crimes were exposed did I set this down. Again, of Jiang Qing's and Zhang Chunqiao's dealings with the Juntong agents in Shanghai in those years, of which I knew a little, I did not dare write until the "Gang of Four" was smashed.

Someone asked me whether there was still anything I had not written. My answer is: "I am now deliberately holding nothing back. If there are things I cannot recall, then when my daughter Meijuan puts my materials in order for me in future, she will write them out."

This collection may well have no few shortcomings and errors, and I sincerely await readers' instruction and criticism.

(Beijing, November 1982)
