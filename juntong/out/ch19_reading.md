## Tang Zong

Tang Zong, zi Naijian, was a native of Lingxian county, Hunan, and a graduate of the sixth class of the Whampoa Military Academy. After Dai Li's death the Juntong secret-service organization split into three factions — a Guangdong faction headed by Zheng Jiemin, a Zhejiang faction headed by Mao Renfeng, and a Hunan faction headed by Tang Zong. After the country's liberation, once Chiang's clique had fled to Taiwan and Mao Renfeng and Zheng Jiemin had died one after another, Tang alone of the great chiefs of the old Juntong days remained, still running secret-service work for Chiang Kai-shek.

From 1932, when I joined the Juntong's work in Shanghai, I had dealings with Tang. During the war we spent five or six years together in Chungking. After the victory, when Tang was running the Police Directorate-General, I very much wanted to move into the police line myself, and being a Hunanese besides, I grew closer to him still. Here I shall only set down some of what I came to understand of him over the ten-odd years I knew him.

### Before the War of Resistance

Tang Zong, to look at, seemed a very cultured sort. He wore platinum-framed spectacles of no great strength, had a pale, thin face, spoke not only in a soft voice but always with a faint smile, and was very quiet in manner. On the surface it was hard to tell he was a soldier, harder still to tell he was a fiendish killer and secret-service chief. Yet behind that unhurried talk and elegant handwriting he had done to death no one knows how many fine Communists and progressives.

I seldom saw him with a harsh look on his face; yet in handling many a matter, his way of holding to his own opinion, of not letting go until others agreed with him, often made everyone who dealt with him feel he was an uncommonly stubborn man. He used this method even on the fiery Dai Li, who was forever flying into rages. He often told me this was "softness conquering hardness."

Tang was a noted schemer in the Juntong, valued by Dai Li for his steadiness and cool head. Whenever Dai came up against some especially large problem he could see no good way through, he would always have Mao Renfeng go to Tang for a plan. After Dai's death Mao Renfeng likewise often went to him for counsel, and many senior agents called him behind his back the "Wisdom Star."

Even during his training in the sixth class at Whampoa, Tang was secretly writing "little reports" to Chiang Kai-shek. Whenever an instructor said anything in his lectures that Tang thought questionable, or voiced any dissatisfaction with the authorities, he reported it to Chiang at once. He kept in mind the every word and act of his fellow students and reported them to Chiang regularly. Because he did it so secretly, none of them found him out. He was very good at falling in with Chiang Kai-shek's mind and knew how to please him, and so kept Chiang's trust all along. Even in his student days no few Communists came to harm at his hands.

I heard these things about Tang Zong told once, by chance, by Dai Li, in 1943. It was at a reception Dai gave at SACO, to which Tang was invited. When Tang reached SACO, the chief secretary there, Pan Qiwu, being too busy, did not receive him well, and Tang, much displeased, left. When the banquet began and Dai Li saw the host's seat set for Tang standing empty, he knew Tang had gone. When the banquet was over that night, Dai gave Pan Qiwu and me a dressing-down, saying he had not attended to the matter and none of us had either, so that Tang had gone away out of humour. Then Dai spoke of Tang's relations with Chiang and told us to be careful in future.

After graduating from Whampoa, Tang stayed on to work at Chiang Kai-shek's side, mainly at secret-service activity. First he gathered intelligence himself, then reviewed it. When the Renaissance Society's Secret-Service Department was set up in 1932, Chiang Kai-shek, uneasy about handing the organ wholly to Dai Li, not only appointed Zheng Jiemin deputy department head but sent Tang to help Dai run the inside work as the department's first secretary. In this Chiang meant, too, to gather and organize the men doing secret-service work for him at his side.

When Tang was secretary, I was working in the Shanghai district and often went to Nanking on business to have him approve travel and special expenses. He was very careful then and would not readily authorize any money beyond the regulations. For instance, in escorting a prisoner from Shanghai to Nanking, if the man was a Communist or an anti-Chiang figure one might, by the rules, take a first-class carriage; escorting an agent, one could only take second, and second-class fare was issued for the return in every case. Once, escorting the Shanghai district group-leader Xu Zhaojun, I took a first-class sleeper on the spot for fear of trouble. When I went to him to claim it, he asked whether I had Dai's permission. In the end it was Zhang Gunfu, Dai's cashier, who said the special case allowed some latitude, and only then did Tang approve "pay as claimed." From this small thing one can see how careful and cautious he was.

Tang was very timid. Each time he came to Shanghai he would first notify me to meet him at the station and rent him a room. He generally stayed at the Xinya Hotel on North Sichuan Road or the Huizhong Inn on Third Avenue, since these two places were not too dear and comparatively uncomplicated. When he arranged to talk with some of the Shanghai district's directly-attached couriers (mostly members of the democratic parties or of anti-Chiang groups), he always had me carry a pistol and stay by to protect him. Sometimes he changed the agreed meeting place at the last moment, for fear someone would do him harm.

About the latter half of 1933, Dai Li went to Henan on business, and Tang acted for him at the Secret-Service Department. At that time the agent Zhao Shirui, head of the investigation section of the Nanking Capital Police, got a secret report that a few anti-Chiang men were holding a secret meeting somewhere, and asked Tang what to do. Tang, following Dai Li's old practice, had them handled by secret arrest. When Zhao Shirui sent agents to arrest these men in secret, it turned out they were all men of Wang Jingwei's Reorganization Clique. Learning of it, Wang Jingwei went to Chiang Kai-shek to demand them back. It is said Wang threatened to resign as president of the Executive Yuan, and Chiang at once had the arrested men released and Tang and Zhao, who had run the case, detained. Dai Li hurried back to Nanking, on the one hand consoling Tang and Zhao, on the other pressing Chiang hard for their release on bail. Zhao Shirui, full of grievance while detained, behaved very badly, but Tang Zong not only made no complaint himself — he counselled Zhao again and again, telling him he should have the spirit to take the blame for another and appreciate a superior's hard position, and that a little wrong suffered was no matter. Hearing of this, Dai Li was much pleased with Tang. After that Dai often said Tang understood the larger picture, and told us all to learn from his spirit.

To placate Wang Jingwei, Chiang Kai-shek removed Tang Zong from the secretaryship. Before long, when the Renaissance Society secret-service chief Feng Ti was appointed military attaché to the German embassy, Tang was made deputy attaché and went with Feng to Germany. Chiang's aim in sending Feng and Tang was to study Hitler's fascist organization and his methods of ruling the German people. Passing through Shanghai before going abroad, Tang was given a dinner by me and several colleagues. He said the task of this trip to Germany was a large one, that he must do all he could to learn something and lay a foundation for the work to come, and said again and again, "I shall not fail the mission."

While in Germany Tang regularly sent Dai Li detailed reports, urging strongly that they copy the German secret police's methods of rule and gradually bring the police, transport, and censorship departments in hand so as to serve secret-service work. He also strongly advocated sending more agents to Germany and Italy to study. Dai Li set much store by these views and picked out several dozen graduates of the regular course of the Zhejiang Police Officers' School — Feng Wenyao, Wang Weizhou, Jiang Zhennan, Fan Xuewen and others — to go and study.

After Dai Li became head of the Secret-Service Department, he had at first no plan for how the organ should develop and did not know what to do. Only after Tang went to Germany and sent his reports back did Dai, step by step, do his utmost to copy the ways of the German fascist secret police. Just what Tang proposed to Dai I did not clearly know; I only heard a little of it by chance when in Nanking, from the department's chief secretary Zhang Shi. Zhang had been secretary in the Shanghai district, and his wife Chen Juying was my mother's goddaughter; on these two counts I would go to Zhang's home whenever I was in Nanking, and learned a little in idle talk. Later Dai Li recommended Wu Shixian as police chief of the Nanking-Shanghai and Shanghai-Hangzhou-Ningbo Railway Bureaus and Shi Renchong to the China Merchants' Steam Navigation Company as head of its guard-and-inspection group; the wartime extension of the secret-service organization into the transport sector all copied the German fascist secret police. I heard Dai Li say to me myself: "Mr. Tang has done no little to develop our organization; in the group he is not only an elder but a man of merit — you should all respect him well."

After Tang studied in Germany and came home, Dai Li firmly invited him back to the Secret-Service Department. Tang's task now was the same as before, save that his title had gained a "chief." For while Tang was abroad, one of the department's secretaries had been Lin Huan, an instructor of the sixth Whampoa class, and to show respect Dai had added "chief" to make it "chief secretary"; thereafter, by usage, the department's secretary was called "chief secretary," and the inside heads of the field districts and stations were called "secretary."

On his return Tang had not wanted to do secret-service work again and much hoped to move to the administrative side; only because Dai Li begged Chiang Kai-shek again and again, and went in person several times to find Tang, did he agree. Many agents said at the time that Dai Li had "thrice visited the thatched cottage," courting the worthy. Later I learned that what Dai first ran in the department had been modelled partly on the Soviet Political Security Bureau, but he was not satisfied with it; after Tang came home from his studies advocating the German model, Dai Li wanted to strengthen the department along the lines Tang had learned, fusing the systems of two wholly different countries, the Soviet Union and Germany, and so pressed Tang hard to come to the department. Seeing there was no getting out of it, and with Chiang also wanting him to go, Tang could only return to the department.

In the spring and summer of 1936, when I was arrested and shot in the leg while raiding a Communist meeting at Jiangwan in Shanghai, I went to Hangzhou to recuperate after the bullet was taken out at the Guangji Hospital in Shanghai. Tang was then lecturing to the special-police class at the Hangzhou Police Officers' School. The day I went to see Dai Li, he told me to go the next day and hear Tang's lecture too. I went to the school and saw Dai Li and no few staff there to listen. Tang spoke a whole morning that day, and some of what he said left me an impression I did not forget for a long time. He described in detail how the German secret agents were loyal to their Führer Hitler, which many Soviet-trained students had never spoken of. The agents present were greatly interested, and Dai Li in particular kept nodding. When Tang had finished a passage, Dai went up to add a few words, urging all present to learn this spirit of "loyalty to the leader."

Later, at many gatherings and lectures, I heard Tang speak more than once of this sort of thing. He said the German agents, to protect Hitler's safety, were ready at any moment to give their lives. Any food that seemed suspect the agents tasted first, to see if it were poisoned. To guard against enemy plots on Hitler, besides carefully checking the places he was to go, they would first sit in the chairs he was to sit in and lie in the beds he was to lie in. Once an explosion did occur, killing several agents, while Hitler came through unhurt.

In his lectures Tang also described how the German secret police recruited helpers from every class of society, watching from all sides both the anti-Hitler and the German people. What most interested the audience was how Germany used advanced science and technology in the service of secret-service work — hidden cameras, ultraviolet lamps for examining letters, electrical eavesdropping devices, electric instruments of torture that did not wound the flesh but only stimulated the nerves, advanced trace analysis, fingerprinting, and so on. Agents used to dealing with the Communists by crude means were all drawn to these "wonders" as to a treasure. Later Dai Li kept trying to get hold of these things; before the war he bought only a few hidden cameras and one ultraviolet lamp, and only during the war did the Americans of SACO supply some such equipment, fulfilling his long-held wish.

Each time Tang finished speaking, the audience asked him many things about the German secret police, and he answered them all gladly. In the Juntong's later copying of the German fascist secret-service ways, Tang put in a very great deal of effort.

### During the War of Resistance

After the war against Japan began, Chiang Kai-shek did his utmost to expand the secret-service organization, reorganizing the former First Department of the Military Affairs Commission's Bureau of Investigation and Statistics into the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics under the Central Party Headquarters, and giving the Juntong Bureau to Dai Li. Now Chiang felt he needed a reliable man at his side to keep track of matters on every front for him. Learning of this, Dai Li recommended Tang for the post. As Tang was still the Juntong Bureau's chief secretary and had done this work at Chiang's side before, he quickly won Chiang's approval and was transferred to be a staff officer of the Personal Staff Office, in charge of intelligence. Back at Chiang's side, Tang worked harder than ever. During the war, after Chiang Kai-shek fled to Chungking, he expanded the organization and powers of the Personal Staff Office to concentrate power and carry out his fascist dictatorship, setting up four departments and taking military, political, personnel, party and financial affairs all into his own hands. The First Department of the Personal Staff Office had six groups, and Tang was promoted from colonel staff officer to major-general head of the sixth group, still in charge of intelligence.

When the Personal Staff Office was preparing to set up a group in the First Department to handle intelligence exclusively, the CC Clique's secret-service chief Chen Lifu recommended a Zhongtong agent to Chiang as group head; and Dai Li, fearing the CC Clique would grab this unit, had T. V. Soong ask Chiang to hold the post himself, with Tang as deputy head acting for him. Later Chiang decided to promote Tang to group head, and Dai was overjoyed to hear it. Once, at a Bureau business meeting, Dai openly declared that with Tang as group head he had become the Juntong Bureau's "immediate superior" (in fact the sixth group could not directly lead the Juntong, being only an advisory body at Chiang's side, but Dai deliberately said so to show how much he valued the unit) and told everyone to respect Tang.

From 1938, when he was transferred to the Personal Staff Office as a staff officer, Tang had no more connection with the Juntong; in 1939 he was promoted to group head. To draw Tang in, Dai laid it down that Tang should be helped in his living. In the winter of 1941, when I was transferred to head the Juntong Bureau's General Affairs Department, Dai specially enjoined me, saying Tang's life was "frugal," that the Personal Staff Office's pay was not high, that Tang's relations to the Juntong's work were of very great weight in future, and that I should go to see him at least once or twice a month and, without waiting for Tang to open his mouth, take the initiative in looking after his living. Tang then lived near Chiang Kai-shek's office at Shangqingsi, in a few small single-storey rooms, with a small door in the courtyard leading through to the Personal Staff Office. I went to his home every month, and besides bringing daily necessities often asked his wife what the household lacked and sent it to her at once. Tang himself never asked for anything. At each New Year and festival Dai Li would as a rule send Tang a sum of cash, also handed to his wife. Only in 1943, after Dai Li took on the SACO directorship, did Chiang Kai-shek order Tang appointed concurrently as assistant to the Juntong Bureau, so that Dai might free up time to work with the Americans; only from then did Tang open his own mouth to ask me for things, his entertaining too being charged to the Juntong. Dai also had me put a car at his sole use, but as his residence had no garage and he needed only to cross the small door to his office, he had no use for a car and left it at the Juntong Bureau, telephoning for it when needed. Tang was very careful in his ways: not only did he not use the car for his own private business, he would not let his wife come forward to ask for it. This was quite unlike Zheng Jiemin and Mao Renfeng, who let their wives constantly ask the Juntong Bureau for this and that.

After Tang became head of the sixth group, Dai Li always hoped he would in every way help the Juntong and hold down the Zhongtong and the other secret-service units. In fact Tang did not act as Dai wished, but had his own plans. Once, when the military police and the Juntong's land-and-water traffic-inspection agents came into conflict and each side attacked the other before Chiang Kai-shek, Dai, fearing his report would arrive too late and that Chiang would see the military police's first and blame him, had me find Tang to explain the situation and hoped Tang would send the Juntong's report to Chiang first. I went that night to find Tang and waited long at his home until he came back. Hearing my errand, he at once looked displeased and said: "How can I so plainly shield one side? The sixth group is not something I alone can run as I please." Hearing this tone, I rose to go. He kept me back and enjoined me: "Go and tell Mr. Dai only that I will surely do my best to think of a way as he wishes. Say nothing more." I went back and told Dai Li all he had said, first and last. Dai heard it without any expression, saying only: "Mr. Tang has his difficulties; one cannot force him."

Tang's way then was to use his position to draw in every side as far as he could, never willing to show that he stood with Dai Li. One of Chiang Kai-shek's aide-secretaries, Cao Shengfen, was a Hunanese, and Dai Li told me to make much of him. Besides sending things and money to Cao directly, Dai had me often treat Cao to a restaurant or send things to keep close. From Cao I learned much about Tang. Cao told me Tang never dared openly shield the Juntong, that he sent Chiang every report attacking the Juntong, and that he also did his utmost to please the CC Clique's chiefs, to keep ties with the deputy military-police commander Zhang Zhen, and to court the Political Study Clique. But Tang had one great strength: he could keep his mouth shut as a bottle and never dared let slip anything Chiang Kai-shek had entrusted to him, or the words traded among the cliques of Chiang's group in their struggles — the thing that most pleased Chiang Kai-shek. Though I could talk with Tang an hour or two a month, I never easily heard him say anything of any confidential matter.

From 1943, though Tang came to the Juntong Bureau as assistant once or twice a week, and chaired the memorial assembly and gave the report when Dai Li was away, he spoke only of the general situation. Many of the Bureau's department heads liked to gather round him and ask this and that when he was resting, and he answered them all with care. Once the Bureau's party-and-government intelligence deputy head Ye Xiangzhi asked him: "Does the Juntong's intelligence rank first among all the intelligence units in the country?" He said with a smile: "For enemy-and-puppet intelligence the Juntong has more ways; for military intelligence the Second Bureau is still better; for intelligence on the democratic parties and the Communists the Zhongtong holds the advantage." There he stopped, and everyone felt this answer touched the mark a little more than usual, rather better than his ordinary way.

Everything Tang said and did in this period was to win Chiang Kai-shek's favour by every means. In his work he was diligent and painstaking, burying himself at his desk day and night, and on every document sent up for Chiang's review he signed his own opinion in person and set it out neatly. In 1943, after Chiang Kai-shek's *China's Destiny* was published under his name, Tang not only advised Dai Li to have every Juntong agent read it closely but directed me to have the General Affairs Department, together with the Training Department, buy five thousand copies from the bookshops and distribute one to every agent in Chungking. He also laid it down that every training class must list the book as required reading. At the Bureau's memorial assembly he stressed again and again that every agent must read it well, calling it the "magic weapon" for "saving" and "reviving" China. But many agents had far less interest in reading this book than in reading racy novels, and though each got a free copy, few finished it.

At that time Tang often gave "morale talks" at the Chungking training classes. Many agent-students hoped he would say more of the work he ran at Chiang Kai-shek's side, so as to find a short road to office and riches after graduating; but he said very little of that. When I happened on his lectures at the classes, I too joined the crowd of agents to hear a session. What he spoke of was mostly Chiang Kai-shek's words and deeds. In his telling, this autocrat became a "born sage without precedent or successor." He liked to tell of small things he had himself seen Chiang do, to prove the truth of his words — how Chiang was "modest and courteous to inferiors" and "humbly took others' advice," citing Chiang's dealings with Weng Wenhao, Hu Shi and the like as examples. When he told how "brilliant and great" Chiang was, he cited the schemes by which, in the old warlord wars, Chiang had drawn in this one to strike that one.

Of the ugly farce Chiang Kai-shek played in the Xi'an Incident, Tang had taken great pains to work up a whole mythical account, which I heard him give two or three times. From some of the attendants who had been at Chiang's side then he had heard some fabricated details, which he embellished and beautified into a vivid story. Dai Li greatly admired this talk of his, and Tang liked to give it often. Because he came at it from the side, not head-on like Zeng Kuoqing or Liu Jianqun, it had all the more effect; and Dai Li admired it because it flattered Chiang and Dai Li at once.

During the war, to further use the resistance to carry out his dictatorship, Chiang Kai-shek openly proposed: "To concentrate strength and win the war, we resolutely oppose individual liberalism." Tang did his utmost to uphold this line of Chiang's, and in almost any setting, whenever it fell to him to speak, he would repeat it, stressing that because the Chinese loved to speak of individual freedom, China had been made a sheet of loose sand and so had brought on itself the foreign aggression that threatened its extinction. At the Bureau business meetings and memorial assemblies he chaired, and in his morale talks at the training classes, I heard him say such things, as though China's backwardness and the Japanese invasion were all because the Chinese loved individual freedom.

In the middle of Tang's reception room hung a photograph of Chiang Kai-shek with Roosevelt and Churchill at the Cairo Conference. I heard him myself, teaching his children, point to this photograph and say: "This is a photograph of our nation's supreme leader together with the leaders of two great powers of the world. Only with so great a leader can our nation be made strong and its people happy."

Another of Tang's ways of currying favour with Chiang Kai-shek was that whatever book or paper Chiang read, he would, however busy, make time to find the same thing and read it, so that when Chiang chanced to mention the matter he could answer fluently. The books he had by heart were Chiang's regular reading — the complete works of Zeng Guofan and the autobiographies of Hitler, Mussolini and the like. He liked to make notes as he read and never broke off his daily diary. Besides recording the important matters he handled each day, he wrote in his reading reflections. He told me more than once that one must keep a diary faithfully, and wanted me to do as he did. He said smugly that once, going with some others to Guiyang with Chiang Kai-shek, Chiang suddenly asked about some matter, and when the party could not find the date and content, having brought no files, Chiang flew into a rage; he then found it in his diary and copied it out for Chiang, and was praised for it. He held this the benefit of keeping a diary. As I was then running General Affairs in the Juntong, my work miscellaneous and disorderly and often cursed by Dai Li, he specially recommended me this experience of his.

To please Chiang Kai-shek, Tang did all he could to get close to Chiang's two sons. When Chiang Ching-kuo, working in Jiangxi, came to Chungking, Dai Li always went with Tang Zong to meet him. When Dai gave a dinner for ordinary guests and asked Tang to attend, Tang would often plead press of business and beg off, unwilling to go four or five times out of ten; but whenever Dai entertained Chiang Ching-kuo or Chiang Wei-kuo, Tang, once notified, would make time however busy to attend. Sometimes he had me prepare food and wine and entertained them alone at his home.

Chiang Wei-kuo often drove his car about recklessly, and once was briefly detained by the Juntong Bureau's guards; though the matter was at once resolved and Dai Li looked into it, Tang Zong still telephoned me and rebuked me in a very severe tone. He was very angry that time; when I explained it was wholly a misunderstanding he was still much displeased, enjoining me again and again that no such thing must happen again. A few days later, when I went to see him, he brought it up again, saying that on no account must one give offence to this man, or it would affect the Juntong's work.

In this period Dai Li grew ever more courteous toward Tang Zong. Seeing Tang's plumage slowly fill out and Tang win Chiang Kai-shek's trust more by the day, Dai could no longer treat him as before and changed to methods of conciliation. In the spring of 1945, at the election of the Sixth Central Executive Committee of the Nationalist Party, Dai put Tang Zong and Zheng Jiemin up together to stand for Central Committee membership. The Juntong then held several thousand votes, all to be cast for the two of them. I remember that when I privately told Tang of this decision of Dai's first, he showed no pleasure on his face, but only asked about the Juntong's special party branch. I had just been named managing director of the special branch and knew nothing yet. He told me to control the votes well and not scatter them — which showed that in fact he was very concerned about the matter. Dai's doing a favour thus, going with the current, Tang saw clearly too, and he was not very grateful. When I congratulated him after his election as a Central Committee member, perhaps because I stressed too much that Dai Li had helped him without standing himself, he blurted out: "In the end, without the Generalissimo's word, no one could have helped." Seeing my expression at this was not quite natural, he added at once: "Without Mr. Dai's putting me up, even had the Generalissimo meant to cultivate me it would have been awkward — one can't just send down a note out of nowhere, since this isn't assigning work." His attitude made me very unhappy inside, and Tang Zong enjoined me again and again not to tell others these things. Though I promised by word of mouth not to, I told Dai Li every bit of it on returning — only to run into a rebuff. When Dai had heard me out, he said to me fiercely: "Don't you meddle in these things hereafter; I won't hear them!" From this I saw there was already friction between them, things Dai Li did not wish me to know much of. In fact Dai Li much liked me to report such things to him truthfully; and though he rebuked me so that time, that day on other matters he showed himself pleased with me throughout.

### After the Victory

After the victory Tang Zong came into great fortune. Before Dai Li's death Chiang Kai-shek had meant to groom Tang, the better to realize his secret-service rule; after Dai's death Chiang set even more store by Tang. In this period I grew closer to Tang than ever, especially after Dai Li's death, when I on the one hand threw in with Mao Renfeng's Zhejiang faction and on the other was reckoned a backbone man of Tang Zong's Hunan faction. Tang had held few cadres in the Juntong Bureau, and as I was a Hunanese who had never in ten-odd years broken relations with him, and as his wife hoped I would take the initiative in sharing more of Dai Li's property with her, he treated me very well, and I came to understand his case rather better than before.

In the winter of 1945 Chiang Kai-shek ordered the Personal Staff Office wound up and set up a Military Affairs Bureau under the National Government's Aide-de-Camp Office, taking over most of the Personal Staff Office's work, with his trusted Yu Jishi as director. Tang Zong was promoted to lieutenant-general aide of the Aide-de-Camp Office, still in charge of national intelligence for Chiang and also keeping an eye on confidential documents concerning police administration, public security and the like. When I went to see him on his appointment and asked whether his work would be as before, he answered simply: "About the same." I knew his old temper, that he would not talk of such things, but was curious and wanted to make out whether he had been moved to a sinecure, so as to decide how to look after his household by the case. If he no longer had Chiang's trust, then by Dai Li's constant practice one need not be so attentive but could take a perfunctory line; otherwise one had to be doubly careful and flattering. To grasp this measure, I used the time he was at his office to go to his home and, on some pretext, talk with his wife of the housing, furniture, cars and the like after the return to the capital. For such matters had to be settled by how much trust he held at Chiang Kai-shek's side, arranged just right, or one would draw Dai Li's blame.

I chose to go to Tang's home about ten in the morning. His wife was seeing to the housework, and I talked with her of the housing after the move back to Nanking, in which of course she was very interested. I took the chance to ask whether Tang's work of late was more leisurely than before, not so heavy as it used to be. At this she poured out a stream, saying how Tang was busier than ever — besides reviewing the old documents he had many new things, and sometimes, just when he came home wanting to rest, Chiang Kai-shek would telephone for him. I took the chance to ask whether she had seen Tang's recent diary. She showed her anger at once and grumbled to me: "He won't let anyone look at his diary. In more than ten years of marriage I've wanted many times to see those precious diaries of his, and quarrelled with him over it several times. If it weren't for all these children, I'd really like to have it out with him." Having gathered this, I went and asked Mao Renfeng too. Mao also told me: "Mr. Tang has the Generalissimo's trust more than ever."

In the spring of 1946, when Chiang Kai-shek went in turn to Nanking, Beiping, Shanghai and other great cities to inspect, Tang, besides Yu Jishi, Chen Xizeng, Zhu Peiji and other attendants, was the only aide to go along. A day or two before each trip with Chiang, he would first go to the Juntong Bureau to have Mao Renfeng brief him on those regions. Any telegram just come to the Bureau from those places, with important matters not yet copied for Chiang, he would himself abstract into his notebook, so as to report to Chiang face to face at any time or have material ready when Chiang asked.

In February 1946, when Tang went with Chiang Kai-shek to inspect Shanghai, the papers announced his appointment as administrative vice-minister of the Interior. The moment he returned to Chungking, I again went with many senior agents to congratulate him. He kept down his joy as much as he could and said modestly that his ability was limited, that he could not shoulder so important a post, that he feared to fail the Generalissimo's cultivation, and so on.

Many senior agents were much excited, thinking the Juntong could now extend into the local administrative units through his connection. He at once politely declined, saying: "Everyone" — meaning the Juntong agents — "will have busier and more important work ahead; I only fear there won't be enough hands, how could I ask people to do that kind of work? I'm afraid that by then I'll want to ask for people and Mr. Dai won't give them to me." Some agents who had thought to become administrative commissioners or provincial department heads through Tang's connection were much disappointed to hear this.

Many came to congratulate Tang that day, and I left first without taking my leave. When his wife ran out to see me off, she said his work would be busier still and she feared his health could not bear it. Knowing this meant she wanted things, I at once had someone buy some tremella, bird's-nest and other tonics and sent them over — it all came out of the Juntong Bureau's account, and I was glad to do the favour.

On the night of 20 March 1946, once the news of Dai Li's death in a plane crash was confirmed and Mao Renfeng was preparing to ask Chiang Kai-shek to appoint someone to run the Juntong, he gathered the few he thought most reliable to confer. Present were the deputy chief secretary Zhang Yanfo, the department heads He Zhiyuan and Zheng Xiuyuan, Dai Li's nephew and clinic head Dai Xiamin, and me. We agreed to ask Chiang Kai-shek to name Zheng Jiemin director. Afterward Mao enjoined us again and again not to let Tang Zong know of it. Not only did Mao not welcome Tang as director; the others present, like Mao, thought Tang too cautious and too small in spirit, with too many awkward corners to serve under. Zheng, on the other hand, never troubled over small matters, and being then at the Military Mediation Executive Headquarters in Beiping, would in succeeding really leave Mao in charge. Were Tang to succeed, he could come to work at once and would surely hold a tight grip, which was why they did not want him.

Despite Mao's repeated injunctions, early the next morning, before I set off for Nanking to collect Dai Li's remains, my car passed Shangqingsi and I quietly went to see Tang. He had just risen, and when I hurriedly told him Mao's decision of the night before, he gave a little smile and said slowly: "They don't want me to go, and I don't care to clear up that mess either." I asked him to make no sign, only to know of it. He said with a smile: "When have I ever spoken carelessly?"

The second morning after I got back to Chungking from Nanking, Tang telephoned for me to come to his home. When I reported to him the ghastly state of Dai Li's sudden death, he showed not the least grief, and listened to my account of Dai's death with an impatient air. I could only finish briefly and hear his view. To my surprise, he said coldly: "This is a thing there was no avoiding; the man is dead, and grieving is no use." Then he said: "My work now is different from before; I have to go out to the office every day. Go back and pick me out a good one of the cars Mr. Dai used to use." Hearing this, my heart was sore; I felt the man too cold, and not wishing to say more, could only promise to see to it at once. Just as I was leaving, he told me: "The Generalissimo has decided Mr. Zheng is to be director and Mr. Mao and I deputy directors; before Zheng comes back I am to act. I could not decline, but in fact I shall not go. They don't like me, and I have no time to go." I urged him hard not to be so, to take Mao's view as if unknown, and hoped the three of them could work as one with all their strength. He said then that the Juntong Bureau's problems would be many, that Dai had offended too many in his lifetime, and that he was glad to keep his hands out.

About the end of March or the start of April, not long after the news of Dai Li's death was published in the papers, there appeared a short notice naming Zheng Jiemin director of the Juntong Bureau and Tang and Mao deputy directors. Zheng Jiemin at once publicly denied it in the press. Tang Zong too telephoned me to ask whether the Juntong had put out this news, and said with much displeasure: "This does no good; why put out such news?" Later I found it had been picked up by a Central News Agency reporter from the Military Affairs Commission office; on my reporting to him, though he did not deny it publicly as Zheng had, he kept saying outside that he had nothing to do with the Juntong Bureau's affairs. In fact he did have very little to do with its work; though Chiang Kai-shek formally ordered him to act as director, he was the same, going only when he could not help it and at Zheng and Mao's repeated urging. By the time the Juntong Bureau was reorganized into the Bureau of Secrets Preservation, this nominal deputy-and-acting directorship of his was abolished too.

### Director of the Police Directorate-General and the National Defence Ministry's Public-Security Affairs Bureau

In his lifetime Dai Li had drawn up a plan to bring the nation's police under unified control, meaning to set up a Police Inspectorate-General with himself as inspector-general. Li Shizhen too was then straining every nerve to seize this post, to realize his "ten-year police-building plan." Then Dai suddenly died, and the general run of the Juntong thought this unit would surely be snatched by Li Shizhen. Just then Chiang Kai-shek called Tang Zong in to study the question. One day Tang suddenly asked Mao Renfeng for the other materials Dai had drawn up on the police-building plan. Though Tang himself let slip nothing of Chiang's having set his mind on him, Mao Renfeng told many senior agents the news that Chiang had called Tang in to study it several times.

The Police Inspectorate-General Dai Li had originally drawn up was huge in scale, about like the organization of the central ministries. I had seen the scheme, and remember it went roughly so: under the inspector-general were a deputy inspector-general and a chief and deputy secretary-general, with four bureaus in charge of administration, public security, criminal affairs and foreign affairs; under the bureaus several departments handling general affairs, education, transport, communications; and several offices in charge of secretariat, personnel, accounting, supervision and the like. In drafting this body Tang Zong first felt the name too glaring and the organization too huge. He strongly favoured shrinking it a little, seeking only that its powers should reach to controlling the nation's police, its name and organization ranking somewhat below the ordinary central ministries. The agents helping him draft — the Interior Ministry's police-administration director E Yukun, the deputy director Wang Bi, the former SACO chief secretary Pan Qiwu and others — advised again and again against much altering it, but he would not accept it. Several times, when Pan Qiwu spoke of it to Mao Renfeng and hoped Mao would go and advise him, Mao only smiled and offered no opinion. In July 1946 Chiang Kai-shek appointed Tang Zong director of the Interior Ministry's Police Directorate-General and relieved him of the post of administrative vice-minister. Tang was overjoyed, for he had never held a chief's post and was well content with such a directorship. But many senior agents who had meant to move into the police world to hold office were much disappointed, saying behind his back: "Mr. Tang has got his; we've got nothing." Tang had always been noted for caution and steadiness, and while the Police Directorate-General was being drafted and set up he made no personnel arrangements in advance. Those with whom he was closest in the Juntong — his Lingxian townsmen Liu Guoxian and Luo Xingfang, both his classmates from primary school all the way to the same squad in the sixth Whampoa class, who through the war had been placed by his introduction as deputy chief inspector of the Chungking inspection office, head of the postal-censorship office, and deputy commander of a Juntong agent regiment; and his cousin Tang Zhenwen and elder cousin Tang Zhensai — went to him almost constantly, hoping for a hint of what posts they would get, but he would give no sign. All these men were close to me too, and often poured out their impatience to me. I once asked Tang in passing whether he could arrange in advance what work he would give them. He said with his usual smile: "I've no certainty even for myself; how can I promise others?" Only when he had his letter of appointment did he confer with Mao Renfeng and Zheng Jiemin on personnel, and after a month or more of trouble reached a rough decision. In mid-August the Police Directorate-General was declared established, and the personnel too first settled, roughly as follows: director Tang Zong, deputy director E Yukun (in 1947 another deputy director was added, the former Chungking police chief Tang Jiao); first-department head Wang Bi, in charge of administration; second-department head Li Zhai, in charge of education; third-department head Li Guojun, in charge of public security; fourth-department head Pan Qiwu, in charge of criminal affairs; fifth-department head Huang Tianmai, in charge of foreign affairs; sixth-department head Li Ye, in charge of general affairs; chief secretary Ma Ce; supervision-office head Hu Guozhen. These department heads changed often afterward, which I shall not list one by one. This personnel arrangement included men of Tang's, Zheng's and Mao's factions. Of them, Li Guojun, Li Ye, Hu Guozhen and Ma Ce were Tang's own; Huang Tianmai was a backbone of Zheng's faction, recommended by Zheng Jiemin; Pan Qiwu was Mao Renfeng's right hand. There were also a great many section and sub-section heads and supervisors, backbone men of the old Mao faction, besides men introduced by Li Shizhen. The first-department head Wang Bi was the deputy director E Yukun's able assistant of many years. When the Police Directorate-General was first set up it was a patchwork hodgepodge, but by the eve of national liberation in 1949 it had gradually become a solid Tang-faction domain.

In drafting the scheme for the Police Directorate-General Tang had already shown his ambition — the one thing even Dai Li had not thought of: to control the provincial public-security corps. Once, going with Tang Zong to Kunming on official business, the Yunnan police-administration director Li Yuzhen reported to Tang that the most vexing problem in the Yunnan office's work was that the powers of the public-security corps and the police-administration office were not clearly divided, so that they could not cooperate well and were forever in friction. Especially in areas where the people's armed guerrilla forces moved, the police bureau's security police and the local public-security corps, in "mopping up," not only could not act in unison but often went their own ways and lost their chances. Hearing this, Tang said simply: "This problem will soon be solved; in future, once authority is unified, it will be easy."

I much wanted then to be a provincial police-administration director, and hearing Li Yuzhen's words I too thought it a great problem, and so was fairly concerned how Tang would resolve this contradiction. I even asked Tang whether, as the Directorate-General's third department handled public-security police, it would also control the public-security corps. He laughed at my ignorance and said the public-security corps was a military organization; how could a police organ control it? But he added: "In future I must find a way to control it."

Early in 1947 the National Defence Ministry set up a Public-Security Affairs Bureau, with Tang Zong concurrently as director. This bureau was in charge of the reorganization, training and equipping of the nation's public-security corps. It had only two departments below it, whose heads Tang chose from Juntong agents just graduated from the Army Staff College's general-officer class, such as Zhao Shirui, with a total staff of only forty-odd. According to Zhao Shirui, though the bureau's establishment was small, it had great power, able to control all the public-security corps in the country.

This new measure of Chiang Kai-shek's surprised many at the time. Knowing I could learn nothing from Tang's mouth, I still asked about the bureau when I went as usual to congratulate him. He said: "Now the leadership of the police and the public-security corps is unified; there'll be no more of that discord that arose in Yunnan." He would say no more, but I later learned something of his concurrent charge of the Public-Security Affairs Bureau anyway.

Once, when the Directorate-General's fourth-department head Pan Qiwu was talking with Mao Renfeng and I went to Mao on business, they were discussing Tang's concurrent public-security directorship, and I sat down to listen. From their talk I learned that in fact, working at Chiang Kai-shek's side, Tang had long understood that after the victory Chiang would wage anti-Communist civil war and was anxious about the Communist-led local armed forces. Tang had then proposed unifying the nation's police and public-security forces, citing as proof the discord between police and security in Yunnan that let the Communist-led local armed forces grow ever stronger. So Chiang Kai-shek handed Tang the authority over the public-security forces as well. In April 1947 the provinces enlarged the powers of their police-administration offices, reorganizing them into police-security offices that concentrated the leadership of the police and the public-security forces. In Chiang Kai-shek's criminal anti-Communist acts, Tang Zong loomed ever larger.

### An Endless Stream of Headaches

After the victory Tang's official star rose smoothly, but his days were far from easy, and vexations came at him without end. Here I shall cite only a few things I know of.

No sooner had Tang been named director of the Police Directorate-General — before it was even formally set up, when he was a director in name only — than the world-shaking "Li-Wen blood case," at which the whole nation was of one mind in fury, broke out in Kunming. To meet the reproaches on every side, Chiang Kai-shek at once ordered this director, only a few days in office, to go in person to "investigate." According to Tang's wife later, Tang was summoned by urgent telegram to Lushan, where Chiang ordered him face to face to go to Kunming and investigate the case; back in Nanking he could not even sleep and cried "such a headache" without cease.

Tang went back to Nanking to seek help of Mao Renfeng, hoping Mao would help with all his strength to complete this first task Chiang Kai-shek had given him, and hoping to take several able assistants to Kunming to work the case together. As Mao had just "returned to the capital" from Chungking and had only a few cadres at hand, he could spare no one to help. The Directorate-General's criminal-police head Pan Qiwu, moreover, pleaded that his appointment was not yet formally announced and that he was winding up at SACO, and would not go with him. It was Mao who gave him the idea of going to Chungking to choose some men, since some had not yet left and some had gone and come back. Having no choice, he could only go to Chungking first. I was then returning from Nanking to Chungking to handle the transport of the Juntong's returning personnel and stores. He first telephoned me long-distance from Nanking, telling me to prepare his lodging and car in Chungking and to meet him the next day. He also had me notify the former Military Affairs Commission postal-censorship head Li Xiaobai and the Juntong action head Zheng Xiuyuan not to leave Chungking but to be ready to go with him to Kunming. After he reached Chungking, I also recommended Xu Jianye — a former Juntong agent-corps commander, inspection head of the Neijiang Garrison Command, then SACO motor-corps commander — to go along.

Tang's investigation of the Li-Wen case in Kunming I have written of in a piece, "A Sidelight on the Li-Wen Blood Case," and shall not repeat here, but only speak of his vexed state of mind then.

In carrying out matters Chiang Kai-shek entrusted to him, Tang had, by his own account, never once failed to set out the very afternoon of the morning he got the order; yet this time he put off flying to Kunming and lingered several days in Chungking. Some said Tang had known already in Nanking that the Li-Wen case was the work of the Yunnan garrison commander-in-chief Huo Kuizhang; from what I know it was not so, for had it been, he need not have gone to Chungking for men. In fact it was only when Mao Renfeng wired him that Huo Kuizhang was under grave suspicion that he decided to tell me not to find men to serve as false culprits, and to set out at once with me and Li Xiaobai, Zheng Xiuyuan and Xu Jianye — four of us — for Kunming. Before Mao Renfeng's telegram came, he paced the garden of his lodging deep into every night and kept coming to me and Li Xiaobai to work it over; his low spirits then were something I had rarely seen.

When Tang reached Kunming and heard the Juntong Yunnan station chief Wang Wei report in detail, learning it was indeed Huo Kuizhang who had sent men to assassinate Li and Wen, he might have laid down the burden, but he still felt it a headache. For Huo Kuizhang was an important pillar of Chen Cheng's system, and Tang feared to offend not only Huo but, more, Chen Cheng, so that how to handle the case gave him much trouble.

Huo Kuizhang held that Chiang Kai-shek's sending Tang Zong to investigate the case was a deliberate shielding of him, and so would not admit the guilt. Thus in his report to Chiang, Tang dared not be sure on some points. Huo Kuizhang admitted only that he had failed to keep his subordinates in strict check, so that this chance affair, born of a moment's passion, had occurred. Seeing Tang's difficulty in handling the matter, Chiang Kai-shek sent Gu Zhutong out as well. When the news reached Kunming, Tang on the one hand was glad, feeling his responsibility lightened and himself spared coming forward to give offence; on the other he felt his face somewhat lost, that as director of the Police Directorate-General his first case in the field should meet so vexing a thing. When Tang got back to Nanking and Mao Renfeng gave him a dinner, with Li Xiaobai and me and others attending, he said: "The climate at Kunming is fine, but this business was too great a headache; I never slept or ate well, and I've lost weight since I went."

The second thing that gave Tang a great headache was the "Golden Capital blood case" that broke out in Shanghai in 1947. Of this great case that shook Shanghai and Nanking the papers then carried long, detailed reports; here I shall speak of it very briefly. It happened at the door of the Golden Capital Theatre in Shanghai, when police and military police came into conflict and both sides opened fire; the military police, more numerous and better armed, killed six policemen on the spot and killed or wounded a dozen passers-by. Though the fighting was stopped at the time, both sides felt aggrieved and would not submit. From the next day the whole city's police went on strike, demanding severe punishment of the offending military police; the military police too all went off post, demanding severe punishment of the police. The Shanghai police chief Yu Shuping and the Songhu garrison commander Xuan Tiewu used their utmost force to press both sides not to fight again, yet in some districts police and military police still clashed from time to time and fired on each other at a word. On the report, Chiang Kai-shek telephoned Xuan Tiewu long-distance and gave him a great scolding, ordering him to find out and report at once the cause and responsibility. In those days the people of Shanghai were on edge and many dared not go out casually, for fear of being caught as stray-bullet victims where police and military police shot at each other.

On the third day after the Golden Capital case, Tang Zong sent the Directorate-General supervisor Li Zhe, together with a section head of the Military Police Command, to Shanghai to investigate on the spot. A few days later, meeting the military-police commander Zhang Zhen at the National Government's memorial assembly, Tang even joked: "Your military police have killed our policemen — are you going to pay in money or in men?" Tang and Zhang had always been on good terms and spoke very freely, but this time it was not as usual; Zhang, just scolded by Chiang Kai-shek, was defending the offending Shanghai military police, saying the police had fired first and several military police had been wounded. Seeing Tang question him so, Zhang suspected Tang had informed on him to Chiang Kai-shek, flared up at once, and said angrily, pointing at Tang's nose: "Don't talk nonsense — have you made out who fired first?" Tang had always been forbearing, and to this sudden reproach he only laughed it off, hastily changed the subject, and with the urging of those present the two men both left. Back at his office, Tang at once had a call put through to Shanghai urging Li Zhe to make out the truth quickly and report it faithfully.

Though the case was mediated, both sides still held their own story and each appealed to Chiang Kai-shek to punish the other severely. Chiang flew into a rage, called in Tang Zong and the National Defence vice-minister Qin Dechun and cursed them roundly, ordering the two to get to the bottom of it quickly. Given the order, Tang again thought it a great headache. The next day he went to Shanghai to investigate in person. Besides hearing the report of the Shanghai police chief Yu Shuping, he called in the Bureau's Shanghai station chief Liu Fangxiong, the Shanghai municipal government's investigation-office head Wang Xinheng, the garrison-command inspection head Tao Yishan, and the Juntong agents working in the police bureau — Zhang Shi, Du Chun, Feng Wenyuan, Song Tingjun — to study it, gathering material from every side. After about a week in Shanghai Tang made out the truth of the matter: the military police had indeed fired first and killed policemen, and the police had returned fire.

Qin Dechun's investigation came out about the same as Tang's. After conferring they decided to bring the several military police who had fired first to Nanking and hand them to the National Defence Ministry's Military Justice Bureau for trial. That day I happened to ride with Tang from Shanghai to Nanking, and in speaking of the case he kept worrying that he would fall out with Zhang Zhen, since before Chiang Kai-shek Zhang held all along that the military police had not fired first. In our talk I took the chance to propose that the powers of military police and police be clearly divided, to avoid such problems in future. He nodded again and again, saying he too was considering it.

After a month or two's trial, the Nanking Military Justice Bureau decided to shoot the military policeman Luo Guoxin, who had fired first and killed policemen, and to sentence the rest to various terms. Though the police won this police-military-police blood case, Zhang Zhen treated Tang Zong as an enemy. For Zhang, cursed hard several times by Chiang Kai-shek, laid all the blame on Tang, and the two did not speak for a long while. Though Tang did his utmost to avoid personal friction, in the end he could not. One day, talking of this with Mao Renfeng at the Bureau, he sighed: "I used to think Mr. Dai loved to fall out with people and so made a bad impression all round. Only today, taking on responsibility, do I feel these things can never be avoided." When Tang had gone, Mao Renfeng said gloatingly to us department heads: "Mr. Tang always used to say others couldn't manage personnel relations; now that it's fallen on his own head he knows the difficulty. Not like being in the Personal Staff Office, out of direct contact with anyone, in conflict with no one, everything easy."

After this great police-military-police quarrel, on Tang's proposal the reactionary government at last formally issued "Regulations Adjusting the Powers of Military Police and Police," laying down clearly that ordinary public-order matters were for the police, and only cases of soldiers breaching public order for the military police; in disputes between soldiers and civilians, soldiers were handed to the military police and civilians to the police. Thus the old problem of military police fighting the local police wherever they were stationed was for a time resolved.

The third thing that gave Tang a great headache was the merging of police and public security. Though he schemed painstakingly to grasp police and public-security corps both into his hands as a mighty force for suppressing the people in the Nationalist rear, and so win Chiang Kai-shek's further favour, in fact it was beset with difficulties and constantly gave him headaches.

Around March 1947 the National Government formally passed a law reorganizing the provincial police-administration offices, merging them with the provincial public-security offices into police-security offices; but right up to national liberation in 1949 this wishful plan of Tang's was never realized, for though he carried out the reorganization as director of the Police Directorate-General and public-security director, it still would not go through. The provincial chairmen agreed on the surface but opposed it in secret. They used delaying tactics, putting off merging the two offices and keeping the real power of the public-security offices in their own hands. This was because the provincial chairmen saw these public-security corps as their very lifeblood and would on no account let go. As the corps' funds came from the local governments, the chairmen used this to threaten, saying that if the corps were to be assigned to the police-security offices, the Centre must bear the cost.

To realize the plan, Tang went in person in June or July 1947 to Zhejiang, Fujian and other provinces to talk, hoping a few provinces might take the lead in a true merger of police and security. He met rebuffs everywhere and had to fall back on a lesser aim, hoping the police-security offices could at least control each county's public-security squad. This concession too got only surface agreement, and in practice was full of problems. The county magistrates, like the provincial chairmen, held that they had to keep that bit of real strength, the county security squad. So the counties too were forever seeing clashes between police and security squads over authority. Over these matters Tang often could not sleep till deep in the night, and in the end could only draw up a "Method for Dividing the Duties of Police and Security Self-Defence Squads," submit it to Chiang Kai-shek, and have the Interior Ministry order the provinces to carry it out.

I no longer remember the contents of this method clearly; I only recall that its main provisions were that the public order and household-registration checks in each county's urban area (including provincial-municipal areas) were for the police; the protection of important points and communication lines, and the "mopping-up" of scattered people's armed forces together with the Nationalist regular troops or public-security corps, for the security squads; and the defence of the villages, the checking of travellers at key passes, and the checking of village households, for the local people's self-defence squads.

After this division of duties was issued, in fact it restored the old state of "separate rule of police and security," and this long-schemed plan for merging police and security became imperceptibly a flower that blooms and fades, a name and nothing more; in some provinces even this nominal merger was never carried out.

The fourth thing that gave Tang a headache was the opposition on every side to the "police-district system" he pushed.

Before the Police Directorate-General was set up, Nanking had already tried the "police-district system." This was a method of secret-service rule over the people through the police, in which a city was divided into a number of "police districts," each of about two or three hundred households, with one policeman to a district who kept a firm grip on all the district's affairs. This policeman could check households or question the district's residents at any time, and on meeting a suspicious person could join the district substation in a search at any time. Tang set great store by this system. On taking office he ordered it spread throughout the great cities of the country. The result was fierce opposition from the provincial and municipal assemblies, which held the police-district system an invasion of the people's rights and demanded its abolition. In the end even the Nationalist Legislative Yuan came out against it, saying it did not accord with the constitution's spirit of guaranteeing human rights. Tang could only report to Chiang Kai-shek. Chiang held the system of great use for rooting out the Communists hidden in the cities and for keeping urban order, and told him to carry it through firmly. Later, when the Legislative Yuan met and had him come to explain, charging that this method was an "illegal" measure, he could not haul out Chiang Kai-shek as a shield, since Chiang still had to keep up a false front, and could only brazen it out, saying the police-district system was really only a form of police duty, a finer division of police work, and that using policemen was only to raise the quality of police work. Some legislators were still not satisfied with this and still demanded he abolish this system that invaded the people's freedom. Though he agreed verbally to consider it, in fact he never changed it right up to national liberation, but carried it out with ever greater rigour. After fleeing to Taiwan he even formally laid it down — though it went on meeting opposition.

The fifth thing that gave him a headache was the question of police not being exempt from military service.

On taking office Tang Zong put forward a fine-sounding slogan to show the importance of police work. He proclaimed everywhere: "In the period of nation-building, the police are more important than the army." So, he held, the police might be exempt from military service.

On this question Tang Zong was wholly disappointed, and it gave him great trouble. For after the victory Chiang Kai-shek was not planning to build the nation at all, but was resolved to launch nationwide anti-Communist civil war. Fighting needed cannon-fodder, and most police were men of military age; many became police for fear of conscription, thinking too that they might be exempt. But the provinces' conscription organs went on drafting these police as usual. There was then no uniform rule on this, and the provincial and municipal police bureaus kept asking the Directorate-General to solve the problem. Tang at first thought it no problem and formally asked the National Defence Ministry's Conscription Bureau to order the provincial military districts to exempt serving police from the draft. But to his surprise his view was not agreed to; instead he was told to order the provincial police units to obey the conscription organs' draft. His one magic weapon was to go to Chiang Kai-shek, but the report went up and long went unapproved. He went directly again to the conscription director Xu Enping to talk, again without result. After long trouble a compromise was reluctantly reached, laying down that police officers and men under twenty-three must still serve, and only those over twenty-three might be deferred, though still liable if need be. He was much dissatisfied with this and grumbled to many at the Bureau that, this being so, who would still be willing to be a policeman?

The sixth thing that gave him a headache was the atrocious case in Chungking of feeding a child's flesh to police dogs.

On taking office Tang had all along flaunted that police work must be "scientific" and "modern." He urged that cases be worked by scientific methods, not by the old reliance on experience for investigation and judgment, and so set much store by scientific equipment and urged the large-scale training of police dogs. But he never dreamed that the matter of training police dogs would give him a great headache too.

It happened in Chungking after the victory. On a hillside near Yanyubao on the south bank was a unit for training police dogs. A shoeshine boy, thirsty and looking for water, ran into the kitchen of this police-dog training station to look. Seeing no one, he thought to steal a little food from a pot, but on lifting the lid saw a dismembered child's corpse being cooked in it, started in fright, and ran out crying all the way: "They're cooking a kid to feed the dogs!" Hearing him, the nearby residents gathered at once, meaning to rush in and see for themselves. The station's agents, hearing the shout, shut the door and hurriedly threw the corpse in the pot down a deep well. When the crowd broke in, the pot held nothing, and searching everywhere they finally found some leftover bones in the rubbish. A resident's child had gone missing a few days before, and they were sure it had been stolen by these agents to feed the dogs, and set up a great wail. At a cry of "Beat them!" the crowd wrecked the whole station; the head, one Xu, and all the "dog trainers" were beaten till their heads were bloody and fled clutching their heads, and the several dozen police dogs were beaten to death or scattered. Still not appeased, the crowd asked the Chungking police bureau to arrest and punish the station's agents, and they appealed to Chiang Kai-shek, who called Tang in for a scolding and told him to send men to investigate and punish severely.

I happened to be in Chungking then on business, and Tang had me go and find out the truth. I asked several agents. It was said there had been no case of killing a live person to feed the dogs, but that, beef being dear and unaffordable, there had been several cases of digging up the freshly buried corpses of children who had just died to feed the dogs. I went to the station for a look, but the buildings were already wrecked and I could make no detailed investigation, so on the strength of the little the agents told me I reported to Tang on my return to Nanking. Hearing me out, he shook his head throughout and hauled out again the catchphrase he was forever using: "Such a headache!" Noting down what I said in his little notebook, he asked me: "These people are too senseless — to save a few dollars on beef they do such a thing and get reported to the Generalissimo. How am I to answer?"

In less than two years the "headaches" of Tang Zong's that I saw were really too many; I have cited these few in passing. There are many more of the kind; besides the ones that gave him even worse "headaches," which I shall write of separately, I shall cite no more of the ordinary sort.

### Reactionary Measures as Director

On 15 August 1946, the day the Police Directorate-General was formally declared established, many of the Bureau's staff were invited to its ceremony. In his speech, besides stressing again and again the importance of police work in the period of nation-building, Tang said that in future a complete system of rules and regulations must be built in a very short time. He said China had had police for over forty years, all copied from foreign methods, without any long-range plan made to its own conditions; each period and each region had run its own set, never unified. He stressed that the police's task was to keep order, and that besides struggling against all criminals, more importantly, how to use police power to consolidate the state's political power. He openly declared that with the Japanese beaten there was still an enemy more terrible than the Japanese preparing at every moment to contest the realm with the Nationalists, that this would be fiercer than any battle of any past battlefield and not to be relaxed for a moment. He explained again and again that this was the central task of the police in the nation-building period, and that he would surely complete this great task well. He required everyone in police work not to slacken, and asked all present to help greatly. In his speech he also put forward two slogans, saying that in his term he would "not waste a single coin, not misuse a single man."

In the past, when the Juntong used the police for anti-Communist work, it was generally done through the districts and stations by way of the police organs they could control. After Tang became director he unified the task and directed all police organs to carry it out through his "instructions," "circulars" and "secret orders." This was far more powerful and far more convenient than before.

During the Nationalist-Communist negotiations, the police bureaus of every great city with a Communist office, and the provincial police-security and police-administration offices, regularly received secret orders from Tang requiring detailed investigation and surveillance of the Communist personnel in these areas and constant reports to him. I often went to these great cities then on business, and no few of the local bureau and office heads spoke to me of this. The Chungking police bureau and criminal-police office in particular were often reproached by Tang, for there were many Communist personnel in Chungking and the bureau and office could not easily investigate them clearly, which made Tang Zong much dissatisfied.

After the negotiations broke down and Chiang Kai-shek recklessly ordered the Communist personnel sent back to Yenan, Tang at once issued directions ordering the local police chiefs to carry it out faithfully, to see that the Communist personnel all withdrew, and to make out carefully whether any Communist personnel remained to go on operating. Once when I was in Chungking, the Chungking police bureau's criminal-police head Tan Rongzhang spoke to me of it, saying Tang was very earnest about this and had secretly ordered him to arrest in secret any Communist personnel who stayed, or to force those who stayed to surrender. In the secret order Tang also laid down that this order was to be carried out by each level's chief in person, not treated as ordinary routine correspondence, and that slack execution would draw severe punishment.

In the winter of 1947 Tang went to Nanking and convened a "police-administration review conference" of the provincial and municipal police heads — the police-security heads of Fujian, Jiangxi, Zhejiang, Henan and other provinces (Yan Zeyuan, Li Jianzhu, Fu Mingtao, Yang Wei) and the police chiefs of Beiping, Chengdu, Anqing and other cities (Tang Yongxian, Liu Chongpu, Lin Chao), some forty-odd in all.

At this review conference Tang admitted that in the struggle against the Communists the police had not done their duty, and took the blame on himself. He said he had not set enough store by the question in the past, that the work had been slackened everywhere, and that nothing to show for it had come out. He stressed that the negotiations had broken down, that the Communists were sworn, irreconcilable enemies, and that they must do their utmost to cooperate with the front's military operations and wipe out the Communists thoroughly in the rear. He said again and again that only if the rear did its work of wiping out the Communists well could the front's armies win.

Chiang Kai-shek set much store by such a nationwide police conference and, in the midst of his busyness, heard Tang Zong's report to him and directed Tang to convey to the attendants his expectation, urging them to do their utmost to keep the rear secure and prevent the Communist underground from operating in the great rear cities. Before the conference closed, Tang asked Chiang to receive the attendants and give them instructions face to face, but Chiang would not. According to Tang, this was because too few had attended, and Chiang was not much pleased.

Tang Zong had planned to call another nationwide police conference in 1948, and Chiang Kai-shek approved it at once and directed that the leaders of every province and city must attend in person. So from the spring of that year Tang began to prepare. But with the repeated crushing defeats of Chiang's armies, this conference too vanished like smoke.

Against the democratic parties that upheld the Communists, and anti-Chiang figures, Tang Zong likewise used police power to persecute and strike at them with all his might. After I was transferred to Yunnan in 1948 and learned that the Yunnan people, under the leadership of the Communists and the Revolutionary Committee, were everywhere launching armed revolutionary struggle, Tang paid it uncommon attention. According to the Yunnan police-security head Qiu Kaiji, Tang had again and again ordered him to use every power to cooperate with the central troops stationed in Yunnan in wiping out the Yunnan people's armed forces, that this great rear must not be left unquiet. At the joint conferences of open and secret units the Bureau's Yunnan station called, Qiu Kaiji several times asked that the Yunnan station and the other Juntong organs in Yunnan do their utmost to help him complete this task. That winter I went to Nanking, and Tang asked much about the situation in Yunnan. He was much displeased with Qiu Kaiji, holding him too lacking in drive, with nothing to show and not even a clear grasp of the situation, and in the end had him replaced.

Not long after the Communist representatives were sent back to Yenan, Chiang Kai-shek announced the dissolution of the Democratic League. Tang's nose was very keen: well before Chiang formally announced the dissolution, he had secretly ordered the provincial and municipal police organs to keep close watch on the local Democratic League organizations' activities and to make all preparations for banning the League. When Tang had a secretary draft this secret order, the deputy director E Yukun even advised holding it back a while, lest it affect the drawing-in of the League, since Chiang Kai-shek was then having Zhang Qun, Wu Tiecheng and others draw in certain League leaders. But Tang's answer was: it will surely come to nothing, and the provinces must be told early to prepare, for when the dissolution is publicly announced some will slip underground again, and then the difficulty will be great.

A few days later Tang drew up a set of methods for banning the League, requiring League members to register and surrender, and laying down that once a member registered and surrendered to a police organ and took no further part in League activities, he would no longer be treated as a League member and the government would protect him; but any who refused to register and went on operating would be banned as failing to obey the constitution and violating the "Outline for Implementing Mobilization and the Suppression of Rebellion," and those whose case was grave arrested at once.

After Chiang Kai-shek formally announced the banning of the League, Tang boasted greatly, saying he had foreseen it and made every preparation a step ahead. He said: "This is worrying and toiling for the leader." According to report, Chiang Kai-shek was much pleased.

Out of curiosity I once asked Tang whether, in such matters, if one judged wrong, it might not stir up trouble — for drawing in on one hand while preparing to ban on the other, was this not adding trouble and giving others a pretext? He answered me with a smile: "The matter is not so simple. Such a measure not only adds no trouble but is good for the work." It was, he said, applying pressure on the League to force it into line. There were only two roads: either they followed the Nationalists or they followed the Communists. Give them a little pressure first, and once they had followed the Nationalists there would be no unpleasantness between us. If they could not be drawn in, the preparations for banning were already made, to spare a last-minute scramble. This, he said, was the method of "forestalling the enemy."

Later, though, Mao Renfeng told me that it was not that Tang had any brilliance, or the nerve to make the first move, but chiefly that from many at Chiang Kai-shek's side he had learned in advance that the drawing-in could not be brought off and would surely come to banning. Because Tang knew the situation first, he dared make the first move.

In his two-odd years as director, besides carrying out Chiang Kai-shek's anti-Communist military activities with various crimes of suppressing the people, Tang also did some work on the police system and regulations. Let me set out what I know.

Before liberation, the police were most interested in fines for police offences — the more offences, the more pickings. But during the war prices soared, the fabi kept losing value, the people could not live, and even the police officers who added to their income by fines complained bitterly. By the National Government's old rule, the maximum police fine was thirty yuan, above which the police organ was "acting illegally." This figure had been set before the war, and after the war, with the fabi ever depreciating, the provincial, municipal and county police organs each raised the fines in disguise, some reckoning by silver dollars, some fining in kind or by the value of kind, with never a uniform, reasonable national rule.

The moment the Police Directorate-General was set up, the provincial and municipal police organs kept asking for a uniform fine method. Some bureaus' own conversions to silver dollars or in-kind fines drew reproach from the provincial and municipal assemblies and could not be kept up. Tang Zong could only handle the question by delay, his replies vaguely worded, saying only that a uniform method was being drafted; but right up to national liberation no good solution was ever reached.

On taking office Tang had Li Ye draw up a uniform set of police regulations and system, but all people ever saw was a uniform police uniform. This, he said, was proceeding from the simple to the complex, starting from appearance and slowly going on to some fundamental regulations.

What Tang counted his proudest works as director were holding a nationwide registration of police officers, drawing up a uniform fingerprint system, pushing the constable system, and revising the police regulations. But the nationwide registration of police officers, worked at for over two years, was never fully completed right up to national liberation; many remote provinces and cities kept putting it off. Tang spent much time drawing up great numbers of personnel-investigation forms, establishment tables and police-officer résumé forms and requiring the provincial and municipal police organs to fill them in. According to the Juntong agents working in the Directorate-General's personnel office, Liu Benqin, Wang Sheji and others, it was never made out just how many police officers and police there were in the country. When Chiang Kai-shek asked several times, Tang could give no figure and would come back and scold the registration staff. Once I too asked in passing how the work was going, and he held he could surely bring it off and would publish a reliable figure in the papers within a year or two. But he added that this was work never done before, and difficulty was of course unavoidable.

He also told me with relish that after the nationwide personnel registration of police officers was done, a screening examination of police officers would follow. Getting the personnel of the nation's police officers clear, he said, would be of great help to the Juntong's work. Having come from the Juntong, he never for a moment forgot how to use the secret-service organization to suppress the people of the whole country.

On the uniform fingerprinting of the nation's police organs, Tang Zong had brought in no few so-called "experts" to study it. I was fairly interested in fingerprint work then and often went to find out about it. It was said Chinese police had used fingerprint registration for a decade or two, with fingerprint units in the police bureaus of a few great cities. Because the fingerprint workers had studied variously in Germany, Britain, America, Austria, France or Japan, each ran his own set, so that it was all very confused. Often a man who had committed a crime in one place, committing another elsewhere, would have his prints taken by a different method and filed under a different classification, so that consulting the files gave much trouble and the prints could not serve their purpose. After some experts' study it was decided to adopt a uniform rule: for taking all ten fingerprints, the Henry-Edward system always; for a single print, the Battley system always — because these two methods were simple and clear in classification and easy to spread. The paper forms for taking prints too had been very disordered, some too simple, some too elaborate; now the Directorate-General set a uniform standard form and issued it to the provincial and municipal police bureaus.

Tang pushed the constable system in order to use the police to realize his anti-Communist, anti-people plots and make the police wholly "secret-serviceized." In the past the police of the great cities were generally of patrolman grade, of uneven quality; a few patrolmen had a middle-school level, but the great majority only a higher- or lower-primary level. He held that such men could not take on the tangled anti-Communist struggle, and that the only way was to raise their treatment to raise their quality. But police funds could not be increased, so he could only solve it by cutting staff and raising pay, using the money of two or three patrolmen to keep one constable. This, he said, was very cost-effective, for a good-quality constable played, in the anti-Communist struggle, not merely the part of two or three poor-quality patrolmen but could stand in for ten.

A very real problem Tang Zong had urgently to solve then was how to place the tens of thousands of petty agents cut loose when the Juntong Bureau was slimmed down. These petty agents, taken on in the wartime expansion of the organization, had neither seniority nor schooling, and when the Juntong was slimmed most were sent to the officers' holding corps. After Tang became police director he sent them in great numbers to the Central Police Officers' Academy's retraining class for transferring officers for short-term police training. These men could do nothing else, but at anti-Communist, anti-people work they were all "old hands." Having changed patrolmen to constables, Tang could place these men without effort, at once adding to his own strength and pleasing Chiang Kai-shek, and so kept the system up. But as the liberated areas grew larger by the day and more and more agents fled to the rear, there was no placing them all. Before liberation, when I was in Yunnan, batch after batch of constables poured in from Nanking to Kunming, and there was no dealing with them.

Under his slogan of "sound police system, police must be modernized and scientific," funds were a great problem, and the only way was to lean on SACO's surplus stores to keep up appearances. His accepting Mao Renfeng's recommendation of the former SACO chief secretary Pan Qiwu as criminal-police head was chiefly for the sake of a batch of criminal-experiment equipment left by the SACO special-police class in Chungking and over a hundred police dogs flown from America. When the Juntong took these over, they were not only of little use but even hard to feed. Zheng Jiemin and Mao Renfeng, glad to make a free favour, gave the equipment and dogs to Tang. So he set up a criminal-experiment laboratory in the Directorate-General, headed by Liu Renkui, former deputy director of the SACO Chungking special-police class's second course. The Juntong Bureau had a police-dog room, headed by Wang Zhilu, with several dozen dogs, which Mao Renfeng gave to Tang Zong men and dogs together. These equipment and dogs became his "treasures" for realizing modernization. More than once he openly received reporters to view this newest American scientific equipment, boasting it the only modern installation in the Far East. At the nationwide police conference he escorted the delegates who had come to Nanking to see these new instruments of torture demonstrated.

The great stock of American handcuffs and shackles left by SACO he also took entire, distributing them to the criminal-police offices of several great cities as tools for suppressing the people.

He had always worshipped the German police system, and after the victory turned to worshipping the American. He had meant to engage some American "criminologist" as adviser to the Directorate-General, the better to copy the American ways. But that "expert" was not satisfied with the sum offered and would not come, which disappointed him greatly.

Under his slogan of "not wasting a single coin," Tang Zong showed to the full his "penny-pinching" style (as the agents grumbled behind his back). In ordinary matters he could be careless, but in spending money nothing would do but his own review. The deputy director and chief secretary could approve other documents but could not sign special expenses for him. Every day, when others had left work, he still sat buried in his office reviewing expense files of a few tens to a few hundred dollars one by one. Those working at his side never got a coin of special consideration from him. He neither sent special funds at New Year and festivals, as Dai Li did, nor allowed advances on salary, so that many complained bitterly. The senior agents had their own ways of making money; the petty agents could only join with outside agents to work some scheme to keep alive.

Tang Zong rarely spent money on entertaining. Once, when he and Mao Renfeng together gave a dinner for the Interior Ministry's vice-minister and directors, Mao told me to charge it all to the Bureau. I said deliberately that since the two of them were hosting jointly, each should bear half. Mao said with a smile: "You know Mr. Tang's temper. If you asked him to pay, it would pain him so he couldn't eat."

Why the two hosted that dinner is itself a bit of inside news. After Hu Zongnan led his great army to take Yenan, he was bent on following Wei Lihuang's precedent of renaming Jinjiazhai as Lihuang County, hoping to rename Yenan "Zongnan County" to show his anti-Communist "great merit." Hu Zongnan thought that once someone proposed it, his dream would be realized smoothly. At his prompting, the Shaanxi provincial assembly first raised it. He thought the Shaanxi provincial government could decide it, not knowing that changing a county name took many procedures through the Interior Ministry. Hu could direct the Shaanxi provincial government and assembly but not the Interior Ministry, so he had his wife Ye Xiadi confer with Mao Renfeng. Mao, thinking Tang had been administrative vice-minister of the Interior, asked Tang to come forward and host a dinner together. Tang too was very willing to draw Hu Zongnan in, and agreed at once. After the banquet, with Tang and Mao working it, the matter was of course resolved smoothly. But just as all the procedures were nearly done and only formal announcement remained, Yenan was retaken by the PLA and Hu Zongnan's forces fled Yenan in a rout. Afterward Mao Renfeng said to me most crestfallen: "Everything was done; it's only that Director Hu was too worthless. All that effort wasted — if he could have held out one more month, wouldn't he have gone down in history forever?"

Tang Zong's slogan of "not misusing a single man" was in fact self-deception. At first, for fear of being called nepotistic, he would not keep his own cousin Tang Zhenwen in the Directorate-General but introduced him to the Traffic Police Directorate as deputy commander of a traffic-police corps. In fact he later had to deal with people introduced from every side. Once Yu Jishi introduced him a dozen-odd relatives and townsmen, specifying that they be made police officers in the great cities, and Tang saw to every one.

On personnel Tang Zong always laboured without thanks. Especially in May 1947, when the first batch of police-security heads was announced, the Juntong agents' share was very small; apart from the Yunnan police-security head Qiu Kaiji, who was Juntong, the rest — Zhejiang's Fu Mingtao, Fujian's Yan Zeyuan, Jiangxi's Li Jianzhu, Hunan's Li Shusen, Hubei's Yang Buyi — some had a slight Juntong connection, some none at all, and the Juntong agents raised a great outcry, holding that on becoming director he had forgotten his roots. I too was much dissatisfied but showed nothing on the surface. I only told him of many senior agents' dissatisfaction, meaning to hear his view. He was very uneasy then; hearing me out, he sighed a long sigh and said slowly: "Everyone misunderstands me too much. These men have just graduated from the Central Police Academy's senior research class; no few of them were army or division commanders in the war and did good service in it. The Generalissimo handed them down to be well placed, and there was no not making them heads." After a pause he added that he would not fail to use the "old comrades," and told me to explain as best I could to those who resented him, that their turn would surely come — for the nationwide merger of police and security was still going on, personnel in many provinces not yet settled, and everyone should not be impatient.

To show he had done his utmost to look after the Juntong agents, he cited the Guiyang provincial-capital police chief Dongfang Bai as an example of how he cherished the "old comrades." Dongfang Bai was a noted butcher who worshipped Buddha at home off duty and gave orders to seize and kill on duty. As Guiyang provincial-capital police chief he even believed a wandering diviner surnamed Wu, set up an alchemy furnace in the police bureau, and made much of some "elixir of immortality." Pious men and women went daily to the bureau to talk of cultivation and alchemy with the chief and this Wu. Someone reported it to Chiang Kai-shek, who had Tang investigate thoroughly. According to Tang, he held that though Dongfang Bai was a bit superstitious, he was very earnest in his duties and had never had any great trouble in suppressing the Guiyang student movement or in seizing Communists and democratic figures. On these grounds he reported to Chiang Kai-shek that Dongfang Bai worked consistently earnestly with good "results," that his personal superstition had not affected his duties, and so kept him as chief, only telling him not to set an alchemy furnace in the police bureau. Then he said with emphasis that it was just because Dongfang Bai was a Juntong "old comrade" that he had done his utmost to protect him. There he sighed a long sigh and said: "The conduct of some of our comrades is really too absurd, all manner of grotesque things, which puts me in a hard spot too; and many old comrades still can't forgive me."

After the first batch of police-security heads drew the Juntong agents' reproach, Tang could not but think the matter over well. Many agents working at his side showed plain discontent, and the talk behind his back grew ever more. At first he took no notice, but later someone told him the senior agents were saying he could only ask the Juntong for things and would not use Juntong men, and that at this rate they might as well all wreck the show. These angry words gave him a headache. He had had no reliable following in the past; those who went with him all hoped to hold high office, and so resented his giving the provincial police-security heads to men unconnected with the Juntong. Mao Renfeng's faction too stirred the pot and spread rumours. In these circumstances, the later batches of police-security heads included a great many Juntong veterans. Those I remember were Jiangsu's Zhang Da, Henan's Liu Yiran, Hebei's Yang Qingzhi, Gansu's Wang Kong'an, Sichuan's Yang Chaoqun; and some non-Juntong men were replaced by Juntong men, as Jiangxi's was by Ke Jian'an and Hunan's by Li Xiaobai.

On soundening the police system he did indeed put in much work. Many of the police departments' old decrees had been carried out province by province each in its own way, and Tang revised them all. Things the police departments had lacked or had not made uniform — a police law, police pension regulations, a reward-and-punishment system, retirement methods — he drew up one by one. He also chose white police buttons as the police emblem and drew up various medals. Each time I went to the Directorate-General I often heard or saw him introduce the schemes or designs for these "new products." He was well satisfied with these "achievements" and liked to introduce them to those fairly close to him. But unlike the ordinary officials of the day, he did not love to blow his own trumpet in the papers or go in for self-advertisement.

He most dreaded meeting reporters, and reporters could not easily get news of the Directorate-General. Only the agents of the Datong News Agency the Juntong ran could come and go there, so of this old headquarters for suppressing the people little was ever openly reported in the press. This was partly because many of the organ's measures were hostile to the people and not to be openly published, and partly because he feared trouble and a scolding from Chiang Kai-shek. On taking office as director, many reporters wanted his photograph and résumé to run in the papers — a thing many then longed for — but he firmly refused. Once when I was at his home a reporter came to interview him, and he had his aide refuse; fearing the aide's manner might give offence, I went out myself to put the reporter off politely. When I came back in, Tang said to me: "I most dislike showing off in the papers, because the Generalissimo most dislikes seeing such things." Hearing this, I understood at last.

### The Open and Hidden Struggle with Li Shizhen

Before becoming director of the Police Directorate-General, Tang Zong had always got on fairly well with the Central Police Officers' Academy's education chief Li Shizhen. When Dai Li's friction with Li Shizhen was at its worst, he never got involved, and sometimes even said Dai went a bit too far with Li. In his years at the Personal Staff Office, Li still often came to him. On becoming director, he too at first much wanted to avoid conflict with Li and hoped Li would cooperate well with him in training police officers. He not only called on Li first but was very complimentary, and I saw him call Li "elder brother" before many people. But Li Shizhen did not, because of Tang's good attitude, truly cooperate with him or clear away his resentment. This was chiefly because Li had not grasped the directorship of the Police Directorate-General, a grudge that could not be resolved. Li had waited more than ten years; seeing Dai Li die an unnatural death, he had thought himself a sure thing to seize the leadership of the nation's police, and never dreamed Tang Zong would snatch the post — how could he be reconciled?

At first Tang set no store by the problem. I remember that soon after he was named director I raised it with him, urging him to beware of Li Shizhen. He answered: "You think a man Mr. Dai couldn't get on with I can't get on with either?" This displeased me and I said no more. He added with a smile some words about his and Li's good past relations, seeming very confident he could turn a former enemy of the Juntong into a friend.

The problem soon came out. In under two months someone told him Li Shizhen was openly criticizing him at the Central Police Officers' Academy, saying he was "a complete outsider," and that for such a man to run the nation's police administration must make a mess of it. Why had they fallen out so fast? It turned out Li had recommended him a batch of backbone men to grab a place in the newly set-up Directorate-General, and though he took a few, he did not fully satisfy Li's wishes. At this Li was greatly dissatisfied, and his inner resentment burst out. But this was only the raised curtain of the prologue; the fierce, tense "hand-to-hand fighting" was still to come.

For years Li Shizhen had prepared a "Ten-Year Police-Building Plan" and sent it to Chiang Kai-shek ahead of Dai Li, sure that when the Police Directorate-General was set up after the victory the first directorship would fall to him. In the end Tang snatched it. Li printed his "Plan" as a booklet and handed it round. But when this plan reached Chiang it met the fate of "read but not approved" and was shelved.

Against the weaknesses of Li's plan, and to fall in with Chiang's intent, Tang rushed out a "Five-Year Police-Building Plan." After all his years at Chiang's side he understood Chiang's mind far better than Li. This plan of his won Chiang's great approval, and Chiang allowed it to be carried out step by step. But Chiang's regime was too short-lived; before the five years were up he was driven out by the people.

The main thrust of Tang's plan was "unify authority, strengthen function," which chimed with Chiang Kai-shek's policy of "suppress rebellion and build the nation." His plan's "merger of police and security," the use of police power to consolidate rear order, and the urban police-district and constable systems making police and secret service one — all served Chiang Kai-shek's anti-Communist, anti-people plots. So Tang Zong the "outsider" was far more in favour before Chiang than the self-styled "insider" Li Shizhen.

To contend further with Li for the power over police education, Tang's plan also adopted some of Li's methods, so that it looked more complete than Li's.

Of Li's plan Tang had once spoken mockingly, calling it "a scholar's view." What hurt Li most was Tang's saying: "From this plan one can see that Elder Brother Li is very keen on the education-chief post and means to hold it another ten years." Naturally these words soon reached Li's ears. It is said Li went white with rage on hearing them and cursed Tang's plan as "amateurish" and "ruffianly." From then on the two not only picked at each other's work but attacked each other personally, their hatred deepening till they were like fire and water. Yet meeting in public they still greeted each other civilly, and Tang would sometimes deliberately make a show of warmth.

The first "hand-to-hand fight" between Tang and Li was over the leadership of a police academy to be set up in the Northeast. First, Li Shizhen had got Chiang Kai-shek's approval to set up a Northeast branch of the Central Police Officers' Academy. So before the Directorate-General was set up he had made every preparation, privately settling on a graduate of the second Whampoa class, one Yan, as the branch's education chief. Yan was Li's classmate and squad-mate from the same Whampoa class, recommended by Li to Chiang and approved. While Li was preparing, Tang Zong sent Chiang a report asking to send men himself to run the Central Police Officers' Academy's Northeast branch. His reason was that setting up a branch in the Northeast then should not be to enrol and train regular students, but to give short-term training to the police officers taken over from the former Manchukuo and the traitors who had served the Japanese as agents, and to use these experienced men for anti-Communist, anti-people work; so, given the situation, it must be run by men he sent, to complete the anti-Communist task smoothly.

Li Shizhen was outraged by this sudden stroke of Tang Zong's. Afterward many agents close to Li's police faction told me: "We've toiled over a decade at police education, watching and waiting for victory, and now the whole leadership of the police has been taken by you people; today you won't even let us eat this bowl of police-education rice — it's really too much!" Tang understood Chiang Kai-shek: put forward any anti-Communist measure and Chiang was always glad to adopt it, so in his report contending with Li for the Northeast branch he stressed this point especially. Just as Chiang was considering giving the Northeast branch to Tang, Li Shizhen threatened to resign as the Central Police Academy's education chief. Considering that he had to keep this faithful running dog who trained his claws and teeth, Chiang Kai-shek decided to give the Northeast branch to Li after all.

One day Chiang called Tang in and explained the course and reason of the changed decision. Tang agreed to it all. In fact he had many other things to do then, and his contending with Li Shizhen for power was really meant only to give Li a taste of his strength, to show him a little colour.

The second round was the question of the leadership of the frontier police bureaus. In the early period of Chiang Kai-shek's rule, the frontier provinces had always been carved up by local warlords, the Centre's power not reaching them, so no frontier police organs had been set up. In the later war years these frontier regions gradually came into Chiang's grasp, and after the victory the Juntong wanted to grasp the frontier police bureaus. Li Shizhen's ten-year plan too dealt specially with this. After Dai Li's death Mao Renfeng once prepared to seize this organ, but with the Juntong Bureau reorganized into the Bureau of Secrets Preservation, he no longer cared to raise it. In the spring of 1947 Tang formally proposed to the Interior Ministry the setting up of eight frontier police bureaus — four on the southwest frontier, in Yunnan and Guangxi; four in the northwest, in Xinjiang and Inner Mongolia. These frontier police bureaus were huge in scale, each with an establishment of over a hundred police officers and several thousand police.

Learning of Tang Zong's plan, Li Shizhen at once concentrated his forces to seize it. He argued that the officers of the frontier police bureaus should be trained by a special class set up by the Central Police Academy for frontier-police work, and that only after these officers graduated could the bureaus be set up. Forming them in haste with existing men, he held, would not only fail the task but make endless laughing-stocks and disgrace the nation, and it were better to keep the status quo for now. He loudly proclaimed that the nation's sovereignty and prestige must come first and opposed rash action. Li Shizhen's proposal won some support then, and even the Legislative Yuan agreed that a batch of men must first be trained before such organs could be set up.

Li Shizhen's report to the Interior Ministry Tang Zong understood in full the very next day. He at once put forward, against Li's view, another set of high-sounding reasons and advocated setting up the frontier police bureaus with all speed. The suit went up to Chiang Kai-shek. Chiang, bent then wholly on his anti-Communist military dispositions, handed the question to the Interior Ministry and directed: the future frontier police bureaus should be directly under the Interior Ministry, not led by the Police Directorate-General; the training of the frontier police officers to be run by the Central Police Officers' Academy.

In this round Tang did not prevail. When the Interior Ministry later called him in to study it, he sent only one or two unimportant men. He said angrily: "We can't get it, and neither" — meaning Li — "will he have any hope. Let no one run it!" And so, right up to national liberation, this frontier police bureau, brewed for over two years, was never set up.

The third round was a fierce "bayonet fight." Having failed to win in the first two contests, Tang Zong used his grip on the provinces' police personnel to make things hard for Li Shizhen. He replaced no few of Li's faction's men on some pretext, or no longer assigned them to important leading work. Li Shizhen "complained" of this to Chiang Kai-shek. Chiang called Tang in to ask, and Tang's answer was: these men had always done military work and had had only short police training; to shoulder responsibility for a whole province was not quite suitable, and they should stay longer at the Directorate-General to learn on the job.

In training police personnel, Li Shizhen required all trainees to join the Chinese Police Scholarship Research Society he led, and after graduating to join the Central Police Academy Alumni Association. Li had always meant to use these two bodies to contend with the Juntong for the leadership of the police.

Against these tactics of Li's, Tang Zong used his authority to draw all these men in personally. He gathered them for talks at the Directorate-General, gave them dinners as usual, and gave each a photograph of himself in the highest police-officer's uniform and a sum of special funds. When the Directorate-General's personnel office sent these men out to their posts, it also enjoined them to join the local China Police Society, explaining that this society was led personally by Tang.

Several times Tang Zong said to me most smugly that men Li Shizhen had spent months at pains to train he had drawn over lightly in half a day's work. Meeting me, these men would often say: "Mr. Tang gives us a dinner and even a sum of special funds — really not easy to come by. If it weren't for Li Shizhen's friction with him, how could we get double special funds?" It was indeed so; Tang's spending this money pained him a little, and his slogan of "not wasting a single coin" did not apply in such a case.

At the same time Tang Zong formally proposed at the nationwide police conference that each province set up a police school and each county a police training station, the provinces training their own police officers without the Central Police Academy's agency. What most displeased Li Shizhen was Tang's meaning to set up in Nanking a Capital Police College to train higher-grade police officers. At this rate the Central Police Academy would have no need to exist.

According to Li Shizhen's faction, on getting this news Li was so angry he could not eat. He grumbled to his subordinates that he set no store by the education-chief post, had long been ready to resign, and had stayed only because Chiang Kai-shek firmly kept him; now that Tang Zong treated him so, he would go on the more, would not let Tang's wishful scheme succeed, and would make Tang understand that Li was not to be trifled with. Right up to national liberation the two contended in a deadlock, and at last Tang rolled up this plan and took it to Taiwan. But the contradiction between them was not eased by their having, after national liberation, nowhere to lay their heads; in Taiwan they went on struggling. It is said that, backed by the old man and the young Chiang, Tang's strength grew by the day, Li was driven out of the Central Police Academy, and on Tang's recommendation the Juntong agent Le Gan became education chief, ending this years-long struggle between Li and Tang.

What most displeased Tang after becoming director was that the post of chief of the Capital Police Board was snatched by the military-police elder Han Wenhuan. Chiang Kai-shek's constant trick for handling men was to throw out a few bones on purpose for his running dogs to fight over, so that they would strive to the death for him. Though he made the Juntong chief Tang Zong director of the Police Directorate-General, he did not hand Tang all the police power.

The Capital Police Board chief Han Wenhuan had grabbed this plum through the strong recommendation to Chiang Kai-shek of Gu Zhenglun, Chiang's noted killer-butcher (nicknamed "Butcher Gu," military-police commander for many years, killer of tens of thousands). Han, thinking he had the military police to back him, an "old-timer" himself, and colluding with Li Shizhen, meant to make the personnel of the Capital Police Board all "his own men," doing his utmost to squeeze out the Juntong men in the board, and so was forever in friction with Tang Zong.

Though Tang did not openly favour firing at Han at once, inwardly he much disliked him. Orders from the Directorate-General Han sometimes handled perfunctorily, sometimes simply shelved and ignored. Once Tang issued an order to prevent student unrest everywhere, and Han, holding it did not fit the capital's situation, did not carry it out and did not even pass it down. Learning of this, and that Han had drawn up his own method without filing it with him, Tang was much displeased and at once telephoned Han to ask. Han's answer displeased Tang, and the two quarrelled on the phone until Han agreed to pass Tang's order down. Han passed it down but did not carry it out. With agents stirring things between them, the contradiction grew ever greater.

One trick the agents used to stir the Tang-Han contradiction was that each time Tang called Han to a meeting at the Directorate-General, they deliberately delivered the notice late, or simply did not deliver it. Their method was clever: in delivering these notices they would first deliver something unimportant. Tang was very careful, and when Han did not come to a meeting on time he would ask whether the notice had been delivered; the agents would produce the delivery book to show it had been delivered on time. In fact what the delivery book recorded was an ordinary document, while the meeting notice was delivered only some time later. When Han could not attend on time or did not attend, Tang always thought Han held him in contempt and looked down on him, and hated Han the more. This the agents did in the hope Tang would seize the Capital Police Board, so that they could do as they pleased in Nanking the more easily.

From about the summer of 1947 Tang resolved to be rid of this thorn in his eye, Han Wenhuan. He did not himself come forward to ask Chiang Kai-shek to replace Han, but often went to the Bureau to ask Zheng Jiemin and Mao Renfeng to help him, setting the Juntong agents in Nanking to gather from every side evidence of Han's corruption and lawbreaking and, through the Bureau, denounce him to Chiang Kai-shek. Since Han had committed many acts of corruption great and small as chief, the agents' organized, planned gathering turned up a great heap in a few months, and Chiang Kai-shek decided to replace Han Wenhuan.

Tang and Mao both learned this from the trusted men at Chiang Kai-shek's side, and both prepared to recommend their own trusted men to succeed Han. Just as Tang and Mao were contending over it, Zheng Jiemin lightly snatched it. Zheng put forward his townsman and closest friend Huang Zhenwu; Chiang Kai-shek had always esteemed this man and approved the recommendation at once. Though Huang Zhenwu was not a Juntong man, the Juntong could wholly control him, and as Huang was an old regiment commander the military police too had ties with him, so he took over the Capital Police Board very smoothly. Huang was all submission to Tang, obedient to his every word, and from then on the state of the Directorate-General's orders meeting a rebuff the moment they left the door was changed.

Tang had no great trouble dealing with the military-police-system Beiping police chief Tang Yongxian. After Han Wenhuan fell, the agents urged Tang to finish the job and pull him down too, replacing him with the Juntong agent Yang Qingzhi. After Chiang Kai-shek approved, the agents were all pleased, holding that these lost "territories" were being won back one after another.

Tang and Mao Renfeng had long wanted to grab the post of Shanghai police chief. After the Huaihai Campaign, the Shanghai police chief Yu Shuping, under attack within and without, was replaced by Mao Sen, whom Tang Enbo came forward to recommend.

In two-odd years Tang Zong got at last all he set out to grab, but the Chinese people drove him from the mainland.

### A Lament Before Fleeing to Taiwan

I went to Kunming in May 1948, and at the end of the year went to Nanking on business. Seeing Tang again after only a few months, he was no longer so full of high spirits and drive. As the main force of Chiang's army had by then largely been wiped out, hearts in Nanking were uneasy, and the great officials' families were all planning to flee to Taiwan, Hong Kong or abroad. Tang's wife raised the matter with me the moment she saw me, much displeased that Tang still put on a calm front and would not send her and the children away. When I asked Tang why he had not yet sent his family off, he sighed and said: "If I make a move, the talk will only grow." After some thought he added: "Sooner or later they must be sent. We must all go." Then he talked with me of the situation and held it hard to be optimistic. He hated Li Zongren greatly, holding that at such a time Li still would not cooperate with Chiang Kai-shek but was scrambling for the presidency. He had a fair understanding of the Communists' policies and the liberated areas, unlike the views of the reactionary chiefs I was then in touch with. He told me: "The Communists' most formidable thing is this united-front policy; in future many high Nationalist officials will be drawn over." He told me on the one hand to guard against such things happening, to report at once the movements of the great officials fleeing from Yunnan to Hong Kong; and on the other warned me alarmingly: "The Communists want no one only from among those who do secret-service work, and hate them to the end, bent on putting them to death." That day I talked with him a long while, his whole talk full of gloom. After that I never saw him again.

After national liberation Tang went on sailing smoothly in Taiwan. It is said he became an able assistant to Chiang Ching-kuo, and I saw in the papers that he served for years as acting secretary-general of the Nationalist Central Party Headquarters. Of the several great chiefs of the old Juntong, he alone was still alive, still at his criminal anti-Communist, anti-people activity.
