## Mao Renfeng, Dai Li's Successor

Mao Renfeng was the successor of the Juntong secret-service chief Dai Li. The blood-debt he ran up against the Communist Party and the Chinese people before Liberation was no less than Dai Li's, and in some respects even exceeded it. I have touched on his case in my two pieces "Inside the National Defence Ministry's Bureau of Secrets Preservation" and "Juntong Operations in Kunming on the Eve of Yunnan's Liberation," but in places I did not write enough — especially about his style, about the means by which he won Chiang Kai-shek's trust, and about his use of Communist turncoats, all of which need further exposure. Setting aside what I have written before, which I shall not repeat except where necessary, let me set down separately here what I came to understand in the ten years or so that I was around him.

In his early Juntong days Mao Renfeng was nothing to speak of; but once Dai Li was dead he suddenly came into great fortune, quickly won Chiang Kai-shek's trust, and inherited Dai Li's mantle. That in so short a time he could, in a fierce internal struggle, beat two rivals far senior to him — Zheng Jiemin and Tang Zong — and take the Juntong's power into his hands, was no accident. Within the Juntong he had never been a department head on the inside staff, nor a station chief in the field, nor a member of the founding "Ten-Man Group"; he had "taken orders late in life," having always done secretariat work. He had little first-hand experience of Juntong operations, yet within a few years he could shoulder the leadership of the whole organization — an astonishing thing.

It was only after the war against Japan began that he was transferred into the Juntong Bureau as a secretary. Being Dai Li's fellow townsman and a distant relation by marriage, he quickly gained Dai's trust. He was also hardworking and steady, gentle with people, showed not the least ambition, and could bear hardship and blame without complaint, so that by about 1941 Dai Li set about grooming him as an able assistant on the inside, giving him the title of acting chief secretary and putting him in charge of the secretariat.

The Juntong Bureau's secretariat was, on the organizational chart, parallel to the various departments and offices, like any other unit. But because Zheng Jiemin was chief secretary, it had in effect become the leading body over the inside departments, all of whose documents it consolidated. General matters could be approved by the chief secretary without going to Dai Li at all. After 1942, as the Juntong expanded, the secretaries grew to a dozen or so, and a deputy chief secretary's post was added, filled by Zhang Yanfo (Yifu). Zhang was a backbone man of Deng Wenyi's secret-service system, and after Deng's agents were merged into the Juntong, Dai Li treated Zhang and the others formerly under Deng Wenyi no differently on the surface, but at heart kept a careful measure. So although Zhang was deputy chief secretary, he still came under the acting chief secretary's direction, and was kept from many important matters.

The chief secretary, Zheng Jiemin, went to the Juntong Bureau only a session or two a week as a rule; only when Dai Li was away from Chungking did he go in often to work. This was mainly because intelligence bearing on the Eighth Route Army and the New Fourth Army all had to pass under his eye. As he concurrently held the deputy directorship of the Second Bureau of the Board of Military Operations (its director was Yang Xuancheng; after 1944 Zheng rose to director), he set himself up as an expert on the Communist question. The Second Bureau likewise had to do its utmost to gather and report such intelligence to Chiang Kai-shek. To keep the two sides' reports from diverging too far and causing trouble before Chiang, Dai Li had Zheng look them over. For some of this material was not easily come by, but was gathered by the agents out of thin air, or even fabricated, to satisfy Chiang's demands in this line. To keep from showing the seams, the Juntong Bureau and the Second Bureau used deception on Chiang Kai-shek, so that everything had to pass Zheng Jiemin's eye and be judged close enough before it went up, lest it go wrong. This part of the work Mao Renfeng could not take over.

Dai Li's grooming of Mao Renfeng consisted mainly in gradually letting Mao take over Zheng Jiemin's other powers in the Juntong, leaving Zheng only an empty title — which is why Mao was not made deputy chief secretary but given the title of acting chief secretary. Zheng Jiemin knew Dai Li's ways well and, in this too, kept out of it as much as he could. And so Mao Renfeng's standing in the Juntong slowly took shape.

Before Dai Li's death, Mao Renfeng was most respectful to both Zheng Jiemin and Tang Zong, saying "reporting, sir" when he spoke and calling himself "your subordinate" in writing, very deferential in manner. No one then imagined he would contend for mastery with Zheng and Tang, still less that it would be he who inherited the "family estate" Dai Li had built. The reasons for Mao Renfeng's rise, beyond what I have written before, must be explained from the following several angles.

### Endure, Wait, Strike Hard

Mao Renfeng loved to gamble, and was especially good at "showhand" (a kind of poker game). He was very good at applying the secret of winning at "showhand" — endure, wait, strike hard — and so won every time he played. People who had once gambled at his house liked to greet one another with: "Today are you Song Ziwen, or Liu Jiwen?" This was because Song Ziwen (T.V. Soong) and Liu Jiwen echo <i>song ji wen</i> ("hand over a few coppers") and <i>liu ji wen</i> ("keep a few coppers"), so people used the two men's names as bywords for losing big and losing little — since no one expected to "win a few coppers," and losing less was already doing well. From playing "showhand" Mao Renfeng grasped the principle of endure, wait, strike hard, and applied it to dealing with people and getting things done. For years he held to this "principle": when the chance had not come, to grit his teeth and endure, to wait patiently; and once fortune turned his way, to seize the moment and strike hard.

During his time as acting chief secretary in the Juntong Bureau he was a noted "smiling tiger," rarely seen to pull a long face or lose his temper, all smiles to everyone. When Dai Li rebuked him he could bear it without the least reluctance; even when a subordinate was surly with him or grumbled, he could bear that too. What especially pleased many of the agents was that he would also take the blame for others without complaint. Dai Li's temper was violent, and the least thing not to his liking would set him raging — often, over trifles, he would curse men, beat them, lock them up. When this happened, Mao Renfeng would always take the fault on himself before Dai Li and shoulder the error.

Once, Dai Li fell out with the Military Police Command over traffic inspection, because the military police posted to the traffic-inspection stations around the country would not take orders. Dai Li reported it to Chiang Kai-shek first, meaning to seize the chance to bring the military police under him, and hauled out all the unlawful conduct of the military police that the Juntong had gathered around the country over the years, writing it up in a report of ten or twenty thousand characters. The secretariat clerk Guo Ziliang, charged with copying out this report, had copied to midnight without finishing and, unable to hold up, went to lie down a while. Dai came in person very early to collect it, could not find Guo Ziliang, and at once had the guards drag Guo up from his bed in his quarters. On asking, and finding the report not yet copied out, Dai was so angry he took up a stick to beat Guo. Mao Renfeng hastily said: "It was I who told him to rest a while; it will surely be copied out this morning." At that Dai could not go on beating, and turned instead to scold Mao Renfeng for being muddled, for not knowing what was urgent, for spoiling his great affair. The moment Dai left, Mao said at once to Guo: "You went off to sleep — why did you not tell me a word? We nearly had a great problem!" Guo Ziliang was moved to tears and thanked him — and only then did I understand how the thing had really gone.

There were many such cases. When this sort of thing happened, Mao Renfeng always took it on himself. I once watched carefully: afterward he would always find a chance, directly or indirectly, to let the other man know it was he who had helped, who had turned the great matter small and the small matter into nothing. And so more and more people warmed to him. This trick of ingratiating himself he used on me too, more than once, and I well understood his intent. I was by then used to serving Dai Li, and one time more or less did not much matter to me; but he was always deliberately letting me see that he was often helping me in secret, and this was an important reason why I later became one of the "Mao faction."

Mao Renfeng could endure not only within the Juntong but outside it too. Once the Chungking inspection chief He Longqing invited him to the No. 1 Storytelling House to see a Sichuan opera, and took a whole row of the best seats. Later some air-force pilots came and shoved in without ceremony, coming to blows with He Longqing. Both sides were used to throwing their weight about, neither would give way, and they fell to fighting. Mao Renfeng kept his usual manner throughout, so that He Longqing took a beating while he took only a scolding. Afterward he told me that had he not been able to endure, he might well have got a few punches too. He took much notice of this affair and often used it to counsel others: only by enduring does one avoid the loss right before one's eyes.

During the war against Japan, Mao Renfeng's spirit of waiting for his chance was beyond the reach of the ordinary senior agent. In the little upstairs room across from the great hall of Bureau headquarters, he waited content for several years. It was an office-cum-bedroom about a <i>zhang</i> and a half square, worse than the offices of the ordinary department heads. He kept to it all day, going over hundreds of documents, resting only at midnight. As acting chief secretary he lived plainly, and any clerk could come to him at any time to have a problem settled. Men like the Military Affairs Commission's special-operations commander Zhou Weilong, the deputy chief of the land-and-water joint inspection office Zhang Yanyuan, the Ordnance Department inspection chief Zhang Shi, and the agent-corps commanders Zhang Ye and Wang Zhaohuai — all Juntong elders — would go to see him, and he always received them most courteously, joking with them first, as a rule, before getting to business. When he was with Zhang Yanyuan the two of them liked to try out a few phrases of broken Shanghainese and talk nonsense, getting to serious matters only after the others had left.

When Dai Li was in Chungking he often chaired the midday conference at Bureau headquarters in person, and the department and section heads always went to that lunch on tenterhooks. Mao Renfeng too would attend as a rule. Whenever Dai had not yet come, it was always Mao who cracked the first joke, stopping only at the sound of Dai Li coming downstairs. When Dai could not attend and had telephoned him beforehand, Mao would deliberately go a little late, and the moment he came in would say: "I've good news for you all — Mr. Dai has business today and can't come, so let's all eat our fill!" At which the whole room rejoiced and the air at once turned lively. Especially when Dai Li was away on business and Mao chaired the midday conference in his stead, it was all the more relaxed and cheerful; he would always lead off by ordering the next day's dishes, and the mess staff worked all the harder, making each lunch especially rich. Once seated, apart from a brief word on work, he mostly rambled on about anything under the sun. For unlike Dai Li he did not hold many concurrent posts or have a busy private round, and while he was often out of touch with Juntong field work, he was in the office all day and could be reached at any time, so the situation was quite clear and there was no need to spend much of that lunch on business. So everyone was full of good cheer from start to finish when he chaired the midday conference, and imperceptibly warmed to him by the day — and he never let slip such a chance to draw people in.

At that time the Juntong Bureau controlled many open units in Chungking, and the officers in charge of the various headquarters departments often used inspection tours as a pretext to look for pickings, always with much to be gained each time. Many heads of open organs often asked him to come and inspect, and he always said: "I am not the head of a unit, only a helper on the inside staff; I have no need to go out inspecting, and it would not be fitting." When the deputy directors of the many special-training classes in Chungking scheduled him for "morale talks," he politely declined too, saying he was not qualified to give morale talks. When he really could not avoid going, he gave only a general report on operations, never setting himself up as a leader.

While Dai Li was away, the Juntong still held its memorial assembly on Monday mornings as usual, and Mao always asked either Zheng Jiemin or Tang Zong to chair it in Dai Li's place, never coming forward to stand in for Dai himself. Hearing of this, Dai took him for a loyal, dependable assistant without designs, and was glad to groom him, deliberately letting him build up an intangible leading position in the Juntong so that he could stand shoulder to shoulder with Zheng and Tang. In the eyes of many agents, after "Mr. Dai" came "Mr. Mao," while Zheng and Tang grew ever more distant. This laid a solid foundation for his later struggle for power with Zheng and Tang in the Juntong. Even had Dai Li not died, he had long been quietly arranging how to seat himself in the Juntong's number-two chair.

Beyond taking the blame for others, Mao Renfeng's means of pleasing some senior agents included doing his utmost to smooth over their difficulties. The deputy chief of the Bureau's party-and-government intelligence department, Ye Xiangzhi (who later headed the "National Defence Ministry's" intelligence bureau on Taiwan and became a Juntong leader), was a habitual lecher and often got into trouble over his affairs with women. Ye Xiangzhi took a fancy to the woman agent Yang Jichang of the detective corps, sought every chance to get close to her, and violated her. The detective-corps commander Tan Rongzhang was also carrying on with Yang. Later, when Yang Jichang was pregnant and sought to be sent elsewhere to give birth, Ye ignored her and told her to go to Tan; Tan would not deal with it either, and the two, shifting the blame back and forth, fell to quarrelling. Yang could only go and have the child aborted, and died of loss of blood. Yang was a graduate of the Juntong's Xifeng special-training class, and when her classmates learned of this they were furious and meant to join together and denounce Ye and Tan to Dai Li.

Mao Renfeng knew that once this came out, Dai Li would be bound to act against someone, so he came to me to work it out and asked me to step in and mediate, knowing that I taught at many training classes and was often in touch with the students. Understanding the situation, I agreed to mediate, and invited a dozen or so of the leading students to a meal, first explaining to them that the dead could not be brought back, that family shame should not be aired, and that they need not make it public. Seeing me come forward to plead, they could only agree not to enlarge the affair, but demanded that the dead be given a proper coffin and that Ye and Tan see her to her grave in person, and further that the two of them go every year to sweep her grave. Ye and Tan both agreed, but Ye Xiangzhi did not keep to it; Tan Rongzhang carried it out in full, going twice a year to sweep the grave in his Chungking years. Having handled the affair, Mao Renfeng won the goodwill of Ye and Tan, and before long reported the course of it to Dai Li. So long as it was not made public, Dai was content to let it lie. One day Dai asked me: "How was the Yang Jichang matter handled?" I gave a brief account, and he said reproachfully: "Why did you not tell me, before or after?" Only then did I see that Mao Renfeng was playing a double game. He schemed like this for many years, and at first no one could see he was a man of ambition — they simply took it that, being junior in the Juntong, he had to do his utmost to balance and please every side.

Mao Renfeng's ruthlessness, in those first years, likewise no one could see. When Dai Li punished agents severely, Mao often pleaded for them and was frequently rebuked by Dai, who criticized him to his face for a "bodhisattva's heart" that would come to nothing. His sham benevolence deceived even Dai Li; only after he grasped the Juntong's power did it come fully to light.

Some of Mao Renfeng's ruthlessness I have mentioned in earlier materials, and some I mean to write up separately; here I shall speak only a little of his dealings with two men, Ma Hansan and Mao Sen. His killing of Ma Hansan was a great sensation at the time, for Ma was not only the Beiping civil-affairs commissioner but a National Assembly delegate, and not easy to kill.

Around 1944, when Ma Hansan was promoted from head of the Wuyuan office to head of the Juntong Bureau's deployment department, Mao Renfeng went to the airfield in person to meet him and gave a banquet at home that evening in his honour. For before this, when Ma had been the Ningxia anti-smuggling chief, he had always brought many gifts for Mao on his trips to Chungking. The large Helan-mountain inkstone on Mao's desk was one Ma had found for him. On each Chungking visit Ma Hansan would as usual play cards a few times at Mao's house, a disguised way of handing over some coppers. This time, when Ma had just been transferred to Chungking, Mao pressed me again and again to find him a house; after I had bought Ma a small house, Mao went to visit him there too. In short, the two got on very well.

After the victory, when Ma was posted as head of the Beiping office, he kept up his old relations with Mao at first. In the spring of 1946, on my first trip to Beiping, Ma asked me to take Mao a small parcel of gifts, all jade ornaments, which Mao gladly accepted. Zheng Jiemin was then working at the Military Mediation Executive Headquarters, not only stationed long in Beiping himself but with his wife often coming to stay. Through Zheng's wife, Ma passed to Zheng Jiemin the money he had embezzled from taking over Japanese-puppet property and from releasing Japanese war criminals, drawing daily closer to Zheng and beginning to cool toward Mao.

Mao Renfeng had long known of Ma Hansan's corrupt ways, but was still waiting for Ma to share some of the spoils with him. But once Ma took Zheng's line and moreover linked up with Li Zongren, he turned a hostile face to Mao. In 1947, when Ma went to Nanking for a meeting, though he gave Mao some pearls and the like of no small value, Mao heeded his wife, who said that Ma split openly with Zheng's wife and gave Mao only this little, and so refused to accept it, hating Ma the more in his heart. After Zheng Jiemin stepped down and Mao formally became director of the Bureau, he decided to make Ma the one to cut down.

Mao Renfeng had long set a piece on the board: he had sent his trusted fellow townsman Wang Puchen to be inspector of the Beiping station, gathering material on Ma in secret. Having won the trust of Zheng Jiemin and Li Zongren, Ma had grown ambitious too, wanting to form his own faction in the Juntong, secretly building a little organization; even the Beiping station chief Qiao Jiacai was drawn in with Ma, and it grew to more than fifty people. This little organization was at first mainly agents from the several North China provinces, but later drew in men from other regions, its main aim to support Zheng against Mao. When Wang Puchen had gathered a roster of this little organization, he ran at once to Nanking to report it to Mao. By now Mao's killing intent was roused. Just then, Li Zongren and Sun Ke were contesting the vice-presidency, and Chiang Kai-shek had ordered all-out support for Sun Ke; every Juntong agent who was a National Assembly delegate had received formal orders not only to vote for Sun Ke himself but to canvass votes for him. Ma Hansan not only refused this order but, besides canvassing for Li, put up no little money to help Li's campaign. So three offences fell together. Two days before Mao decided to submit his report to Chiang Kai-shek, he specially called me in to ask about all manner of things concerning Ma Hansan.

After Mao Renfeng handed Chiang Kai-shek in person the report asking that Ma be dealt with severely, Chiang, it was said, considered a long while, for as Ma was a National Assembly delegate he had to be careful. Chiang did not, as usual, write "execution by shooting approved," but only "detention and interrogation approved." Mao then decided to hurry to Beiping in person to arrest Ma Hansan. The day before he set out he said to Pan Qiwu and me, smiling, that in his ten-odd years in the Juntong this was the first time he had gone to arrest a man himself.

On his first day in Beiping Mao Renfeng still chatted and laughed at ease with Ma Hansan and the others; only on the second day, at a hastily convened emergency meeting, did he arrest Ma Hansan, Qiao Jiacai, Liu Yuzhu and the rest on the spot, and order all the agents who had joined Ma's organization to write letters of repentance denouncing Ma's unlawful doings — if they did not confess and repent properly, they would be dealt with severely, with Ma and the rest as the example. And so this little clique in the Juntong was at one stroke broken by him.

After Ma Hansan and the others were sent to Nanking, the news quickly spread within the Juntong. From the time he arrested Ma Hansan, Mao Renfeng never saw Ma again. Ma and the others were held in a cell of the Bureau's set-up on Ninghai Road No. 19 in Nanking, which took over the traitor secret-service organizations. On the strength of the mass of material Wang Puchen and the others had gathered in Beiping, and the record of Ma Hansan's confession, Mao again submitted a report to Chiang Kai-shek to have Ma and the rest shot. Chiang approved the killing of only two, Ma Hansan and Liu Yuzhu. In the lockup Ma several times begged to see Mao, always through the judicial-affairs chief Li Xicheng. Once I was present too, and Mao gave a cruel laugh and said: "Whatever he has to say, let him say it to you" — meaning Li — "what should I see him for?"

After Ma was detained, Zheng Jiemin and his wife were much alarmed, but could not well come forward to shield him, nor openly ask anyone to plead, for they knew Mao Renfeng hated to the bone the senior agents of Zheng's faction. Zheng's wife came to me to find out, and I told her the truth, hinting that Ma had already laid bare the whole business of splitting embezzled money with many people. She was much alarmed to hear it. When I mentioned this to Mao, he said smugly: "Well answered! It saves them coming to pester me again. What plea can there be in this?"

The day Chiang Kai-shek approved the shooting of Ma and Liu, Mao Renfeng ordered the judicial-affairs chief Li Xicheng to carry it out in the prison at once. After Ma was killed there were many tales. Some said Mao Renfeng had gone to see Ma before the execution; others that Ma had asked for an anaesthetic before he was shot. From what Li Xicheng told me himself, when Ma was notified and asked if he had anything to say, he was very composed and asked only that his family be looked after so they could go on living — he made no request at all for an anaesthetic. Only Liu Yuzhu wept and cried out before the execution, and Ma even rebuked him, telling him not to make a laughing-stock of himself before he died. After Ma and Liu were killed, even many Mao-faction agents, talking of it behind his back, felt it had been done a bit too ruthlessly.

His treatment of his own clansman-nephew Mao Sen likewise shows that ruthless, kinship-denying streak. Mao Sen was a graduate of the Juntong's Hangzhou special-training class, and early in the war was chief of the Hangzhou sub-station of the Juntong's Zhejiang station, later serving as an action-corps commander, deputy director of the SACO demolition-training class, and director of the forward command post of the Loyal and Patriotic Army, always operating in the Southeast. On his trips to Chungking Mao Sen came and went most at Mao Renfeng's house, and Mao Renfeng meant to groom him, so that after the victory Mao Sen served as second-section chief of Tang Enbo's Nanjing-Shanghai Garrison Command and second-section chief of the Quzhou Pacification Office. Through Mao Renfeng's connection he had much of Tang Enbo's trust. Before Shanghai's liberation, when Tang Enbo's forces were stationed there, Mao Sen got to be chief of the Shanghai municipal police. He was a butcher who killed without a blink, and many in Shanghai hated him to the bone, adding two characters to his name and calling him "Mao-gu-sen-ran" — a play on <i>maogu-songran</i>, "hair-raising" — to describe his cruelty. Mao Renfeng at that time trusted him to the utmost, consulting him on everything.

After Shanghai's liberation Mao Sen fled to Fujian, served as Xiamen garrison commander, and before long linked up directly with the American imperialists. The Americans were then using the method of direct aid, handing weapons to the reactionary chiefs of the reactionaries rather than through reactionary government organs. This let these reactionary creatures take up American arms the sooner to slaughter Chinese, and let the Americans control and direct them the better and more directly. Mao Renfeng, then in low spirits and seeing that the American agents were slow to come and help him directly, was much put out to hear that Mao Sen had won his American masters' regard ahead of him, and thought to use his personal tie and the Juntong's organizational tie to have Mao Sen hand over his whole connection with the Americans, to be run in unified fashion by the Bureau, restoring the old Juntong-era cooperation with the Americans. But not only would Mao Sen not agree — the American side too would only give direct, individual support, which let them pick the loyal lackeys they judged most useful and avoid formally showing their hand. Mao Renfeng ordered it again and again, but Mao Sen, his wings now grown and himself prized by his American masters, paid no heed to Mao Renfeng's line. He got a batch of weapons and radios directly from the Americans and operated along the Southeast coast.

Once uncle and nephew had fallen out, Mao Renfeng, unable to move openly against this cur he had reared with his own hand, decided to send men to assassinate him. Mao Sen had always done such work in the Juntong, and knew Mao Renfeng would move against him once he stopped taking orders, so he guarded himself closely. In the end Mao Renfeng had to resort to an open warrant, preparing to arrest Mao Sen. But right up to the eve of national liberation, when the reactionaries had no foothold left on the mainland, he was still planning to kill Mao Sen, and only for want of an opening came to nothing.

Of Mao Renfeng's ruthless cruelty toward Communists and democratic figures I have spoken in several other materials. He was a consistent advocate of "pulling up the grass and the roots," and so killed no few even of the children.

In the spring of 1949 I was in Chungking, and one day a student of mine, Chen Yuehua, came to ask me to plead with Mao for the release on bail of General Huang Xiansheng. I spoke to Mao. He heard me, smiled, gazed at me a long while, and said softly: "Would it do us any good to let a man like that out only now? You really understand nothing of the political struggle!" Later, in the great massacre Mao Renfeng directed in Chungking, the first to be killed was General Huang Xiansheng.

I also once asked Mao Renfeng to release on bail a Juntong agent, Chen Weicheng. He too held that letting such a man out would harm the Juntong in future, and had him killed along with several other agents in the great massacre.

As for his ways with certain Communist turncoats, more ruthless than Dai Li's, I shall write of these separately below.

### How He Won Chiang Kai-shek's Trust

After I was transferred to the Juntong Bureau as head of the General Affairs Department, I often heard Mao Renfeng, chatting with the department heads, tell most complacently of the first time he went with Dai Li to see Chiang Kai-shek. He said that when Chiang received them in his office his manner was very casual, not at all as stern as when he received ordinary generals. As he talked with them, Chiang had a plate of Ningbo-style pastries brought out, eating and listening at once. This casual manner Mao Renfeng took as the mark of the greatest intimacy and trust.

From 1942 on, each time Dai Li left Chungking he first reported to Chiang Kai-shek and handed the Juntong's work to Mao Renfeng to take charge of in his stead. At first Chiang thought little of Mao Renfeng, and when anything came up would still tell Zheng Jiemin and Tang Zong to their faces and have them pass it on to Mao to carry out. Wherever Dai Li went he always had reports to send Chiang, often by telegram to Chungking, which Mao Renfeng had someone copy out neatly and deliver in person. When Mao Renfeng went, he mostly saw Tang Zong first (Tang was then head of the sixth group of Chiang's personal staff office, in charge of the intelligence sent to Chiang from every quarter), who brought him in; general matters were handed to Tang Zong to pass on. As time went by, Chiang, seeing that Mao handled things steadily and was practised at the work, gradually came to seek him out directly. About the spring of 1943, with Dai Li away from Chungking, Mao Renfeng was talking over some matter with several of us department heads when he suddenly got a call from Chiang Kai-shek's secretary saying Chiang wanted him to come at once. Overjoyed, he changed his clothes and went, and came back to tell us with relish how Chiang had received him. After that Chiang often sought him out when Dai Li was away, and he gradually grew used to it, no longer so tense.

Chiang Kai-shek's view of Mao Renfeng, right up to Dai Li's death, was only that of a seasoned, steady inside-staff man, good at holding what had been built. For in those years, when Dai was away, he had never dared put his own views on the work to Chiang, but only forwarded Dai's reports and passed Chiang's directives to Dai. So when Dai Li died and Chiang decided that Zheng Jiemin, then working at the Military Mediation Executive Headquarters in Beiping, should succeed him, he still enjoined Mao Renfeng again and again, face to face and by wire, to assist Zheng Jiemin well, with no hint at first that Mao should inherit Dai Li's place. But Mao's ways slowly changed. This was partly because Zheng Jiemin was very busy, the mediation work already giving him headaches, while he had always had little to do with Juntong work and had no large body of his own cadres, so that the leadership of the Juntong naturally shifted more and more onto Mao Renfeng's shoulders.

The main reason Mao Renfeng could further win Chiang Kai-shek's trust was that he was good at reading people and knew how to fall in with Chiang's mind. While Dai Li lived, Chiang trusted him greatly and could hardly do without him; but after the victory Chiang already felt Dai Li held too many concurrent posts, and especially that since his dealings with the Americans his ambitions had grown ever larger — the Americans had openly agreed to back him in building a navy — so that he could not give his whole mind to Juntong secret-service work, and Chiang was not much pleased. These things Tang Zong said openly, after Dai Li's death, in the Bureau to Mao Renfeng and several department-head-level senior agents. I was present too, and was much surprised to hear it. Only Mao Renfeng said a word — that those who do secret-service work should best hold no open posts, or few — while the rest said nothing, for most hoped to hold more.

Not long after the Juntong moved to Nanking, while it was still working at No. 22 Matai Street, Mao Renfeng often talked with me in idle moments of Dai Li's private life and of Dai's ways of dealing with certain military and political chiefs. Mao Renfeng knew the Juntong's inside work like the back of his hand, but could not fully know much of what Dai Li did outside. When senior field agents spoke of the many autocratic things Dai Li had done in his lifetime, especially the matters that had displeased so many, Mao Renfeng always questioned them in detail. At first I thought Mao Renfeng was preparing to write some biography to commemorate Dai Li; no one guessed he was preparing to win Chiang Kai-shek's trust for himself, avoiding Dai Li's faults as a lesson for his own climb.

Mao Renfeng could write himself, and always modelled his important reports on Dai Li's own hand when sending them to Chiang Kai-shek. He had a habit of secrecy: when he was writing or going over documents and someone came to him with a matter, he would cover the file, put down his pen, and talk with the man all smiles. Except for routine general correspondence, he rarely approved anything before others, so that what he was up to in secret was hard to make out.

One afternoon, having a document I urgently needed him to approve, and not having seen it come back down since I sent it up in the morning, I went to find him. Just as I walked into his office he had stepped out, and seeing a file lying on the desk, I turned it over before he came back to see whether he had approved it. When I opened the file I got a real shock: it was a report he was writing to Chiang Kai-shek, most reverently, on fine xuan paper. As his characters were always large, curiosity drove me to run my eye hastily over one page, and to my astonishment he was actually attacking Dai Li's past ways. I cannot recall the exact words, but I remember his wording was very roundabout, only setting out that Juntong work should henceforth be "professionalized." He pointed out that in the past Dai Li had held too many open posts and so had to fend and shuffle with every side, which not only cost time but forced him to defer to certain people — a way of working most harmful to the task. I could not go on reading; though I had always been more free-and-easy with him than with Dai Li, had he found me sneaking a look at these things he would have fallen out with me too. But I paid much attention to this matter afterward, and came to see his character differently.

To show Chiang Kai-shek that he was more loyal than Dai Li, he seized on Dai Li's greatest weakness for his theme. For by the war's end Dai held no few open posts, and with his swelling ambitions after the victory had already drawn Chiang's displeasure. Had Dai Li not died, Chiang would not have handed the leadership of the nation's police to the Juntong. To grasp the leadership of the nation's police, Chiang did not accept the men Dai Li recommended but himself appointed the chief of the Capital Police and the police chiefs of Shanghai, Beiping and other great cities. Chiang also summoned Li Shizhen and had his personal staff study Li Shizhen's proposed ten-year police-building plan and a series of such measures. From this whole train of events it is not hard to see that Chiang was unwilling for Dai Li to hold too many posts. Mao Renfeng chose this point to declare his own attitude — that he had no ambition of any kind — and so naturally won Chiang's further trust.

Ever since I saw those few lines of Mao Renfeng's, I kept watching his attitude on the question. At the time I was a hundred times displeased with his ways, holding that he did too great a wrong to the dead Dai Li, that it was a touch of ingratitude, that for his own advancement he stopped at nothing. About a week later, coming back one day from seeing Chiang Kai-shek, Mao Renfeng gladly gathered several of those closest to him for a talk, and I was among them. He spoke with relish of how Juntong work must henceforth be "professionalized," that the senior agents doing secret work should no longer hold open posts not specially needed for their own line, and that open organs and secret units should be separated. He also said that Juntong agents great and small must not privately run businesses, and that all past ventures must stop; henceforth, apart from the "Sanyou Company" run by the Bureau, no individual might buy into or trade in any form or under a relative's name, and any found doing so would be dealt with for breach of discipline. He said too that in future one must not, in the work, show favour to outsiders, and required all agents to serve Chiang Kai-shek with a single heart.

I knew then in my heart that his report had surely won Chiang Kai-shek's approval and praise. Problems in that report I had not seen came out too through that talk. From then on, in the reshuffle of the Bureau's field station chiefs, the posts of some senior-agent station chiefs who also held open office began to be removed. After the victory many agents had been quietly running speculative ventures; while Dai Li lived there was still something to fear, but after his death these men put Juntong work almost second, their chief business being to loot enemy-and-puppet goods and to speculate on a grand scale.

Mao Renfeng's playing to Chiang Kai-shek on these matters naturally roused no little resentment. Many said behind his back that his opposing agents' holding many open posts was because of his own origins. He had no military-academy record, and had held only the open post of a county-government secretary, so that before the victory, when the Juntong's inside agents were all anxious to secure officer's rank and office, he was unwilling to. For though he sometimes said he had spent a few days in the third class at Whampoa before leaving, meaning thereby to claim he too was a Whampoa student, no one acknowledged the tie, and he could not use that origin. At the same time his wife did no small trade, so of course there was no need for him to come forward.

In fact his method of pleasing Chiang Kai-shek to win trust was something even he could not live up to, and some of the rules were changed one after another before long. The new provincial station chiefs he promoted were all training-class graduates he judged most loyal and reliable — like the Chungking station chief Lü Shikun, the Guizhou station chief Qian Wulin, the Xikang station chief Dong Shili, and so on, all training-class men. But those holding open posts — like the Chungking inspection chief Luo Guojia, the Pacification Office's second-section chief Xu Yuanju, the Guiyang provincial-capital police chief Dongfang Bai, and the deputy garrison commander Chen Shixian — were all Juntong veterans who would never accept the leadership of these juniors. Within a year Mao Renfeng had to change his method, and again had senior agents heading open units serve concurrently as provincial station chiefs, giving unified leadership over each province's work.

The order forbidding agents to run businesses, enforced vigorously for a spell, was likewise not kept up; in fact no one would obey it. Mao Renfeng several times urged me to set the example first by stopping the business I ran in Changsha, and granted me a month's leave to go and settle it. I went to Changsha, but not only did not do as he said — I joined with the Hunan station chief Huang Kangyong and the Changsha police chief Li Xiaobai to put in capital and do even more business. It was much the same elsewhere. When he learned of it he only asked that it not be too flagrant, that no one report it to Chiang Kai-shek, and then let it be.

Chiang Kai-shek, hearing his proposals, actually took them for real, held that after his repeated rectifications things were indeed much better than before, and praised him to his face. Though pleased on his return, Mao often showed a hidden worry too. For he knew very well he was deceiving Chiang Kai-shek — luckily, in those days when no official was not corrupt and officials shielded one another, no one would find fault with the agents on such matters and report them to Chiang. Even those who knew the agents were in business would frankly admit it without the least concealment, and even ask for help, for once the name "cover for the work" was added, no one would look into it.

### Cultivating and Using the Powerful

Mao Renfeng's skill at drawing in the powerful families and grandees was, one may say, more painstaking than Dai Li's. Dai Li, long trusted by Chiang Kai-shek, had grown ever more arrogant and overbearing, and many of the powerful, dealing with him, went in some fear of him, so that in a dozen years he made no few enemies. In this Mao Renfeng's style was quite unlike Dai Li's; because his tie to Chiang was not deep and his standing in the reactionary regime shallow, he everywhere copied the ways of Zheng Jiemin and Tang Zong, and would never lightly give offence.

Chen Cheng, the most favoured man in Chiang's regime then, had always disliked the Juntong and Dai Li, and after Dai's death wished all the more to seize the chance to strike at it. On such a formidable foe Mao Renfeng indeed spent much thought. Chen Cheng's temper was so strong and his arrogance so marked that to turn foe into friend was truly no simple thing.

During the war Mao Renfeng and Chen Cheng lived in Yang Sen's Chungking house next to Bureau headquarters. When they met coming and going each day, however courteously and civilly Mao greeted Chen, Chen would only give him a look, half-noticing, and the two exchanged very few words. After Dai's death Chen was more powerful than before. Once, speaking in the Military Affairs Commission's great hall in Chungking, he openly cursed the Juntong and said that the miscellaneous guerrilla units the Juntong had run during the war were worse than bandits. Hearing this, Mao Renfeng heaved several long sighs, feeling deeply that this was the greatest obstacle. From then on he resolved to think of every means to dissolve Chen's ill-will toward the Juntong. Besides going humbly to ask his counsel, he had Yang Sen approach Chen to make ties, but with little effect. After Chen Cheng went to Nanking he cursed the Juntong openly as before, but then suddenly changed his attitude. That Chen and Mao came to associate so intimately came about through a process like this.

While Dai Li lived, he opposed Chen Cheng openly, but as both were Chiang Kai-shek's trusted men neither could do anything to the other. Once the postal-censorship office intercepted a letter, from several officers working in Chen Cheng's Sixth War Zone command in touch with Army Staff College classmates in Chungking. They had a very naive notion — that Chiang Kai-shek himself very much wished to set China right, but was surrounded by corrupt officials and muddled dotards, which was why China had come to such a state — and so joined with some others to launch a military coup, a movement to "cleanse the ruler's entourage." They wished loyally to uphold Chiang Kai-shek, hoping this autocrat could govern China well.

Such an intelligence lead was not worth making a great affair of; ordinarily one would only note their words and doings, see whether anything developed, and then decide. For these men were not troop commanders but mostly staff aides, less than a few dozen in all. At first the intelligence was not even sent to Dai Li; Mao Renfeng merely approved it as usual with a few words — "continue investigation, keep under surveillance" — and let it go. One midday at lunch, hearing Dai Li speak of some being dissatisfied with He Yingqin and others, Mao Renfeng brought this up as a joke, setting many present laughing. Dai Li, on hearing that Sixth War Zone men were among them, took special notice and resolved to make a mountain of this molehill, at once telling Mao to send him the file and ordering a close watch and the gathering of evidence.

But a long time passed, right up to Chen Cheng's transfer to command the Expeditionary Force, with no new development, and everyone gradually forgot the matter. Later the Yunnan station again got some material on this little organization from the Expeditionary Force command, which drew Dai's attention anew. Later still the Yunnan station got hold of the organization's charter and some action plans. Checking the original file, Dai found it was still these same men, all transferred from the Sixth War Zone to Expeditionary Force headquarters. What struck Dai most was that among them was a close relation of Chen Cheng's — now he had something to work with. On the one hand he reported to Chiang Kai-shek that these men were preparing a mutiny; on the other he hinted to He Yingqin and others that cadres of Chen Cheng's system meant to assassinate many senior generals.

Chiang Kai-shek at first wavered, but under Dai Li's sowing of discord, and with many of those about Chiang all day putting in their word, Chiang finally issued an order to Chen Cheng to hand over these men at Expeditionary Force headquarters and send them to Chungking for trial. To clear himself, Chen had to have his secret-service chief Zhang Zhenguo detain these men and send them to Chungking to the Juntong. At the same time the Juntong also detained several connected men in Chungking.

When these men were sent to SACO, Dai Li interrogated them himself through the night. That afternoon the Juntong's judicial-section chief Mao Tiyuan (also called Mao Diyuan) asked me for a car to go to SACO, and as I had business in the countryside we went together. On the way he told me about the case, and I much hoped the plot could be cleared up and Chen Cheng, who was behind it, brought down, to show the Juntong's strength a little. But a few days later, when Mao Tiyuan again asked me for a car and I asked how it was going, he shook his head and said Dai had questioned them twice himself with no result; now it was handed to him, and what could he do?

These men were held secretly, kept apart, in the guard-corps quarters within SACO for a long while, and Mao Renfeng even had the General Affairs Department buy them daily necessities once or twice. Question them as they might, the men would not admit acting on Chen Cheng's instructions, but only that they had started it themselves. For want of evidence there was no way to report to Chiang Kai-shek, and the case dragged on unsettled even after Dai Li's death.

When Chen Cheng was doing his utmost to strike at the Juntong, and Mao Renfeng, feeling that this was a man even Dai Li could not handle, was bent on changing his attitude and cultivating him, he suddenly thought of this case and at once reported to Chiang Kai-shek to reverse it for these men, using it to please Chen Cheng. On the surface it was said to be clearing a backlog, handing them to the Military Justice Bureau for open handling; in fact, once Chiang approved, they were released. These men had been held three or four years, and in the end became Mao Renfeng's capital for pleasing Chen Cheng — and sure enough, before long Chen Cheng's attitude changed. Mao Renfeng seized the chance to see Chen several times and expressed his willingness to take Chen's directions.

At the time no few agents talked behind his back of his bowing and scraping to beg of people, while he was pleased with himself at having broken through this hard pass and often boasted of his ability to some senior agents. Later, when Chen Cheng was Southeast military-and-political governor, he even proposed of his own accord to bear the whole cost of the Bureau's largest field unit, the technical corps, and gave great facilities besides for the Bureau's people fleeing to Taiwan. In the spring of 1949, when I was in Shanghai, Mao Renfeng told me that but for his managing to change his relations with Chen Cheng, there was no telling how much trouble the Bureau's many people and stores would have met in moving to Taiwan.

Some of Dai Li's personal ties Mao Renfeng also contrived to inherit. To please He Yingqin, besides most reverently giving He one of the four new sedans the Americans had given Dai Li, he sought chances in every way to make ties with He. When I went to Yunnan as station chief in 1948, he enjoined me again and again to keep close touch with He's nephew (in fact He's adopted son) He Shaozhou. He Shaozhou was then the Yunnan garrison commander-in-chief, always at odds with the Yunnan chairman Lu Han, and meant to drive Lu Han out and hold the chairmanship himself. Soon after reaching Kunming I understood this and wrote to report it to Mao. He wrote back telling me to support He's plan in every way. He too, sure he had a backer and had the Juntong's support, thought his dream of the chairmanship would soon come true.

Several Yunnan notables who spent all day hunting with He Shaozhou, though old subordinates of Lu Han's, likewise backed He. Resting after a hunt in the field, we talked of everything, openly discussing how to bring Lu down, even arranging personnel in advance — who would be the capital's secretary-general, who the civil-affairs commissioner, and so on. The Yunnan police-affairs chief Li Yuzhen was then a Juntong veteran, and Mao wanted me to join with him in secretly bringing Lu down. But Li, being a Yunnanese himself, was somewhat unwilling at He's reckless, all-out extortion, and even advised me to keep more in touch with Lu and not join the struggle between He and Lu. After I told Mao the truth, when the Yunnan police-affairs office was enlarged into the Yunnan police-security office, Mao proposed to Tang Zong that the post of police-security chief not go again to Li Yuzhen; Tang agreed, and the Yunnanese Qiu Kaiji, an opponent of Lu's, was named police-security chief, with Li demoted to deputy. When He Shaozhou went to Nanking to see He Yingqin, Mao Renfeng gave a special banquet for He Shaozhou and said he would do his utmost to back him for the chairmanship. In the end, because Chiang Kai-shek would not agree, his pains went for nothing.

A newspaper the Juntong ran in Guiyang once said He Yingqin was Guizhou's first great rich man; He was furious to learn it and at once telephoned Mao Renfeng to ask about the matter. Mao, alarmed to death, at once ordered the paper closed and dismissed the Guizhou station chief and Security Command intelligence chief Zhou Yanghao. When Mao reported his handling to He, even He felt the punishment too heavy and was somewhat sorry. When no few Juntong agents cursed him behind his back for deferring too far to these grandees, he even openly required agents to let no such incident happen again, lest it add to his troubles. More than once he said to people: "Why should we give offence over what even the Generalissimo tolerates? What good does it do us?" Once the agents understood this way of his, they took it as a warning and dared not touch these grandees again, fearing to stir up trouble and draw a severe punishment.

Mao Renfeng's winning Chiang Kai-shek's trust and beating Zheng Jiemin — senior to him, of higher prestige, and long trusted by Chiang — cannot be separated from his pleasing of two men, Yu Jishi and Chiang Ching-kuo. To Yu Jishi it was "Chief Aide" at every turn, his manner and tone wholly those of a subordinate to a superior. Yu's goodwill toward Mao, his willingness to put in a good word for him before Chiang Kai-shek, came partly from Mao's flattery and their being fellow Zhejiang provincials, but more importantly from Mao's ability to give Yu what he liked, forever making him gifts of costly things — for Yu had always seen his post as a place to make money.

Zheng Jiemin fiercely feigned incorruptibility, never coming forward to make money himself but setting his wife to it; yet once the money reached her hands she would never bring it out again, so Zheng Jiemin got not a drop of gravy, and dared not himself take Juntong goods for private hospitality. Mao Renfeng likewise used his wife to make money, but this woman was far shrewder than Zheng Jiemin's. She was a trained woman agent who had once done the work of handling Yin Rugeng. After marrying Mao Renfeng she was a great help to him in the work, unlike Zheng Jiemin's wife, who often did her husband more harm than good. To help Mao climb, she used the money she had embezzled to work on his behalf, and it was often she who came forward to give gifts to Yu Jishi. She often urged others to learn from her, saying: "If you want to steal a chicken, you have to scatter a handful of rice as bait."

Mao Renfeng put much effort into Chiang Ching-kuo too. He knew that with this "crown prince" one could not open the road by spending a little money as with Yu. As Dai Li had done, he had the Juntong's Russia-trained senior agents, Wang Xinheng and the like, keep in touch. Chiang Ching-kuo's ambition was great, and he was forever preparing to inherit his father's estate, so Mao played to that. In 1948, when Chiang Ching-kuo went by his father's order to Shanghai to push the gold-yuan note, curb prices, and thoroughly plunder the people's wealth, Mao ordered every Bureau unit and agent in Shanghai to help carry out the task with all their strength. He himself often hurried to Shanghai to supply him information and lay plans, seeking in every way to please. When Chiang Ching-kuo, to establish his authority, prepared to detain Du Weiping, son of the Shanghai Green Gang chief Du Yuesheng, Mao, fearing it might end badly and leave him no way out, tried to dissuade him. But Chiang Ching-kuo held that unless this man was dealt with the situation could not be opened up, and stuck to his own view, so Mao could only agree. In the end, after Du Weiping was detained, the matter reached a deadlock, and Mao again ran about on every side over it, until it came to a tiger's head and a snake's tail — and Chiang Ching-kuo still complained that the agents had not backed him with all their strength.

On the day in September 1949 that Chiang Kai-shek went to Kunming, Mao Renfeng and I went ahead to prepare the airfield. Chiang Ching-kuo came first, and the abject air Mao put on toward Chiang Ching-kuo in the waiting room was truly sickening. When Chiang Ching-kuo asked about my Yunnan-station work, Mao, afraid I would answer unfittingly, said to me very gravely: "Give a good report of the important matters. Toward Mr. Ching-kuo you should be as toward the Director-General." After I had finished, whenever Chiang Ching-kuo put a question, Mao would always answer first, his tone most deferential. Coming back that day he said to me: "However well we do our work, if no one speaks for us before the Director-General, it is still as good as done for nothing."

Besides this, his ties with many other reactionary chiefs long deeply connected with the Juntong — Hu Zongnan, T.V. Soong, Tang Enbo, Yang Sen and the like — he also kept very close, which I shall not list one by one.

Mao Renfeng always held that many of the military and political notables of the Chiang dynasty were willing to help the Juntong's work. So he even cherished illusions about certain generals who had revolted during the War of Liberation. One night while staying at my house, chatting with me, he said smugly: "You see, these generals who have gone over to the Communists still show us mercy, seeing our responsible comrades safely out beforehand; such friendship we may yet find a way to use in future." He stressed again and again that this was no small matter, that there was much to be made of it. He also told me that when Chiang Kai-shek heard of these things he was surprised at first, but afterward felt gratified, holding it the most successful part of the Juntong's work.

When Mao Renfeng left Kunming, the whole trend in the Southwest was plain to everyone, and I could not but ask him constantly about my own safety and work. As he boarded the plane he drew me aside and said quietly: "You needn't worry too much; even with great changes your head won't be lost — look at the cases elsewhere and you'll know how it turns out." Still uneasy about my future responsibility, I asked him again and again for a clear directive. He said quietly, with deep meaning: "There is always friendship between men. You've got on well here — just be careful, that's all." I said: "But if it should happen that—" and before I could finish he cut in: "If there's no time to get away, and you can't carry out the task I've given you" — meaning, if necessary, to assassinate Lu Han and the rest — "can't you learn from Wang Zuo?" He laughed a while, then, stroking my arm, added: "You needn't cut off your arm — staying behind, you'll be of great use yet!"

### His Methods with Turncoats

Mao Renfeng's sinister cruelty in many places even surpassed Dai Li's. He often stressed that the situation was different from before and one had to take more vicious measures. Of his savage crimes in killing Communists and democratic figures I have said something in other materials and shall not repeat here; now I shall speak only of his vicious ways with Communist turncoats.

More than once I heard him tell me: "In the old days when the Nationalists were strong, some Russia-trained students and captured, turned Communists betrayed the Party not only for the safety of their lives but for their own advancement and riches, and so were willing to give sincere effort. Now it is different; those who turn do so only to escape death, and have no sincerity to see it through with us. So we can only use them where they can be used to the utmost, forcing them so they can never go back." Thus, in his time as director, he always did everything to use the newer turncoats.

His most vicious stroke came when the Bureau was fleeing to Taiwan on the eve of national liberation. No few turncoats, more frightened than ordinary men, were racing to get away first; but he deliberately kept these people back and forbade them to go to Taiwan. Before Nanking's liberation, the turncoats working in the Bureau's special political-problems research group were all held back by him — he did not even take away the group's head, Qu Mengqiu. I found this very strange, and later, when Mao Renfeng came to stay at my house in Kunming, I asked him the reason. He told me smugly that this was where he was most satisfied and had won Chiang Kai-shek's special commendation.

He analysed it for me. These turncoats had been used about as far as they could be used; gone to Taiwan, strangers to the place, they could do little. Taiwan was small and did not need so many; going, they would only add to the burden, while leaving them behind brought great advantage. Bending his fingers, he counted it out for me one by one. First: in the past the Nationalists, having caught these men, not only did not kill them but gave them office, so of course these men had harmed the Communists. If the Communists caught them again they would surely settle that account, and they might well be killed. Then we could make great propaganda: men we did not even kill, killed by the Communists. This is the best material, and their relatives and friends will feel great resentment, thinking us far the better. Second: leaving these men behind adds no little trouble for the Communists, and must implicate no few of their relatives and friends too — so that at least much manpower must go into clearing up their cases, while our own people left behind run less risk. Third: if these men are not killed but win the Communists' trust again and are given work, that proves they concealed much; then we can send men to contact them again, and if they will not act, we can threaten them, better to perish together. Fourth: while these men worked for us their pay and treatment were good; afterward, within the Communist ranks, they will not only be looked down on but live poorly, and by contrast will still hanker after us.

Counting and talking, he held the advantages of leaving the turncoats behind past counting. Under this measure of his, no few turncoats who had once broken faith for fear of death were thrown aside together — men like the XXX who had headed the Eighth Route Army's Luoyang office during the war; the XX who was operations-section chief of the Fourth Field Army's staff office (captured and turned at the battle of Siping Street); the XXX who was a municipal Party secretary captured and turned in Chungking before Liberation; and the few who turned among a batch of underground Party members arrested in Beiping and Xi'an in 1947. He told me smugly that some turncoats, on hearing they were to stay and not go to Taiwan, even knelt and wept, and still he would not agree. At the time many senior agents got even the old maidservants of their households onto the planes, while these men waited tearfully to be taken prisoner. Even a batch of old turncoats who had toiled desperately for the Juntong for years he cast aside at the last. The Bureau's second-department deputy chief Huang XX, who had worked with Zhang Guotao during the war and headed the Shaanbei station that specialized in Yenan intelligence, on the eve of Liberation found Mao Renfeng in Chengdu with his whole family in tow, begging again and again to go to Taiwan. Mao flatly refused and ordered him to go on operating in Xikang. Or again, the Soviet-trained student Ye XX, who had done many years' training work in the Juntong and headed the Central China region, and Xu XX, who had headed the Juntong's inspectorate, and many others — all were cast aside by him.

But those turncoats and lapsed Party members he judged might return to the people's ranks he insisted go to Taiwan, fearing they would harm the Juntong's work in future. The most typical case was Yu Lexing, who had run training work in the Juntong for many years. Yu Lexing was a work-study student in France and later studied in the Soviet Union. Mao Renfeng, knowing he was acquainted with some France- and Russia-trained Communist leaders and fearing he would not go, pressed him again and again to go to Taiwan. Yu Lexing could only send his wife and children ahead to Taiwan while he stayed in Shanghai to wind things up. Later, hearing someone say Yu Lexing's movements were suspicious, Mao would not let him go after all, and prepared to arrest him, but for want of evidence and fear of internal resentment did not — for Yu had more than forty relatives working in the Juntong, and students by the thousand, so Mao dared not act rashly.

It happened that I went to Nanking at the end of 1948, and Mao Renfeng told me Yu was a problem and asked me to find evidence of Yu's ties to the Communists, even a few words would do. I went to Shanghai to find Yu and stayed in his house. It was Yu Lexing who had brought me into the Juntong, and over the years we could speak of anything. So deep was the tie that he never guessed I had come on Mao Renfeng's instructions to gather evidence to catch and kill him. Because in less than ten years my standing in the Juntong had climbed to equal his, and I had all along been even more trusted by the chief than he, at such a life-and-death pass he was very careful, never daring to talk with me face to face of the matter, and taking a wholly opposite line from before, doing his utmost to avoid talking with me of the future.

I stayed two days without result, and went back to report to Mao, proposing to let Yu go to Taiwan first. Mao would not agree; he must clear up Yu's case. After I returned to Yunnan, I heard that just before Shanghai's liberation Mao Renfeng had sent men to arrest Yu. But Yu Lexing, warned by a phone call from a student working in the inspection office, escaped Mao's clutches. Later, speaking to me of this, Mao still felt great regret, holding that had this man been caught and killed, he could have been made a typical example to show many turncoats and lapsed Party members who thought of going back what awaited them.

### Fragments of Mao Renfeng's Life

Here I do not mean to say much of the general corruption Mao Renfeng shared with many reactionary rulers, but only to touch briefly on a few of the more striking matters in his life.

After the return of the capital from Chungking to Nanking, Mao Renfeng first lived near the Communist Party's Nanking office at Meiyuan New Village. One day, having as usual sent a car to fetch the best back-scrubber from a certain Nanking bathhouse to his home to scrape and knead his feet, he suddenly got a call from Yu Jishi saying Chiang Kai-shek wanted material on Communist activity in Shanghai, Nanking, Chungking and other great cities, and that Mao Renfeng was to put in order at once the intelligence he had gathered on this and bring it in person to see Chiang. Mao telephoned the Bureau and had his confidential secretary Mao Zhongxin tell the intelligence department to put the material in order, to be sent to him by a courier of the communications section.

This courier had been to Mao Renfeng's house only once, and that at night; taking these papers, in his muddle he delivered them to the Communist Party's office. The Communist office's receiving clerk, seeing a document marked "to be opened by Mr. Mao in person," took it in as a matter of course and stamped a receipt in the delivery book. Mao Renfeng, his bath and foot-kneading done, telephoned to press for the document. Mao Zhongxin went to the communications section to inquire, and the courier, just back, showed him the delivery book. Seeing the receipt-stamp of the Communist Party's Nanking office, he broke into a cold sweat, and in three or two bounds brought the courier to my office, told me the situation, and asked what to do.

I thought a moment and told him not to be in a hurry to report to Mao Renfeng — I would find a way to get the thing back. On the one hand I had the courier take the delivery book at once to fetch the document back, saying only that a little more had to be added and it must be taken back a moment; at the same time I took six plainclothes guards and, in a car, followed the pedicab carrying the document, ready, if the office would not return it, to seize it back at all costs. As my car waited at a spot not far from Meiyuan New Village, the courier went in and in a little while brought the original letter back, and this criminal outrage did not come to pass. Learning of it afterward, Mao Renfeng too broke into a sweat. He said: "Lucky the original was got back unopened. Had this fallen into Communist hands, that would have been the end — there's no telling how many heads would have rolled." Later the courier too was detained on grave suspicion and investigated for a long while before release.

Mao Renfeng was very superstitious about physiognomy, fortune-telling, geomancy and the like. His closest helper, the head of the Bureau director's office Pan Qiwu, had no small influence on him in this. Whether in Chungking, Nanking or Shanghai, when Pan Qiwu found some famous physiognomist, he would take Mao to try him. When the reactionaries, utterly defeated, fled to the Southwest to prepare their last struggle, Mao Renfeng too fell in with a laughable notion of some reactionary military and political chiefs — that the Nationalists' rout was not owing to their own rottenness and loss of the nation's heart, and the Communists' victory not owing to the whole people's support and their leaders' wisdom, but chiefly to a fault in the Nationalist government's flag. For it was a blue sky and white sun over a field of red, symbolizing the Nationalists surrounded by the Communists, and so ending in a field of red all over. Fleeing to the Southwest, they seemed to wake as from a dream and prepared to propose formally to Chiang Kai-shek that the flag be corrected at once. They wished to make the national flag like the Nationalist Party flag — all blue sky and white sun — and shrink the field of red into a single dot of red set in the middle of the white sun, meaning thereby to symbolize the Nationalists surrounding and swallowing the Communists. In October 1949, when Mao spoke of this with relish to me in Kunming, he still felt the discovery had come a little too late.

Mao Renfeng did not much care to use Dai Li's things. He had never taken any interest in the sedan Dai used in his lifetime. Before Mao returned to Nanking I had given out several of the cars Dai Li commonly used to him and to Zheng Jiemin and Tang Zong. He rode in his a few times and then had me hand it on to the deputy chief secretary Zhang Yanfo. Of the houses Dai took over in Nanking, Shanghai and elsewhere he would take not one, preferring to find his own. Some took him for cleverer than Zheng and Tang, but in fact it was superstition, a fear of ill luck; he even shunned the finest office in the new Juntong building Dai Li had designed himself, unwilling to work there, and chose an office whose aspect he judged most fitting for him, which he would never change. When he rose from deputy director to director, many urged him to move into Zheng Jiemin's director's office, and he flatly refused. When I asked why, he said with a smile: "This office of mine is very lucky; why move it?" Because his fate lacked fire, his alias was "Yiyan" ["taking fire"], which he used for many years.

Mao Renfeng was especially fond of Peking opera, and in his month-odd in Kunming went to see one or even two performances almost every day. When Ma Lianliang and Yu Suqiu were performing at the Yunnan Grand Theatre, he went every evening, so that the Kunming police criminal-corps commander Zhou Boxian booked him a dozen-odd seats at the theatre each night, posting agents in the two rows front and back and keeping the five or six best seats in the middle row for him. Zhou Boxian also posted a batch of agents at the theatre door to wait on him; the moment he stepped from his car the agents ushered him in with a great to-do. Such a lordly show made the whole theatre look askance, but he was very pleased with it, and however busy would find time to see the closing star turn.

Mao Renfeng several times wished to engage Yu Suqiu for Taiwan. Yu Suqiu was often close to him and called on him at my house several times. The moment Mao heard Yu had come he would put aside all his business and talk with her for hours, and no one dared disturb them. Once Yu Suqiu had a performance to give by day, and when the theatre manager could not find her at the time and, on inquiring, learned she had gone to my house, he sent to fetch her back to prepare. Trading on Mao Renfeng's power, she said to the man: "Are you afraid the guests will want refunds today, or that you'll do no business afterward? Mr. Mao has kept me to a meal — do you dare say no?" The theatre's man went off without a word, and could only go back and hang up a sign — "Yu Suqiu cannot perform owing to sudden illness" — begging the audience's pardon.

Mao Renfeng, like Dai Li, loved to toy with women, but Mao's advantages were not so great as Dai's, and he could often only use chances and take advantage of others' straits to gain his ends. During the war the Juntong agent Li Guanghe had some influence in the Catholic Church in Shanxi, and once used this tie to do secret-service work and dealt with the enemy and puppets. Not then understanding that Chiang Kai-shek was already practising a policy of "saving the nation by a roundabout road," he dared not report his ties with the Japanese to the Juntong truthfully. When he came to Chungking on the orders of a Japanese secret-service organ, Dai Li found him out and detained him. At this Li's wife Liu Qingfang went about everywhere hoping to see Dai Li; failing to, she saw Mao Renfeng first. Seeing how coquettish Liu Qingfang was, Mao conceived a base design. When she begged to see Li Guanghe, Mao, with Dai's consent, personally accompanied her to see Li. After a few such meetings Mao gained his end. After Dai Li's death, not only was Li released, but before long Mao made him inspection chief of the Tianjin Garrison Command. Later Liu Qingfang wished to use her tie with Mao to run for a National Assembly seat, and Mao agreed to back her. So she travelled ceaselessly between Tianjin and Nanking, her relations with Mao growing ever less concealed. There was also the wife of the Chungking police criminal-affairs chief Tan Rongzhang, one XXX, who often had matters to ask Mao Renfeng's help with and before long fell into his snare too; Mao likewise agreed to help her run for a National Assembly seat. These are but two examples; of such things I shall say no more.

After Dai Li's death, Mao Renfeng led the Juntong, that consummation of all evil, for but two or three years before the whole people drove it from the mainland. In that short time, like Dai Li, he steeped both hands in the blood of Chinese Communists and the Chinese people. Before he had met his due punishment he died, in 1956. The people will forever hate such a fierce and savage butcher. My past relations with Mao Renfeng were of course not bad, but my understanding of his case is still not complete. To lay bare the whole of such a fiend's crimes, this is but a beginning, and awaits comrades who know the facts to write it out more fully.
