## Inside the Bureau of Secrets Preservation

The Nationalist Defence Ministry's Bureau of Secrets Preservation was the largest-scale secret-service organ in Chiang Kai-shek's clique before liberation. It was formed by reorganizing the Military Affairs Commission's Bureau of Investigation and Statistics (the Juntong for short). From the day this Bureau was established, 1 October 1946, until April 1948, I served in the Bureau's head office as director of the General Affairs Department and concurrently deputy chairman of the Property Clearance Committee. From May 1948 until just before the peaceful liberation of Yunnan on 9 December 1949, I was chief of the Bureau's Yunnan provincial station. I therefore know something of the situation around the Bureau's founding, of its many anti-Communist, anti-people crimes, and of the criminal activities of its successive great massacres and acts of destruction. I now set down, item by item, what I personally experienced, saw, and heard. From this one can see the monstrous crimes the Chiang dynasty committed against the Chinese people, and the cruel means it used to carry out its bloody rule over them. But because the range of what I know is limited, I hope others who understand the Bureau's inner workings will keep adding to it, so that the whole of the criminal activity of this notorious secret-service organization may be truthfully exposed before the people of the whole nation.

### A Change of Face; Cull the Weak, Keep the Strong

After the various departments and bureaus of the Defence Ministry were set up on 1 July 1946, it was not until 1 October of the same year that the Nationalist Military Affairs Commission's Bureau of Investigation and Statistics formally announced its dissolution and was reorganized into the Defence Ministry's Bureau of Secrets Preservation, with the head of the Defence Ministry's Second Department, Zheng Jiemin, concurrently as director, and the former Juntong chief secretary Mao Renfeng as deputy director.

When the acting Juntong director Dai Li was killed in a plane crash on 17 March 1946, the war against Japan had not long been won, and the whole nation longed to reach a stage of true unity and peaceful construction; everyone hoped this huge secret-service organ, long hated by the whole people, would be abolished at the earliest day, in line with the proposals the Chinese Communist Party had put forward. But Chiang Kai-shek still meant to keep to his old anti-Communist, anti-people road, and so did everything possible to preserve this huge secret-service group intact, since it was the most faithful and most effective at carrying out his anti-Communist policy. No sooner was Dai Li dead than Chiang, anxious to pick Dai's successor, agreed to the proposal Mao Renfeng put to him, choosing Zheng Jiemin over Tang Zong as acting Juntong director, with Mao Renfeng in fact running everything.

Mao Renfeng had his own calculation in making this proposal. For whenever Dai Li went away he always left the running of the Juntong to Mao Renfeng, while nominally asking Zheng Jiemin to take charge. Zheng held many concurrent posts, understood Dai's character well, and had never cared to interfere much in the Juntong's work, personnel, or funds. During Dai's absences Zheng would spare a little time to go to the Juntong office, but only to write "approved" or "yes" on documents Mao Renfeng had already drafted, rarely altering Mao's views, so that Zheng and Mao got on well. Tang Zong was a careful man who had to do everything himself. During his time as assistant to the Juntong, though he did not concern himself with personnel or funds, on matters of work he often differed with Mao Renfeng. Fearing that after Dai's death the directorship might fall to Tang Zong, Mao Renfeng told Chiang Kai-shek that most of the Juntong's senior men got on well with Zheng and were more distant from Tang. Chiang took Mao's advice and squeezed Tang Zong out. At this time Zheng Jiemin was in Beiping as the Nationalist member of the Executive Headquarters of the Truce Mission, carrying out the task Chiang had given him of dealing with the Communists, and had no time to attend to the Bureau's work; Mao Renfeng used this chance to expand his personal power to the utmost, gradually weakening Zheng Jiemin's ties within the Bureau and seeking out his faults to attack. After this series of maneuvers, on 5 December 1947 Mao Renfeng at last supplanted Zheng Jiemin and became director of the Bureau of Secrets Preservation.

After Dai Li's death, within this secret-service group the struggle for power and profit among the Guangdong faction headed by Zheng Jiemin, the Zhejiang faction headed by Mao Renfeng, and the Hunan faction headed by Tang Zong was very fierce. On the surface they were all harmony; at bottom they were as incompatible as fire and water, to the point of murdering one another. I shall devote a whole chapter to these matters, and leave them out here.

No sooner was Dai Li dead than some within the Nationalist Party too raised the idea of cutting back the Juntong. This was because, although they shared the Juntong's aim and agreed with it on being anti-Communist and anti-people, they loathed the Juntong's and Dai Li's many practices, or had conflicts of interest with the Juntong. While Dai Li lived, since Dai was Chiang Kai-shek's favourite, everyone feared him somewhat and dared not, or found it awkward to, raise objections. After Dai's death these people wanted to seize the chance for revenge or for a share of the spoils. They by no means truly wished, as the whole nation did, to abolish this secret-service organ; each had his own calculation and aim. For instance, Chen Cheng, who opposed the Juntong most strongly, wanted, after cutting it back, to expand his own secret-service system, hoping his secret-service chief Zhang Zhenguo could take over part of the Juntong's machinery and personnel. The CC Clique chiefs Chen Lifu and Chen Guofu wanted to seize the chance to crush the Juntong and enlarge the Zhongtong secret-service organization and strengthen its powers. Kong Xiangxi, too, said at the time that the Juntong ought not to keep as huge an organization and as vast an outlay as before, this because Dai Li had killed his trusted man Lin Shiliang, who had raised money for him. As for some of the "young lords" of the old Renaissance Society, men like Deng Wenyi, Kang Ze, and He Zhonghan, they wanted to take part of the secret-service organization into their own hands and expand their personal power, and so they too joined the clamour to cut the Juntong down. Amid these cries from every side, it was thanks to Chiang Kai-shek's protection and the support of T.V. Soong, Hu Zongnan, and others that this secret-service organization was preserved under a change of face.

Before it was decided to attach the Bureau of Secrets Preservation to the Defence Ministry, Mao Renfeng and Tang Zong had once discussed proposing to Chiang Kai-shek that the Juntong be renamed the "National Defence Police Bureau." Chiang did not agree, holding the name unsuited to the scope of the Juntong's authority. Later, when it was decided to attach it to the newly formed Defence Ministry, Mao Renfeng proposed keeping the name Bureau of Investigation and Statistics after all. Chiang himself knew very well that the signboard of the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics was smeared with too much blood, and to bring it out again would be too unsightly, so he adopted the more veiled name "Bureau of Secrets Preservation." Only after the names, organization, personnel, and funds of the Defence Ministry's other departments and bureaus had all been settled was the Bureau of Secrets Preservation finally brought forward, which shows that within the Nationalist Party at the time this organization too went through a "difficult birth."

Around the founding of the Bureau of Secrets Preservation, the question Chiang Kai-shek most cared about and pondered was not this name or that, but whether Dai Li's successor could, like Dai Li, lead well the huge group Dai had built up over more than a decade and go on serving him; and next, how this group was to meet its vast expenditure beyond the budget. While Dai Li lived, this large sum that could not bear the light of day was mostly raised on its own. During the war Dai Li had used the method of manufacturing the puppet currency circulating in the occupied areas, wringing the blood and sweat of the people there to swell the secret-service funds; after the victory this method could no longer be used. To avoid having always to issue personal orders himself, month by month, to pay out the huge special funds beyond the budget, Chiang Kai-shek considered how to make the Bureau of Secrets Preservation "self-reliant." He met several times with Mao Renfeng and Zheng Jiemin to study the question, and in the end it was decided that the cash and treasure the Juntong took over from the enemy and puppets in various places would not be handed to the Bureau for the Disposal of Enemy and Puppet Property, but that the Bureau of Secrets Preservation might keep it as a fund. In addition, Chiang Kai-shek allowed the Bureau of Secrets Preservation to run some of the enterprises taken over from the enemy and puppets, and handed over to it as well the surplus stores of the "Sino-American Special Technical Cooperative Organization" (SACO), which had worked with the American secret service, so that the profits might serve as secret-service funds beyond the budget. But Chiang could not set his mind at ease over Zheng Jiemin, Mao Renfeng, and Tang Zong as he had over Dai Li, and so he sent in as well Mao Qingxiang, head of his confidential office for many years, to run this work jointly, the four of them forming a committee. After much study it was decided to gather the enemy-and-puppet properties and enterprises the Juntong had taken over in Shanghai, Nanking, Tianjin, Beiping, and elsewhere, together with the two thousand ten-wheel trucks, printing machinery, and photographic equipment left by SACO, and form a "Three-Haves Company," with Mao Qingxiang as chairman of the board, the others as directors, and the great Juntong agent Dai Songyi as general manager; the head company was set up on Yuanmingyuan Road in Shanghai.

The enterprises then run by the "Three-Haves Company" were, on the Nanking side, the Yufeng Cotton Mill, taken over from the Japanese, with the Bureau's management-department head Guo Xu concurrently as manager; and the Tingzi Photo Studio, set up opposite the Anle Hotel in the busiest district, on Huapailou, its equipment all left by SACO, with the Juntong photographer Wang Wenzhao as manager. This was the largest photo studio in Nanking then, and the best-funded and best-equipped. Besides photography it sold all sorts of equipment, rented out cameras, and shot home movies for clients. It made money for the Bureau on the one hand and carried on some secret-service activity on the other. There was also the Hongye Printing and Stationery Company on Zhongshan Road, with the head of the Juntong's April the First Printing Works, Li Ruji, as manager. This too was the largest printing company in Nanking then, well funded, using the newest American electric presses left by SACO, and dealing in stationery and paper besides. There had also been a plan to work with the Nanking Jiangnan Bus Company, the Bureau providing five hundred trucks to be converted into passenger coaches; I negotiated with the Jiangnan company's general manager Wu Zhuozhi several times over it, but later the other side, afraid it could not feed this crowd, backed out. On the Shanghai side there was the Eastern Fishery Company, once run by the Japanese, which had forty fishing boats able to fish the open sea and its own cold-storage warehouses. There was also the Qiming Transport Company, formed from SACO's five hundred ten-wheel trucks, which handled freight on the routes near Shanghai, with Lu Genfan, the third boss of the Shanghai Lu Genji construction firm, as manager. There were also a large sawmill and a plywood works, both taken over from the Japanese, likewise run for the Bureau by Lu Genquan. On the Beiping side there was a Japanese-run wireless-equipment factory, the Beiping Grand Hotel on Xidan, and several warehouses. Tianjin too had some warehouses and cold stores, all run by Bai Liancheng, who had always handled commerce in the Juntong.

Besides running the above enterprises, the "Three-Haves Company" also engaged in speculation and hoarding. Because the Bureau of Secrets Preservation held economic intelligence for the whole country, was well capitalized, and could call up large numbers of men at any time, it fancied it enjoyed the best conditions of timing, terrain, and men, that all would go smoothly, that a single capital would yield ten thousandfold, and that henceforth the Bureau would have profits it could never use up; and so the name "Three-Haves Company" was chosen. But some merchants who knew the company's inside story called it "Have-Power, Have-Money, Have-People," and none dared provoke it, afraid of losing their capital and being sued into the bargain.

Chiang Kai-shek had taken great pains over this move on the board, and at first, since the conditions really were superior, it did tolerably well and made no little money; but afterwards it went from bad to worse. For though the conditions were superior, those who ran it were all laymen, not only putting on great official airs but forever domineering and unreasonable, so that once a customer had come the once he dared not come again. Above all, the men in charge at every level embezzled public funds to run their own businesses, and with the fabi depreciating by the day, the cash taken in, if handed over even a day or two late, could yield no small profit, while the goods sold off could never be bought back. Once every agent handling the business had stuffed his pockets with gold and dollars, the "Three-Haves Company" was in the end brought to collapse. When the war had just been won Chiang Kai-shek still had his scruples; but once he had launched the all-out civil war and his anti-Communist, anti-people face was fully exposed, there was no more need to hide, and so he doled out secret-service funds as freely as before. Now he asked only that the agents throw their whole energy into anti-Communist activity, and so he agreed to the proposal of Mao Qingxiang and Mao Renfeng to sell off all the "Three-Haves Company" properties and run it no longer.

Even before the Bureau of Secrets Preservation was founded, the Juntong's funds already fell short of outlay. Seeing this, Zheng Jiemin knew things would be intolerable if they dragged on. He often said he could not, like Dai Li, make money by clever means, but could only spend within his income. Around May 1946 he hurried back from Beiping to Chungking, and after consulting Mao Renfeng and Tang Zong decided to cut back both office and field personnel. In Dai Li's day the practice had been to admit only, never to release. Joining the Juntong had always been seen as grasping an "iron rice bowl," good for a lifetime; now this "iron rice bowl" too was smashed to bits, like the special plane Dai Li had ridden. Zheng Jiemin not only allowed anyone to take long leave and seek his own work, but for those of thinner seniority, fewer connections, or lesser ability, he also used the method of sending them to the officers' holding corps to transfer to other work, or simply dismissing them, to solve the problem.

During the war, at the Juntong's height, the office and field agents, not counting the armed secret-service troops, numbered close to fifty thousand. Those drawing salary directly from the Juntong numbered over 17600 in the rear, over 6000 in the occupied areas, and over 2000 overseas; the other half were scattered among the various open secret-service units and not paid by the Juntong. After the victory a number of wartime organs were dissolved one after another, and a great many agents formerly not on the Juntong's payroll came back to the Juntong to ask for new assignments. By Juntong custom, agents "awaiting orders" before an assignment was fixed still had to be paid living expenses. This made funds tighter still, and almost every month an overdraft had to be sought from the Quartermaster General. Chiang Kai-shek too had a great headache over this, and agreed to the methods of culling the weak and keeping the strong and putting the finances in order, allowing part of the property and jewellery taken from traitors to be sold off to pay severance. For the families of dead agents, a lump-sum pension would be paid once and their living expenses borne no more. By the old Juntong method, the bereaved families of agents dead in the line of duty were to receive living or supplementary expenses monthly or yearly, and their children's schooling was borne by the Juntong too. Over more than a decade the accumulated widows and orphans numbered in the thousands, and this outlay too was no small thing, and could not be accounted for. This time each was given about three to six months' living expenses and then left to fend for itself.

These measures then seemed to Zheng Jiemin and Mao Renfeng their masterstroke. This cut-the-knot-with-a-sharp-knife method did indeed gradually put later income and outlay on track, no longer grabbing and spending wildly as before. Dai Li had brought in no little money, but had built up no reserve; the more he brought in, the larger the outlay grew, and the accounts department was forever crying out on the eve of the New Year that there was not enough. After this great overhaul things improved somewhat. A few of the senior agents who took long leave of their own accord even praised the arrangement warmly. For most of them were already stuffed with wealth; while Dai Li lived they had daily feared exposure and trouble and could not have left even had they wished, and the long-leave method suited them exactly, letting them go and enjoy the leisure of their later years without a care.

But those dismissed or sent to the officers' holding corps, who ordinarily had few outside connections, had gained little through graft and extortion, or had spent freely because it had come easily, all fancying that with the Juntong as their backer they need never want for money, now raised a chorus of complaint. Above all, while they had worked in the Juntong they had had a crowd of wine-and-meat friends in society, each using the other, and were made much of on every side; but once they left the Juntong and went back to these friends, all took an evasive, perfunctory line. For instance, some agents who did inspection work, when they had swaggered about the docks and wharves, were flattered to the skies by the responsible men on the steamers, the private-car bosses, and the transport merchants; but once these learned the agents had left the Juntong and wanted a little work, their manner changed at once, they no longer called them brothers, some handing over a few coins to see them off, some simply ignoring them.

Those who went to the officers' holding corps could draw only a limited living allowance, lodged in crowded dormitories, eating the same rations as the troops. To agents used to a comfortable life and free spending, endless complaint was only natural. Some of the agents in Chungking would often go and weep at the "Dai Memorial Shrine" that venerated Dai Li. The bereaved families who had always lived on Juntong pensions, having quickly spent that little pension money, would often make a scene demanding more. From its founding to its flight to Taiwan, the Bureau of Secrets Preservation never once ceased dealing with problems of this kind.

The standard for this cutting of personnel was mainly to keep the "core members" and "basic personnel," and to select among the ordinary agents. The "core members" the Juntong then spoke of mostly meant old agents who had joined before the war against Japan; the "basic personnel" were mostly graduates of the Juntong's various wartime agent-training classes; the "ordinary members" were mostly people taken on during the war from the various open organs, brought in by personal introduction, or promoted from the ranks. The agents the Juntong then sent to the officers' holding corps in various places to transfer to other work came, all told, to about twenty thousand, Chungking having the most with over 5000, Xi'an about 3000, Jiangxi over 800, and the rest a few hundred to a thousand or two each.

When the Juntong cut personnel on a large scale, both those cut and those kept had some who were dissatisfied, shamelessly reckoning themselves men of merit in the war against Japan and against the Communists. Mao Renfeng held this view too, and in his heart hoped to keep more men. Zheng Jiemin held the opposite, urging that more of the less useful be cut to reduce the target. He hurried to Chungking in early June 1946, called together the heads of the various units, and explained that the Juntong's work henceforth would be struggle with the Communist Party; that it was not a matter of numbers, but that only able and capable men could shoulder the task, so it were better to have fewer but abler men, and the smaller the target the better.

Zheng Jiemin also specially put before everyone once more the passage from the Eighteenth Group Army commander-in-chief Zhu's telegram to Chiang Kai-shek on abolishing the secret-service organs, stressing that the Communists had long since publicly raised this demand and it had won wide agreement. He said that if they could not shrink the establishment and cut personnel of their own accord first, then not only would they fail to pass muster when a new government was formed, but they would arouse great resentment and, above all, add to Chiang Kai-shek's troubles. Again and again he asked everyone to appreciate the leader's hard position, take the larger view, and conform to the times, and on no account, for the small, spoil the great. To set the cut men's minds at rest, he added that those cut should count only as temporarily leaving Juntong work, that they might keep their organizational ties, and that when the establishment was later enlarged they would have the right to be assigned first. After this the clamour eased a little.

At that time, to place more men in the open units the Juntong controlled, Zheng Jiemin and Mao Renfeng went in person again and again to work on the agents in charge of open units. While Dai Li lived, the personnel arrangements of any open organ the Juntong controlled were handled almost entirely by the Juntong's personnel department, and the heads could not appoint their own men on their own authority. After Dai's death things changed; the heads of some open units mostly paid lip service to the head office's orders, filling vacancies without reporting them and finding their own men, and refusing men sent to them either by saying there was no vacancy or by pleading unfit seniority. That Zheng and Mao went in person to the various departments was in fact a last resort. Some senior agents had found themselves good posts, such as Zhang Guozhen, who worked his way into the directorship of the Jiangxi relief sub-office, Huang Zhanghua into the directorship of the Guangxi relief sub-office, and Yu Lexing into the directorship of the motor-management office of the Relief Administration's Shanghai branch; Zheng and Mao would recommend some men and ask these people to help place them separately. Because Juntong agents were unwelcome everywhere, many organs would think up ways to refuse them the moment they heard, and as more and more great cities were liberated and more and more fugitive agents came asking for work, this transfer-and-placement work was not fully solved even after more than two years. In the second half of 1947, to place these men, a training class for transferring personnel was set up in Suzhou, taking in over 3000.

From the reorganization of the Juntong into the Bureau of Secrets Preservation after Dai Li's death, the once-a-year "April First" congress of the Juntong period was changed, from 1947 on, to the "March Seventeenth" congress, to mark Dai Li's death on that day. The "March Seventeenth congress" was identical in nature with the "April First congress," being chiefly the once-a-year national work conference. The schedule too was as before: on the first morning a public memorial for the dead agents, followed by the work conference. At the midday meal it was the rule to drink three toasts: the first to Chiang Kai-shek's good health, the second to the good health of all agents, and only the third changed, to the good health of Zheng, Tang, and Mao.

Those attending the work conference were, from the head office, generally section heads and above; the field delegates were generally station chiefs or deputy chiefs and the heads and deputies of the various open units. Chiang Kai-shek did not attend the congress in person again, and received only once the heads of the various provincial stations and the department-head-rank senior agents of the head office who attended the meeting.

The Bureau's leaders Zheng Jiemin and Mao Renfeng did not, like Dai Li, change their aliases often. Zheng Jiemin always used the name "Jiefu" to endorse documents and issue orders, and Mao Renfeng always used "Yiyan"; only Tang Zong used his style-name "Naijian" in endorsing documents. But the head office's aliases were often changed. Every department and office had its own special alias for outgoing correspondence, redefined at short intervals and notified by list to the field provincial stations. The rules for wireless codes were tighter than before too. A single provincial station had several codebooks, the method of translating them was often changed, and some codebooks were used only a few times and then dropped. This was for fear the Communist side would intercept the codes and decipher them.

After the Bureau of Secrets Preservation was founded, though the old Juntong machinery was kept, its internal organization was partly shrunk, partly merged, and some parts even abolished; but the Special Guard Group protecting Chiang Kai-shek's personal safety was not only kept wholly intact but expanded from over two hundred men to over three hundred. The "Attendant Guard Group" then guarding Chiang's person had always been funded through the Attendants' Office, its task being to guard Chiang's residences and offices and to attend him front and back when he went out. There was also a "Special Guard Group" charged solely with the outer cordon; when Chiang Kai-shek went out it would first deploy along the road, a man every few hundred paces, each marked by holding a newspaper in his left hand. This lone despot most feared assassination, and each time he went out he would look carefully to both sides to see whether these men were there guarding him; if he went far, he had to send the Special Guard Group ahead to deploy first before he dared go. This group was headed successively by Zhu Jinnan and Liang Shaozhou.

### Reorganizing the Apparatus; More Frenzied Than Ever in Anti-Communist, Anti-People Work

Not long after the Bureau of Secrets Preservation was founded, about the winter of 1946 or the spring of 1947, Chiang Kai-shek, in a talk to the Bureau's department-head-rank senior agents, dwelt on the Bureau's future tasks. I was one of those summoned to hear the instruction. After Mao Renfeng handed him the list of those present, he called the roll one by one. First, unable to forget Dai Li, he mentioned him again and again, praising this running dog to the skies for serving the party and state with all his life, telling everyone to learn from him and carry on his will, and, under the leadership of Zheng Jiemin and Mao Renfeng, to redouble their efforts to build on past achievements and keep past honours. Then he spoke of the chief enemy henceforth being the Communist Party, saying that struggle with the Communists was far harder than struggle with the Japanese and traitors had been. He demanded that everyone throw in his all, saying that the least carelessness would not only endanger the party and state but leave one dead without a grave. He hoped everyone would study more methods, offer ideas, and constantly sum up experience and lessons, so as to shoulder well this task of struggling directly with the Communists.

In May 1948, when I was transferred to be chief of the Yunnan provincial station, Mao Renfeng specially gathered about thirty provincial station chiefs and held a station-chiefs' study class in Nanking. After the month's study, Chiang Kai-shek again received all the station chiefs called in for training and the delegates attending the "March Seventeenth" work conference, again at his official office behind the Central Military Academy on Huangpu Road. After calling the roll as usual, he first expressed the greatest approval of Mao Renfeng's method of adjusting the provincial station chiefs.

For after the Bureau was founded, Mao Renfeng, worried that most of the provincial station chiefs were old cadres who did not readily obey, had promoted a batch of men to replace them from graduates of the Juntong's Linli special training class and from some middle-ranking cadres. Later he found that these newcomers, thin in Juntong seniority, could not lead the open units in the provinces, whose heads were all old Juntong cadres and would not take orders from the newly promoted chiefs, so that the stations' work could not be pushed forward. So after Mao Renfeng supplanted Zheng Jiemin as director, feeling things could not go on this way, he decided to transfer out a batch of general-rank senior agents to be station chiefs, and, under his own direction, gathered these newly transferred chiefs and some of the old chiefs to be kept for a month's training in Nanking, demoting some of the thin-seniority chiefs to deputy chiefs. Chiang Kai-shek was well pleased with this work of strengthening the field provincial stations. In his talk he pointed out that the provincial stations henceforth should still, as in the Juntong period, secretly lead the open, and that the focus of each province's work should be placed on the provincial station.

The focus of Chiang Kai-shek's talk this time was to require the heads of the provincial stations to adjust the field agents. The Bureau's field personnel should be socialized, each having an open profession as cover, so as to penetrate the various democratic parties and progressive bodies; besides drawing in more waverers among the democratic parties to work for them, the chief task was by every means, and at any cost, to penetrate the Communist organizations everywhere, so as to wipe out the Communists in each province and secure the rear. Personnel who were unfit or whose identities were already exposed should be placed in open organs; those kept must be capable men with a line of work, and new members must be constantly taken in. He required the provincial heads not merely to direct the work but to engage personally in higher-grade intelligence activity. He then proposed that station chiefs might be additionally commissioned as Defence Ministry commissioners, using this title to act openly in the locality, while all below the station chief was to be kept strictly secret. He also required the provincial heads to cultivate local relations, keeping close ties with the local garrison commanders and administrative heads, and mobilizing forces on every side to do the secret-service work well. He told us to value the use of Communist turncoats, but also to guard against these people. In a rather warm tone he said to these senior agents who exhausted themselves as his claws and teeth: "You are all old subordinates who have followed Director Dai for years, all the most revolutionary and most loyal to party and state. With you leading the Bureau's organization in the provinces, I am much at ease. In carrying on the struggle with the Communists in every place henceforth, with you leading it, I am sure you will not fail my hopes and will achieve outstanding results. Whatever difficulties you meet in your work, I will take responsibility for solving them, so that you can work with a single mind." At the end, as usual, he heaped slander on the Communists and intimidated those present, saying that if they did not strive to defeat the enemy they would surely be left dead without a grave.

When the Bureau of Secrets Preservation was founded on 1 October 1946, it had its office at No. 22 Matai Street, Nanking, and only in the first half of 1947 moved into the newly built mansion at Honggongci. The Defence Ministry fixed the Bureau's total personnel: the office departments and rooms were cut from over a thousand in the Juntong period to 335, the field to 6023, and the wireless section kept at 1000, a total nearly twenty thousand fewer than before. In 1948 the field personnel were increased again by 2880. When the Bureau was first founded Zheng Jiemin was concurrently director and Mao Renfeng deputy director. On 5 December 1947 Zheng Jiemin was removed from the directorship and from the headship of the Defence Ministry's Second Department and transferred to be vice-minister of national defence, with Mao Renfeng promoted to director and the former Military Affairs Commission commando commander Xu Zhidao transferred in as deputy director.

From its founding to its flight to Taiwan on the mainland's liberation, this Bureau changed the names of its office and field machinery very little, unlike the Juntong period with its several changes and expansions a year. Below the director and deputy director was the director's office, headed by a chief. When Zheng Jiemin was director, Huang Tianmai and Zhang Jixun were successively director's-office chiefs. After Mao Renfeng became director he replaced Zheng's men, transferring his most trusted man Pan Qiwu to be director's-office chief. Under the director's office were about ten commissioners, secretaries, and assistant secretaries; Wang Qing, Xiao Manliu, Liu Zhenfang, and others served successively as commissioners; Mao Zhongxin, Yuan Jibin, Zhou Nianxing, Zheng Erkuang, Xu Changjun, and others as secretaries. Directly led by the office was also a documents section, headed by Zhou Shisu.

The head office had seven departments. The First was the Intelligence Department, headed by He Zhiyuan, formerly head of the Juntong's Second Department, with military, party-and-government, economic, and international intelligence sections. The Second was the Operations Department, headed by Ye Xiangzhi, with an operations section (in charge of arrest, assassination, and sabotage), a counter-espionage section, an inducement-to-defect section (this section led many defection-inducement stations specializing in inducing the PLA and local progressive armed forces to defect), and a psychological-warfare section. The Third was the Personnel Department, headed by Zheng Xiuyuan, with personnel-administration, examination, and card-index sections. The Fourth, as in the Juntong period, was the Wireless Department, headed by Yang Zhenyi, with communications, machinery, and works sections. The Fifth was the Judicial Department, headed by Li Xicheng, with interrogation and prison-management sections (the two detention houses of Zhazidong and Baigongguan at Ciqikou, Chungking, and the detention houses and concentration camps in Xi'an, Shanghai, Beiping, Nanking, and elsewhere were all led by this department). The Sixth was the Management Department, headed by Guo Xu, with audit, accounting, book-keeping, and cashier sections, in charge of finance. The Seventh was the General Affairs Department, headed by me (after May 1948, deputy head Cheng Xichao rose to head), with general-service and administration sections and two directly attached units, receipt-and-dispatch and communications, plus an attached motor pool headed by Sun Chao and a telephone unit headed by Zheng Xiuyuan.

The head office also had three rooms: a supervision room, headed by Liao Huaping; a chief-auditing room, headed by Zhang Guanfu; and a budget room, headed by Zhu Guangdou. Besides these departments and rooms there were several groups: a confidential group, a special-political-problems research group, a special-technical research group, a deployment group, and others. The confidential group, headed by Jiang Tingying, the Juntong's only woman major-general, was in charge of decoding and codes. The special-political-problems research group, formerly run by Zhang Guotao, who left the Juntong at the reorganization, was headed by Sha Mengqiu, and studied every question concerning the Communists, its members all Communist turncoats. Though Mao Renfeng used these people's old connections as far as he could for the Bureau's work, he did not trust them, so this group did not have its office within the Bureau but found a place outside, and its members were called to the head office only when necessary, to take part in some unimportant meetings, none of them allowed into the Bureau otherwise. The special-technical research group, headed by Liu Shaofu, studied assassination, blasting, sabotage, and arson techniques, and made all kinds of time bombs, poisons, poison shells, poison knives, and other killing tools, plus incendiary umbrellas, incendiary fountain pens, railway-wrecking devices, and the like. In 1948 a part of the personnel department was split off to form a deployment group, headed by Zhao Binbo, with a first and second section headed respectively by Li Baochu and Ren Hongchuan, specializing in planting undercover deployments in the liberated areas and in preparing, in areas about to be liberated, undercover agents and radio stations in advance. The old Juntong's Design Committee was kept as before, solely to hold senior agents in reserve; Zhang Yanfo, Liu Qirui, and others had served as its chairmen. There were also forty senior intelligence agents led directly by the Bureau's head office; some were senior aides in the Nationalist Government's central departments, some big merchants, some out-of-office bureaucrats and politicians, all working on so-called upper-level intelligence.

When the Juntong moved from Chungking back to Nanking, for more than half a year after the Bureau was founded a "wind-up office," formed by merging the Juntong and SACO, was still kept in Chungking under Zhang Yanfo, to handle the closing work of the Juntong and SACO. Besides placing large numbers of cut personnel, this office spent more than a year handling the great quantity of stores left in Chungking and did not finish. They sold off part of the equipment and gear, handed a "April First" hospital and some medical equipment hard to move to the Chungking Central Hospital, and, after arming the traffic-police corps with the American weapons, still had much left. On the eve of liberation, at the same time as the Bureau carried out the great massacre within SACO in Chungking, it also set fire to several warehouses of equipment, stores, and weapons and burned them for two days and two nights.

The establishment of the Bureau's field provincial stations was divided, by the numbers the Defence Ministry fixed, into three kinds. Larger areas such as Shanghai, Nanking, Tianjin, Beiping, Sichuan, and Yunnan were Grade-A stations of 160 men; Anhui, Guizhou, Xinjiang, and the like were Grade-B stations of 110; smaller places were Grade-C stations of only 60. The actual numbers in each place, the requirements being too strict, often fell short of the establishment. To take a Grade-A station as an example: besides chief and deputy chief there was a secretary, one or two assistant secretaries, an intelligence editor, one or two assistant editors, two clerks, a cipher clerk; besides these, personnel handling personnel, general affairs, accounts, and communications, a student-movement director and a labour-movement director, and several intelligence agents directly attached to the station; and one supervisor sent by the head office's supervision room, under the dual leadership of that room and the station chief. A provincial station generally had a wireless sub-station assigned by the wireless department, with each group having a group set, the sub-station keeping direct contact with the Bureau's main station. This sub-station was an attached unit of the station, not under the station chief's leadership. A sub-station generally had two or three large sets, the group sets all being small special-agent sets.

Stations abroad were in Korea, Saigon, Singapore, Bangkok, Cairo, Karachi, the Philippines, and elsewhere. Later, above the stations, there were some higher-grade organizations, such as the Northeast Directing Room, headed by Wen Qiang; the Southwest Special District, headed by Xu Yuanju; and the Northwest Special District, headed by Hu Ziping. When the reactionary government fled to Guangzhou in 1949, a Guangzhou office was set up, headed concurrently by Guo Xu; later, on the move to Chungking, a Chungking office was set up too, headed by Tang Zhi.

Mao Renfeng ran the Bureau's work still by Dai Li's old methods. Head-office personnel of deputy-department-head rank and above still lunched together, discussing general matters over the meal, settling personnel, funds, and the like on the spot. When field station chiefs and deputies came to Nanking they too were often invited to these meals, and some of their problems could be quickly settled. The heads of the various units discussing face to face, without going through sections and sub-sections, forced each head to read the documents, or he would be caught out on the spot when a matter came up he could not answer. Mao Renfeng gripped this work tightly; barring some special mishap he attended almost every time, and every time raised some matters to discuss.

Besides, in keeping ties with some open secret-service organs, Mao Renfeng also kept up a set of practices from the Juntong period, inviting the gendarmerie commander, the Zhongtong director, the police director-general, the head of the Defence Ministry's Second Department, and others to a meeting once a month. Mao Renfeng specially bought a house on the road from Fuhougang to Xuanwu Gate, naming it "Cheng Lodge," for these unit heads to meet in. Matters concerning dealing with the Communists and suppressing the democratic patriotic movement were raised at this meeting, so the various secret-service organs could take a common line. He also laid it down that the provincial station chiefs must attend the provincial-level special conferences in their areas, so as to keep in touch with all sides on the anti-Communist front and coordinate. Mao Renfeng also required each provincial station chief to convene at least once a month an open-and-secret conference, called by the station, which the heads of the open secret-service organs the province controlled had to attend in person, usually in the form of a dinner or gathering. At the meeting the station chief first reported the province's general situation and the Bureau's relevant instructions, then each unit head reported in turn on his unit's work over the month and raised common problems for study and discussion. After the meeting, a report still had to be made to the Bureau. This was the Juntong's consistent old method of secretly leading the open. But in the Bureau period, in some places it had already become a mere form.

The open secret-service organs the Bureau controlled were not as many as in the Juntong period, and the degree of control was far looser too. The largest open, nationwide secret-service organ then was the police director-general's office; since it was led by Tang Zong, though it nominally accepted the Bureau's control and use, it in fact had a set of its own. For instance, Tang Zong proposed that police personnel be regularized, that no one not from a police academy could be a police officer. This amounted to a public declaration refusing to take in the personnel the Juntong was then cutting. Though some of the personnel officials in the director-general's office personnel room were mostly recommended by the Bureau's personnel department, such as Liu Benqin and Wang Sheji, both old hands of the Juntong-period personnel department, they could not, as before, be under dual leadership; everything was decided by Tang Zong. So when the security offices and police offices in the provinces were merged and reorganized into police-security offices, many police-security heads were not Juntong men but transferred senior officers. Later, when Tang Zong concurrently headed the Defence Ministry's Public-Security Affairs Bureau, he too picked only a few Juntong men such as Zhao Shirui for it. As for the replacement of the great-city police chiefs, all had to be decided by Chiang Kai-shek in person; Zheng Jiemin and Mao Renfeng found it awkward to go over Tang Zong's head straight to Chiang, and Tang Zong would not fight for the Bureau. Dai Li had, back in the war years, made arrangements in advance for the police-chief candidates of some great cities, but after the victory these were all appointed anew by Chiang Kai-shek, as Shanghai went to Xuan Tiewu and Beiping to Chen Zhuo. Dai had only grabbed the Tianjin police chief's post for Li Hanyuan. Li Hanyuan was a "Tianjin hand" who had served foreigners and done police work in the Tianjin concessions for over a decade. As for the post of Nanking Capital Police chief, Dai Li had for years been grooming Ren Jianpeng. On the Nanking withdrawal, Ren Jianpeng was commander of the Capital Police security corps; when he fled toward Chungking with a batch of armed security police he took the Capital Police seal along with him. On reaching Chungking he became head of the Interior Ministry's police corps, watching wistfully for eight years; when at last Japan surrendered, sure he could become Capital Police chief, in the end Chiang Kai-shek did not grant Dai Li's request but gave the post to the gendarmerie chief of staff Han Wenhuan. The Capital Police seal Ren Jianpeng had carried with him for eight years, never once used, he handed over. Dai Li was terribly grieved over this and managed, with difficulty, to win Ren the post of Hankou police chief. These several great police-chief posts only slowly fell into Juntong hands after 1948. Beiping went to Yang Qingzhi, and Shanghai, before liberation, to Mao Sen. The Nanking police office never fell to the Juntong at all.

The second open organ the Bureau controlled was the Communications Ministry's National Traffic Police Directorate, headed successively by Ji Zhangjian, Zhou Weilong, and Ma Zhichao. Its members were mostly patched together from the former Military Affairs Commission's Unified Land-and-Water Communications Inspection Office, the Commission's commando command, the Loyal and Patriotic Army, and other units, and could on the whole accept the Bureau's leadership. The police heads of several railways then, such as Wang Zhaohuai of the Nanking-Shanghai and Shanghai-Hangzhou-Ningbo lines, Zhu Ruoshuai of the Beiping-Hankou line, Wu Anzhi of the Beiping-Liaoning line, Cheng Yiming of the Longhai line, Bi Yuan of the Tianjin-Pukou line, Zhang Fubang of the Guangzhou-Hankou line, Shi Ming of the Hunan-Guangxi line, Mao Wanli of the Zhejiang-Fujian line, Li Xichun of the Shanxi-? line, Zeng Qingchu of the Chengtu-Chungking line (the Chengtu-Chungking railway had not yet begun construction then, yet a railway administration and police office had been set up in advance), and Tian Dongyun of the Yunnan-Vietnam line, were all old Juntong. On the highway side, besides the highway directorate's guard room, headed successively by Liu Ziqin and Cai Shenchu, the guard-and-inspection groups of the various highway offices, the police-guard rooms of the various transport offices, and the police-guard group of the Merchants' Steam Navigation Company (water transport, its head Shi Renchong) were all controlled by Juntong agents. This directorate took in all the armed secret-service troops and reorganized them into over twenty traffic-police corps.

By old Juntong tradition, the Defence Ministry's Second Department too should have been controlled by the Bureau. Zheng Jiemin was concurrently Bureau director and Second-Department head, so in principle it should have been no problem. But he himself was often not in Nanking, and the Second Department's deputy head Hou Teng was not only not Juntong but flew his own flag and would not accept the Bureau's leadership. While Zheng Jiemin was concurrently head, Hou Teng still went through the motions; but once Zheng was removed from the Bureau directorship and Second-Department headship and made vice-minister, and Hou Teng became Second-Department head, things changed at once. Not only could the Bureau no longer control the Second Department, but Hou Teng even wanted to squeeze out gradually the Juntong men who had worked there, rivalling the Bureau in military intelligence, keeping military intelligence in his own grip and leaving the Bureau to handle only party-and-government intelligence. Mao Renfeng himself knew well that the Bureau could not out-compete the Second Department in military intelligence, but would not give up the work; he secretly directed the Juntong men still working in the Second-Department system to keep their ties with the Bureau and to obstruct Hou Teng by every means. Right up to the flight to Taiwan the two were still scheming and jostling against each other.

The units the Bureau then controlled most were the inspection offices in various places, the open units first grabbed in the great cities after the victory. Thus the inspection-office head of the Songhu Garrison Command in Shanghai, Cheng Yiming; the inspection-office head of the Nanking Garrison Command, He Longqing; the inspection-office head of the Beiping Garrison Command, Ni Chaofan; Chen Xianzhou of Tianjin; and so on, were all placed by Dai Li in person and stayed thereafter in the Bureau's hands. As for some areas of the former great rear such as Chungking, Chengtu, Xi'an, and Lanzhou, they too remained unchanged, still in the Bureau's grip. Besides, the detective brigades or criminal-police offices of the great cities were all in the Bureau's hands too, such as the Beiping criminal-police brigade commander Li Lianfu, the Shanghai criminal-police head Liao Gongdong, and the Chungking criminal-police head Tan Rongzhang, all Juntong men.

With the inspection offices and criminal-police offices, these two kinds of open secret-service organ, in hand in various places, the Bureau still had great facilities for arrest, search, imprisonment, and the like. Mao Renfeng valued these units especially and, once he held them, would not let go for his life. In 1947 the Chungking police chief Shi Juemin, who had served in Chiang Kai-shek's Attendants' Office and so fancied himself of great standing, would not defer to the Bureau, and removed the criminal-police brigade commander the Juntong had placed there, appointing his own trusted man He to succeed him. Though Mao Renfeng agreed on the surface, in secret he directed the Chungking agents to make trouble, and within a few months a run of strange cases broke out one after another that this He could not crack. Shi Juemin had no choice but to give up the criminal-police brigade commandership again and ask Mao Renfeng to send a man for the post.

The agents who did this criminal-police work were mostly themselves gang and secret-society bosses, and controlled a large crowd of thieves, pickpockets, and habitual burglars of every stripe. When a powerful, well-placed man lost something, they could trace it out and return the article within two or three days; but when a man without power or place was robbed, however he might beg, the case would never be cracked, and even if it were, the goods could not all be recovered. The Bureau's chief reason for holding these units was to use them for arbitrary search and arrest, and to fasten fabricated charges and false evidence on political prisoners against whom there was no evidence, and take them in.

Besides, under the Bureau's control there was also the Ordnance Directorate's inspection office. This office had been set up early in the war, originally called the guard-inspection office, and was kept on after the victory under the changed name inspection office. Within the Bureau it was called the ordnance inspection office, to distinguish it from the garrison-command inspection offices. This office was set up solely to prevent Communist activity in the various arsenals and to suppress the arsenal workers. The Nationalists listed all arsenal workers as men on active service, forbade them to form unions, and readily "dealt with them by military law"; this office was charged solely with supervising the workers' activities. Every arsenal had a police-guard group and guard brigade; during the war Zhang Shi was the office head, and after the victory Liao Shiyang. Both were Communist turncoats who fancied they understood the Communists' ways, but except for persecuting no few workers they never rooted out a Communist organization in any arsenal.

The largest of the Bureau's peripheral organizations was the "China New Society Undertakings Construction Association." This organization had grown out of the "Military Affairs Commission People's Mobilization Committee" run by Dai Li in the war years. Dai Li was very ambitious and wanted to unify all the country's Green Gang, Hong Gang, Paoge, Gelao, and other organizations under his leadership, to strengthen the Juntong's peripheral forces. The committee members then invited to join this organization were Du Yuesheng, Tong Songpo, Mei Guangpei, Liu Zhilu, Yang Qingshan, Yang Hu, Lu Jingshi, Shi Xiaoxian, Tian Desheng, Fan Shaozeng, Tang Shaowu, Xu Liang, and others. When the founding congress was held in 1946 at the Lido Garden on Bubbling Well Road in Shanghai, representatives of every sort of scum hurried to attend, an event rare even in the old society. After Dai Li's death this organization was handed over to the great Juntong agent Xu Liang, and things changed. Xu could not lead the organization, and above all, in developing it, met attack and obstruction from the CC Clique, the Political Study Clique, and others. The main reason was that they feared that once the Bureau held the gang organizations everywhere, it would sway the elections for the National Assembly, the Legislature, and the like. Seeing the wind ill, Zheng Jiemin and Mao Renfeng, unwilling to offend all sides over the matter, let this organization quietly cease activity.

In its work, besides carrying on the whole of the Juntong's business, the Bureau also learned the American imperialists' set of rumour-and-propaganda methods, and set up in the Second Department a psychological-warfare section to run this work. The chief peripheral body and organization under this section was the Nanking Datong News Agency. This agency was headed by the old Juntong agent Liu Juju, with the Xifeng-special-training-class graduate Ding Kuanghua as chief editor, and specialized in manufacturing slander against the central authorities, the Soviet Union, and the socialist countries. When its field reporters went out to work, they did not gather any news but used the reporter's identity as a cover for secret-service activity. Its copy came from the psychological-warfare section and was put out through it. The psychological-warfare section's copy was concocted by taking scraps from the intelligence sent back by agents near the liberated areas and turning black into white and fabricating wildly.

At the time there was also a Hanchao News Agency in Wuhan doing the same tricks. And in Shanghai, the Northeast, Chungking, Guizhou, Kunming, and many other places, some such news agencies or papers were run one after another, the most shameless of them being the *Xinhua Times* run in Chungking.

The *Xinhua Daily* the Communists then published in Chungking won praise from people of all sides and had great political influence; though the agents had for years, day and night, thought up ways to strangle it, exhausting every base trick—smashing the office, beating the paper boys, refusing to carry it, having the post office impound it—the paper was never destroyed. On the contrary, the more they did so, the more people loved to read it and trusted it. At their wits' end, the Chungking criminal-police brigade commander (later, when it became the criminal-police office, its head) Tan Rongzhang volunteered, proposing to the Bureau to fight the Communists in propaganda, paper against paper. Mao Renfeng at once approved the proposal and promised support in money, materials, men, and copy. Over the paper's name the agents racked their brains, and at last decided on *Xinhua Times*, first to confuse the public, and second to show it meant to compete with the *Xinhua Daily*. Tan Rongzhang made himself the publisher, brought out his wife, the Juntong Qianyang-special-training-class woman agent Xue Shuhua, as president, took a Chen so-and-so who specialized in press-world activity as chief editor, and drew in some worthless literati and cartoonists. Besides allotting them premises one after another, the Juntong handed over the presses, workers, and all of the former April First printing works, fancying that with this paper's issue it could crush the *Xinhua Daily* in Chungking.

When the *Xinhua Times* came out, besides much loud blowing at every level, members of the criminal-police brigade went the rounds of the shops forcing merchants and townsfolk to subscribe. To slander the Communists, Tan Rongzhang specially sent men to the places the Red Army had passed on the Long March in western Sichuan to gather so-called material, digging open the grave mounds of the masses of innocent people (children included) the Nationalist troops had massacred in pursuit of the Red Army, photographing the heaps of white bones, and turning black into white to call it evidence that the Red Army had massacred the people. For all their scheming, they could not attain their aim at all; fewer and fewer read the paper, and it only exposed their ugly faces further.

By February 1947, when the Chungking *Xinhua Daily* was forced to suspend and withdraw, Tan Rongzhang went to Hongyan Village in his capacity as police criminal-office head to supervise the withdrawal. He was hugely pleased with himself then, thinking a thorn in his eye was gone. When he talked with some *Xinhua Daily* staff, he said in the most arrogant tone that henceforth he would go on competing in political propaganda, and set himself up as a victor. A *Xinhua Daily* staff member answered him: "If you compete by proper means, the Communists absolutely welcome it." "We are leaving Chungking for a while, but before long we shall be back." I happened then to be in Chungking on business, and Tan Rongzhang told me all this, and reckoned there was no rival to compete with any more. Tan Rongzhang hoped on the one hand to use the paper as a tool of extortion to get rich, and on the other as a propaganda tool for his wife's candidacy for the National Assembly, so the paper grew more and more vile, and in the end could not be sold even by forced peddling. Cut from a full sheet to a half, still no one read it, and in 1948 it had to be wound up.

Another of the Bureau's peripheral organizations was the Eastern Economic News Agency, taken over from the hands of the Japanese secret service. This agency, run by Deng Baoguang, worked on economic intelligence, put out copy as well, had branches in Beiping, Wuhan, Chungking, and other great cities, and was equipped with its own wireless, specializing in gathering economic intelligence. They also ran a *North China Economic Herald* in Beiping, run by Qin so-and-so.

### Bloody and Vicious Criminal Activities

In the two or three years after the Bureau of Secrets Preservation was founded, in anti-Communist activity Mao Renfeng always reckoned it had done better than in the Juntong period. What most pleased him was that he won ever greater reward from Chiang Kai-shek. The chief instances were:

(1) In 1946 in Shanghai it wrecked a Communist financial-and-trade organization in the northern-Jiangsu area, intercepted a small steam launch and a great deal of goods bound for the liberated areas, and arrested over ten underground workers. Among them were Zhang Yuan and Yin Lansu, who worked at the Shanghai maritime customs; both were killed.

(2) In 1947 in Beiping it wrecked a Communist underground radio, arresting the radio operator Li Zhengxuan and his wife the cipher clerk Zhang Houxiu, and the operator Xun Liangyu and his wife the cipher clerk Li Yuping; at the same time it rooted out several important underground-party cadres, the acting head (formerly a section head) of the Beiping municipal land-administration bureau, Dong Jianping, the Beiping Beiman Girls' School teacher Tian Zhongying, the Beijing University student Li Fuyi, and others. The detection of Communist radios then relied on detection stations densely deployed in the great cities. The wireless-inspection sections of the garrison-command inspection offices in various places did just this work. They first made a detailed register of the call signs, wavelengths, and communication times of all open radios; the moment a detection station found an unregistered station calling, it took bearings on its direction and position, gradually finding the station's location from far to near, and then carried out the search and arrest.

(3) Through the wrecking of the Beiping underground radio, at the same time there were rooted out within the Baoding Pacification Headquarters the staff-department head Xie Shiyan, the deputy head Ding Xing (the two, after being sent to Nanking, were killed at Yuhuatai), the military-justice-department head one Wang, and the pacification-headquarters design committee member Yu Xinqing, and others. Through these leads, Communist underground organizations and radios were then found in Xi'an and Lanzhou too. Chiang Kai-shek was overjoyed on getting the report, at once had the Air Force headquarters ready a plane, and Mao Renfeng sent the Second-Department head Ye Xiangzhi leading a great crowd of agents to fly overnight to Xi'an, where at the Qiyuan Teahouse on Yandian Street they arrested Mei Yonghe, Zhao Yaobin, and others, cracked a radio, and arrested a dozen underground workers. At the time even a secretary of Hu Zongnan's and a head of the Northwest News Agency he ran were implicated, giving Hu Zongnan a great shock. He had truly never expected there were Communists at his own side. When he heard Ye Xiangzhi say there were leads on the Lanzhou side too, he at once had Ye Xiangzhi take his own small plane and hurry there; unexpectedly the plane developed a fault over Pingliang and nearly killed Ye. Hu asked the Air Force to send another special plane to carry Ye from Pingliang to Lanzhou, where several Communist underground workers such as Yin Jiaming were arrested, and Ye Xiangzhi took one back to Nanking. Besides giving a bonus of ten thousand yuan, Chiang Kai-shek specially awarded Ye Xiangzhi and the Beiping field-office wireless-inspection-section head Zhao Rongde fourth-class Precious Tripod decorations. This case also implicated the Northeast Field Headquarters press-office commissioner Li Nian, the acting press-office head of the Shenyang Defence Command Wang Shucha, and Gong Tianmin and others. In the end it was even found that a daughter of Chen Bulei, who had long handled confidential work at Chiang Kai-shek's side, was a Communist underground-party member too, which greatly shocked and enraged Chiang Kai-shek. He again and again charged Mao Renfeng that he must, by every possible means, thoroughly root out the Communist underground-party members who had penetrated the various military and government departments, better to arrest and kill wrongly than to let a single one slip. Even Mao Renfeng felt this thorny and astonishing then; one noon, at lunch with the Bureau's department-head-rank agents, he said with much feeling: "We arrest like this, we kill like this, and still there are so many who dare come charging in with their heads in their hands—I really don't know why." No one present made a sound. How could these agents, who rode on the people's backs and lived amid wine and song, understand the great spirit of the Communists, who for the revolution feared not to shed their heads and blood, who faced death as a homecoming, who dared pull a tooth from the tiger's mouth!

(4) In 1948, the Chungking Pacification Headquarters Second-Department head Xu Yuanju, in close cooperation with the Bureau's Chungking station chief Lü Shikun, after wrecking the Chungking underground party's *Advance Herald* in Chungking, went on to wreck the organization of the Communist Chungking municipal committee. Through the defection of the Chungking municipal committee secretary Liu so-and-so, nearly a hundred underground workers in Chungking and other areas of Sichuan were arrested. Mao Renfeng valued this work especially, sent Ye Xiangzhi again to hurry to Chungking to take charge, and brought Liu so-and-so to Nanking to have him wreck the organization of the Communist Yangtze Bureau. Later, when I went from Kunming to Chungking, I met this turncoat, who was even ready to go with me to Kunming to wreck the Yunnan working committee's organization. By his account he could still recognize a few of the responsible people working in Yunnan and could help the Yunnan station in this activity. Later, because I went to Shanghai and then, on my return, to the Northwest and Chengtu, he did not go to Kunming with me.

After Mao Renfeng finished this case, he doubly praised Xu Yuanju, and besides winning him a decoration and giving him a bonus, decided to set up the Bureau's Southwest Special District, appointing Xu as its head, in charge of the work of the four southwestern provinces, so as to concentrate strength on wrecking the Communist underground-party organizations. At the same time Mao Renfeng sent another Communist turncoat, Wang so-and-so, to the Southwest Special District as a commissioner to help Xu Yuanju. Wang had been a section head of the Northeast Military Region staff department, defected after capture in the Siping battle, and had worked a while in the Bureau's special-political-problems research group. Mao Renfeng then hoped Wang could persuade the Communists captured in Sichuan to defect, but without result. Earlier, after Liu so-and-so defected, many he had led, though sold out by him, would not, like him, betray the organization and sell their souls in surrender to the enemy. Later these loyal, unbending, fine party members were all massacred before liberation in Zhazidong detention house.

In late October 1949, the Juntong killed at Daping in Chungking the Communist Sichuan provincial committee's Pu Huafu, the Chungking municipal committee's Wang Pu, and Tu Xiaoyi, Liu Guozhi, Cheng Shanmou, Deng Xingbang, and others, ten in all. Then it secretly killed over thirty more at the old radio-station site within the former SACO grounds, among them Jiang Zhujun (Sister Jiang). The martyrs killed in the massacre of 27 November were: Xu Xiaoxuan, Chen Ran, He Xuesong, Li Qinglin, He Jingping, Shen Diqun, Zhang Xianhua, Yang Ao, Huang Xiansheng, Li Chenglin, Han Zizhong, Li Youlin, Zhou Junshi, Wang Baiyu, Zhou Conghua, Yan Jimin, Zhang Xingmin, and others; together with those formerly imprisoned in the New World Hotel—Wang Yaxi, Ai Zhonghuang, Shan Benshan, Zhong Qi, and others—they came to over three hundred. Besides, at the Twelve Bridges in Chengtu they killed Xu Shouzhen, Yang Bokai, Yu Yuan, Jiang Qianliang, Peng Daixi, Liu Zhongxuan, and others, over thirty, making a massacre of a cruelty beyond human bounds that shocked the world.

Apart from the above, the criminal activities of the Bureau of Secrets Preservation and of Mao Renfeng were many more.

Before liberation, the armed revolutionary activity of the Revolutionary Committee of the Kuomintang in the southwest was of considerable force and momentum. Chiang Kai-shek felt very uneasy about the people's armed-uprising activity in the rear, and it too was hard to guard against. Besides charging the local garrisons everywhere to "mop up" at any time, he ordered the Bureau to do all it could to wreck and prevent it. Within the documents of the Revolutionary Committee's armed organization that the Southwest Special District found one after another in the Huaying Mountain area of Sichuan, it discovered among the chief people engaged in these activities the former Nationalist Army War College president Yang Jie, and prepared to gather the material and arrest and interrogate Yang in Chungking. Because Yang Jie had many personnel ties within the reactionary forces, no few Nationalist generals being his students, Chiang Kai-shek held that if this man were not removed it would be greatly to the Nationalists' disadvantage.

Besides keeping close watch on Yang, the agents bought over his secretary and threatened a nurse who was to marry Yang to work for them. Later Yang Jie found out and secretly fled to Kunming, entrusting himself to Lu Han. But Chiang Kai-shek did not let Yang Jie go for having left Chungking, and had Mao Renfeng order me again and again (I was then chief of the Bureau's Yunnan station) to assassinate Yang in Kunming, to remove this thorn in his side. For various reasons I put it off and off, and did not carry it out. In September 1949 Yang Jie fled from Kunming to Hong Kong. When Mao Renfeng found Yang's connections in Hong Kong, he sent Ye Xiangzhi leading the agent Han Shichang and five others from Taiwan to assassinate Yang in Hong Kong. But the Revolutionary Committee's armed forces in the southwest did not cease activity for this; on the contrary, they grew ever more active. Mao Renfeng took several scoldings from Chiang Kai-shek over it.

Mao Renfeng also spent no little thought on defection-inducement work. The Bureau's Second Department had a special inducement-to-defect section, fancying it could induce the PLA and defected units to turn. Besides setting up defection stations in Kaifeng, Zhengzhou, Qingdao, and elsewhere, the Second Department also sent its deputy head Huang Xigong, together with the Bureau Design Committee chairman Zhang Yanfo, to the Wuhan "Peace and Patriotism Corps" to pick men, secretly train them, and send them to areas near liberation to carry on plots. The members of the "Peace and Patriotism Corps" were all captured PLA officers and civilian personnel; the Bureau picked those with more personnel connections, brought them back to Nanking, trained them, and sent them out again. But once sent out, these men never induced a single PLA unit to turn to the Nationalist side.

In defection-inducement work, the case Mao Renfeng strove hardest to claim credit for before Chiang Kai-shek was the inducement of Hao Shanju. Many sides were then working on it. Xue Yue said he had brought it off single-handed; Mao Renfeng insisted it was his own decisive role, through his tie with Wu Huawen. When Wu Huawen defected, Mao Renfeng, sure he could induce Wu to turn back, ended by getting nothing but a scolding from Chiang Kai-shek.

In defection-inducement work the Bureau also did many a losing deal. In inducing Niu Zilong of the Henan Military Region, for instance, it lost both the man it sent and the radio along with him. Niu Zilong had once worked in the Juntong, and, his words and acts sympathetic to the Communists being found out, was held in the Juntong's Northwest detention house in Xi'an. In the detention house Niu made contact with a few trusted men, and when the guards were off guard seized their weapons, killed several, escaped the prison, and joined the PLA. Mao Renfeng thought that, on the strength of the old tie, he need only not pursue the prison-break charge but promise office and money, and could induce Niu to turn. In the end, once the man and the radio were sent, they went and never came back. Such things happened several times, but Mao Renfeng would not give up. He always held that one success would yield ten thousandfold. Right up to the winter of 1948 he was still actively at these activities. After 1949 he no longer cared to spend effort on them; he knew the game was lost and no one would leave the light for the dark again.

Sending armed secret-service troops to attack the liberated areas took a new turn in the Bureau period. Now that Chiang Kai-shek had launched the all-out civil war, Zheng Jiemin and Mao Renfeng sent the traffic-police corps they held to the various fronts, batch after batch, to take direct part in the war against the people. At first Mao Renfeng feared these troops could not fight, but in the two battles to stop the PLA liberating Datong and Baotou he found the armed agents played some part, and grew bolder and bolder. But once several corps under Zhang Guozhen, Xiong Jiandong, and others were wholly wiped out by the PLA, Mao Renfeng was heartsick and somewhat panicked; yet Chiang Kai-shek, in his death struggle, still kept throwing these troops into the field. In the fighting on Shanghai's outskirts the Second, Fifth, Sixth, Eleventh, Twelfth, Fifteenth, Eighteenth, and Water corps were all thrown in; in the end, apart from two or three broken corps his trusted man Zhou Wenxin took to Taiwan, almost all were wiped out, and the last remnant, under the acting commander Li Tiefu, laid down its arms and turned to the people.

Zhou Weilong, who became traffic-police directorate head in early 1949, had always been very ambitious. After grabbing this post by every means—above all through colluding with Mao Renfeng's wife, Xiang Yingxin—he would no longer take Mao Renfeng's orders but flew his own flag. He most opposed throwing the traffic-police corps into frontline fighting, and hoped to keep more strength in reserve. Then Tang Shengming was secretly inducing Nationalist armed units in Hunan to defect, and once in touch with Zhou, Zhou contrived to shift the traffic-police corps slowly to Hunan to concentrate, sending Wang Chunhui and five other corps there one after another. When he even wanted to pull out the units guarding Chiang Kai-shek at Fenghua, Mao Renfeng found out and at once reported to Chiang Kai-shek, and Zhou Weilong was arrested in the International Hotel in Shanghai. Among the letters found in the search, ties with Tang Shengming were discovered. Mao Renfeng had at first meant to arrest Tang Shengming at once, but because Hunan was then brewing a peace movement, he instead sent the agents Sun Kun, Jiao Yuyin, and others to Changsha to assassinate Tang. The agents once fired a shot at him near his residence but missed. When Cheng Qian led his troops in the Changsha uprising, those who had worked in Hunan—Zhang Yanfo, Liu Renkui, Liu Rendao, and others—all took part. Fearing other agents would follow suit, Mao Renfeng had Jiao Yuyin and others assassinate Liu Renshan, who had temporarily become Changsha police chief after taking part in the Hunan uprising, at his home at No. 22 Liuyangmen Zhengjie in Changsha. After the assassins escaped they were reassigned to Yunnan, and told me of this course of events.

During the War of Liberation, to suppress the democratic patriotic movements everywhere, the Bureau specially held two training classes for judicial personnel. The reason for setting up this class was chiefly to study how, after the Nationalist constitution was promulgated, the Bureau could on the surface not violate the constitution and yet carry out the Juntong's intentions at will, arresting revolutionaries and blocking the people's activity. So after this class was founded, the judicial personnel privately called it the "law-breaking training class." By early 1948, when Chiang Kai-shek promulgated the "Organizational Regulations for Special Criminal Courts," he kicked the sham things like the constitution's guarantee of personal freedom clean away, and the agents pressed their palms in congratulation, and no longer studied the means to get round the "constitution" but carried on, as undisguisedly as before, their arrests, searches, interrogations, and other deeds harmful to the people by the old set of methods.

During the War of Liberation, besides doing directly a great many criminal deeds—wrecking Communist organizations, massacring Communist party members, suppressing democratic patriotic movements, arresting and imprisoning revolutionaries in large numbers, and carrying out great destruction and great massacres in the various great cities—the Bureau could never open up intelligence work in the liberated areas. Mao Renfeng was often scolded by Chiang Kai-shek over this; after each dressing-down, he would as usual issue instructions and orders to the field units, demanding they truly find ways to strengthen this work.

I remember Mao Renfeng then thought the work done comparatively well by the North China Work Directing Corps led by the Catholic priest Raymond de Jaegher. Using Catholic ties, they had believers in or near the liberated areas engage in this work, gathering no little, often several times as much intelligence as many of the provincial stations near the liberated areas obtained.

After Hu Zongnan took Yenan, the Bureau, to get intelligence, at once set up a group in Yenan, sending Cui Hangbin to be inspection-office head at Yenan. Chiang Kai-shek was then anxious to know the whereabouts of Chairman Mao, the Communist Party Central Committee, and the Eighth Route Army headquarters. He instructed Mao Renfeng that no cost of men or money was too great, that whatever the price, he must find out where the Communist leaders were. In those days Chiang Kai-shek asked Mao Renfeng daily whether there was any intelligence. But the inspection office, apart from catching in the neighbourhood of Yenan a few old, sick, and crippled people unable to move, could find no clue at all. Mao Renfeng was in a great sweat too, holding this a fine chance to capture the Communist leaders alive; again and again he sternly demanded the Shaanxi station strengthen the Yenan group's work and, whatever the cost, satisfy Chiang Kai-shek's demand. Hu Zongnan then telegraphed Mao Renfeng too, asking him to strengthen this activity especially. Driven to eat and sleep in disquiet, Mao Renfeng talked of the matter daily at lunch and instructed the Shaanxi station by urgent telegram without cease. He said very angrily: "Our work cannot coordinate with the military victory, and fails the leader's hopes. To let slip a chance like this in a thousand years is really inexcusable." Right up to October 1949, when Mao Renfeng talked with me of this work, he had to admit that in the underground struggle with the Communists the Bureau had failed.

From the founding of the Bureau, Mao Renfeng had wanted to walk Dai Li's old road and hoped to get direct American support, joining hands with the American secret service once more. Not long after Dai Li's death, when the former SACO deputy director, the American secret-service chief Miles, came specially from America to Nanking to mourn this friend, Mao Renfeng had shown this wish. Miles had then promised to make some more effort on the Communist question. Later, to reward the great Juntong agents who had helped SACO's work, the Americans specially gave Zheng Jiemin, Tang Zong, Mao Renfeng, Pan Qiwu, Li Chongshi, Tao Yishan, six men, a Freedom Medal each, and both Zheng Jiemin and Mao Renfeng, at the reception welcoming the American special envoy who conferred the medals, again voiced their wish for American support. But Sino-American secret-service cooperation, for reasons unknown, was long slow to materialize. Only after the whole mainland was liberated and the Bureau fled to Taiwan did the American secret service come tardily, setting up a Sino-American Office headed by Liu Zhenfang, who had been SACO's chief interpreter. The American side sent over twenty American agents who had worked in SACO to Taiwan.

In the autumn of 1948 Chiang Kai-shek specially allowed the Bureau to add 2880 to its establishment and budget. These over two thousand men were mostly placed in the newly formed Technical Corps, headed by Du Changcheng, which specialized in destruction at the moment the great cities were liberated. Before the Technical Corps was formed, a blasting-personnel training class had been run in Suzhou, training men specially for over three months. Besides taking part in laying mines in the river and on its banks to stop the PLA crossing the Yangtze, this corps joined in the great destruction before the withdrawals from Nanking and Shanghai. Before the retreat and flight from Chungking, the Bureau set up in Chungking a "Destruction Command," also called the "Factory-wrecking Office," with staff, technical, and transport groups, to wreck the arsenals, power plants, and warehouses of Chungking. During the war the Nationalists had built a dozen arsenals in and around Chungking; fearing they would fall into PLA hands, Chiang Kai-shek decided to have Mao Renfeng send men to wreck them thoroughly. This command was run by the ordnance inspection-office head Liao Zongze and the Bureau's Southwest Special District head Xu Yuanju, with Du Changcheng leading the Technical Corps agents in the technical work; in November 1949 they wrecked one by one the important equipment of the Dadukou Steelworks, the Longxi River hydro-power plant at Changshou, the arsenals at Xigongyan on the south bank, and others, and no few workers died heroically defending their plants. When the PLA crossed Guizhou and pushed straight for Chungking, Mao Renfeng even had Du Changcheng send men to wreck the Qijiang bridge. When two trucks loaded with explosives and a great crowd of agents hurried to the Qi River, the bridge had already been taken by the PLA's advance units, who killed two agents; the rest abandoned the trucks and fled back in a rout. At last they decided to wreck the Chungking Daxigou power plant, but the whole plant's workers rose as one to protect it and fought stoutly, and the agents could not get the explosives into the plant, so the Chungking power plant was preserved intact. Before Guangzhou's liberation, Mao Renfeng sent this corps' men to Guangzhou to blow up the Pearl River bridge. In Hong Kong they also sank the steamer *Zesheng*, about to sail for liberated Shanghai. Seeing this corps could play some part in destruction, Chen Cheng had Mao Renfeng put it all under his own command, renaming it the Southeast Field Headquarters Technical Corps, and specially allowed all its two-thousand-odd agents to go to Taiwan.

In the winter of 1948, the Bureau also prepared to form an Action Corps as huge as the Technical Corps, to strengthen the suppression of the surging democratic patriotic movements in the great and medium cities of the rear, charged solely with arresting and massacring patriots. Chiang Kai-shek approved Mao Renfeng's plan and agreed to add another 2000 to the establishment and budget. So in early November Mao Renfeng transferred me to Nanking to relieve Ye Xiangzhi, and, while preparing the plan to assassinate Li Zongren, had me also prepare to form this corps. From the several hundred men the personnel department offered, I picked fewer than fifty, since most were unfit for or unwilling to take on this work.

At the time some senior agents, feeling pessimistic and despairing over the war's prospects, wavered no little. As early as the spring of 1948 the intelligence-department head He Zhiyuan took long leave to Taiwan, meaning to change trades and become a merchant. Though Mao Renfeng cursed He Zhiyuan for a "coward," there was no way to keep him. Then the Shanghai deputy police chief Zhang Shi asked to resign and go to Taiwan to run a farm. Once the news spread, more of the moneyed senior agents planned for their own way out. They wanted to leave the mainland early, and if they could not go abroad to live idle, hoped at least to go to Taiwan first.

What most astonished Mao Renfeng was that the head of the Shanghai Relief Administration's motor-management office, Yu Lexing (also called Yu Zengsheng), had actually made contact with the Communist underground party and sheltered an underground radio at his home on Changyuan Road. Mao Renfeng was terribly afraid of Juntong agents leaving the dark for the light, and was bent on arresting and killing Yu Lexing to make an example. When he ordered the Shanghai inspection-office agents to arrest Yu, the office's section- and sub-section-head-rank agents were mostly Yu's students, and secretly telephoned to warn him to flee. Mao Renfeng flew into a rage and gave the inspection-office head Huang Jiachi a severe scolding.

The constant recurrence of such things had a great effect on some middle- and lower-ranking agents too. So some people I thought quite fit for action work were now begging off on one pretext or another. Later the personnel department told me there was still a batch of unemployed men in Shanghai and Suzhou, and had me go and pick some. I went to Shanghai to talk with them, but again most hoped to work in the southwest or other rear areas. Seeing this, I did not want to go to Suzhou either, but went back to Nanking to prepare solely the assassination of Li Zongren, and the forming of the Action Corps was shelved.

But before the withdrawal from Nanking, Mao Renfeng arranged one more mass arrest. He had received Chiang Kai-shek's instruction that the military and government notables still in Nanking near its liberation must be meaning to turn to the Communists after liberation; besides first advising them to leave early, those who had not left by the time must be arrested wholesale. The Nanking Garrison Command inspection office and the Capital Police criminal-police corps both received this order. But by the time the Bureau fled Nanking and set up offices in Shanghai and Fuzhou, preparing to move to Taiwan, the senior agents left in Nanking were all busy preparing their own flight. They feared on the one hand they could not get away at liberation, and on the other that once Li Zongren took power the Guangxi-clique agents would arrest them. So once Chiang Kai-shek announced his retirement, the senior agents scrambled to flee toward Shanghai, and Mao Renfeng could not stop them; his arrest plan came to nothing.

After I brought the action agents and a large batch of weapons from Nanking to Shanghai, I asked Mao Renfeng what to do with the weapons. He told me that apart from the part I wished to take to Kunming, I was to store the rest somehow in Shanghai, ready to be brought out and used after Shanghai's liberation. Following his instruction, I bought one large and one small coffin, carried them to a temporary detention house the Bureau had in Nanshi in Shanghai, had the boards at the back of the large coffin taken out and refitted as a small door easier to open, packed in ten Thompson submachine guns, each with two full magazines, sealed the lid with a great hook when done, hired a truck to load the large coffin, and sent it to the Siming Guild Hall where coffins were kept, paid two years' storage, and then nailed to the front of the coffin a wooden tablet reading "Spirit-tablet of the venerable elder Tang Xiangxiang"—meaning Thompson submachine gun. Such places for keeping coffins of the dead had few visitors by day and none at night, so it was fairly ideal for storing weapons. In the small coffin I packed twenty Mausers and Brownings, and the next day hired another small vehicle and sent it to a burial ground near Hongqiao airfield, where it was buried, and set up a stone tablet reading "Grave of the girl Mao Ling"—meaning Mauser and Browning. Once all was in place, I photographed the terrain, bearings, and roads nearby. The remaining ten shell-Mausers I packed in a large wooden crate and, through someone, stored under the name of the "Deji" hardware store in a warehouse at Shiliupu. I told Mao Renfeng where these were stored and handed him the photographs and receipts. After liberation, when I reported the above to the relevant authorities, before long they told me only the guns and ammunition stored in the warehouse had been recovered; the place keeping the large coffin had been turned into a factory workshop, and unclaimed coffins had been buried; the burial ground where the small coffin lay had been turned into a vegetable garden, and the "Grave of Mao Ling" could not be found.

Before Shanghai's liberation, Mao Renfeng had the agents carry out one more great massacre, great destruction, and great robbery. The Shanghai police chief Mao Sen carried out Mao Renfeng's massacre policy; the revolutionaries he killed one after another whose names I can still remember are: the liberation Nanking-Shanghai special commissioner Liu Junsheng, the staff officer Huang Peizhong, the liaison man Chen Yushan, the intelligence-group head Zhang Wei, the intelligence agent Yang Jianmin, and besides the Ninth-Sector staff officer Tang Xinmin, five in all. The patriotic democrats Mao Sen killed were the chairman of the Revolutionary Committee's Nanking branch, Meng Shiheng, and its propaganda member Wu Shiwen; besides, a former Nationalist gendarme who had become a Revolutionary Committee liaison man, Xiao Jiankui, was killed along with them. There were also people of some other democratic parties, such as Chen Tilu, Zhang Dasheng, Fang Zhinong, Zhu Datong, Wang Wenzong, and several dozen others. Communist underground-party members arrested and killed numbered close to a hundred. Some who traded a few silver dollars were killed too, over ten of them, on the trumped-up charges of wrecking the market or carrying out underground activity for the Communists. Those later killed openly at Songgong Park and elsewhere, already seen in the papers of the day, I shall not list one by one. In short, on the eve of Shanghai's liberation the people the agents killed were very many, beyond reckoning.

As for wrecking Shanghai's public utilities, because Tang Enbo still meant to fight a street battle in Shanghai with the PLA, it was not carried out. Only after liberation were planes sent to bomb the Yangshupu and other power plants.

What most displeased and grieved Mao Renfeng was that his plan for robbery and extortion did not come off. In February 1949, when he talked of it with me and Xu Yuanju and others in Kunming, he was still cursing the Shanghai inspection-office head Huang Jiachi for being too useless and disobeying his orders. When Shanghai was besieged he had told the garrison-command inspection office to make ready for robbery. He held that if the goods were not taken away, they would fall into Communist hands anyway. By his plan, at least several tens of thousands of taels of gold and a great deal of dollars could have been grabbed, enough for a year or two of the Bureau's funds. Because Huang Jiachi's preparations were not thorough beforehand, not only could the moneyed capitalists, who had gone into hiding, not be found, but where their money was kept could not be made out either. In the end, apart from what the agents each grabbed by their own devices on the spot, the planned large-scale robbery could not be carried out.

Around the liberation of Nanking and Shanghai, the Bureau, carrying out Chiang Kai-shek's order, developed the killing of revolutionaries and innocent people to a still more appalling degree. Chiang Kai-shek's instruction to Mao Renfeng then was: "In the past, because we killed too few, and did not kill off some of those who opposed us, we made more and more people unafraid of us. Henceforth only by killing more can we retrieve this situation unfavourable to us." Mao Renfeng faithfully carried out this instruction of Chiang Kai-shek's, explaining again and again to the Bureau's senior agents that this method was the wisest, and demanding they all take it to heart; so the later it got, the more they killed and the more cruelly.

When I was in Nanking in the winter of 1948, it happened that Chiang Kai-shek was having Mao Renfeng send men to Beiping to assassinate He Siyuan, who was working for peace. Mao Renfeng found the Second-Department head Ye Xiangzhi, who ran this business, and the special-technical research group head Liu Shaofu and me, and the four of us studied it twice over. At first we meant to assassinate He with pistols near his home when he went out, but considering that would make the assassins' escape hard, we later decided to place a time bomb in his home, so that after the explosion the killer could get away at leisure. To be safe, Mao Renfeng specially had Ye Xiangzhi lead four agents by plane to Beiping, notifying the agents still there beforehand to help. Later, when they failed to kill Mr. He Siyuan but killed his daughter by mistake, Chiang Kai-shek was furious and gave Mao Renfeng a scolding. Mao Renfeng then blamed Ye Xiangzhi for not ambushing on the road as he had meant, and, out of concern for the killer's safety, spoiling the great matter. Mao Renfeng said sacrificing a man or two to exchange for one He Siyuan was always worth it.

In 1949, when Zhang Zhizhong, Shao Lizi, Liu Fei, Qu Wu, and others went to Beiping and would not go back, Chiang Kai-shek's hatred reached the extreme, and he told Mao Renfeng he must find a way to kill one or two. But by then even Mao Renfeng felt powerless, though he found it awkward to refuse Chiang's order. Right up to September 1949, when he went to Kunming, he was still studying with me how to do this work with any assurance. There were then two men in the Bureau who specialized in assassination work, whom I had brought from Nanking to Kunming. Mao Renfeng even asked them what social connections they had in Beiping. They too thought going to Beiping offered no assurance, so they were not sent. In talking of this, Mao Renfeng said again and again: "The old man is very displeased that we've not killed these few; every time he asks me, I always say it's still in preparation, and now the more I look the less can be done. Coming here"—he sighed—"I feel our work grows worse and worse!" For all that, in the autumn of that year Chiang Kai-shek still ordered Mao Renfeng to carry out a massacre of a cruelty beyond human bounds.

Chiang Kai-shek first had Mao Renfeng kill in Chungking General Yang Hucheng, imprisoned for years, sparing not one of his family. In October he had Mao Renfeng kill thirty-two Communists captured in the southwest, killing them near the former Juntong radio main station east of SACO in Chungking. Just before Chungking's liberation, he had the Bureau kill together the three batches held in the two detention houses of Baigongguan and Zhazidong within SACO, and in the New World Hotel detention house of the Chungking Garrison Command's counter-defence office at Xiaoyanzi, about three hundred in all—this being the "November 27" (27 November) Chungking massacre case.

Before carrying out this massacre, Chiang Kai-shek instructed Mao Renfeng: all Communist party members, democratic figures, and suspects arrested were to be killed, and even Communists released earlier on bail were to be killed alike. At the time the Communist Yang Hanxiu, niece of the Chungking mayor and garrison commander Yang Sen, had already been bailed out by Yang Sen, but she too was killed by the police criminal-office head Zhang Mingxuan, who led agents in person and killed her in a cave on Geleshan. Of the thirty-odd held in Baigongguan, transferred there from the Juntong's Xifeng prison, most had been held seven or eight, or a dozen, years. Such as General Zhang Xueliang's old subordinate the deputy army commander Huang Xiansheng, held over ten years; and four middle-school students who had strayed into the SACO grounds during the war, been branded Communist suspects, and held over ten years—all were killed too. Not only that: when Mao Renfeng had the Bureau's judge Xu Zhongqi clear out the Baigongguan prisoners, even a Juntong veteran temporarily imprisoned there, the police criminal-office supervisor Chen Weicheng (held a few months for breaching Juntong discipline, ordinarily a matter that would blow over), was this time likewise put in for execution by signed request, and Chiang Kai-shek approved it as usual.

The massacre began on 25 November, first killing the prisoners of Baigongguan. To avoid alarming the prisoners in the nearby Zhazidong (Zhazidong) detention house, it was decided to use knives and pistols. The first that morning, they called out General Huang Xiansheng, luring him to the hills near Buyun Bridge about half a li from Baigongguan, and while Huang was walking a killer shot him in the back of the head and killed him. The rest too were lured out one by one and knifed to death in a gully near Buyun Bridge. The woman Communist Li Jianshuang had borne a child in prison, then not yet a year old. When she was lured out and entered the killing ground and saw so many corpses moaning in pools of blood, knowing she could not escape, she begged to keep the child, but the killer snatched the child and dashed it to the ground. Li flung herself at the killer to fight to the death; another killer beside him cut her across the waist with a knife and felled her, yet she still struggled toward the child to stroke the half-dead infant dashed to the ground. That day, after thirty-odd were thus lured out and killed one by one, the detention-house head Lu Jingqing at once sought Mao Renfeng, clamouring to fly to Taiwan immediately, for even he had grown afraid. He said: "Killing people like this, if the Communists catch us later, can we come out alive?"

The nearly one hundred imprisoned in the New World Hotel detention house had all been arrested during 1949, mostly suspects; of the thirty-odd said to be sentenced to death, only a few, such as Wang Yaxi (a woman), were Communists. This time it took no account at all of who was or was not a Communist; Mao Renfeng notified Yang Sen to kill them all. This batch was taken on the 26th to SACO for execution. The agents deceived them that they were being moved to another place of imprisonment, and these people all brought their luggage; but on reaching the silk-reeling-factory killing ground and seeing the great pit freshly dug, they all knew they had no way to live, and no few shouted "Long live the Communist Party!" before execution. In a burst of wild gunfire their lives ended. Some, not hit in a vital spot, were thrown into the pit and buried alive.

On the 27th, in massacring the more than a hundred in Zhazidong detention house, they locked all the cell doors, then fired Thompson submachine guns in through the doors and window openings; after one or two volleys, they doused the place with petrol and burned it, house and all. The few not shot dead were burned alive inside. Those imprisoned in this detention house were mostly Communists and members of the democratic parties and the Democratic League arrested during 1948 and 1949 by the Bureau's Southwest Special District and the Southwest Field Headquarters Second Department, most of them fine comrades who through every threat and inducement would not turn traitor to the enemy.

The Zhazidong (Zhazidong) detention-house head Li Lei fled before Chungking's liberation. After the other agents finished the massacre within SACO, under the direction of the Bureau's General Affairs Department head Cheng Xichao they set fire to several great warehouses full of weapons and materials and burned them, and only then fled.

In Chengtu, Mao Renfeng notified the Sichuan provincial chairman Wang Lingji to shoot together at the Twelve Bridges in Chengtu a dozen Communists and democratic figures arrested by the Sichuan special conference. Mao Renfeng had also meant to kill a large batch of the four hundred-odd arrested in Kunming, but because Lu Han firmly refused it was not done. This he counted his greatest regret.

From 1948 on, the Bureau's schemes and measures had mainly the following several items.

The first was undercover deployment. Before 1948, the Bureau's aim had been to send agents into the liberated areas to build organizations, but it failed repeatedly and was often scolded by Chiang Kai-shek. After 1948, as the great and medium cities formerly held by the Nationalists were liberated one after another, it adopted the method of deploying undercover organizations in advance, ready to resume activity at once after liberation. The method of deploying the undercover agent organization then was to set up in Nanking an all-purpose training class, run by the wireless-department head Yang Zhenyi, to intensively train a batch of so-called all-purpose intelligence agents. The members of this class were all operators trained in wireless transmitting and receiving who could handle radio work independently; after intelligence-gathering training they were sent to live in the great cities of the front, told first to find a cover profession, to have no lateral tie with the Bureau's former stations and groups, and to be led directly by the Bureau's undercover-deployment group.

Later a two-layer deployment was also adopted, taking in a batch of new personnel in the great and medium cities. The condition for this work was to have a proper profession, such as doctor, nurse, teacher, accountant, engineer, and the like. Among them, those most suitable were those who had some kinship or friendship with the agents yet had not been found to have any dealings with them. Every great city was to set up one or two such groups, the group head generally a Juntong agent whose identity was not exposed. Each group had one or two radios. The pay of undercover agents was reckoned in gold, several times higher than ordinary agents'. A group head who was also radio operator could get about two taels of gold a month, rent and allowance included. In areas about to be liberated, half a year's or a year's funds could be issued at once. Ordinary members' monthly pay ranged from three to five or six mace of gold. Before liberation they only did some preparatory work, not formal activity. The undercover groups' chief task was to gather intelligence after liberation. Chiang Kai-shek most hoped these men could work their way into government organs after liberation, and above all into the Communist Party. So these agents ordinarily acted very "left" and worked very hard, for only thus could they win trust. The Bureau also deployed some individuals undercover in the liberated areas to specialize in destruction and rumour-mongering. These people generally acted alone, formed no groups, were not given radios, but relied on the individual for their reactionary activity, having no lateral tie with the undercover groups. The radios the undercover groups used were generally two-and-a-half-watt American-made special-agent sets, hidden by various means. Some were fitted in the back half of an office-desk drawer, some concealed in radios, gramophones, table lamps, and the like. Batteries too were generally issued for half a year to a year. The reactionaries then reckoned that within a year all the liberated great and medium cities could be retaken one after another with the American imperialists' direct help, so all planned at most for a year.

Undercover deployment in the southwest did not formally begin until after April 1949. In that year, in some month, I went to Chungking to attend a meeting Mao Renfeng convened of the heads of the southwest and northwest regions. After the meeting I was sent to Shanghai to draw funds from the Bureau office, and only on my return did I bring back the instructions and regulations for deploying the undercover organization in the southwest. At that time they thought the southwest could still serve as a base for counter-attack and would not soon be liberated. But by that summer, feeling even the southwest could hardly be held, they hastily sent the Bureau's undercover-deployment group second-section head Ren Hongchuan to Chungking, concurrently as the Southwest Special District's undercover-deployment commissioner, to guide the provincial stations in deployment.

Ren Hongchuan and the Chungking station chief Li Xiukai, after first making deployments in Chungking and eastern Sichuan, reached Kunming only in July, where they set up one undercover group and one independent undercover station. For the undercover group I found two doctors through an introduction, one named Ming Shaowu. Learning that Ming Shaowu wanted to open a private clinic, Ren Hongchuan promised him at once a thousand Yunnan half-dollar coins. Mao Renfeng then meant to gather such talents with heavy money, so they might serve the Nationalists after liberation. Ren Hongchuan, after hastily deploying in Kunming, went on to Guiyang. By the Bureau's rules, besides the one or two groups to be deployed in Kunming, one group each was to be set up in Baoshan and Zhaotong in Yunnan. But communications being difficult, Ren Hongchuan would not wait long and left first.

In September, Mao Renfeng, feeling the deployment of undercover groups in the southwest still not ideal, and with some of the undercover groups in north, east, and central China being cracked one after another after liberation, set up, to strengthen deployment in the southwest and restore the organizations in the liberated areas, an all-purpose intelligence-personnel training class in the silk-reeling factory in Chungking. This class, run by Mao Renfeng concurrently with Wang Puchen as deputy, was to train several hundred undercover agents for dispatch everywhere. But no sooner had training begun than the PLA was marching on the southwest; with these men not yet trained, they had to move to Chengtu, and ended up scattered in disorder on the road. On reaching Chengtu, knowing there was no time to train at leisure, they folded these men into the traffic-police first brigade under He Longqing, meaning to have them fight as guerrillas.

While deploying undercover groups, the Bureau also made some arrangements specially for assassination and destruction in the great and medium cities after liberation. Of other places I do not know; I know only something of the Shanghai situation. This kind of arrangement was to store pistols and explosives beforehand in reliable places easy to get at, not in the homes of those doing the destruction and assassination, but to be fetched by men sent when needed.

The second was to launch a "back-to-the-villages movement." In the spring of 1949, when most of the country was liberated, agents who had fled from various places poured into the rear. Having heard Chiang Kai-shek's deceitful propaganda for years, not one was unafraid, and no few hoped to go to Taiwan early. Seeing this, Mao Renfeng asked Chiang Kai-shek for instructions, and the reply was that except for a few senior agents allowed to go to Taiwan when necessary, the rest should stay behind to go on struggling with the Communists to the end. On this instruction, Mao Renfeng convened a meeting in Chungking in March attended by the responsible agents of the southwest, northwest, Guangxi, Hunan, and other provinces. Mao put forward this method: each was to return to his own home area to carry on activity singly, contriving to gain a footing locally.

This method on the one hand could send large numbers of agents to every corner to go on with anti-Communist activity, and on the other could reduce the personnel awaiting orders crowded in the rear cities—in fact it meant no longer taking responsibility for placing them. For as the area shrank day by day and the men grew day by day, there was truly no way to place them. This method that killed several birds with one stone Chiang Kai-shek much approved.

In announcing this method, Mao Renfeng required those engaged in the back-to-the-villages movement to hold out in their home areas for a year or two, telling them to believe Chiang Kai-shek could soon bring his troops back with the American imperialists' help, that they need only bear a short time to be given important use. Mao Renfeng also instructed them that, on going to the liberated areas or on being liberated later, they might frankly account to the Communists for their past history and need not hide their identity; but one thing to note was that they must not give up the organization, must above all not inform on other agents, and must not expose others' crimes. Anyone who did not observe this would not only be punished later but might be disciplined by men sent at once. Mao further told them they might confess some historical matters, so as to win trust further. If they could get work in an organ, men would naturally be sent to make contact then; before contact was made they might act as circumstances allowed. Destruction they were sure of they must carry out, and on success might flee to the rear or Taiwan for reward. Those going back to unliberated areas were first to gain a footing, contrive to grasp local militia forces, and, when sure, join the guerrillas; when liberation came, if there was no other way, they might join an uprising, so as to work their way into the PLA.

Though Mao Renfeng's methods were thought out very carefully and his demands not too strict, those willing to do the back-to-the-villages work were pitifully few. These people could not shake off their fear of the Communists and all dreaded returning to the liberated areas. For the Nationalists, on the one hand doing all they could to spread slander about how the liberated areas killed people, and on the other encouraging them to go back, was more than anyone could believe. Though no one opposed Mao Renfeng's proposal then, it was never carried out.

At that meeting Mao Renfeng also meant to use the Juntong agents' ties in society to form an open political group to carry on anti-Communist activity in the rear. Many, mindful of the lesson of the "New Society Undertakings Construction Association," which had had to cease activity through obstruction from the Nationalists themselves, disapproved of the method, feeling the back-to-the-villages measure might yet yield some slight effect.

The third was preparing to wage guerrilla war and turn bandit in the southwest. At the meeting he convened in Chungking, Mao Renfeng required each provincial station head to draw up at once a "contingency plan," telling everyone to make full preparation for the great destruction, great arrests, and great massacre before liberation, and to prepare quickly to take to the hills as bandits. What he spoke of somewhat concretely was guerrilla war, the main strength to rely on being He Longqing's two traffic-police first-brigade corps. The Guizhou station chief Chen Shixian said he and She Wanxuan could form one or two corps. On the Yunnan side there were then some traffic police who could make up a corps. Adding the guard troops of the dozen-odd arsenals in the southwest, plus some open units and undercover organizations the agents held, gathering tens of thousands was no problem.

Mao Renfeng said confidently that the Communists had risen by guerrilla war, and the Juntong agents too knew this trade, and in this respect were far stronger than ordinary regular troops, so keeping a few strongholds on the mainland was assured. At the same time he got the Sichuan provincial chairman Wang Lingji to grant him the Luzhou special district, introduced Luo Guohuai to be its commissioner, and sent several agents as county heads, meaning to make this place a base too. But when the PLA marched on the southwest, apart from He Longqing's traffic-police brigade, dragged to Chengtu and there dealt with, the rest had no time to assemble at all. Guizhou's Chen Shixian, She Wanxuan, and the like had long since slipped away. Only one traffic-police corps, driven out of Yunnan, was dragged by Tian Dongyun to the border of his home county of Yiliang and Sichuan. At this the Taiwan side was overjoyed, calling it "the Taiwan on the mainland," and gave Tian Dongyun the title of Southwest Military-and-Political Governor, hoping he could hold out. But this band of bandits was wholly wiped out within three months, and Tian Dongyun was captured alive.

The fourth was to keep large numbers of Communist turncoats behind and not let them go to Taiwan. In early October 1949, one evening, the head of the Kunming aviation inspection office, Li Ruifeng, brought a great batch of application forms filled out by people asking to leave for Hong Kong, Rangoon, Taiwan, and elsewhere, wanting me to make the final decision. Mao Renfeng was then staying at my house, working in a large reception room upstairs, while I worked in a small reception room next door. Xu Yuanju was chatting with me, so I had Li Ruifeng leave the forms and return them to him after I had looked. As soon as Li went downstairs, Mao Renfeng called me and Xu Yuanju to his office, telling us to note one matter especially. He said Chiang Kai-shek had instructed him face to face to keep the Communist turncoats from going to Taiwan as far as possible and to make them stay. He said Chiang had even had Zhang Guotao come and tell him so. I asked Mao Renfeng why turncoats should be kept from going as far as possible. He said this was where Chiang Kai-shek was wise, and he greatly admired it, and told me and Xu Yuanju to control strictly the plane tickets to Taiwan. For a turncoat going to Taiwan was all harm and no good; the main reason was that with the Communists victorious, they would never again follow the Nationalists whole-heartedly. Besides, some turncoats had no connections in Taiwan, could no longer be used to wreck the underground party, and were only a burden. Once in Taiwan, we should not only have to feed a batch of them but guard against their turning back to the Communists, working from within in Taiwan; whereas keeping them behind had all too many advantages. The reasons he gave are detailed in "Mao Renfeng, the Successor of the Juntong Secret-Service Chief Dai Li," and are left out here. As far as I know, because of this measure of Chiang Kai-shek's, the turncoat Liu so-and-so, once Chungking municipal committee secretary, who had betrayed nearly a hundred underground-party members such as Jiang Zhujun and Wang Pu in Chungking before liberation, though he had indeed rendered the Nationalists "great service," was in the end, when he knelt before Mao Renfeng begging to be let go to Taiwan, kicked away by Mao Renfeng, and after liberation fell into the net of the people's law. Besides, among the turncoats who had rendered the Nationalists great service yet could not flee to Taiwan, there was one who had worked in the Juntong for years and risen to general's rank, one who had been Zhang Guotao's secretary, one who had been the Yulin station chief, one who had been deputy head of the Bureau's head-office Second Department, one who had been a business-department head, one who had been the ordnance inspection-office head, one who had been the Chengtu-Chungking railway police head, and so on—I shall not list their names here.

### The Use of Traitors

On the set of anti-Communist methods of Dai Li and the Juntong agents, and how they found and wrecked the Communist underground-party organizations, I have already spoken briefly in the essay "What I Know of Dai Li"; now I shall set out in more concrete detail some of the methods the Juntong regularly used on the revolutionaries it captured.

### From Massacre to Exploitation

Ever since Chiang Kai-shek betrayed the revolution in the "April Twelfth" coup, he had from the outset used massacre against the Communists, meaning to kill off the Chinese Communists entirely—"better to kill three thousand by mistake than let one slip." But after a few years, the Communists, far from being killed off, grew more numerous the more they were killed. Later he changed his method, using turncoats to wreck the underground-party organizations, so as to massacre more Communists. He began using this method, I heard, after the capture in Wuhan of the Communist Yangtze Bureau responsible man Gu Shunzhang.

I never met Gu Shunzhang, and have only heard many tales of him. He was arrested by the Investigation Section of the Nationalist Central Party Headquarters' party-affairs work (the forerunner of the "Zhongtong"). This timid coward, once caught, begged to have his dog's life spared, and at once declared that if only he were not killed, he could wreck the party central organization in Shanghai and arrest Communists by the hundred. The reactionaries agreed to his request, resolved to give it a try. Though the Communist Central Committee's organization, on hearing of his defection, quickly moved, many provincial, municipal, and county underground organizations were wrecked, no few documents seized, and over two hundred Communists arrested one after another. Chiang Kai-shek was overjoyed and greatly praised it, and thereupon changed his consistent practice of killing every one he caught. He held that wrecking the Communist underground organizations through turncoats and capturing large numbers of underground-party members would soon wipe out the Communists—a far more effective method.

To use the turncoat Gu Shunzhang, the agents took him to Shanghai. At first, because after Gu Shunzhang's defection the Shanghai party organizations formerly tied to him had long since moved, the agents came up empty several times. Later Gu Shunzhang took the agents and the concession detectives about the concessions and the Chinese city all day looking, and whenever he met someone he knew he at once pointed him out to the agents for arrest. Thus no few party members fell into the murderers' hands. In the end the Chinese Communist Party carried out disciplinary sanction on Gu Shunzhang.

I do not know the organization the Chinese Communist Party charged with carrying out disciplinary sanction on turncoats. I remember that in 1934 and 1935 there were two turncoats serving as supervisors in the Shanghai municipal public-security bureau, Ma so-and-so and Huang so-and-so, both formerly Jiangsu provincial committee responsible men, who were likewise sanctioned by the Communists after betraying large numbers of underground-party members. Then a turncoat named He so-and-so, a directly attached courier in the secret-service department's Shanghai special district, was also sanctioned, but unluckily was not killed on the spot. Dai Li was then in Shanghai, and ordered the Songhu Garrison Command reconnaissance-brigade commander Wang Guanghuai, together with the concession police station and the Shanghai municipal public-security bureau detective corps head Lu Ying and others, to use this gravely wounded but not dead turncoat as bait and set a trap to arrest and kill Communists.

They ran a notice in a tabloid saying that on such a day at such a place a "crime of passion" had occurred, that this He was wounded, but that he knew the gunman who had shot him well, and meant, once his wound healed, to lead the security organs to arrest the man who had shot him. The notice deliberately hinted which hospital the wounded man was in. The underground party's disciplinary-sanction body did indeed send men again to shoot He so-and-so dead in that hospital. But the dozens of agents densely deployed near the hospital finally tailed and wrecked this secret body, arresting Duan Zhiguang and others and seizing pistols and other things. The details of this case I have recorded in the essay "The Criminal Activities of the Juntong Agents in Shanghai Before the War," and shall not write more here. The party members arrested that time admitted they were members of the "Dog-Beating Corps," which specialized in beating drowned-dog turncoats, and did not carry out assassinations against Nationalist personnel.

### Using Traitors as Eyes and Ears

The chief reason the agents could wreck the Communist underground-party organizations on a large scale continuously was the part played by individual turncoats willing to sell their souls. Without turncoats they caught at most one or two men; with a turncoat they could arrest continuously. So the agents set great store by how to make captured underground-party members turn traitor. The facts I heard and saw myself show that the moment a man sells his soul and becomes a turncoat, he at once turns into a frenzied enemy, a butcher against the Communists and the people. For, dreading that party members, revolutionaries, and those who knew his circumstances would expose his crime of betraying the revolution and sanction him, he would often, at all costs and by every means, wreck the party organization of that area and net every party member and revolutionary, to repay his master and secure his own safety. So turncoats were especially vicious toward the Communist organizations, the Communist party members, and the revolutionaries.

Just because of this, once the agents captured an underground-party member, they did not, as many films and novels depict, torture him with dozens of cruel punishments the moment he was taken, but used many methods and means against him, the aim being to make him turn traitor.

Generally speaking, each time an underground-party member was caught, especially an important leader or backbone member, they first used the bait of office and wealth to tempt him. This was aimed at the underground-party members' condition then of having neither money nor office, the aim being to make him sell his soul.

The Juntong establishment had very low ranks, so many turncoats, though they could get much money, could not get high rank. For instance, after Zhang Guotao defected, though Chiang Kai-shek gave him the title of Military Affairs Commission lieutenant-general member, in the Juntong establishment he was only major-general head of the special-political-problems research office. This was because Dai Li himself had only a lieutenant-general title and was appointed as major-general. (In the Nationalist army and military organs, an officer's rank was of two kinds, appointed rank and duty rank. If the duty was lieutenant-general army-group commander, the appointed rank might be only major-general. Appointed rank went by a fixed seniority; once appointed to a rank, it was a lifelong rank. Duty rank was sometimes not limited by seniority and could be promoted or demoted as needed. After the appointed rank was set, it generally could only rise, not fall.) Only after Dai Li was killed did the Nationalist Government, on 1 June 1946, posthumously confer on him the rank of army lieutenant-general. Again, after the Eighth Route Army Luoyang office head Zhen so-and-so defected, though he betrayed over eighty underground-party members at one stroke, Dai Li gave him only a colonelcy. In 1948, after the Communist Chungking municipal committee secretary Liu so-and-so defected and betrayed over a hundred underground-party members, he got only a lieutenant-colonelcy in exchange.

Besides tempting with name and profit, another of the Juntong's methods was to use old and great turncoats to talk round the newly captured underground-party members. I myself heard Zhang Guotao try to talk round an underground-party member captured secretly on the south bank in Chungking in 1942. Zhang Guotao began by introducing himself, giving the other a start. Then he used a shameless spiel—that even an "old party member" of his high standing was no longer a Communist and willing to turn to the Nationalists, and so on—asking the other to consider. But quite unexpectedly, that underground-party member answered very firmly: "I cannot do this—what is there to fear in death!" In the end Zhang Guotao could only say brazenly: "I'm doing this for your good, think it over carefully." Then he slunk away. Of course, this loyal, unbending hero became in the end a revolutionary martyr.

Before the war, when I worked in Shanghai, I several times saw a turncoat who, using his ties as a former Jiangsu provincial committee man and organization worker, roamed the streets all day, seizing any underground-party member he knew, and after the arrest did the base work of enticement and persuasion in person. Once he was trying to make an underground-party member he had formerly led turn traitor, and got a sound cursing for it. Enraged, he struck at the other's vital spot on the head with a pistol butt, meaning to kill him at a blow; I hurried to stop him, wanting to extort a confession by interrogation, but he still kicked the man viciously several times, and only left in a fury when he had kicked him to the ground.

There was another turncoat who, after defecting, had betrayed over a hundred men in Wuhan; the underground party sent men to sanction him, and cut him eight cleaver-strokes without killing him, so in Hunanese fashion we called him "the eight-cleaver man." After being sanctioned by the Communists, far from any thought of remorse, he grew more frenzied in persecuting captured underground-party members. His head wounds barely healed, the bandages not yet off, he would in person put captured party members who would not turn traitor to cruel torture. Once, torturing another, he used such force that his own head wound split open, and though blood covered his face he would not stop. This soul-selling running dog, after becoming a colonel operations-section head in the Juntong, failed in a task of wrecking an underground-party organization, lost Dai Li's trust, and was sent to be an instructor in a special-training class. In the end he became a monk, and after liberation fell at last into the net of the people's law.

When giving money and promising office and persuasion still failed to move a captured Communist, the agents next used intimidation, and if that failed, torture. In torturing too, they went from light to heavy, until they used the "tiger bench," "chilli-water," and the rest all together. The reason these murderers-without-batting-an-eye, these butchers, did this was chiefly to make Communists into soul-selling turncoats to help them arrest continuously. "No turncoats, no eyes and ears"; "no turncoats, no results"—this was the Juntong agents' pet phrase. So the aim of torture was to make him confess, give up the underground organization and other party members, and hope he would turn traitor and become one of the agents' gang.

### How the Agents Observed and Lured the Captured Revolutionaries

Whether a Communist, once arrested, would turn traitor was a question the agents watched and studied closely. For instance, some, after cruel torture, returned to the cell caring for nothing—even flies alighting to suck at their open, bleeding wounds seemed to go unfelt—only staring at the cell ceiling, motionless, deep in thought; such men the agents reckoned hard to deal with. Some, after torture, returned to the cell and busied themselves tending their wounds, wanting to find a comfortable spot to lie down, or to pile the straw bedding thicker, and so on. Seeing this, the agents would consider: this man cares about his wounds, which shows he fears pain and still wants to live, so let him suffer heavier torture to hurt him more; as for wanting to lie more comfortably, satisfy that to soften him; or make his conditions worse to force him to yield. In a word, against the various psychologies observed, they thought up ways to deal with each.

The agents also watched closely each person's conduct during the "prisoners'" meals. Some, for instance, would not even glance at the food brought, and only when truly hungry ate a few careless mouthfuls; some, on seeing the food come, first felt whether it was hot, sniffed whether it smelled good, and picked out the best to eat, and so on. Generally the former were hard to deal with, the latter easier. Some, once in the cell, cared for nothing, sitting or lying wherever they could, heeding no filth that fell on them, not even smoothing their hair on rising, letting it stay tangled, not even washing their faces. Some cherished their clothes, shoes, and caps, brushing off even a fleck of dust once in the cell. Generally the former were hard to break, the latter easy to overcome. That said, in fact the Communists were very hard to read. Jiang Zhujun, for instance, in prison always kept her hair neat and her clothes tidy, ate normally, and yet not a word could be got from her, so firm was she. Once Xu Yuanju (Xu Pengfei) interrogated Jiang Zhujun in person, and I, having gone to see Xu just then, witnessed a moving scene. Xu Yuanju was then in a jealous quarrel with Zhou Yanghao (Shen Yangzhai), and Mao Renfeng had me go to mediate, so I flew specially from Kunming to Chungking on the errand. When I went to find them, apart from their inner rooms at home, which had to be knocked at, I could walk straight into their offices at any time without being announced. That day Xu Yuanju was interrogating Sister Jiang in a room; I pushed the door and went in, and he half rose, then had me sit beside him. He asked several times running, and Sister Jiang answered not a word. His temper was ever like thunder, and he gave a great shout: "Strip off her clothes for me!" I knew he was again about to use his most barbaric method of interrogating women—first stripping off their trousers to shame them, then pricking the nipples with needles, then whipping the vulva and other most sensitive, most painful places with a small switch. At his great shout, the several butchers standing on either side rushed at Sister Jiang like fiends. At this, Sister Jiang, silent so long, suddenly gave a great cry: "You shall not run wild!" Xu Yuanju, thinking this had cowed Jiang, said at once with a fawning smile: "Then out with it, quick!" Sister Jiang answered very calmly: "Of course I will speak." Then in a stern voice she loosed a string of questions: "Were you not born of your mother? Is your mother not a woman? Your wife, your sisters, your daughter—are they not women? You think to insult me by such means, thinking I will be afraid? I fear not even death—what should I fear of insult! But let me tell you, by this base method you insult not only me but your mother, your wife, your sisters, your daughter, and all women! Are you worthy of your mother?" Sister Jiang's fury, like a string of cannon-fire, put Xu Yuanju to shame. I hurriedly nudged his leg with my foot and said softly: "Can you not use another way?" Afterward he ordered the butchers to drive bamboo slivers into several of Sister Jiang's fingertips, but still could get no confession. Sister Jiang's just and stern reproach still rings in my ears to this day.

In interrogating revolutionaries the agents had a set of intimidation methods: letting a man hear the screams of others under torture, or see comrades suffer cruel punishment. Besides, there was the use of mock trials, or killing men before those who would not confess; or digging a pit, leading a man to its edge, and making every gesture of readiness to bury him alive.

To extract confessions from revolutionaries who stood unbending after capture, the agents also often used a trick called the "self-injury ruse." They picked a capable agent and dressed him up as a captured revolutionary, his body too bruised black and blue with "wounds," his hair matted and face grimy, his clothes ragged, sometimes even his hands bound behind him and heavy fetters on his feet, as if he had suffered cruel torment. Such a "prisoner" would often, in the dead of night, be thrust into the cell holding revolutionaries, and on entering would fall to the ground moaning. The fellow prisoners, out of sympathy, would often come at once to tend this newcomer, but he would often pretend deliberately to distrust others. After a few days he would deliberately act very mysterious, and also seem to trust his cellmates greatly, and while the guarding agent or sentry was away would tell others quietly: "Quick! Quick, take out the little note sewn in my collar (or elsewhere) and destroy it!"—using this vivid performance to show he was a grave "important criminal," and so most easily win others' sympathy. These hidden things were often really underground-party secret documents; even a veteran underground-party member often could not spot the flaw. The reason was simple: such secret documents were sometimes taken by the agents off other underground-party members; even if forged, they were made by turncoats after the pattern of things they had used before, so they could well deceive some people.

The use of this "self-injury ruse" often took ten days or half a month, or longer, to take effect. In this time the "important criminal" would deliberately not eat or drink, and when a fellow prisoner urged him he ate only a little and lay down again, showing a look of deep dejection. But each day, or every other day, when he was "hauled up for trial," he could sit on a comfortable office sofa and eat a fine meal, making up for it with good things, though he was not allowed wine or tobacco. Others could not discover this. In those few days or a little longer, if the people in the cell were careless and let out information in conversation, or he observed something, this "important criminal" would note it in his heart and report it to his superiors when "hauled up for trial." When the agent chiefs reckoned they had basically drawn out the situation of the party members who would not confess, this "agent prisoner" would suddenly, at one "trial," not return to the cell. He would only come out again to testify and expose when the revolutionaries were hauled up for trial.

The agents used this "self-injury ruse" only on prisoners very hard to deal with. In the six or so years I worked in Shanghai before the war I saw it only four or five times. This "trump card" was more vicious than torture alone, and some people did indeed fall for it.

### The Tangled and Complex Struggles

When the agents used turncoats to wreck the underground-party organizations and hunt down underground-party members, they also met many loyal, unbending party members who faced death as a homecoming, who in turn used the agents' searches and arrests to pass word out, alert the party organization, and so protect it and their comrades.

Also before the war, in Shanghai, the agents once arrested in Pudong a courier the Communist Jiangsu provincial committee organization department had sent to keep contact with a factory party organization. He had been betrayed by a turncoat, and the agents waited over half a month before catching him. Over this Dai Li hurried from Nanking to Shanghai to arrange it in person, meaning through this captured courier to wreck the Jiangsu provincial committee organization and arrest large numbers of underground-party members. After the courier was caught, he knew he had been sold out. Deeply fearing the turncoat would go on betraying other party members and wreck the provincial committee, when Dai Li talked with him and promised him a large bonus and office, asking him to hand over at once the provincial committee's location and leaders, he pretended willing to accept the terms, saying he would lead them next morning to search the provincial committee organization department, where the rolls of the provincial, prefectural, and county committees could be found and the relevant people arrested.

Dai Li was overjoyed, and besides telling me to treat this courier well—let him sleep and eat well—so that at daybreak he could contact the French-concession police station and be led by the courier to search a certain number on Rue Chapsal in the French concession, he also made every preparation for a mass arrest. I once suggested to Dai Li that it would be best to go that very night, or the next night. Dai gave a whole heap of reasons for going the next morning. He said the Communists all worked by night, not returning till near daybreak, and were mostly still abed in the morning, the best time for a search and arrest.

The next morning, when many of the French-concession police station's men had just come on duty, they escorted us in police cars, the courier's hands cuffed, and took him to point the way. On reaching the spot he named, we got out, and the courier deliberately stood in the middle of the road, stamping his heel and looking at the windows of several doorways. I urged him to lead us in quickly, but he said the place had been moved into only a few days before, that he had come only once, and could not tell at once which house it was. We had to seal both ends of the road, letting no one pass for the time and none in that stretch come out or go in. After a while the courier named a house. The agents at once guarded the front and back doors and rushed in, ransacking from the ground floor up to the third; not only did they seize nothing, but the residents were almost wholly unrelated families, not one worth suspecting, and there were no documents at all.

I pressed the courier again and again as to what on earth was going on; he said he might have misremembered, and wanted to go back and think it over and come again. The agents had no choice but to take him back. He thought a day, two days, three days, and still said he could not recall. On the fourth day, when Dai Li sternly demanded an answer and made ready to torture him, he burst out laughing. It turned out he had deliberately gone to carry word to the provincial committee organ, to tell them he had been arrested and have them move quickly. His method had been to stand in the road deliberately exposing himself so they could see him, and only at the end pretended to point out the house opposite for the agents to search. When the search yielded nothing, he again deliberately stood in the middle of the road looking about, and only after reckoning the party comrades had seen him did he come back with us. With the tone of a schemer victorious, he told Dai Li that after his arrest he had had no way to warn his superiors to move quickly, so he had had to make the whole crowd walk a trip with him, and that the true place was diagonally opposite the house searched. Hearing this, Dai Li was beside himself with rage and at once sent men to look; sure enough, the three-storey house opposite the one searched had been moved out clean that very afternoon. Dai Li then ordered the courier sent to Nanking at once, and before long he was secretly executed. Sadly I can no longer recall his name.

After that lesson, whenever the agents met a like situation, they would first carefully ask out many concrete details—how many families lived in the building, what furnishings were in the rooms, what people were in the household, what safety or danger signs were set at the door or windows, and so on. Having asked all this clear, they would at once send a man disguised as a meter-reader to go in and look, and only then move their men to search and arrest, so as not to be fooled again and let the revolutionaries go.

Among the large numbers of turncoats working in the Juntong, some sincerely turned to the enemy as claws and teeth; some had left the party (mostly having lost contact) yet had never betrayed the organization or other party members—such people made up a fair number of the Juntong's senior agents. There were also a few who feigned being "turncoats" to work their way into the Juntong, using it as cover to go on with revolutionary activity. After liberation I met a man who had worked in the Juntong for years; though the agents had once suspected him, they found no evidence and so only kept an eye on him without arresting him. In 1963, when he came to study at the Socialist Institute in Beijing and met me, he spoke of how he had worked in the Juntong with his head in his hands, and only then did I see it in a flash, and I felt boundless admiration for his spirit of fearing no sacrifice for the revolution.

### The Open and Hidden Strife of the Zheng, Mao, and Tang Factions

While Dai Li lived, control within the Juntong was very strict and its guard close; though the agents differed in closeness to him, they dared not openly form factions. The moment Dai Li died, the three men the agents had always called the "three chiefs"—Zheng Jiemin, Mao Renfeng, and Tang Zong—split at once into the Guangdong, Zhejiang, and Hunan factions. Before the Bureau of Secrets Preservation was founded, they had a first scramble over dividing up the Juntong's property and Dai Li's estate, and though they made a fine spectacle of themselves, neither would yield to the others.

First, over dividing the houses and cars, they made a foul mess of it, and I felt myself in a hard place. After Zheng Jiemin took the large villa Dai Li had on Linsen Road in Shanghai, Mao and Tang each got a smaller one. The American secret service had given Dai Li four new cars, and after one was set aside for Chiang Kai-shek, the three men were to have one each. Later He Yingqin learned of it and took one, leaving two. Zheng and Mao each wanted one, and had me explain to Tang Zong and ask him to pick one from the older ones on hand and all the good cars taken from traitors, which left Tang thoroughly displeased. Just then a Hu so-and-so barged in, kicking up a great row. Hu, after joining the Juntong, had only been deputy head of the Xifeng training class and the Hengyang inspection-cadre class, yet, on the strength of having helped Dai Li when he was a secretary at Chiang Kai-shek's side, was bent on muscling in among Zheng, Mao, and Tang for an equal share. When no one heeded him, he once feigned drunken madness, ran to the Juntong office to beat Tang Zong and Mao Renfeng, and, stopped by me, played the utter rogue, lying on the ground and refusing to rise. Mao Renfeng had no way out but to give him too a villa and a car. At this many wanted to follow suit, and rows over dividing things up broke out often.

After the Bureau was founded, Zheng Jiemin, to guard against Mao Renfeng's monopolizing power, sent his trusted fellow townsman and Moscow Sun Yat-sen University schoolmate Zhang Jixun to be director's-office chief, laying it down that documents from every department and office go first to the director's office; he also sent another trusted fellow townsman, Wang Qing, as office commissioner to help Zhang deal with Mao Renfeng. Mao had meant to have his most trusted man Pan Qiwu take this post, and seeing Zheng's arrangement, could only ask Tang Zong to place Pan Qiwu as criminal-police-department head in the police director-general's office. Tang Zong, having unexpectedly gained the post of national police director-general, was content and did not wish to reach into the Bureau to contend for leadership; he only took the chance to get a large house at No. 22 Matai Street for the police director-general's office. To draw Tang in and deal solely with Zheng Jiemin, Mao Renfeng of his own accord gave several cars to the police director-general's office, and agreed to let Tang draw cadres from the Bureau.

I then leaned to Mao Renfeng's Zhejiang faction. Because I handled money and property, Zheng Jiemin's wife Ke Shufen thought me partial in dividing things and often made trouble. Mao Renfeng used my displeasure with Zheng's wife to have me make some trouble for Zheng and enlarge people's ill impression of him. I once seized on the occasion of Zheng Jiemin's fiftieth birthday to make my move. Zheng had always feared his wife. On his fiftieth birthday in 1947, afraid of trouble, he dared not celebrate with fanfare, but Mao Renfeng had me press his wife to insist on it, and spread word among many agents, hinting they send rich gifts. Two days before the birthday, Zheng, in a temper, went off to Shanghai. I then sent men to lay it on lavishly for him, decking out his residence on Xihe Road in Nanking anew. On the day, the gold birthday-peaches and the many gold and silver vessels and gift certificates the open units sent were all displayed together in the birthday hall. Just as the great feast was in full swing, I also sent men to No. 53 Jihuangbu guesthouse to tell a crowd of agent-dependants staying there to go and offer birthday wishes and cadge a meal. These people, their pensions spent and the Bureau unwilling to attend to them any longer, were all crammed there and quarrelling all day. Hearing that Zheng Jiemin was throwing a grand birthday, they rushed off in droves, old and young in tow. When the agents I had sent came back and told me they had set out, I passed this on to Zheng's wife too. Hearing it, she knew that once this crowd arrived a great scene would break out at once, and I too, fearing the thing would blow up beyond fixing, at her repeated pleading played the good man and hurried to talk the crowd into going back, promising them extra hospitality at the guesthouse. Mao Renfeng at once reported to Chiang Kai-shek that Zheng Jiemin was throwing a birthday and that agent-dependants had meant to make a scene at his home, which made Chiang very angry to hear.

After this affair, Zheng's faction, unresigned, before long traced it out and, finding it had to do with me, wanted to deal with me first. Since I had handled much property, they started there to find fault with me. Failing to find evidence to bring me to book, they found fault with my subordinates. The General Affairs Department administration-section head Deng Duofu had privately sold off a batch of foreign locks, and they found it out and detained Deng. By ordinary handling he would at most have got a few years, but Zheng Jiemin insisted on severe punishment. Because Deng Duofu was, among the Linli-special-training-class students, the staunchest supporter of Mao Renfeng, Zheng asked Chiang Kai-shek to shoot him on a charge of embezzlement-by-theft-of-one's-own-charge.

After the conflict developed to this point, Mao Renfeng had me secretly collect material on Zheng. The corruption of Zheng's I then got hold of was: he had built a villa on Moganshan Road in Nanking; in the Japanese concession in Hankou he had made his own a Japanese villa the Juntong had taken over, transferring it into his wife's name; his household's every monthly outlay was charged to the General Affairs Department, even his children's toys and a Hunan-embroidered Guanyin figure going on the public account; Zheng had also privately given his brother Zheng Tingfeng forty of the pistols and submachine guns SACO had handed the Juntong. Mao Renfeng thought this not enough, and had his trusted fellow townsman Wang Puchen collect much material on Zheng in Beiping too, and reported it all together to Chiang Kai-shek. At the time the military-affairs-bureau head at Chiang Kai-shek's side, Yu Jishi, who had also long disliked Zheng and was on good terms with Mao, joined Mao in attacking Zheng on many fronts, and in the end Chiang Kai-shek, on 5 December 1947, removed Zheng Jiemin from the Bureau directorship and the Second-Department headship, and let him succeed Huang Zhenqiu as vice-minister of national defence, in charge of defence materials.

When Mao Renfeng took over as Bureau director, Zheng Jiemin came to the Bureau for the handover ceremony, and on the surface the two were still very courteous. After Zheng Jiemin spoke, Mao said only a few words, saying he would still have to rely on Mr. Zheng's leadership henceforth, and that since a superior (meaning Zheng) was present today he would not say much. Seeing Zheng to his car, Mao even opened the door for him in person. But the moment he turned round, he set at once to clearing out Zheng's trusted men in the Bureau, driving off one by one the director's-office chief Zhang Jixun and the commissioners Wang Qing, Xiao Manliu, and others, and then grabbing the open organs the Zheng faction had held. The Shanghai Garrison Command inspection-office head Cheng Yiming was transferred out and replaced by Mao's man Tao Yishan, and the traffic-police directorate head Ji Zhangjian was changed to Zhou Weilong too.

After Zheng Jiemin's men in and out of the Bureau were squeezed out, Mao Renfeng, still unsatisfied, next struck at Ma Hansan, who had become Beiping civil-affairs bureau head on the strength of his tie with Zheng. Ma had been the Juntong's North China office head; handling enemy-and-puppet property he had embezzled much, and while Zheng was in Beiping had grown close to him, often sending rich gifts to Zheng's wife. Though Mao had before gathered much evidence on Ma Hansan, on account of the Zheng tie he had found it awkward to act. Once Zheng was transferred, Mao Renfeng detained Ma Hansan and Ma's assistant Liu Yuzhu, and because certain matters implicated the Beiping station chief Qiao Jiacai and others, Mao said they were forming a clique to set up their own faction in the Bureau. Reckoning that a charge of corruption and clique-forming would not easily let him kill a few, Mao Renfeng seized the moment when Chiang Kai-shek was in a great rage that Li Zongren had been elected vice-president while his own designated Sun Ke had not, and charged that Ma Hansan had disobeyed the order to support Sun Ke and had supported Li Zongren instead, asking Chiang Kai-shek to approve shooting Ma Hansan and Liu Yuzhu and confiscating all their property for the Bureau. After this blow, the Zheng faction was utterly finished. The Tang-faction Bureau Design Committee chairman Zhang Yanfo was before long squeezed by Mao Renfeng back to Hunan too. The Bureau then became an all-Mao-faction realm.

Mao's squeezing out of Zhang had some inside story too. Mao and Zhang had always got on fairly well; when Mao was acting chief secretary in the Juntong they had worked together for years. After Dai Li's death, the Mao-faction Chungking aviation-inspection-office head Wu Maoxian was running smuggled goods for Mao's wife; Zhang, then head of the wind-up office in Chungking, seeing Wu without a permit and unwilling to defer to him, detained Wu and reported it to Zheng Jiemin. Mao bore this in mind, and once he became director at once released Wu and drove Zhang off.

Later the conflict between Mao and Tang, though not as fierce as that between Mao and Zheng, went on always in secret scheming. Mao Renfeng wanted, as in the Juntong period, to control the personnel of the police director-general's office. Tang Zong had noted this early and laid it down that anyone recommended by the Bureau to work in the police director-general's office, or hoping through it to be sent to the provincial and municipal police organs, could not be handled by the personnel room without his approval. Mao Renfeng was greatly displeased with this rule and raised it many times at lunch, holding that Tang controlled too strictly, would not place his men, and liked to use outsiders. Since the stakes were not too great and Tang did not wish to build up his own strength within the Bureau, the two men's conflict was not very sharp and they could rub along.

Besides, after Dai Li's death the three men also had many a quarrel over grabbing the graduates of the agent-training classes. From 1946 on, the students of the successive Juntong special-training classes felt that unless they banded together they would be pushed aside, and so joined up under the names of alumni or fellow-townsman associations. First the Linli-class and Qian-class students set up a Binhu Alumni Association in Chungking, with Zhang Mingxuan, Li Baochu, Deng Yifu, Yi Buofu, Liu Benqin, and others secretly doing the liaison work. Then the students of the Lanzhou, Xifeng, Southeast, and intelligence-staff classes secretly organized one after another too. All wanting a backer, those doing military work sought Zheng's door, those wanting police work went to Tang, and those willing to go on as agents turned to Mao. Because Zheng, Tang, and Mao had none of them had a batch of cadres they thought loyal and reliable, and Dai's death was just the fine chance to win the world and contend for power and profit, all wanted to grab some men at this time.

Seeing this, Mao Renfeng called together seven or eight men who supported him—Pan Qiwu, Yuan Jibin, Li Xiukai, Mao Zhongxin, Shen Zui, and others—and decided to set up a "Unified Alumni Association of the Graduates of the Juntong's Training Classes," laying it down that all students who had graduated from the Juntong's various wartime training classes must join this body, and none might set up any other class sub-organization; existing ones must all cease activity, or be punished on a charge of clique-forming. It was decided too that after the unified alumni association was founded, Zheng and Tang would be invited as honorary presidents and Mao Renfeng as president, and some senior agents connected with the training classes would be engaged as advisers. Thus these tens of thousands of students all fell wholly into the Mao faction's grip.

When the unified alumni association held its founding congress in 1947 in the Bureau's great hall at Honggongci in Nanking, the graduates of the Juntong's dozens of training classes all sent representatives; but the two honorary presidents, much displeased over the matter, did not attend. The association, run by Mao Renfeng, was thereafter attached within the Bureau's personnel department, with Li Baochu, Deng Yifu, Xu Feng, and others handling the work as general-association officers, with liaison, registration, welfare, and other groups, and branches set up in the provinces. The graduates of the various Juntong special-training classes who joined came to about twenty thousand, each member issued a membership badge, a round brass design of an arrow shooting through a dove of peace. Before liberation, agents wearing this badge could be seen everywhere in the Nationalist-held areas; only those on secret work were forbidden to wear it. At the time this little badge became virtually a licence for these agents to run lawless everywhere. When men doing secret-service work in the ordinary open organs met one wearing this badge, whether they knew him or not they would defer three parts. Near liberation this association too fled with the Bureau to Taiwan, controlled throughout by Mao Renfeng.

Zheng, Tang, and Mao each had his own set, and each his own style. Zheng Jiemin had always won Chiang Kai-shek's trust by being seasoned, weighty, and far-sighted. Ordinarily he kept his pleasure and displeasure off his face, controlled himself well, read books and periodicals in his spare time, would put his mind to studying problems, and liked to analyse the international and domestic situations. When he spoke at the Juntong's and the Bureau's memorial-week meetings, he always gave current-affairs reports and rarely touched on work matters. Sometimes when I went to his home to see him, he liked to give his views on the situation too.

I remember that during the war, once while he was holding forth to us on the situation of the war with Japan, his wife Ke Shufen cut in from the side, telling us not to believe his stuff. For at the start of the war, on his analysis and judgment, Hong Kong would be no problem, and his wife, believing him, had stored many things in Hong Kong, and lost them all when the Japanese occupied it. She had several times, in front of us, angrily demanded of Zheng: "You said Hong Kong was no problem—what now? Give me back my things!" To this loss of face before us Zheng paid no mind at all, only laughing it off.

Zheng fancied himself long versed in the Communist question. When Dai Li lived, he too often had Mao Renfeng consult Zheng on some Communist matters. I remember once, after Zheng had presided over the memorial week at the Juntong, he was resting when the Chungking postal-and-telegraph censorship-office head Liu Zhipan brought him a batch of impounded papers and periodicals to look at. Picking out one or two and pointing to one, he said to Liu: "This kind of thing looks at a glance like the Communists', but it's really put out by our own trade—only put out rather well, so that even our own comrades take it for true, and ordinary people find it harder still to tell apart." Then he gave a set of instructions on the censorship work, teaching them to study problems closely and not be slapdash, and said dealing with the Communists was many-sided, that some of our units specialized in carrying on anti-Communist propaganda under the Communists' name, and that if such things were impounded too, we would in effect be doing the Communists' work.

After Chiang Kai-shek made Zheng Jiemin the Nationalist representative on the Executive Headquarters of the Truce Mission, Zheng ran to the Juntong and consulted Mao Renfeng at length, then called in the heads of the personnel, military-intelligence, and party-and-government-intelligence departments for a talk, and at last called me in, telling me to help his work on transport and the like. What he discussed with Mao Renfeng I do not know; I only knew later that in asking the Juntong to send personnel he wanted men from military schools who had held open military posts, avoiding an agent's identity as far as possible, and hoped the Juntong would keep supplying him relevant intelligence.

The men Zheng Jiemin sent to serve on the executive teams in various places were all decided by himself. When the former War College investigation-group head Zou Lufu and the Juntong military-intelligence-department deputy head Hu Pinghan were assigned to him, he at first thought it not quite fitting, but as no suitable men could really be found he grudgingly agreed to their transfer. Not long after Zheng went to Beiping, he transferred the Juntong Communist-affairs-section head Guo Ziming to Beiping to be his adviser.

While working in Beiping, Zheng showed John Leighton Stuart special warmth, and was especially close with Stuart's trusted secretary Fu Jingbo, keeping constant close contact. One day Zheng suddenly issued an order for the personnel department to appoint at once a major who had been head of the Fuling inspection sub-office of the Chungking inspection office to be deputy police head of the Beiping-Liaoning railway. Everyone found this strange, for to jump a major to colonel at a stroke like this had never happened in his day. On inquiry, it turned out this major was Fu Jingbo's brother-in-law, and once Fu had raised it, Zheng at once gave him this super-grade important post.

Mao Renfeng's seniority in the Juntong was less than that of the various elders of Dai Li's Ten-Man Group, and less even than that of his deputy chief secretary Zhang Yanfo. Before the Xi'an Incident he had not yet joined the Juntong, being only a secretary in a certain county government in Shaanxi. Later his brother Mao Wanli introduced him to Dai Li; he first worked in the secret-investigation group of the Hankou opium-suppression supervision office, and at the "August Thirteenth" Shanghai war was transferred to be secretary of the Jiangsu-Zhejiang Action Committee. Only after the secret-service department was enlarged into the Juntong did he come to the Juntong as a secretary. Being a fellow townsman of Dai Li's, working very hard ordinarily, his greatest strength being his ability to keep secrets, plus a mild manner and diligent, careful ways, he gradually, step by step, won Dai Li's trust and became Dai's right-hand man. By the late war years Dai Li trusted him more than Zheng Jiemin, Tang Zong, or Pan Qiwu, and each time Dai went away he left almost the whole Juntong to him. After 1944 Chiang Kai-shek too took a liking to Mao Renfeng, and when Dai Li was away would often call him to ask about matters, and he answered fluently.

But once Dai Li died and Mao became deputy director of the Bureau, his manner changed with it. He often put on a stern face to rebuke subordinates, and dared to contend for favour with Zheng Jiemin and Tang Zong before Chiang Kai-shek.

Mao Renfeng pondered problems very hard, and when others sought his instruction on a major matter he never readily gave an answer at once. In his work style he imitated Dai Li at every turn, and meant to surpass Dai Li. In the year or more after he became Bureau director, he killed even more people than Dai Li had. He guarded closely against the Juntong's own too, fearing others would not obey him; especially uneasy about the field provincial station chiefs, he, like Dai Li, sent a batch of cipher clerks of his home county to work in the provincial stations, secretly watching the field unit heads.

In killing, Mao Renfeng not only followed Chiang Kai-shek's intent of holding killing more the higher merit, but advocated pulling up the roots, killing even revolutionaries' children. In the Chungking massacre, when someone asked him what to do with the children, he answered: "Kill them too, of course. Are we to leave them to grow up and take revenge?" In fact, when Chiang Kai-shek instructed him to kill Yang Hucheng, he did not say to kill the children too. Mao Renfeng dealt thus with revolutionaries, and dealt with the Bureau's own by killing as well. Before liberation, the moment an agent showed the least sign of turning to the people and was caught, he was killed at once. To my knowledge, the former Juntong Nanking station chief Zhou Zhen, the former Chungking inspection-office foreign-affairs-group head Wu Runle, and Liu Renshan, who had risen with Mr. Cheng Songyun in Changsha, were all killed by him. After Hunan's peaceful liberation, some senior agents who could not flee in time he sent men to assassinate. So the Hunan police-security head and Changsha mayor Li Xiaobai, the Changsha Pacification Headquarters department head Yang Jirong, and the Hunan station chief Huang Kangyong, having fled the mainland, all dared not go to Taiwan. When Yunnan was peacefully liberated and I took part in the uprising, he sent Zheng Shixun from Taiwan to Kunming to assassinate me. When the Nationalist deputy army commander-in-chief Tang went from Taiwan to Yunnan to command Li Mi's and Yu Chengwan's remnants in an attack on Kunming, Mao Renfeng told Tang again and again that once Kunming was taken he was to arrest me at once and send me to Taiwan. Later, when Zheng Shixun and Tang were captured and held with me, they both told me of this.

Tang Zong had always been careful and cautious. Before the war, when he was a secretary in the secret-service department, because Dai Li had gone away and under his charge a few Reorganization Clique men were arrested, Wang Jingwei raised it with Chiang Kai-shek, and Tang was dismissed for it. Later he went with Dai Li to Germany, served as deputy military attaché in the embassy there, and on his return grew more careful still. Whatever tasks his superiors gave him he always contrived to complete, and could obey orders absolutely. So Dai Li recommended him to Chiang Kai-shek's side to be a staff officer, and in a few years he rose from lieutenant-colonel to major-general head of the Attendants' Office First Department's Sixth Group, in charge of intelligence within the Chiang Kai-shek clique. He held that as long as he kept Chiang Kai-shek's trust he need not fear for his prospects. He did not wish to offend many, and so got on well with both the Political Study Clique and the CC Clique.

Of the three men Zheng, Tang, and Mao, Tang was the least corrupt; in making money he did not come forward himself but had his wife do it. He lived fairly frugally, and though in his youth he had carried on hotly with a woman reporter in Nanking, once his position rose he dared not run wild again, and so won Chiang Kai-shek's ever greater trust and use.

### The Trick of the Real and Fake Bureaus of Secrets Preservation

In January 1949, before Chiang Kai-shek prepared to "retire," he called Mao Renfeng in and told him to withdraw the Bureau of Secrets Preservation from Nanking at once, sending the head office and files first to Taiwan and setting up an office in Shanghai to go on leading the work everywhere. Chiang's meaning was to keep the whole organization of the Bureau intact and under his own control, not to be handed to Li Zongren; but to fob off the acting president, he set aside a few dozen men to form a fake Bureau of Secrets Preservation. Chiang told Mao Renfeng to resign the directorship to the Defence Ministry and let the former deputy director Xu Zhidao be director of this open Bureau, with Lin Chao, who had been Fujian station chief, as deputy director. Since Xu and Lin had both worked long in the Fifth War Zone during the war and had always had dealings with the Guangxi clique, they could be used to treat with and fend off Li Zongren and Bai Chongxi. The real Bureau of Secrets Preservation Mao Renfeng held was still directed by Chiang Kai-shek in person.

The fake Bureau of Secrets Preservation handed to Li Zongren had, besides director and deputy director, Tu Shoumei as chief secretary, with business and general-affairs departments under it. The business department was headed by the Second-Department deputy head Huang Yigong, whom Mao Renfeng designated; the general-affairs-department head was Niu Chen, a relative Xu Zhidao found; there were only ninety-two office personnel in all, and not one field man or organization. It neither supplied Li Zongren intelligence nor accepted work the acting president assigned. In fact it was only an empty shell; the secret tasks it did not carry out, and the open organs it had held had long been ordered not to accept its command. Apart from drawing funds in name and dealing with the Defence Ministry on some routine matters for the Bureau Mao Renfeng really led, it had nothing to do.

But Xu Zhidao was very ambitious, and after becoming director of this open Bureau, was bent on squeezing Mao Renfeng out and taking full charge himself. When the Defence Ministry fled to Guangzhou, he fell out with Mao Renfeng. He held the Bureau's seal, and once he drew the funds he would not give them to Mao Renfeng, and prepared to set up his own field organization, to turn the sham real. When he sent men to the Shiwan Mountains to bring local strongman forces to heel and to deploy an intelligence group in Guangzhou, Mao Renfeng directed the business-department head Huang Yigong, whom he had sent, not to obey Xu's set and to go and undercut Xu. Unexpectedly Huang Yigong was of one mind with Xu, and, feeling that as business-department head he had no business to run, wanted to seize the chance to show himself, and so did not act on Mao's instructions but sincerely helped Xu instead. Mao Renfeng complained to Chiang Kai-shek, meaning to remove Xu Zhidao and find another man, but Chiang Kai-shek would not agree.

At a meeting in Chungking in March 1949, Mao sent the ordnance inspection-office head Liao Zongze to fly to Guangzhou to talk Xu round, telling him not to go against the spirit and intent of Chiang Kai-shek's instruction to split the Bureau in two, and showing him the pros and cons. Xu too, considering it not easy to attain his aim at once, grudgingly agreed to split the funds he had drawn, hoping only to get a little more special funds himself. When the Defence Ministry moved to Chungking, Xu Zhidao brought this fake Bureau of Secrets Preservation to Chungking too. In this period the Defence Ministry cut personnel again and again, and by November, when the fake Bureau fled to Chengtu, only the director, department heads, and a few others were left. Huang Yigong then asked to go to Taiwan and return to the Bureau as its Second-Department deputy head. Mao Renfeng, hating him for his past disobedience, firmly refused and told him to stay and work on the mainland. Later Huang fled to Xichang, and with no road left to flee, Mao Renfeng still would not let him go to Taiwan.

After Chengtu's liberation, this Bureau of Secrets Preservation once called Xu's by the agents was thereupon declared finished. When Li Zongren went abroad and Chiang Kai-shek resumed the title of president, Mao's Bureau of Secrets Preservation then formally appeared in Taiwan along with him.
