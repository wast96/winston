## The Assassinations of Yang Xingfo and Shi Liangcai

In 1933 and 1934 Yang Xingfo and Shi Liangcai were assassinated by Juntong agents, the one in Shanghai and the other on the Shanghai-Hangchow highway, and a great deal was said about both cases outside at the time. When I joined the Juntong's secret service in 1932 — it was then the Special Services Department of the Renaissance Society and only later became the Juntong — I was in the East China district in Shanghai, afterwards the Shanghai special district, where I served as district courier and as a group head, and in both these killings I was a participant at one remove. During the war against Japan, when the Juntong was running training classes for agents on a large scale, I was for a long period the principal instructor in "operational technique" at many of them — the subject that covers arrest, assassination, kidnapping, sabotage and the rest of that criminal business — and I was responsible for editing the operational-technique lecture notes used at all the Juntong's special classes, in which both these killings were set down as teaching material. In the course of compiling those notes I collected material on both cases more than once from Zhao Lijun, the criminal who directed them in person, and I consulted the relevant files. What follows is an account of each case so far as I can recall it. Since so many years have passed there may still be omissions and errors in it, and I would ask anyone who knows the circumstances to add to it and correct it, so that these criminal outrages, which shocked people at the time, may be exposed more fully and the world may see more clearly how vicious the reactionary clique was.

The killing of Mr. Yang Xingfo, secretary-general of the Kuomintang's Academia Sinica, in the morning of 18 June 1933 on Avenue du Roi Albert in the French Concession in Shanghai, caused a sensation at the time and was taken very seriously on all sides. It was the first bloody crime that Dai Li's agents committed inside the Shanghai concessions, and for many years afterwards it was the "masterpiece" that Juntong agents most loved to dwell on, while outsiders knew very little of what lay behind it. When the China League for Civil Rights, which Madame Sun Yat-sen had founded, was set up early in 1933, and Yang Xingfo became its vice-chairman and secretary-general, they at once became objects of Chiang Kai-shek's dislike and he wanted them out of the way. But since there were still a great many reasons for hesitating to move directly against Madame Sun, it was decided to kill a "suitable" person instead, as a way of frightening her. On top of that, Yang had gone north in the spring of 1933 on a tour of activity, proclaiming the sacred task of the League for Civil Rights, waging a determined struggle against Chiang Kai-shek's various contempts for human rights and his lawless practices, and pressing strongly for an end to the civil war and a united resistance to Japan. All this ran directly counter to Chiang Kai-shek's policy, and it is the chief reason he was murdered. Dai Li received the order to kill Yang in April or May of 1933, shortly after Yang had returned to Shanghai from Peiping, and he began his arrangements at once.

The agents first reconnoitred Yang's daily movements and learned that he lived on the upper floor of the Academia Sinica, that he was fond of riding, that he kept two good horses on Great Western Road, and that when he had time in the mornings he would go riding for an hour or two about Great Western Road and Chungshan Road. The agents judged that this was the time and the place at which they had the best and the surest chance of shooting him. But while they were preparing for that, Chiang Kai-shek indicated that he did not approve, because killing Yang outside the concessions would not achieve the object of frightening Madame Sun and might well draw criticism from every quarter and merely make trouble for the government. He insisted that it be done inside the French Concession, near Madame Sun's residence: that way the agents' reach would be demonstrated and Madame Sun intimidated, while the responsibility for solving the case would not be his. Dai Li had to change his plan, and decided to make his arrangements near the Academia Sinica, meaning to strike when Yang went out for a walk or on his way to Madame Sun's house. That area was all residential and few people passed through it. Dai Li went up to Shanghai in person early in June to direct the arrangements, his own residence being near Fenglin Bridge in the French Concession.

The man responsible for carrying out the assassination was Zhao Lijun, head of the East China district's operations group. He used the aliases Zhao Lijun and Tao Shineng, was a Sichuanese, a graduate of the fifth Whampoa class, and was responsible for the Juntong's operations work in Shanghai; during the war he was head of the operations section of the operations department at Juntong headquarters. Zhao himself lived above the Defeng Russian restaurant in the middle stretch of Avenue Joffre in the French Concession. The deputy head of the operations group, Wang Kequan, and several of its operatives lived in a three-storey house on Route Cardinal Mercier in the French Concession, where the group's office also was. The six killers who took part in this operation had all sworn an oath beforehand that they would either succeed or die with the attempt, and that if they had the misfortune to be caught they were to kill themselves at once rather than let anything out.

The agents had in fact meant to act on the morning of the seventeenth, but no sooner had they reached the neighbourhood of the Academia Sinica than they ran into a French police patrol car, and after that a squad of constables passing on their change of watch, so they dared not act and slipped away separately. A little after six on the morning of the eighteenth Zhao Lijun came in person, bringing Li Ada, Guo Decheng, Shi Yunzhi and the others. Zhao himself stayed in the car, which was parked at the corner of Avenue du Roi Albert and Rue Massenet. Li Ada, Guo Decheng and two others waited scattered about near the Academia Sinica, with a man at either end keeping watch and giving cover. At about eight o'clock, when Yang Xingfo came out into the courtyard with his son Xiaofo and was about to get into his car, the agents moved up to the gate ready to act. But Yang got in and then got out again, and the agents, supposing he had noticed something and about to rush in, saw him lead his son to another car instead. As that car came slowly out of the gate of No. 32 Avenue du Roi Albert, four pistols fired into it together. The moment Mr. Yang heard the shots he knew they had come to kill him.

More than a month earlier he had received several threatening letters and a bullet sent by the agents; he had not merely ignored them but had thrown himself into his work more energetically than before, never expecting that the agents would really dare go so far. At that instant, knowing he could not escape, and out of love for his boy, he at once threw his body over Xiaofo to shield him. So although the agents fired more than ten shots, they killed only Yang and the chauffeur, and Xiaofo took a single bullet in the leg and survived. Seeing that their object was achieved, the killers ran headlong for the car parked nearby and scrambled aboard. Zhao Lijun, hearing the shots, had already told the driver to start. Guo Decheng, in the confusion, had run the wrong way, and by the time he turned back and made after the car it was a long way off. He ran shouting, "Wait for me!" Zhao, seeing that he was still a good thirty feet behind and hearing police whistles going wildly nearby, would not stop and wait; and for fear that if Guo were taken he would let something out, Zhao fired a shot at him from the car, missed anything vital in his haste, and drove off at full speed. The killer Guo Decheng, having just carried out his task, found himself shot by his own side instead.

He tried to struggle away and save his life, but the constables closing in from every side were already upon him. Knowing there was no escape, he could only raise his pistol and shoot himself. The bullet passed through the side of his chest; though he collapsed in agony he did not die, and in the end he was taken by the constables and sent at once, along with Yang and his son, to the Guangci Hospital on Route Père Robert for emergency treatment.

Mr. Yang died soon after reaching the hospital, his wounds beyond saving. The killer Guo Decheng, after emergency treatment, was able to speak by the afternoon. Questioned by men the police station sent, he said his name was Gao Dechen — the alias he had taken on joining the Juntong — that he was a Shandong man, that he had come to Shanghai to look up a relative, and so on; but he did not yet dare tell the truth.

When Dai Li heard that Yang had been shot dead he was delighted, but on hearing that Guo Decheng had been taken alive and had given his alias he was furious. He at once notified Fan Guangzhen, the Juntong agent who served as a Chinese detective inspector in the French concession police, to take a packet of poison and use his position in the force to get near Guo. That same evening this killer too died "of wounds beyond treatment." When the papers carried the story of Yang's murder next day they mentioned only the name Gao Dechen, and said that Gao had been hit by one of his own side while the four killers fired at Yang from opposite sides, and so had been captured. On the evening of the twentieth Dai Li went back to Nanking well pleased with himself, to report to Chiang Kai-shek and claim his reward.

Besides making a hypocritical show of grief over Guo Decheng, who had given his life for him, Dai Li paid compensation to his family, and Guo Decheng's son was brought up at the Juntong's expense from then on. During the war against Japan Dai Li repeatedly held up Guo Decheng's spirit — that "his task completed and no way out, he took his own life and died a hero" — as an example to his agent-students, and praised him again and again. Later, when roads were being built inside the SACO compound at Chungking, Dai Li named one of them Guo Decheng Road.

When Mr. Yang Xingfo's body was taken to the International Funeral Parlour to be laid out, and when the memorial service was held, agents of the East China district doing intelligence work went there constantly to watch and observe who came to pay their respects and what they said while they were there. Of those who came, the one the agents watched most closely was Madame Sun Yat-sen. She came to pay her respects on the afternoon of the twentieth with two women secretaries, in the greatest grief and indignation, and spoke in very forceful terms. When a crowd of journalists surrounded her — among them several agents using a reporter's cover — she said that she had already issued a statement on the affair, announcing to the whole world that this was a planned and organized political assassination, and that she would not be frightened by methods of that kind. Others who came to pay their respects included Lu Xun, He Xiangning, Shen Junru and Li Siguang. The agents watched these men closely too, and everything about their coming and everything they said there was gathered up and sent to Nanking to be passed on to Chiang Kai-shek.

A great deal was said outside at the time about why Mr. Shi Liangcai was assassinated. From what I know, the main reason was that Chiang Kai-shek had received intelligence that Shi was at that time much in sympathy with the Communists and had helped the Shanghai underground Party organization with money, so he determined to have him killed and to warn other people sympathetic to the Communists by it. Besides that, the *Shen Bao*, which Shi Liangcai ran, regularly carried comment critical of the darkness of Chiang's rule.

When the East China district's operations group was ordered to kill Shi Liangcai, Dai Li said that after Shi became chairman of the Shanghai municipal council he had plotted a movement for Shanghai's independence, meaning to take the city out from under Kuomintang rule. When I was revising the Juntong's operational-technique teaching material at Chungking during the war, I knew perfectly well that Shi Liangcai had been murdered because of his connections with the Chinese Communist Party; yet in citing the case in class I still said he had been running an independence movement for Shanghai with Japanese backing, and that this was "the crime of betraying the country and selling out the interests of the nation," because I dared not let the students know the real reason. Had it been merely that the *Shen Bao* had published some anti-Chiang comment and annoyed the CC clique, the Juntong would not have done the killing at all: it would have been carried out by the Zhongtong, which the CC clique directed. Dai Li was at loggerheads with the Zhongtong throughout, and would never have done their work for them.

In October 1934 Shi Liangcai went to Hangchow to be treated for a stomach complaint, which gave the agents the best possible opening. He had always been on his guard against the reactionaries, and besides using a car fitted with protection he kept private guards — bodyguards, as Shanghai people call them. Dai Li had received Chiang Kai-shek's order to assassinate him in the summer or autumn of that year. The original intention was to act inside the Shanghai concessions, and premises were looked for at one point near the *Shen Bao* office and near Shi's house; but nothing suitable was found, and Dai Li went up to Shanghai himself to make the arrangements. What he feared most was another affair like the killing of Yang Xingfo, in which a killer could not get away and let something out, causing serious trouble. Afterwards the agents watching Shi got to know his chauffeur through Shanghai gang connections and learned that Shi went regularly to Hangchow, and Dai Li then decided not to act inside the Shanghai concessions at all.

Before long Shi Liangcai did indeed take his family to Hangchow, and Dai Li went down again with Zhao Lijun and the others to make the arrangements. The first idea was to watch for an opportunity near Shi's house at the Qiushui Villa; but it was considered that if Shi were assassinated inside the city of Hangchow the head of the provincial capital police bureau could not escape being implicated and would be blamed on all sides, and the case would have to be solved. That police bureau was not merely headed by a Juntong agent, Zhao Longwen: the whole of it was in Juntong hands, and to commit the crime in Hangchow would have been to make trouble for ourselves. So in the end a place outside the city was chosen.

Besides Zhao Lijun, the agents who took part in this criminal operation were the deputy head of the operations group, Wang Kequan — an Anhui man who during the war was deputy head of the inspectorate of the Chungking Garrison Command and afterwards killed himself — and the operatives Li Ada, a habitual criminal of the northern Kiangsu gang in Shanghai and the group's principal killer, together with Shi Yizhi, Xu Jianye and others, six men in all, and the driver Zhang Bingwu, a Hopei man who during the war commanded the Juntong's motor brigade. The car they used was an old open Buick which the Juntong regularly used for assassinations and kidnappings, normally kept at the Juntong's contact point at No. 53 Ji'e Alley in Nanking.

A day or two before Shi Liangcai's decision to return to Shanghai from Hangchow in his own car on 14 November, the agents had the news from the chauffeur. Zhao Lijun went first to look over the place for the attack, and chose a spot on the Shanghai-Hangchow highway near Bo'ai in the fourth district of Haining county, about four li from Wengjiabu. The agents had prepared two temporary number plates: one, Peking No. 72, was simply a forgery — it was only learned afterwards that it belonged to the Southern Salt Industry Bank — and the other was a trade plate of the Hangchow police bureau. The car had been driven down from Nanking to Hangchow around the end of October and had stood ever since in the garage of the Chekiang police academy at Shangcangqiao in Hangchow. During the killing the Peking 72 plate was used; on the way back to Nanking the trade plate was substituted; and only close to Nanking were the original plates put back, the car reaching Ji'e Alley in the middle of the night. Zhang Bingwu talked to me about this more than once. The Juntong's older drivers always called that car the "meritorious car," since the Juntong had very few cars at the time and it was in constant use. During the war Dai Li even had it driven to Chungking, though it was seldom used after that.

The pistols the killers carried this time were all Mausers and "Powerful" model pistols with great penetrating power, so that they could shoot through the protection on Shi's car. On the day of the shooting the agents went to wait in position very early. When Shi's car reached the spot they had fixed on, it found another car standing across the road, feigning a breakdown and under repair. Seeing Shi's car come up, the agents signalled with their hands for it to slow down while drawing their pistols and firing at its tyres, and at the same moment two of them, detailed for the purpose, fired at the chauffeur and at Deng Zuxun, a fellow student of Shi's son who was sitting beside him. This was because the agents had mistaken Deng for Shi's bodyguard and were afraid he would return fire, and because they were afraid the chauffeur would afterwards give away the agents he knew, so he had to be killed first to close his mouth.

With the bullets flying, Shi Liangcai and his son Shi Yonggeng scrambled out of the car and ran off in different directions. The killers mistook the son for the father, and three of them went after Shi Yonggeng, firing more than twenty rounds without hitting him; he got clean away across the fields. Shi Liangcai, being in poor health, ran more slowly, and in his confusion took refuge in a thatched hut nearby. When two agents came pressing after him, Shi went out again by the back door and hid in a dried-up pond behind the house, where Zhao Lijun, directing operations from the road, caught sight of him. Shouting "He is here!" Zhao fired shot after shot at him, and one bullet struck Shi in the head; he fell at once. Li Ada then ran up and fired another shot into him at close quarters, and the blood poured out. Seeing their object achieved, the killers gathered, climbed into the car and drove off at speed. About half an hour after the shooting stopped, Shi Yonggeng found some people nearby and came back with them to the scene, and borrowed a lorry from the aviation school to carry the three bodies back to Hangchow. After this bloody affair the whole country was shocked and reproaches came from every side.

Chiang Kai-shek and Wang Jingwei, playing cat and mouse, issued stern orders to the provinces of Kiangsu and Chekiang and to the municipalities of Shanghai and Hangchow to arrest the killers, and offered a reward of ten thousand yuan. The agents meanwhile drew a reward of five thousand yuan from Chiang Kai-shek's own hand. Afterwards, when they met, they used to joke about it and say: "If we solved our own case, we could pick up another five thousand in reward money!"
