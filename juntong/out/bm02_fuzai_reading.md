## Appendix: An Interview with Shen Zui

### I Have Come to Understand the Principle of Being a Person

### —An Interview with Shen Zui, a Newly Added CPPCC Member

### By Hu Sisheng, People's Daily reporter

Not a few Chinese of middle age and older know the name Shen Zui. In the 1940s he served successively as a major-general department head in the Kuomintang's Juntong and as the Defence Ministry's commissioner for the Yunnan region and concurrently chief of the Bureau of Secrets Preservation's Yunnan station — a secret-service chief through and through. Now he attends the meeting as a newly added CPPCC member, in his own words "filled with mingled gratitude and shame."

Shen Zui joined the Kuomintang secret-service organization at eighteen and became a major-general department head in his twenties, planning and carrying out in person such secret-service work as arrests, torture and assassination — a plain historical fact he himself admits. But there is one historical fact that Shen Zui did not, of his own accord, raise during his imprisonment after liberation: that in December 1949, seeing our great army pressing south and Chiang Kai-shek's cause lost, he took part at the last moment in Lu Han's Yunnan uprising. Last year, after investigation by the relevant authorities, various material evidence confirmed that Shen Zui had signed the uprising telegram, made the uprising broadcast, and in his own hand issued orders that the secret-service organizations under him hand over their equipment and report to a designated place. Under the Party's policy, his own status was changed last year from war criminal to uprising general, and his administrative grade was raised. Shen Zui said to the reporter: "This time they have specially invited me to be a member of the National CPPCC too — how could I not be filled with mingled gratitude and shame?" Late last year Shen Zui, accompanying his only daughter on the mainland, Shen Meijuan, to visit relatives in Hong Kong, became an object of attention in Hong Kong and Taiwan alike. In the early 1950s Taiwan had declared Shen Zui a martyr who had "given his life for party and state," his tablet "enshrined in the Shrine of the Loyal and Righteous," and paid his children in Taiwan a monthly pension. To the surprise of the other side of the strait, Shen Zui was not only alive but had come south with his daughter to Hong Kong, enjoying full freedom of movement. A Hong Kong newspaper came to the hotel where he was staying to ask for an article, and Shen Zui drafted it on the spot. The man who had come for the article asked, weighing his words: "You didn't consult Beijing — you dare decide for yourself?" Shen Zui answered with a smile: "I am responsible for what I write; no one has ever told me how to write." A few former colleagues urged him to seize the chance to flee far away, saying with a double meaning: "The sea of bitterness is boundless; turn your head and there is the shore." Shen Zui understood their meaning and answered: "I accept." The men were overjoyed: "When will you go? We'll charter you a plane." Then he turned droll and spoke plainly: "No need for a plane; the train will do. Come and see the mainland — my shore is in Beijing." When some tempted him with money and goods, Shen Zui said: "Material life on the mainland is a little poorer, but I did not come for money. My mother told me in her lifetime: a man may hold no office, but he must be a person. On the mainland I have truly come to understand the principle of life. In the past, for advancement and wealth, I could throw away all conscience; now you could not move me with gold." The man grew angry: "Then go back to the mainland and gnaw on cornbread!" Shen Zui answered very calmly: "Come and see what I eat on the mainland; and if I really must eat cornbread, I do so gladly." His relatives had also hoped his thirty-five-year-old daughter Shen Meijuan would stay in Hong Kong to "enjoy ease," but she said: "I do not want ready-made ease. I want to go back to the mainland with my father. A country of a billion people is sure to be built up well."

Shen Zui and his daughter visited relatives and friends in Hong Kong for over a month. His daughter in America and his nephew and niece-in-law in Canada came from afar to see the father and uncle they had been parted from for over thirty years; but to his regret, his several children in Taiwan, though close at hand, could not get permission to leave and meet him. Shen Zui said to the reporter: "In 1949, when Chiang Kai-shek sent me to Yunnan to carry out his mission, Mao Renfeng sent my mother, my wife and my six children to Hong Kong as hostages in all but name. And when my daughter and I applied to visit Hong Kong, the Party and the government fully trusted me; yet my sons in Taiwan cannot leave — my feelings about this are many."

The talk turned naturally to the question of reunifying Taiwan and the mainland.

Shen Zui said: "Now that the Communists have issued the call for a third round of Kuomintang-Communist cooperation, those of us who understand the history are deeply moved. When Japan invaded China, the Communists set aside old grievances, called for a united front against the enemy, and carried out Kuomintang-Communist cooperation. When Chiang Kai-shek was detained at Xi'an by Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng, it was Zhou Enlai who hurried from Yenan to save his life and so brought about the second Kuomintang-Communist cooperation. This is the united-front policy — what is there to fear in it? Today the Communists do not set themselves up as victors but propose reopening equal Kuomintang-Communist talks — how broad a mind that is!"

When they parted in 1949, Shen Zui had some forty-odd close relatives in Hong Kong, Taiwan, America, Canada and elsewhere; now they have become more than eighty. He urged them all to come and see the mainland, to see its changes.

Shen Zui has many old friends and relatives in Taiwan, and a great many former students besides. He said: "The nation's division was brought about in our generation, and should be ended in our generation. Then, living, we can face our descendants, and dead, we can face our ancestors. Whatever different roads we walked in the past, from today on, whether a man leaves a fragrance for a hundred ages or a stench for ten thousand years depends on whether he put out effort for the reunification of the motherland or the opposite. If anyone doubts it, I can come forward as witness: though I did no few bad things in the past — one might even say my major-general's post was bought with the blood of Communists — yet the moment I did one thing of benefit to the motherland, the Communists and the people let bygones be bygones and gave me good political and material treatment besides. This is no 'united-front propaganda,' but a living fact."

(Originally published in the People's Daily, 11 December 1981)
