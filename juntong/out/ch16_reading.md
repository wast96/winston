## A Case of Framing the Communists

### A Sidelight on the Assassination of Li Gongpu and Wen Yiduo

In July 1946, after the two gentlemen Li Gongpu and Wen Yiduo were assassinated one after another in Kunming, many people held that it was the work of Juntong agents. I was then head of the General Affairs Department of the Juntong Bureau and knew nothing of the inside of the matter; even the man at Bureau headquarters in charge of that kind of work was in the dark. When Chiang Kai-shek telephoned long-distance from Lushan to Nanking to demand an account of Mao Renfeng, Mao too could not say who had done it, and could only say he had not ordered it. Chiang, it was said, was very angry over the affair, for he was just then plotting to launch full-scale civil war while feigning democracy on the surface, still meaning to deceive the whole nation; and such a move — beating the grass and startling the snake — had exposed his hand.

After these bloody cases came one on the heels of another, opinion across the country was in an uproar and reproaches poured in. Chiang Kai-shek then sent the newly appointed national police director-general Tang Zong to Kunming to investigate thoroughly, and ordered that any recurrence of such incidents be sternly prevented.

Having received his orders, Tang Zong first worked out a scheme with Mao Renfeng, and only then flew from Nanking to Chungking. By then most of the Juntong Bureau had moved back to Nanking and was busy being reorganized into the Bureau of Secrets Preservation; in Chungking there remained only a few senior agents and some of the staff winding things up. In Chungking Tang convened the Military Affairs Commission's postal-and-air censorship chief Li Xiaobai, the Juntong Bureau's third-section chief Zheng Xiuyuan — who was in charge of assassination and the like — the SACO motor corps commander Xu Jianye, and me, who was then clearing up Juntong property in Chungking, to confer.

Tang Zong was very anxious. He feared that if it could not be made clear who had done it, it would be too great a disgrace for him as national police director-general; at the same time he meant to turn the thing to account and, on this pretext, frame the Communists once. In our discussions it was decided first to prepare one or two men in Chungking to take to Kunming, to have them confess at the open trial that they were the killers, and moreover to say haltingly that they had ties to the Communists — yet not to admit outright to being Communists. In this way the whole nation would be made to see it was not the Nationalists' doing, while it would be implied that the Communists had put men up to it. To keep the Communists from demanding to take part in the trial or to rebut it, the thing had to be thought out with the greatest care, so that no telltale trace showed.

That day Tang decided I should go to the two lockups, Baigongguan and the Bath Cave (Zhazidong), to pick out from the Juntong men held there for breaches of discipline someone to stand in as the culprit. He told me in all seriousness that the man who took the part must be an agent who had never held any public post, and one who could stand up under repeated testing without recanting. Tang also let out that this "killer," after several interrogations, would at once be sentenced to death, but at the execution would be swapped out and another prisoner already sentenced to death or to life in a Kunming prison put in his place. He promised, too, that any Juntong man willing to take the part would — whatever his crime and however long his term — be released at once on completing this task of the false killer, and be sent to the Northeast or Northwest to a better post by way of reward.

I went that night to the two lockups to make my choice, and among the twenty-odd Juntong agents held there found only one, a man surnamed Wang who had been a copyist in the secretariat of Bureau headquarters, who met the conditions; but he was very timid and feared that once the death sentence was pronounced it would be carried out on the spot before he could be swapped out. He knew all too well that in such a situation the senior agents could make a sacrificial victim of him as easily as they took a meal. I reassured him again and again, telling him that everything was in our hands and the timing of the execution entirely ours to decide, and that no one would ever make out that we had put another in his place. I also took it on myself to add a bonus of five taels of gold, and only then did he agree. When I reported to Tang Zong the next day, he said one man was not enough and two would be best. I promised to pick another from the agents doing secret work at the Chungking station. He pressed me to have it done soon, for he could not stay long in Chungking — Chiang Kai-shek had told him to fly to Kunming at once.

At noon that day, while I was still making my selection, Tang Zong suddenly sent men everywhere to find me and had me come to lunch at his lodging — the mansion Dai Li had built at Shenxiandong in Chungking. When I arrived, the three of them — Li Xiaobai, Zheng Xiuyuan and Xu Jianye — were already there. On seeing me Tang said there was no need to find more men; we would go to Kunming first, for Mao Renfeng had telephoned to say the Yunnan station had found a lead. After lunch the five of us went together to the airfield, where a few passengers were pulled off on the spot to let us take that day's flight to Kunming.

When we reached Kunming, the Yunnan police-affairs chief Li Yuzhen, the Kunming police chief Gong Shaoxia, and the Yunnan station chief Wang Wei met us and took us to Li Yuzhen's home, where a meeting was held at once. Wang Wei reported that the Kunming group of the Yunnan station had already identified six or seven of the men who had killed Li and Wen, all of them from the special-agent battalion and inspection section of the Yunnan Garrison Headquarters, and that the killers' posts and names, the prior arrangements, and how the deed was done were all clear. Hearing this, Tang decided we need not come forward, and that he would first talk with the garrison commander-in-chief Huo Kuizhang and decide what to do according to how it went. For he and Huo were fellow townsmen from Lingxian in Hunan and had always been on good private terms, and he wished to avoid provoking Huo and stirring up trouble. Tang further decided that he himself would stay at the home, in Fuxing New Village, of Lu Genfan, the third boss of the Lugenji Construction firm, while the rest of us stayed at Li Yuzhen's home, meeting each evening to talk things over.

The next day Tang Zong talked with Huo Kuizhang for a whole morning, without result. Huo would not admit the thing at all, and even asked Tang to help him crack the case. At this Tang finally said he had brought several seasoned Juntong investigators who could help with the inquiry. At the same time Tang reported to Chiang Kai-shek all that the Yunnan station had found, explaining that Huo was trying to deny the case, so that he found it very awkward to handle. We urged that the Yunnan station's material be put to Huo to see how he would react, but Tang Zong would not have it done, fearing it would drive the matter to deadlock and leave no way out.

Over the following days, though Tang hinted to Huo that he knew the affair involved men of the Garrison Headquarters, Huo still flatly denied it. Because the democratic parties with one voice demanded that the killers be punished and the lives of democratic figures guaranteed, Chiang Kai-shek then sent Gu Zhutong to Kunming to look into it as well. At the same time Chiang summoned Huo Kuizhang to Lushan to question him face to face, and Huo had no choice but to admit that his subordinates had done it. Chiang merely dismissed Huo, and had the special-agent battalion company commander Tang Shiliang and platoon leader Li Wenshan publicly shot to close the case. In fact these two men proclaimed as shot were also impersonated by others.

Before this, Huo Kuizhang had meant to use the affair for a piece of killing with a borrowed knife — to deceive opinion and shift the blame, and to seize the chance to deal a heavy blow to Yunnan's local forces. To that end he once put it about that it had been done by Long San, the third son of the former Yunnan chairman Long Yun, and at once began making arrests. Getting wind of it, Long San fled overnight to his old home at Zhaotong to hide; but several old subordinates connected with him were taken. Tang Zong, fearing this would only make more trouble, advised Huo not to do it. In the end Huo took from prison two false killers who had been sentenced to death but not executed, to stand in for the special-agent battalion company commander Tang Shiliang and platoon leader Li Wenshan, and had them shot. Before the execution they first got these two scapegoats drunk on wine; along the route the guard was strict, and after the shooting they were buried at once. Meanwhile the real killers — the Garrison Headquarters intelligence chief Wang Zimin, who had directed the crime on Huo Kuizhang's order, together with Tang Shiliang, Li Wenshan and more than ten others, and their families, over twenty in all — had already been sent by Huo Kuizhang overnight by special vehicle to Dali and handed to Wang Lingyun, the second-army commander and western-Yunnan garrison commander. Wang Lingyun lodged them in the Wuwei Temple on the hill behind Dali and entertained them with good food and wine. Not until 1947, when Wang Lingyun's unit was transferred to Wuhan and reorganized as the Reorganized Ninth Division, were Wang Zimin and the rest received by men sent by the succeeding Yunnan garrison commander He Shaozhou and kept in protection still.

Afterwards I learned from Huo's chief of staff Liu Yige, his staff-office chief Guo Yeru, and the second-army commander Wang Lingyun, among others, that the aim of killing these democratic figures had been to curry favour with Chiang Kai-shek, in the hope of being made Yunnan provincial chairman. Huo was an important pillar of the Chen Cheng clique, and had gathered from Chen Cheng that Chiang Kai-shek had always hated to the bone the democratic figures who sympathized with the Communists; he had imagined that this would win him still more of Chiang's favour, never thinking it would bring him the opposite fate. During the war Huo had been garrison commander of the Dongting Lake district, when I was inspection chief at Changde and knew him well. After his dismissal in 1947 he lived as a gentleman of leisure in a newly built villa, "Song Manor," outside the Xiaowu Gate of Changsha, and I once went to see him. Speaking to me of the affair, he held only that the time had not been ripe and he had acted a little too early, and that this was what had brought the trouble on. With boundless feeling he said: "Had it been done today, it would not be a fault but a merit." Only after Liberation were Tang Shiliang, Li Wenshan and the rest run down and brought into the net of the people's law.
