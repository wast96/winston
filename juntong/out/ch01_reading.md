## Inside the Juntong's Training of Agents

*The breeding of secret agents, seen through the Juntong's Linli special training class*

### How the Linli Class Came to Be

The founding of the Juntong's first training class for agents on a really large scale, the Linli Special Training Class — the Linli class, for short — had its own particular purpose and its own particular reasons.

No sooner had Chiang Kai-shek been forced into resisting Japan than Dai Li, the Juntong's chief, set about carrying out his master's orders: using resistance to Japan as the pretext, he began energetically preparing for work whose muzzles pointed inward. After fighting broke out in Shanghai on 13 August, Dai Li's original plan had been to run a large training class for agents in Shanghai or Nanking, but he had not foreseen how quickly both cities would fall into enemy hands, and nothing came of it. In the winter of 1937, having fled to Wuhan, he sent Yu Lexing and Xie Ligong — who had been running the training class for armed secret-service cadres of the Jiangsu-Zhejiang Action Committee in Songjiang and Qingpu, the two counties near Shanghai — down to Hunan to get the preparations moving, and issued a written order to Li Renshi, the Juntong's Hunan station chief, to give them every possible assistance.

In January 1938 Yu Lexing arrived back in Changsha and at once cleared out the whole of a large Western-style house, the Rong Garden at No. 4 Xuming Lane, Tian'etang, outside the south gate of Changsha, to serve as the preparatory office, where work went on hard, day and night. Among the senior agents Dai Li picked out for the class were Xie Ligong, Wang Chongwu, Lu Suichu, Wang Banlian, He Yuan, Xu Yongnian and Wu Jingzhong. Every one of these key men was a Communist renegade who had studied in the Soviet Union. That fact alone tells you plainly enough what kind of class it was going to be.

At first the intention was to site the class somewhere near Changsha, but as Japanese air raids made that unsafe, Li Renshi, the Hunan station chief, settled on the county middle school at Linli, north of Changde. Dai Li sent Yang Jirong, head of the Juntong's First Department, and Yu Lexing to look the place over; they judged it convenient for transport and yet out of the way, with roomy buildings besides, and it was decided to put the class there.

The moment the preparatory office was set up, the Juntong's personnel section circulated an order to every provincial station, sub-station and group in the field, instructing every agent to recommend reliable friends and relatives for training. The conditions were: politically reliable, of "clean" family background, either sex, aged between eighteen and twenty-five, educated to junior middle school or above, in good health, with no conspicuous distinguishing features and no hidden ailments. It was further laid down that serving Juntong personnel, at headquarters or in the field, might also be sent for training if they wished to go — such men being useful for keeping an eye on the students and reporting on them. In practice the age limit was soon disregarded, and there were a good many in their thirties. The circular also required that students put forward by each place be vetted first by the head of the provincial station or group, to prevent members of hostile parties from slipping in. If a student turned out badly later, the unit that had selected or sponsored him — a serving officer sent for training was said to be "sponsored" — bore the whole responsibility.

Since it was difficult at that time for the provinces of the north, the south-east and the north-west to send students to Hunan, only the provinces of central and south-western China were asked to do so. In fact most of the students came from another source altogether: young refugees, out of school and out of work, whom Liang Ganqiao, head of the Juntong's Zhengzhou office, had recruited in Shandong, Jiangsu and Henan. Liang Ganqiao was at that time a man of large personal ambition, and meant to use the chance to build up a following of his own. Anti-Communist though this Trotskyite bandit was, and exceptionally energetic about it, Dai Li was never willing to let him develop a force of his own. So when Liang had gone to enormous trouble to recruit nearly a thousand young people, Dai Li required him to send the lot of them to Hunan for training and forbade him to run a class himself.

When this body of students left Zhengzhou for Hunan around February 1938, a good many of the Henan natives quietly slipped away, and by the time they boarded the train only some eight hundred were left. Yang Qingzhi, Wang Banlian and I were escorting them, and when we passed through Wuhan I went to see Dai Li, who questioned me closely about where they had come from. I told him they had all been recruited through agents' friends and relatives and had been vetted once already; that politically there was mostly nothing wrong with them; and that it was only their schooling that varied wildly, some having finished senior middle school and others no more than higher primary. His instruction to me then was that provided there was nothing wrong politically, the weaker ones should be kept on too, since once trained they could be used for operations. What he bitterly regretted at that moment was not having recruited young people in bulk everywhere before the places fell, because the Communists would take the chance to draw them in, which meant fewer men for his own side and more for "the enemy."

Talking about the work ahead, Dai Li said what he lacked above everything was cadres, because the work was going to be ten or a hundred times what it had been, and so he wanted to take in as many as could possibly be got. Of this body of students, more slipped away along the road from Wuhan to Changsha, so that not even eight hundred arrived; and apart from a very few who really did not meet the conditions, nearly all of them were kept. The students sent in from the various provinces came to fewer than three hundred. When the Linli special training class opened, the total was more than eleven hundred, and Dai Li still thought that too few — above all he thought the proportion of women too small, under one in ten. He therefore arranged matters with Hu Zongnan and insisted on being handed the whole of a body of some sixty women students who had been recruited in Changsha under the name of the Central Military Academy's Seventh Branch. Since there could be no open recruiting, the agents pulled in candidates by every means they could devise, and it was laid down that students might introduce students in order to swell the numbers; the result was a whole crop of oddities in that class, husbands and wives as fellow students, and even mothers and daughters. Among the married couples whose names I remember are Liao Zhongwen and Zhang Zhikun, Li Jiaji and Chen Zerong, and Yi Yuda and Zhou Chong; among the mothers and daughters, Chen Yuehua and Ma Youwen. As for brothers, brothers and sisters, and sisters together, there were more still, and I shall not list them here.

I had known most of the students of this class since the winter of 1937, when I helped Liang Ganqiao with the recruiting at Zhengzhou. Afterwards I escorted them to Linli, stayed on at the class as an instructor, and handled general affairs for a period, so I came to know that intake fairly well. I taught part-time at more than twenty Juntong training classes, but Linli was the only one where I was a full-time instructor, and my connection with its students is therefore the closest of any. After they graduated, a good many of them worked alongside me for years. And after Dai Li's death, at the request of some of them, I became chairman of a little clique of theirs called the Binli Alumni Association. So I have a fairly clear picture of that class from its founding to its graduation, of how its people were posted, and of the part its graduates went on to play in the Juntong's secret-service organization.

The workings of this one training class also show a good deal of the Juntong's inner life and its criminal activities. The agent-students it turned out numbered only about a thousand, yet the harm they did the revolution, and the crimes they committed against the Communists and against the people in Chiang's gang, were very great indeed. Among the Juntong men who fled to Taiwan these people are still an important core, and they carry on as enemies of the people to this day.

### Organization and Personnel

The class was at first called the Special Training Class of the Military Affairs Commission, and that was the name used while it was being prepared and when it first opened; but the Commission's own secretariat refused to recognize it and never issued it a seal, so the preparatory office simply cut a rubber stamp for itself. Dai Li at that time also held the chairmanship of the board of the Central Police Academy, and with an eye to seizing control of the national police he formally renamed the class, in April 1938, the Training Class for Special Police Personnel of the Central Police Academy. Inside the Juntong, though, it went on being called the Linli class, and its graduates were listed as the first intake of the Central Police Academy's special training class, or the first special class for short.

The class was formally founded in February 1938, when it moved from the preparatory office in Changsha to the county middle school in the Guixing Building at Linli; teaching proper began in March, and it was then that the students from Zhengzhou and elsewhere arrived one group after another. Before classes started, students and staff together put the buildings in order and ran up a temporary assembly hall of thatch and bamboo scaffolding big enough to hold over a thousand people. Behind the hall the deputy director, Yu Lexing, wanting to please Dai Li, built a walk named after Dai Li's style-name — the Yunong Embankment — as a place for the students to take their ease.

From the founding of the class to its close Dai Li went there only twice, yet every important appointment and every part of the curriculum was decided by him personally. From March to June 1938 came what was called recruit training, which in practice meant vetting the students politically and investigating their family backgrounds, their opinions and their conduct, and considering, in the light of each one's particular qualities, what sort of work he would suit later on; from June the training was divided by unit. This "recruit training" was not like that of an ordinary military school: after a month of basic military instruction, courses in the various branches of secret-service general knowledge were added by degrees. Professional training proper only began when the students were divided by unit and by speciality. The organization described here is the one that existed after that division.

The directorship of the class was held by Dai Li himself. The post of deputy director was filled at the beginning and at the end by Yu Lexing, and for a short period in between by the corps commander, Tao Yishan. Around May or June, soon after Tao had been appointed corps commander and had reached Linli, Dai Li — who was far from easy in his mind about Yu Lexing — telegraphed an order for Tao to take over the deputy directorship, leaving Yu to be dean of studies and nothing else. The change caused resentment among most of the staff and many of the students, and within a month Dai telegraphed again for Tao to hand the deputy directorship back, so that Yu returned to it as before, with the chief instructor Xie Ligong doubling as dean of studies to leave Yu free to get on with his work.

Although Dai Li did not come and work there, a director's office was set up all the same, with Ding Ruoping appointed as its secretary, as a check on the deputy director. Besides Ding, this director's office contained another secretary, Liao Gongxia, whom Yu Lexing had brought in, and two clerks, Wang Xiao'ou and Wang Zichang. Below the director and deputy director came the chief instructor, Xie Ligong. He and Yu Lexing were both well-known "training specialists" in the Juntong.

Besides the office of the dean of studies there were a political training office and a general affairs office. The head of political training, Wang Zuhua, was a celebrated "anti-Communist propagandist" of the Juntong; political education and the political instructors of the battalions and companies were all under him, and besides being responsible for pumping in fascist doctrine, his more important business was anti-Communist propaganda and the political vetting of the students.

The head of general affairs, Wang Xiang, had been a county magistrate for many years, and after the class moved to Qianyang, Dai Li had him made magistrate of Qianyang county.

The Juntong ran several dozen training classes of one kind or another during the war against Japan, and the Linli special class was the only one organized in three offices; later classes all called their units sections instead.

Under the class headquarters there was also an accounts room, whose head was Liu Yusi, with Xu Hansheng as accountant and Zhou Ju'en as cashier.

There was also a medical room, with a director surnamed Liu, two doctors and two nurses. All of these had been brought in on the recommendation of some agent or other rather than posted by the Juntong.

At that time only the general affairs office had a sub-section under it, the affairs sub-section, headed successively by me and by Xu Yongnian, with Su Zhentong, Liu Tong, Yang Guangwang, Yao Ruixiang and Wang Yawen as its staff. None of the other offices had sub-sections or sub-departments then, and none had deputy heads. Under the dean of studies came teaching officers, and under political training, political training officers.

The students were administered by the corps headquarters. The appointment of a corps commander was long delayed, and in the early period the deputy commander, Wang Lepo, acted in the post; only in May was Tao Yishan named to it.

Under the corps headquarters were two battalions and an independent women's company. The commander of the First Battalion was Wang Banlian, his deputy Chen Zhendong, the battalion political instructor Wu Shangying, and the adjutant Dong Wei.

The First Battalion had three companies under it. The First Company was commanded by Duan Jiangong with He Jixiang as political instructor; when the specialities were divided out this became an intelligence company. The Second Company was commanded by Xiang Lian with Wu Jingzhong as political instructor, and was also an intelligence company. The Third Company was commanded by Luo Xing with Fu Youquan as political instructor, and this one studied operations exclusively.

The Second Battalion likewise had three companies, with Yang Qingzhi as commander, Wang Baigang as deputy, He Yuan as battalion instructor and Shen Zhixiong as adjutant. The Fourth Company was commanded by Jin Shuyun with Xu Wenyuan as political instructor, and studied military espionage and staff work exclusively, whence it was also called the espionage-staff company. The Fifth Company was commanded by Chen Judong with Huang Kangyong as political instructor. The Sixth Company was commanded by Lin Tingji with Jin Changman as political instructor. Both of these studied guerrilla fighting and were also called the military companies.

The independent women's company was commanded first by An Zhanjiang. This woman agent was famous for her ferocity, and even her husband, Xu Yuyi, had been on the receiving end of her pistol. After she was transferred, Wu Hangkun took over. Its political instructors, in succession, were Wu Meihua, Zhao Shiying, Peng Jiacai and Wu Kuiyuan, all of them well-known women agents of the Juntong.

The standard for choosing who went into which speciality was this: students educated to senior middle school or above who were also reasonably quick and alert were picked for the intelligence company; those who were strong in body and bolder than they were learned went into the operations company; those with some grounding in military science went into the espionage-staff company; and the rest, the least schooled, went into the military companies to learn guerrilla fighting. Women students were assigned to the intelligence and operations companies according to each one's particular case. In July two further specialities, telecommunications and accounting, were added, and most of the women were picked to study these. On the face of it the students' own wishes were canvassed and they chose freely; in reality the decisions had been made beforehand, and where a student's own choice did not suit, he was assigned to whatever the class headquarters and the corps had already decided on.

Each company was further divided into three platoons. The platoon commanders whose names I remember are Wang Gang, Su Ziwen, Chen Weicheng, Wu Ying, Gao Shang, Liu Rentian, Yi Xuezhong, Yao Fengguan and Lan Jiuxi.

As for the instructors: demolition was taught by Liu Shaofu, formerly manager of the Gong county arsenal, and by Huang Linyu, the Juntong's specialist in demolition work, with an assistant, Wu Zhijiang, to supervise the students' practical work. Intelligence was taught by the chief instructor Xie Ligong and also by Lu Suichu and Dan Jia. Secret-service general knowledge was taught by the deputy director Yu Lexing and by four men just back from studying secret-service work in Germany and Italy: Fan Xuewen, Jiang Zhennan, Feng Wenyao and Wang Weizhou. Operational technique was taught by me, with the unarmed-combat instructor Jin Minjie, a Korean, the Chinese-boxing instructor Wu Shitai, and Liu Rence, who taught the use of the arresting-cord and nothing else. Photography was taught by Wang Wenzhao, photographer to the Juntong and to Dai Li privately. Sign language and signal codes were taught by Nie Jubiao and others. Besides secret-service technique the class laid great weight on anti-Communist political education, which was taught mainly by the head of political training, Wang Zuhua, by the renegade Liao Huaping, who had once been a political instructor at the Whampoa academy, and by the political instructors of the battalions and companies. Dai Li also sent Chen Xudong and Wu Sai, former subordinates of General Zhang Xueliang, to teach political and military subjects respectively, and engaged You Fengchi, an instructor in military geography at the Army Staff College, to teach part-time. Later, when the two specialities of telecommunications and accounting were added, the Juntong's telecommunications office sent Lin Yumin and others to take charge of that side of the training. Accounting and statistics were also taught by Wei Wenhai, who had studied in France.

Besides this large body of full-time and part-time instructors, Dai Li was constantly sending some senior Juntong agent to the class for a few days' teaching. Those who went at that period included the Hunan station chief Li Renshi, the head of the Juntong's First Department Yang Jirong, and Fu Shenglan and Li Guobin, both of whom had at one time acted as the Juntong's secretary-general. Senior agents who had been earmarked for work in the areas the Japanese had occupied were not only told to go and teach for a few days but were allowed, while they were there, to pick out among the women students whichever one they fancied as a "working wife." Li Guobin, for instance, who was being sent to Wuhan as district chief, picked a Hunanese woman student, Cheng ⸻, for his "working wife." Fu Shenglan, who was sent to take charge in Shanghai and later went over to the enemy as puppet mayor of Hangchow, likewise took a "working wife" away with him from this class.

When Dai Li set up the Linli class he brought together, as he often told me afterwards, virtually every "man of talent" the Juntong then had. Looking at what came later, one may say it was true. A great many of those who worked at Linli went on to be the mainstays of his other training classes: Liao Huaping as deputy director of the Lanzhou special class, the military instructor Wu Lang later as deputy director of the Qianyang special class, Jin Shuyun as deputy director of the south-eastern special class, Liu Shaofu and Wang Lepo one after the other as deputy directors of the demolition class, Tao Yishan as deputy director of the Chungking special class, Wei Wenhai as deputy director of the accounting class, Jin Minjie as deputy director of the operations class, and so on.

Dai Li was so fond of this class that his fondness extended to everything about it. Even a so-called class song, which the class had adopted more or less as a joke, became not only the class song of every later special training class but the "bureau song" of the Juntong itself. Every Juntong agent of the old days could sing it. It was sung not only at the close of the annual "April the First Assembly" but at the weekly "memorial meeting" and at any gathering whatever. Plenty of agents were unclear why a class song had to be sung at every meeting, when the meeting was not the training class's and those present had never been its students. The reason was simple: Dai Li had taken a liking to it. On his first visit to Linli he heard the students singing it and immediately called for the words to be brought to him. The song opens by calling on the young men of the revolution to make themselves ready and to be complete in wisdom, benevolence and courage, and that was enough for him at once. He was more pleased still with two lines in the middle — "safeguard the security of our leader, defend the territory and sovereignty of the state" — which he read over and over, pronouncing them very well put and very apt to the Juntong's situation. And so he ruled that this song was to be sung at every gathering.

The birth of that song is a laughable business. It was put together by Jiang Zhennan, an instructor just back from studying in Germany, who had learned the party anthem of the German fascists there and adapted it. When he first produced this masterpiece of his at a staff meeting, he had nearly all the several dozen instructors and officers present laughing out loud; he pressed on regardless, singing and acting it out at the same time. Seeing him so much in earnest, the deputy director Yu Lexing could only agree, unwillingly, that he might find a few people with some knowledge of music to help him revise it. At every subsequent staff meeting Jiang Zhennan would sing his latest revision to the company. The first few times people offered comments; after that they grew rather sick of him, and to save further trouble said grudgingly that it would do. Only then was he allowed to teach it to the students. Nobody had reckoned on Dai Li's taking a fancy to it and its rising in the world overnight.

*Appendix: the words of the class song. The book prints the song here in full. It is a short marching lyric in the manner of its period: it summons the young men of the revolution to be ready and to be complete in wisdom, benevolence and courage, calls on them to take hold of the artery of the present age and stand at the head of it, borrows Mencius's formula that neither poverty nor force can bend a man, sets safeguarding the leader's security beside defending the territory and sovereignty of the state, enjoins firmness, gravity, endurance and singleness of purpose, and ends by naming the singers themselves the Great Wall of the nation and the vanguard of the race before repeating its opening line. Its interest is entirely in the two things Shen Zui has just pointed out: that the tune was adapted from a Nazi party song, and that what fixed Dai Li's attention was the line putting the leader's safety first and the state's second.*

### The Training

The first problem the Linli special training class ran into when it opened was that the students did not want to join the Juntong's secret service. Most of them had been sent from Zhengzhou, refugee youngsters out of school and out of work, most with no home to go back to; arriving in Hunan they were strangers in a strange place with not a soul they knew, and though many wanted to withdraw, they dared not say so. A small number had been introduced by agents and knew beforehand what they were joining, and with them of course there was no difficulty. But when Dai Li obtained from Hu Zongnan the several dozen women students recruited by examination in Changsha for the Seventh Branch of the Central Military Academy, and these reached Linli and learned what kind of class it was, more than half of them asked to be sent back to the Seventh Branch. Their reason was a simple one: they had thrown down the pen and taken up arms in order to fight Japan, and they had no wish to be secret agents. Seeing this, the deputy director Yu Lexing was at first for applying pressure, but reflected that the reason they gave was a perfectly proper one; that Cai Xueqin, Yang Jingru, Li Puping, Zhu Zhemin, Pan Shujie, Chai Xiangqun and others had families in Changsha, so that if the thing were mishandled these hot-blooded girls might simply run home; that they could not well have charges got up against them and be punished; and that Hu Zongnan's face had to be considered too, which ruled out any high-handed treatment. After an emergency staff meeting it was decided to try persuasion first. The chief instructor Xie Ligong and the head of political training Wang Zuhua volunteered to gather them together and work on them in the manner of a heart-to-heart talk, after which the deputy director would call them in one by one. They were at it a whole day before they could get these people quieted down, even for the moment. When Dai Li's report reached him he at once directed that some concrete method be worked out to stop anything of the kind happening again. Three days later the class settled on the device of filling in forms, writing autobiographies and swearing collectively to join the Juntong — a way of tying every student down.

At that time not only had the students not gone through this formality; a good many of the staff who had been brought in casually on some agent's recommendation had not gone through it either, and the occasion was used to get them all done at once.

Having learned the lesson of the Linli special training class, Dai Li laid it down that at every subsequent class the students must be put through the formalities of joining the Juntong before teaching began. Many of them had been tricked or forced into such classes, and when they heard that they were to be set to killing and burning, arresting and kidnapping, they were frightened and full of regret and asked in numbers to withdraw; but with the chain already round their necks, they could be dealt with.

From the very start of recruit training, anti-Communist and anti-people indoctrination was pressed hard alongside it. In the beginning, before the great hall was finished, the deputy director, the head of political training and the chief instructor assembled all the students every morning for a "morning meeting." Standing on a high platform knocked together out of timber, they gesticulated and poured out slander and abuse of the Communist Party. So determined was Wang Zuhua that neither students nor staff should believe the army led by the Chinese Communists was genuinely fighting Japan that he went so far as to distort the facts and deliver a piece of slanderous propaganda about the great victory at Pingxingguan. Afterwards, hearing that some students were saying things like "so even a battle the Communist army wins is a fake," he made two or three further speeches on the subject, trying to rub that brilliant victory out of the students' minds with his own tongue. At the two roll-calls held morning and evening, the battalion and company political instructors were more energetic still, wagging their nimble tongues and spraying spittle as they poured poison into the students, to make them believe the Communists were not fighting Japan at all but scrambling with the Kuomintang for power and advantage. They also told the students that the Kuomintang alone was truly resisting Japan, and that the Juntong was the most revolutionary group of all. And they were constantly taking students aside for private talks, instilling anti-Communist ideas and at the same time examining what the students thought.

When it was discovered that students were passing round the *New China Daily*, the class fastened on the matter at once: besides taking the readers aside for private talks, the company instructors collected their names and kept their words and conduct under secret observation. After this the class began inspecting incoming and outgoing correspondence, holding back every progressive book or paper addressed to a student. Letters the students sent out could not be entirely controlled, but everything that came to the class had to be opened and read by the battalion or company instructor before it was handed over. Many of the women students, finding that love letters from their young men had been opened and read, were beside themselves with rage. Some students got shops in the town to forward letters for them; this too was found out before long, and besides being expressly forbidden, notice was given to every shop and household that they were not to forward letters for students. The biggest general store in Linli at that time was called the Anfu, and it was through the Anfu that most of the students' letters went. The proprietor agreed to their faces to forward them, so as to draw their custom, and behind their backs took the letters to the class first, to be inspected before delivery.

For all the methods used to stop the students betraying the training class's secrets in their letters, it was not long before a great many people knew that a secret-service class on this scale had been set up in Linli county. Fellow students and relatives of some of the students who were studying at the Lu Xun Academy of Arts in Yan'an even wrote urging them to find some way of getting out of it. Once such letters were found, they naturally never reached the students' hands. To frighten the students further, the agents built a detention room at the class headquarters, and any student who broke the regulations could be sent there and locked up at any time.

Whatever his speciality, every student in the class had to learn a certain amount of basic secret-service technique. The deputy director Yu Lexing compiled a set of lecture notes called "Secret-Service General Knowledge" and lectured on it himself; it covered the basic techniques of intelligence, operations, reconnaissance, disguise, secret communication, the use of poisons, demolition, and the censorship of post and telegraph. Once the training divided by speciality, each subject grew far more detailed and particular. Students of intelligence, for instance, besides the collection, editing, assessment and classification of intelligence, had a whole series of courses on setting up secret establishments, on liaison, on shadowing and on shaking off a shadow, on laying out an intelligence net and on the methods of passing intelligence along it. These students also had to give particular attention to a political course on "analysis of the parties." This was a subject devoted to slandering and vilifying the Communist Party and the democratic parties and standing black and white on their heads, so as to fix one idea immovably in the students' minds: that outside the Kuomintang there was not one party that was revolutionary or resisting Japan. When the students had taken this course the class held regular tests on it, to consolidate what they had been taught.

The work of Juntong agents fell into two great branches: intelligence and operations. The scope of operations work was very wide, and it is the general name for all the Juntong's criminal activities — arrest, kidnapping, interrogation under torture, the escorting of prisoners, guarding them, assassination, arson, theft and destruction of every kind all fall within it. Demolition properly belongs to operations too, but because it holds an important place there and is a good deal more technical, in the Juntong's training classes it was made a separate speciality and not included in the operational technique course. Many classes for training operations agents included demolition, whereas a class training demolition agents specially did not necessarily deal with operations at all, and if conditions permitted would touch only on the general principles. Learning demolition was dangerous: students frequently caused casualties through carelessness, and even instructors were killed by explosions. On one occasion a demolition instructor named Cai, sent by the Juntong to the training office of the Third War Zone — then at Sishidu, Shangrao, in Jiangxi — was blown up on the spot in his own lecture room while teaching. The main reason for this was that the Juntong, always feeling it had too few agents, was in too great a hurry for results, and in training gave no thought to the students' safety, wanting only speed; so accidents happened all the time. An agent being trained for operations could generally master the basic techniques in six months; a demolition man needed six months too before he could work on his own, and for that reason ordinary operations training could cover no more than the general principles of demolition, the handling of demolition stores, and the making up of simple charges.

The Third Company trained chiefly in operations and demolition. The operational technique taught in this class was chiefly arrest and assassination, with something first about methods of shadowing and about the use of the pistol, the short knife (the dagger), the poisoned blade, the razor and the axe, and about pursuit and escape. Before the course on "arrest technique" began, several hours had to be spent on what looks like the simplest thing in the world — how to lay hold of your man — because there are problems here that a person who has not done this criminal work himself could never imagine. Two or three men taking hold of one man is simple enough, of course; but when one man has to take one man, or even one man two, it is not simple at all. Then there is the question of getting the arrested man away in different surroundings, which needs study from every angle. Bringing a man down from the third or fourth floor, for example, raises the nice question of whether the operations agent should go in front or behind: in front, and he may very well be pushed down; behind, and the man may throw himself down the stairs or out of a window regardless of his own life. The agents all knew from experience that a determined Communist does not submit to being tied up so easily, and that it often takes a desperate struggle for life and death. So in his lectures the instructor had to go in detail, for every agent doing operations work, into all the problems that had ever arisen and all that might. In making an arrest, besides guarding against resistance, a set of methods had to be worked out for each different kind of person. Old people, women and children, for instance, when arrested will often not resist but simply refuse to budge, or scream and shout — and with old women in particular the agents could not conveniently beat or curse them, which would draw a sympathetic crowd and make trouble, nor could they be moved by promises or threats. With people of this kind the agents had to get in first, using a special method that left the body in some pain and without the strength to struggle or cry out, yet not in very great pain, so that the person had no choice but to come along.

All these courses were taught and practised together: the instructor would finish explaining a method and demonstrate it there and then in the lecture room, and the material he practised on was naturally the students. Some of the cleverer ones, when called out to take part, swallowed what they got and let it go at that. Those who would not take it were often left with torn muscles and broken bones on the spot. I still remember a student named Yan Xie who, during a class in unarmed combat, deliberately made things difficult for the instructor Jin Minjie, and whom Jin dealt with viciously, striking him hard in a vital place and causing internal injuries. He would still not give in, and later, in my own class, volunteered to try a bout with me. I had been on my guard against him, and when he was putting out all his strength to throw me I gave him a heavy blow of the same kind. After these two occasions Yan Xie was so badly injured that he could not be saved, and before long, with another illness coming on top of it, he died. The agents then held a memorial meeting for him, the cat weeping for the mouse. The operations instructors were forever discussing among themselves how to avoid being made fools of in class, and all agreed that a mischievous or pig-headed student should be given a bad time then and there — the instructors' so-called prestige being worth a student's life. After that, students would not act as practice material for an instructor unless they had to.

A good many exercises did not need a student for material and were performed for the students to watch, rather like a music-hall turn. The students generally enjoyed these enormously. Take the carrying and use of the pistol: the instructor would come to the lecture with several pistols about him, and while explaining that for a secret-service man his pistol is his first life, that it must not show or draw attention, and that it must come out fast and shoot straight so as to get in first, he would whip out a pistol and put a round into a target hung up in the room beforehand. When the shot went off and the target was left swinging, many of the students had not even seen it happen and would ask for it again. The instructor would then draw every pistol he had about him, quickly, for the students to see clearly, open his clothing and put them back one by one. So too with opening handcuffs and locks without a key, using whatever came to hand, and having the cuff or the lock open in a moment; that interested them as well. The instructors did this so that the business of arresting and killing should not be taught in a way to make the hair stand on end and put people off learning it at all.

For experiments with poisons a dog was used instead. A few drops of croton oil on a piece of beef, eaten by a dog, would have it rolling on the ground within minutes and finished inside half an hour, though it does a man little harm. In fact some poisons the Juntong did not teach openly at all: when they were to be used, the made-up preparation was mixed into food and handed to the agents to use, without their being told its name or how it was compounded. What was taught was only the ordinary sort of thing.

When the Linli class came to the course on interrogation, it happened that a medical officer who had been engaged casually was under suspicion of embezzlement and of buying in fake drugs, and he was hauled in to serve as "practice material." He was a man of no consequence, a friend or relative of some minor agent; his embezzlement had its limits, and he had bought a little fake medicine merely out of greed for a cheap price, without any intention of harming the students. He was nevertheless accused of deliberately trying to poison the staff and students, and it was decided at a staff meeting to try him publicly as "practice material" for the students. The instructors began by working through the interrogation methods they taught in class, without the slightest result. The man flatly denied any intention of harming anyone in the class, and after several hours of it the students were growing bored and the instructors flustered; whereupon they brought out the tricks of their trade, and every method of torture they taught which there was any means of trying was tried on that doctor. Some of the women students grew frightened at the sight and gave the company commanders a piece of their minds. In the end this "practice material" was beaten into a mass of wounds without either confession or evidence being obtained, so there was nothing for it but to hand him back to the man who had introduced him, dismissed on a guarantee that he would make no complaint about it. Behaviour of this lawless kind had a great effect on the students of the class after they graduated: it left them feeling that this organization possessed every privilege and need take no account of any law whatever.

Students of the intelligence and operations companies all had to do practical work in shadowing and in shaking off a shadow. Several dozen of the so-called star pupils of the intelligence company, Wen Zicui, Li Jiafu and the rest, even went to Changsha, where the Hunan provincial station helped them carry out an exercise. Dai Li was very pleased with the report on it, since in a few short months of training these new disciples of his had already mastered something of the technique.

Another thing regularly practised at the class was demolition. As this subject was rather dangerous, the making up of ordinary explosive charges was usually only talked through in the lecture room, or demonstrated once or twice by some simple method; the explosions themselves were done with finished products, TNT or dynamite, to let the students see their power and learn to calculate quantities and so on. Once these explosives had been drawn, some of the agents at the class were forever taking them off to the ponds and streams nearby to blow up fish to eat, so that the neighbourhood was constantly shaken by enormous detonations. It was not only the fish in the pond that suffered: several stretches of the embankment along the river were cracked by the blasting. The following year, when I was working in Changde, the class had already moved away, yet I still heard the local people cursing it from time to time, because when the water rose in spring a number of embankments that had been shaken gave way, and several breaches caused floods that cost the farmers of that county a good deal. There was no wickedness these agents were not up to; nowhere they passed through or stayed in escaped some calamity.

Where a course could not be practised, the instructor would illustrate it with real cases from the Juntong's past work. In these actual cases the target, or the enemy, was nearly always an underground Communist, the object being to work up the students' anti-Communist feeling. Sometimes it was stated outright that their principal enemy in future would be the Communist Party. Those were the days when the whole nation was united in demanding resistance to Japan, so the instructors could not avoid mentioning some examples of dealing with the Japanese or with traitors; but the proportion was very small. In the operations course I taught, for instance, when it came to arrest technique nearly every example I used was drawn from past arrests of Communists.

The course on arrest technique was very finely divided, distinguishing arrest by identification, arrest by search, arrest by lying in wait, arrest by encirclement, arrest by pursuit, secret arrest and so on according to the circumstances. I will say something briefly about only the first of these. Arrest by identification means that when someone under arrest cannot stand the torture and gives up the people connected with him, the agents take him along with them to arrest the man he has named. In teaching this the whole emphasis was on how to avoid being taken in and how not to startle the snake in the grass, because the agents had been deceived a great many times over this and had come off badly again and again. Some Communists, seeming to be unable to bear the torture and willing to sell their organization, were on the contrary made use of as a warning signal when the agents cheerfully took them off to arrest the person named, so that the leading body and everyone else got safely away. This is the prisoner deceiving the agents: he gives a false address for the underground organization, often across the street from the real one or close by, and when the agents bring him along to make the arrest, the underground people are not only put on their guard at once but can see that their comrade has been taken, and move away fast. In the past the agents often came away empty-handed in this fashion, having been used as a means of passing the warning. So in teaching this course the instructors dwelt on past experience and on how to prevent it. Once a prisoner had given up the address of his leading body and the build and features of its members, for example, the interrogation was not to stop there: he was to be pressed for full particulars of the house, what objects were used as safe and danger signals, how the rooms were furnished inside the door, everything about the premises; then agents disguised as men come to read the electricity or water meter would go in first to see whether it matched what he had said. If it did, they went in and made the arrest; if it did not, they came back and set to work on him with harsher methods still.

Once the account had been checked and found true, a section of the whole street, or the lane, had to be sealed off during the search, to stop the man from deliberately naming a minor address and giving the important people a chance to escape. If the place proved empty, several agents had to be left to watch the neighbours secretly and see whether anything unusual happened after the raid. All the courses on the various kinds of arrest work were taught with the destruction of Communist underground organizations as the worked example; I will not go through them one by one.

The Juntong's kidnapping work likewise took the Communists for its object throughout. Before the war the Juntong's kidnappings of Communists inside the foreign concessions in Shanghai, Tientsin and Hankow were always regarded by Dai Li as masterpieces of his own, so the instructors gave the students a great many examples of that kind too. The Juntong's organized and planned kidnapping work was a set of methods arrived at by learning from professional kidnappers and by continual study afterwards, which was why it could be applied in all sorts of different places, against different targets, by different means, and still achieve its object. This course took seven or eight hours to get through.

In teaching assassination work the instructors always liked to take for their material the killings of the democratic and progressive figures Yang Xingfo and Shi Liangcai, cases the Juntong was united in boasting of. They would add examples of Communists secretly murdered, most of whose names nobody could remember, so as to teach these students to surpass their masters when they went out to slaughter Communists themselves.

In the Juntong's special training classes, when secret-service technique was being taught, the students were always told to bear in mind that the excellence of the application lies in the mind of the one who applies it. Which is to say: stick at no method whatever, take no account of any law, and consider only how the object is to be attained. This was the working standard every agent was to act on at all times.

At this class, and at all the other training classes for agents afterwards, ordinary courses in secret-service technique were taught with printed lecture notes, or the students copied down what was said. But for "operational technique" no notes were ever printed and students were not allowed to take any: the instructor simply spoke. If a student had not followed something or was unclear, he could raise it after the class and ask to have it gone over again. Instructors particularly welcomed students who came to them outside class hours, because everyone, the agents included, knew that this business of killing and burning would not bear the light, and any young person with a shred of conscience found it too brutal when he heard it; so that although plenty attended the lectures, those who took a real interest were always few. A student who came outside class hours was therefore regarded as a rare good pupil, cultivated with care and instructed with enthusiasm until he became a "specialist." This counted not only to the instructor's own credit but as part of the work Dai Li valued above all others, the raising of successors.

There was one other course at this class for which no notes and not even an outline were issued: how to get inside Communist organizations or the democratic parties and carry on secret-service work there. Sometimes it was taught on its own; sometimes it was inserted into the course on analysis of the parties. It was generally given by renegades from the Communist Party, who understood conditions inside the Party better, and who used documents relating to the Communists obtained from postal censorship and from various other quarters as their material, teaching the students the Party's terms of art and a general knowledge of it, so that they could pass themselves off as progressives and find it easier to get close to Communists and other progressive people. But since they were also afraid these young people might be influenced by communist ideas, they had in lecturing to twist and blacken Marxism-Leninism, and the Party's policies and lines especially, from every angle. Many of the renegades who took this course found the lecturing full of contradiction and very painful: if they did not go into detail they were afraid the students would not be able to apply it in their work, and if they said too much they were afraid other agents would take them to be doing the Communists' propaganda for them in disguised form; so they felt they could go neither lightly nor heavily and were in difficulties whichever way they turned. For a good many agents at this class would take their little reports straight to Dai Li to curry favour with him. Dai Li himself was never at the class, yet he knew everything about it: not merely the state of mind of staff and students, but the private life of every important officer. On his first inspection visit he was able to quote, word for word, the corps commander Tao Yishan's habitual remark over the mahjong table after dinner — that four rounds after a meal is mahjong for the health — so it is no wonder everyone had to be on his guard the whole time.

Besides the courses described above, the Fourth Company, the espionage-staff company, gave particular attention to subjects bearing on military intelligence, while the Fifth and Sixth studied guerrilla tactics. Hearing that Ye Jianying was teaching this subject at the guerrilla cadre class at Nanyue in Hunan, Dai Li very much wanted to learn the Communists' methods of guerrilla warfare so as to use them against the Communists later, and arranged to have the two deputy battalion commanders of the Linli class, Chen Zhendong and Wang Baigang, introduced there for work, so as to get to the bottom of it. Afterwards Wang Baigang applied the agents' "technique of theft" and stole a considerable sum in cash from the guerrilla cadre class, and was denounced. This agent's performance was so clumsy that he was caught with the goods on him, which left Dai Li looking very badly; theft is not in itself a capital offence, but as he had failed in his task and shamed Dai Li into the bargain, Dai Li in a rage had him brought back and shot.

In the autumn of 1938, when Dai Li came to inspect the Linli special training class for the first time, the deputy director Yu Lexing, hearing that he was coming and wanting to please him — and wanting also to let the students see something of the director's dignity — sent out the students of two companies on the morning of the day to act as a guard, so that in the small county town of Linli there was a sentry every three paces and a picket every five. As Dai Li's car came up to the town, Yu Lexing had long been waiting on the road at the head of the whole staff and a body of students, and when one bugle sounded the general salute, several more took it up at once, and the whole town was thrown into a state of tension. The armed students posted at the main road junctions promptly carried out their orders and closed the roads. Now Dai Li had come in the first place because he had heard a great pile of complaints about Yu Lexing, and was already out of temper; the sight of a whole town under guard to welcome him was oil on the fire, and he began on it in the road. He demanded angrily of Yu Lexing what such elaborate precautions were for. He was not a warlord of the old days, he said; and if Chiang Kai-shek were to hear of him putting on airs like this, where would it end? He was in a temper the whole way and still in one when he reached the class, so that everyone who had never seen him before was thoroughly frightened and felt the director's dignity was considerable indeed.

The next morning, when Dai Li presided over a staff meeting for the first time, he abused people from beginning to end, and everyone from the deputy director down to the clerks came in for it. Yet from my years at his side afterwards I know he was in fact very pleased with this class, which had turned out more than a thousand students for him at a stroke, and had trained them conscientiously with no bad results. What he was most dissatisfied with at the time was that he had never had time to go and speak to the students, so that in their minds there was a deputy director but no director. Yu Lexing understood a great deal about secret-service work, and the important courses — general knowledge, disguise, poisons, chemical communication — were all taught by him. Besides that he was forever giving the students inspirational talks, and in the ordinary way of life he could mix with them on their own level, so that in a few months the students were extremely fond of him. Some of the agents were green with envy and reported to Dai Li continually that Yu was very ambitious. Dai had once put Tao Yishan in as deputy director, and within a month things were in such a state that he could only put Yu back in charge. That in itself made all those reports look truer, and Dai's dissatisfaction with Yu grew. It is the reason he went on looking for a chance to deal with Yu ever afterwards.

At this staff meeting almost everyone was cursed, and one man alone was praised: the political instructor Liao Huaping, a Communist renegade. Dai Li had heard early on that as soon as Liao Huaping reached Linli he had thrown himself into anti-Communist agitation. Many said Liao Huaping was an alarmist, but he went on insisting at the top of his voice that the Communists were the greatest and most dangerous enemy of all. If the Communists were not wiped out, he said, none of us will have a grave to be buried in. When others told him he was building up other people's morale and destroying his own side's, he stuck to his opinion — and this recommended him strongly to Dai Li. He had, besides, openly attacked the deputy director, the corps commander and the chief instructor at the staff meeting Dai Li was presiding over, for the dissipation of their private lives, for slackness in instructing the students, for laxity in vetting the students politically, and for the number of instructors and company commanders carrying on love affairs with women students. The two things he pressed hardest were, first, that the office of the dean of studies and the corps headquarters had compelled every student to learn to swim, with the result that a Henanese woman student, Liu Ying, who had never been in the water in her life, was drowned; and second, that Yan Xie had been injured by an instructor and had sickened and died of it. Hearing this, Dai Li flew into a worse rage, and the more he cursed the angrier he grew. When he had finished cursing everybody, he praised Liao warmly there and then.

Dai Li stayed at the Linli class three or four days that time, and spoke to the students almost every day. Besides boasting to them about how revolutionary the Juntong was and what a future it had, he never failed to slander the Chinese Communist Party each time, telling the students to be clear in their minds about who their greatest enemy from now on would be.

Dai Li's second inspection visit to the class was in the winter of the same year. He hurried up from Changsha to Linli, and passing through Changde invited Tang Shengming, then garrison commander there, together with his wife Xu Lai and Xu's woman friend Zhang Suzhen, to come with him. On this visit, besides presiding over the Linli class's graduation ceremony, he had to decide, in view of the fighting, that western Hunan was no longer a safe place and that the class should move to Qianyang county and carry on there; and he meant to replace the deputy director Yu Lexing. That was why he made time to come in the middle of everything else. The first intake had not yet had a full year's training, while recruiting for the second had already begun, and several dozen students sponsored from various places had reached Linli and were sitting in for the time being with a newly formed recruits' company. So some of the agent-students entered at Linli and graduated at the Qianyang class. The Fourth Company, the espionage-staff company, also graduated only at Qianyang. Although the Juntong listed them as second-intake graduates of the Qianyang class, they always described themselves as Linli men.

At the staff meeting this time Dai Li did not lose his temper and abuse people as before, but said that this experiment in large-scale training had produced results, thanked the whole staff for their trouble and told the students he was satisfied with them. It was only as he was about to leave that he formally announced the personnel changes to follow the move to Qianyang: the deputy directorship would go to the military instructor Wu Lang, while the existing office heads, the chief instructor and the corps commander would all be posted elsewhere; and he directed that under the class headquarters there were to be no more offices, all of them becoming sections. Besides announcing to the students at the graduation ceremony what work they were to do, he had individual talks with a number whose work had been good, and separately gathered together the agents who were staying on at the class and those whose transfers had been announced, and talked with them too.

At the evening entertainment after the graduation ceremony, instructors and students alike wanted to show off their "special skills" and give a display of what they could do. Among the various novel turns, Dai Li was much taken with the breathing exercises of an operations student, Li Sichun, who could have a large stone slab laid on his stomach and another man break it with a sledgehammer without taking the least injury. Li Sichun's later promotion by Dai Li to be deputy director of the Chungking combat class — the class that trained operations agents in the martial arts — dates from Dai's noticing him that night. At the same entertainment the Korean instructor Jin Minjie also performed one of his special feats. He brought a boy orderly of a dozen or so named Jin Gang up onto the stage and, gripping his throat with nothing but his wrist, had him unconscious and insensible in a few seconds; then a slap on the back and Jin Gang came round again. The whole audience applauded, but Dai Li was thoroughly displeased and gave immediate instructions that such things were not to be used as entertainment. Some of the agents who had prepared similar turns therefore dared not bring them out, or the evening would have turned into a trial of strength among agents.

There was a great deal of talk at the time about what lay behind this instruction of Dai Li's: some said he was being humane and was afraid the boy might be killed and fail to come round; others that it was because ladies were present and he was afraid of alarming them. Only later, when I was working at his side and the thing happened to be mentioned, did he tell me frankly. Methods of that kind, for use against an enemy, he said, ought to be kept secret; how could they be put on for a public performance? Then I understood: what he had been afraid of was giving away the Juntong's stock of brutal methods, and letting people see them.

As to the students' allowances during training, they had twelve yuan a month, of which the mess charge generally came to three or four, leaving eight or nine for their own use. Their clothing was grey cotton uniform. On graduation the usual pay was that of a second lieutenant, forty yuan, but promotion came fast. A few who had been uneasy to begin with, seeing here a short road to office and riches, set to with a will.

### The Assignment of Work, and the Friction Inside

Some students of the Linli special training class were pulled out and posted to work while the training was still going on. Dai Li thought a year's training too long, and repeatedly gave instructions and expressed the hope that it might be shortened; he was also so impatient that he would personally telegraph to have the better students picked out and posted early, using them before they had graduated. In the summer of 1938, besides withdrawing early a good many of the trainees who had been serving Juntong agents to begin with, he ordered thirty women students, Xu Jihong among them, to be selected and sent off in one batch to work with the "battlefield service corps" run by a Miss Hu in Jiangxi.

Dai Li never let slip an opportunity of getting inside the wartime service bodies then being formed by ordinary people. He was afraid such popular resistance organizations would fall into the hands of the Communists or other progressive parties and be hard to control later. He took the view that organizations of this kind could very well be used for the Juntong's secret-service work, since nobody would think to look at them. So when Miss Hu of the Jiangxi battlefield service corps saw him and said she was short of hands, he promised her support at once, using the chance to work agents in. The Linli class immediately followed his instructions and picked out the students it considered reliable and hard-working, and held a farewell dinner for them with some ceremony. When they passed through Wuhan, Dai Li spoke to them as well and told them to their faces what their task in Jiangxi was. He put his finger on it exactly: Jiangxi being an old Communist base area, this mission was of particular importance. Afterwards Xu Jihong and the others even wrote back to the class setting out what Dai Li had said when he received them, and reporting on everything that had happened since they left.

The Linli class also picked a hundred students, men and women, and sent them to Wuhan to take part in the defence of the city. Once the hundred had been chosen I escorted them there; the men, more than eighty of them, included Huang Bingyan and Shu Mingli, and the women, more than ten, included Yang Wenhui. On arrival in Wuhan they were quartered in a Japanese primary school on Nanxiao Road in the former Japanese concession in Hankow. The next morning Dai Li came in person to call the roll and speak to them, urging them to make the Juntong's special contribution felt in the defence of Wuhan. At midday he dined with them and issued each of them a clothing allowance; and the day after, all hundred of those agent-students in grey cotton uniform had bought themselves plain clothes. I stayed a week in Wuhan, and Dai Li then told me to go back to Linli and carry on training students. He explained to me how important training was at that moment: chiefly because the Juntong's work was expanding by the day, and its work both outward, against the Japanese, and inward, against the Chinese Communists and the people, was growing heavier and heavier, while what it lacked most was cadres. He stressed that this line of work was not one that just anybody could do, and that men could not be used freely until they had been trained and vetted politically. Twice I asked to be let off going back to training; after he put it like that I had no choice but to return to Linli.

In the autumn of 1938 the Linli class picked more than a hundred students from the Fifth and Sixth military companies, before they had graduated, and sent them off to the south-east to work with the Loyal and Patriotic Army, which the Juntong controlled. That was an area close to the New Fourth Army, and the Juntong had put particular weight on strengthening its work there from the beginning of the war.

The Juntong was also infiltrating agents into the areas the Japanese had occupied, in a token sort of way. Zhou Xiliang, Bi Jiukui and Zhu Shanyuan, for instance, knowing Shanghai well from the past, were sent to work there; after that two or three more batches of those who had the right qualifications were sent, only four or five men at a time. Zhao Lufeng and a few others who had connections in the occupied part of Shandong were also sent back there in the autumn. So that by the time the students of the Linli class graduated that winter, there were in fact only some seven hundred left. Those who had not been posted early had had, all told, no more than about ten months' training, and most of them were sent to work in the south-western rear areas, the greatest number to the Juntong headquarters itself and to Sichuan province; a very few, such as Fang Dingya and Cheng Zaide, stayed on as cadres for the second intake.

Dai Li was especially fond of this graduating class, and the several dozen posted to the Juntong's own headquarters were all picked by him personally and very quickly given responsible work. Li Jiafu and Liu Ziying, for instance, became assistant secretaries of his confidential office, also called Office A — Dai Li had copied Chiang Kai-shek in this, and it amounted to his personal staff office. Of those posted to the personnel office, later the personnel department, that is the Sixth Department, some ten men — Li Cangzhou, Li Zhuoyuan, Wu Yinyuan, Chen Xipu, Mi Zishu, Cen Jibin, Zhou Goushu, Wang Ze'ao — soon became sub-section heads and deputy sub-section heads. Because of Dai Li's favour towards this class, there was not one department, office, section or district of the Juntong's headquarters staff without Linli students in important posts, and the districts, stations and groups in the field were full of them too. The Chungking special district at that time contained seven or eight of them — Lü Shikun, Liu Dewen, Yang Zheng, Chen Yuehua, Deng Tingfu and others. The inspectorate of the Chungking Garrison Command actually had more than thirty students from this class: not only were they in every section and sub-section of the office, but the heads and inspectors of the inspection posts in more than ten counties were almost all of them Linli men. More than ten of these — Yi Xiaofu, Xiao ⸻, Wu Dehou, Guan Baozhong, Cheng Chuanluo, Chong Dongchou, Lu Dongfan, Zhang Mingxuan, Deng Zipei, Yang Xingxian, Wu Jusheng, Tang Zhiya, Zhu Die, Mao Hongming — were not only very soon carrying a whole area on their own but were as quickly promoted to captain or major.

This state of affairs caused great resentment among the many agents who had served the Juntong for years, whose seniority was far greater than these students', and who were not being given responsible work. They grumbled behind people's backs that the world had come to a pass where nothing that was not Li was proper and nothing that was not Li would be used — a play on the name of the place, since Li means propriety as well. What set their backs up still more was that these people, fresh out of the thatched hut, behaved arrogantly and showed the old agents no courtesy. These young men would let it be known in any company that they were Dai Li's own students. In speech and in writing everyone else generally called Dai Li "Mr. Dai," while these students without exception said "the Director," to mark themselves off from the ordinary run of agents. Because of this the envy and resentment, at first confined to a few minor agents, gradually spread until section and department heads were somewhat sick of them too.

The reasons the students of the Linli special training class won Dai Li's confidence were these. They were the first body trained on a large scale, and they graduated just as Dai Li's fortunes were rising and cadres were exactly what he needed, so they were given responsible posts out of the ordinary course. At the same time they were all very good at pleasing Dai Li and constantly reported to him on the doings of the old agents, which made the senior men, long used to their corrupt private lives, dislike them more than ever. As for the students, they reckoned that so long as they had the Director's confidence there was nobody they need consider. Little by little these newly trained young agents took to advertising one another, covering for one another, and working together inside the service and out. Even in addressing one another they were unlike everybody else: instead of "comrade" they said "fellow student," to mark the intimacy. And when they met one of the old Linli instructors or company commanders they would not use his present title but called him "teacher." When I was head of the General Affairs Department at the Juntong's headquarters, the students always greeted me warmly as "Teacher Shen" — until in the end the other department heads mocked me for it to my face and took to calling me "Teacher Shen" as well.

All this set the senior agents of the bureau thinking of ways to deal with them. The Juntong's personnel rules were strict: the officer in charge of a unit could not simply bring in his own friends and relatives to work under him, and a friend or relative who joined the Juntong had to be posted by the personnel department. Dai Li kept a tight control on this point, to stop senior agents from using their own people and making common cause up and down the line to deceive him. He planted his own students in every unit to watch the senior men in charge of it. Later, although a number of Qianyang graduates were brought in to fill posts in the headquarters departments, many of the Qianyang students had come over from Linli and had gone through the joining formalities at Linli, and the Linli men drew them in with the slogan that Linli and Qianyang were one family. Some of the senior agents then deliberately had a batch of students brought from the Lanzhou special class to Chungking as a counterweight, to set them at odds with one another and so reduce the attention they paid to their seniors. The young agents' little reports did the senior men no great harm in themselves, but they regularly gave Dai Li good material for cursing people at the weekly memorial meeting, and being cursed is never comfortable.

Dai Li's purpose in setting up the Lanzhou special class in the first place had been convenience in supplying cadres to the north-west, so as to save the fares and the time of moving men back and forth. As for the cadres Chungking needed, there were already plenty of Linli and Qianyang graduates, and when the Qianyang class later moved to Xifeng in Guizhou it was mainly to train the cadres the south-west required; Chungking in particular already had ten or so classes of one sort or another and had no need whatever to bring students all the way from Lanzhou. But some of the senior agents — Mao Renfeng among them — were rather sick of the Linli students, and so did not grudge the distance. Sure enough, once the Lanzhou students had been worked into the various parts of the Juntong they too made common cause among themselves and gradually formed a body set against the Linli men. Later the Xifeng special class ran three intakes one after another, and its graduates were brought in as well to compete for favour with the men of the other two classes, so that within the Juntong there took shape, without anyone intending it, three cliques of graduates. And while they were playing their underhand games against one another, the attention they paid to the senior agents grew slacker and slacker.

Of the three circles the training classes formed, the Linli men — including a large part of the Qianyang class — always had the advantage all round, because their standing was older, they had graduated a year or two earlier, and their position was already established. They did not altogether hide their methods of working together inside and out from me, so I know something of them. They used their fellow students inside the Juntong headquarters to help their fellow students in the field units to opportunities for office and money, while those outside regularly helped those at headquarters with material things; on anything important they kept each other informed, staying in close touch and using dinners and the like openly for the purpose. Whenever a Linli man came to Chungking from another province, several or several dozen of them would give a dinner, and Dai Li never forbade it when he heard.

What Dai Li did not at all like was these students being too warm towards their old teachers at the class. Every time the former deputy director Yu Lexing came to Chungking, for instance, a hundred or more students would give a dinner in his honour and, as usual, ask him to speak. Yu Lexing never failed on these occasions to urge the students to work well and be loyal to the Juntong and to Dai Li, yet Dai Li disliked him intensely all the same, and afterwards even found a pretext to have him detained, holding him for more than a year without a hearing or a question. Still, it was thanks to those students that Yu Lexing did not in the end die under the Juntong's knife. Before the liberation of Shanghai, Mao Renfeng received a secret report that Yu had gone over to the Communists, and pressed him several times to go to Taiwan; Yu kept putting it off. Mao then ordered the agents of the Shanghai inspectorate to arrest him. As it turned out, the order had barely arrived when the students in the inspectorate learned of it and telephoned him at once, so that Yu was able to get away at his leisure.

As the war against Japan drew towards victory the Linli students bestirred themselves in a big way, very much wanting to make the most of the moment. Then, out of a clear sky, this Director of theirs on whom they depended was killed in an air crash in March 1946, and they wept as though for their own parents and felt their prospects had gone dark. Senior agents used the chance to bring in their own people wholesale and hit out on every side at these darlings of heaven. In the Juntong's reduction a good many of them were cut and left a long time without a posting. They felt this bitterly. To fight for a way forward, some twenty of those left in Chungking — Zhang Mingxuan, Kuang Jusheng, Li Baochu, Liu Dewen, Deng Zipei, Zuo Zhiliang and others — took the initiative in forming a Binli Alumni Association to strengthen their solidarity. Liu Benqin, Deng Yifu and Niu Shukun, who were working in Nanking, at once declared themselves for it. As they had always kept in touch, two or three hundred agreed in no time, and the association was founded at Chungking in the autumn of 1946.

I was going up to Chungking constantly at that time to deal with the Juntong's property, and they put the proposal to me. Using my position over the property, I gave them financial help of every kind by the device of buying things in cheap, so that those without postings could keep themselves. They then asked me to be their chairman, hoping not only that I would help them with money but that I would find them openings inside the Juntong. I had some ambitions myself just then and wanted to draw a little strength together, so I recommended Li Baochu to Mao Renfeng for a section head's post in the personnel department of the Bureau of Secrets Preservation, recommended Liu Benqin to Tang Zong for a section head's post in the personnel office of the police headquarters, and recommended Liu Ziying to the traffic police headquarters to take charge of personnel. At that time I had a great deal of Juntong property and a number of houses in the big cities under my hand, and these secret-service chiefs all wanted to get something out of me, so this plan of mine went through very smoothly. Before long the Linli students were slowly lifting their heads again.

By 1947 the Juntong's three great men — Zheng Jiemin, Mao Renfeng and Tang Zong — had each got what he wanted after a fierce struggle for power and advantage, and began to pay attention to gathering cadres. By then students of the other special classes had followed the Linli example and formed alumni associations of their own. Mao Renfeng got in first: he formally ordered every one of the class alumni associations dissolved and forbade any further activity by them, setting up instead a single "united alumni association" for all the Juntong's special training classes; and he employed the Linli students on the same footing as the rest, so that at a stroke he had drawn in ten thousand agent-students. He also promoted Linli men out of the ordinary course to be provincial station chiefs — Qian Tilin as chief of the Guizhou station, Lü Shikun of the Chungking station, Dong Shili of the Xikang station, and Zhang Mingxuan as head of the criminal police department of the Chungking police bureau. That done, the students took Mao Renfeng for their patron instead, and I no longer dared court them, keeping up only private relations.

### The Criminal Activities of the Class's Graduates

From more than ten years of contact with the students of the Linli special training class, I came to know something of the criminal activities of a number of them. If I set out part of it here, briefly, it will be seen what monstrous crimes the agents this class trained committed against the Chinese Communist Party and against the people.

Before liberation, the heads of the First and Second Sections of the Bureau of Secrets Preservation, which planted men to lie low in the liberated areas — Li Baochu and Ren Hongchuan — had done nothing but this work from 1948 onwards, and went on doing it after they fled to Taiwan.

Among the agents who took part in the great destruction and the massacre at Chungking before liberation, this class contributed: Zhang Mingxuan, head of the criminal police department of the Chungking police bureau; Zhou Wanchang, its deputy head; Zhong Shu, chief of its supervisory branch; and Lei Tianyuan, head of the judicial section of the Second Department of the South-West Field Headquarters, the body that ran the massacre in the Bath Cave at the SACO compound in Chungking. When I was in charge of the provincial station in Yunnan, almost every one of the important cadres doing secret-service work in the province was a student of this class. For instance: the deputy chief of the Yunnan station, Pi Shaojin; the deputy head of the security department of the Yunnan Pacification Headquarters, Zai ⸻; the chief inspector Wu Dehou; the chief of staff Song Junhui; the head of general affairs Wang Jinghan and his deputy Zhang Jingfu; the head of the interrogation section Zuo Zhiliang; the head of the operations section Du Yuzhou and his deputy Huang Tan; the head of the air inspection group Li Ruifeng; the head of the postal and telegraph censorship group An Zhuoli; the head of the Kunming group Dai Jiliang; the head of the Xinping group Hu Xiaobo; the deputy head of the Baoshan group Qin Shanji; the head of the investigation group attached to the Twenty-Sixth Army, Zhang Yichuan; the head of the group attached to the Eighth Army, Zheng Shixun; and the inspectors Zhou Yunfeng, Li Cangzhou, Wu Jusheng, Wang Tingxiu and Zhong Tiyi — more than twenty men in all.

I will mention only the crimes of Xiao ⸻ here, and it will be seen that these men were butchers with blood on both hands. During the war against Japan, Xiao ⸻ was head of the inspection posts at Jiangjin and at Dangling in turn, under the Chungking inspectorate, and in those two places alone he destroyed two or three Communist underground organizations. His own wife, whose name I withhold, was an underground Party member he had arrested; he raped her, took her by force as his wife, and made her publish a notice in the press renouncing the Party. Before the liberation of Peiping he was deputy head of the police department of the Peiping-Liaoning railway administration, and constantly led traffic police units in fighting the people's guerrillas along the line. By his own boast, the guerrillas he had killed, wounded and taken alive numbered more than three hundred. Before the liberation of Yunnan he and I together directed the mass arrests at Kunming and other criminal activities. He was also in league with He Jinshu, then head of the inspectorate of the Guiyang garrison command; Zhu Huoji, chief inspector of the provincial capital police bureau at Guiyang; the police district head Zhang Songtao; the inspectorate section head Li Cangzhou; and a dozen more Linli graduates, preparing armed guerrilla operations by agents on the Yunnan-Guizhou border. Afterwards, when Li Cangzhou and the others fled to Kunming, he brought them into the security department too.

Mao Sen, the notorious butcher who operated in the south-east for years and slaughtered revolutionaries in Shanghai before its liberation, had as his most capable assistants Huang Bingyan and Yuan Mingding, both students of this class. There were more than twenty more of them among the important members of the Shanghai inspectorate, such as Ji Zhongpeng and Li Renzhang.

After Hu Zongnan attacked Yan'an in 1947, the first man to go there as head of the investigation office was Cui Wangbin of this class, which shows how highly the Juntong thought of these agents. Among the senior agents the Juntong selected to study in the United States just after the war against Japan ended were two of this class's students, Yu Shi and Jiao Jindang, from which it can be seen how much pains Dai Li took over their advancement. It caused a good deal of resentment among senior agents at the time, because those sent to America were mostly old hands of departmental-head or provincial-station-chief rank, and Dai Li was treating these students as their equals.

In the eleven years from their graduation in 1938 to 1949, several of this class's students rose as far as major general: Zhang Jiakuang, head of the investigation office of the Eleventh War Zone; Yi Xiaofu, head of the Second Department of Sun Yuanliang's Sixteenth Army Group; Xiao ⸻, deputy head of the security department of the Yunnan Pacification Headquarters; and Liu Ziying and Tang Zhenwen of the traffic police headquarters.

Ten-odd of these students rose to section head at the headquarters of the Juntong and of the Bureau of Secrets Preservation — Yu Shi, Deng Yifu, Liu Ziying, Niu Shukun, Ren Mingchuan, Li Baochu, Tang Yadong and others. In the field several became provincial station chiefs with a whole province on their hands. Those who headed inspectorates, criminal police departments, detective squads, police bureaus and supervisory branches, and other open secret-service bodies, were more numerous still. In short, of the agents this class turned out, those who had not left the Juntong by liberation had for the most part become middle-ranking cadres or higher, and a good many of them are carrying on their evil work in Taiwan to this day.
