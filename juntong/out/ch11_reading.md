## Hu Zongnan's Relations with Dai Li and Mao Renfeng

The Juntong secret-service chief Dai Li gradually won Chiang Kai-shek's trust through Hu Zongnan's recommendation. That Hu Zongnan could later win Chiang Kai-shek's favour all the more was, in turn, owed to no little help from Dai Li. The two were a pair of running dogs loyal to Chiang Kai-shek, forever puffing each other up before him, colluding inside and out, so that their power grew day by day. I set down here some of what I came to know personally. As for the many rumours over the years of the Juntong agents' various dealings with Hu in the Northwest, since I have no first-hand knowledge of them, I do not propose to say much here.

The first time I met Hu Zongnan was in the spring of 1937. Dai Li was then suffering from acute appendicitis and had just been operated on at the Hongen Hospital in Shanghai; he assigned me to take a group (a dozen or so agents) to protect him. Before this I had known only that Dai and Hu were on close terms — Dai had had us in Shanghai order custom military supplies for Hu several times. Though I had done some things for him, I had never met the man.

One day, as I was coming out of Dai Li's sickroom, meaning to go round and look at the agents on duty, I suddenly saw a man not tall but with a very spirited air walking straight toward Dai's room. I was about to challenge him when he spoke first: "Is Mr. Dai staying here?" I did not answer, but asked instead: "Whom are you looking for?" He said quickly: "Don't you know me? I am Hu Zongnan." I reflected that on entering the building he must already have been challenged by the agents, for very few Chinese came to this hospital, and on the floor where Dai stayed he was the only Chinese. Before I could reply, Chen Zhiping, the Shanghai special-district inspector who was keeping Dai company in the room, had recognized his voice, opened the door at once to greet him, and said to me: "Don't you even know Commander Hu?"

When we went into Dai's sickroom together, Dai was reclining against the bed; the moment he saw Hu come in he struggled to sit up. Hu rushed over to hold him down and let him lean back, asking again and again: "How are you? How are you?" Dai, answering about his condition after the operation, could not wait to tell him: "Madame came by a couple of days ago." Hearing that Soong Mei-ling had come, Hu clapped Dai on the shoulder over and over and said: "Wonderful! Wonderful! It's no small thing for Madame to come and see you in person!" At this point Chen Zhiping invited me and the special nurse to withdraw together to the outer sitting room. From the outer room we heard only the wild laughter of the two men, breaking off and starting again, and gathered that Hu was delighted Dai could win such concern from Chiang Kai-shek, and praised more than once how remarkably Dai had conducted himself in the "Xi'an Incident."

After about an hour Hu took his leave, and Chen Zhiping saw him to the door of the floor. Riding down in the lift with him, I felt him clap me on the shoulder: "Young fellow! Work a bit harder — Shanghai is a complicated place." Then he asked my name and wrote it in his notebook. As he went out he enjoined me once more: "Keep your eyes well open!" Two days later Hu came to see Dai again, and this time they talked more than two hours; after that I did not see him come again.

During the war against Japan, serving as head of the General Affairs Department at the Juntong bureau, I had occasion to be in frequent contact with Hu. Every time he came from Xi'an to Chungking he liked to stay at Dai Li's mansion at Zengjiayan; and after Dai Li's mansion at Shenxiandong was finished in 1944, he stayed there. Once, when Dai was away from Chungking, Mao Renfeng got a telegram that Hu was coming and told me to prepare to entertain him, saying I must bring out everything Dai used to eat. I felt awkward at the time, for Dai, generous as he seemed, could sometimes be extremely mean, and about what he ate and used above all he was not readily willing to let others touch anything. Mao saw what was on my mind and explained: toward Hu Zongnan, Dai was far more affectionate than toward his own brothers, and I must entertain Hu exactly as I would Dai, or if Dai learned of the least slip he would fly into a rage when he got back. So I did as told.

In the past, when Hu came to visit, Dai handled it himself and I knew little of it; but this time, with Dai away from home, I had to go and look in every day, and only then found that Hu, staying in Dai's house, behaved exactly as in his own — he stayed several days, entertained guests almost every day, and had everything done just as he wished without the least ceremony. As time went on I came to understand that it was so not only in Chungking but everywhere Dai Li had a mansion, Chengtu and Lanzhou and the rest alike. Not only could Hu freely enjoy whatever was Dai's; even some of Hu's intimates and senior officers, men like Fan Hanjie and Sheng Wen, could receive the same favoured treatment.

In the years I was at Dai Li's side, I noticed that before each time Hu came to Chungking to see Chiang Kai-shek, he would first study with Dai Li the questions he meant to put to Chiang, and ask Dai how Chiang Kai-shek's mood had been these last days; when Chiang was out of sorts he would wait a day or two before going, so as not to run into a rebuff, and when Chiang was in good humour Dai would urge him to go quickly. Once, just off the plane, Hu came straight to Dai's house and took out the document he had written for Chiang Kai-shek to show Dai; what was in it I do not know, but I saw Dai nodding steadily as he read, an unbroken murmur of approval, and Hu too looking very pleased with himself.

More than once Dai Li verbally instructed Mao Renfeng that any intelligence the Juntong obtained about Hu Zongnan personally or about his subordinates was to be handled with great care: as a rule it must all be passed to Dai to read himself, and matters that absolutely had to be reported to Chiang Kai-shek were all to have the big made small and the small made nothing. This was the exact opposite of the way they picked bones out of an egg with certain others; here it was favour at every turn. Not only that: Tang Zong too was directed by Dai that when intelligence denouncing Hu came in from other quarters to the Sixth Group of Chiang Kai-shek's Attendants' Office, such intelligence should so far as possible be referred first to the Juntong for re-examination, so that it could go to Chiang Kai-shek together with the Juntong's re-checked findings — which invisibly provided Hu with cover. Since Tang Zong had always been very cautious, and since handling it this way was still within procedure, he was able to do as asked. So what reached Chiang Kai-shek's ears and eyes was all good words about Hu. I heard from Chiang's attendant secretary Cao Shengfen that if anyone reported Hu's dereliction to Chiang to his face, Chiang did not like to hear it. To men of somewhat higher rank who said such things Chiang would put them off with a few words; to those of lower rank he would often even scold them, so that they came away crestfallen.

When Hu and Dai were together they held nothing back and, once started, never stopped. Sometimes, when I had urgent business Dai had to settle, I could not help running in to break off their talk just as they were most absorbed. I knew very well that so long as it was not something that would make Dai lose face in front of Hu, but a matter Dai thought he could take pride in — such as how much the Americans had given the Juntong, asking his instructions on how to dispose of it, or which construction project should be inspected before work began, and the like — I could raise it without the slightest reserve in front of Hu. Dai would listen to my report, explain it to Hu, and answer me with satisfaction all at once. Going in and out of the room many times while they talked, I heard bits and pieces of their conversation. What they talked of ranged from anti-Communism and pleasing Chiang Kai-shek all the way to food and clothes and women. Each loved to boast how capable his own subordinates were, to show off that he himself was the more able. The most comical thing was one morning when Dai Li had torn me to shreds, saying a whole string of things about how I was no good at this and no good at that; and in the afternoon, after Hu Zongnan remarked, "The Juntong's general-affairs work is very good," Dai turned round and made me out to be a highly capable specialist in affairs work, leaving me quite at a loss whether to laugh or cry. Hu, not to be outdone, made his own aide-de-camp out to be an all-round genius.

Hu and Dai often talked things over and sought each other's views. Hu Zongnan was the "King of the Northwest," holding military and civil power in his hands. Once he was preparing to remove the Shaanxi provincial chairman Xiong Bin and recommend Zhu Shaozhou to take his place. Zhu was a noted executioner who had won Hu's favour by killing people wantonly and in great numbers, and Dai too much admired the man. After the two of them had settled it, the Juntong agent Zuo Ming happened to overhear. Zuo Ming was a fellow member of Dai Li's own detachment in the sixth Whampoa class, and was then serving as chief of the inspectorate of the Hanchung Garrison Headquarters, of which Zhu was the commander. Hearing this news in Xi'an, Zuo Ming, to ingratiate himself with Zhu Shaozhou, let the secret out to him. Zhu in turn told his wife, and the result was that it soon reached Xiong Bin's ears. Xiong Bin asked Dai Li about it; Dai flatly denied there was any such thing and at once investigated who had leaked it, in the end tracing it to Zuo Ming. Originally, hearing this news from Zuo, Zhu had been overjoyed and promised him on the spot that once he became chairman he would invite Zuo Ming to help him and give him a chance at promotion. But this turn of events made Dai Li fly into a rage: he at once dismissed Zuo Ming and transferred him to Chungking under probation, and openly held the affair up as material for the instruction of his subordinates.

Dai Li also consulted Hu on the Juntong's own major problems and asked his help. Dai had no experience running armed secret-service troops and often sought Hu's advice. The Juntong then had a large, mixed "Loyal and Patriotic Army," commanded at first by Yu Zuobai. Dai, having heard that Yu had shown real capacity when acting as chairman of Guangxi, handed this force to his command. But because Yu's standing in the Juntong was insufficient, some of the agents would not much heed him, and as he himself proved to have only an empty name and could produce no methods, Dai grew more and more dissatisfied with him. Yet no suitable man could easily be found within the Juntong to replace Yu, so Dai asked Hu to find him someone to put the force in order. Besides telling him some methods for handling troops, Hu recommended Ma Zhichao of the first Whampoa class as commander of the Loyal and Patriotic Army. Ma had, before the war, served as education aide-de-camp at the Hangzhou special training class Dai Li ran, and had later left Dai and gone over to Hu; Dai knew this man's history well and was much disappointed that Hu should recommend such a person. I remember that when Ma flew from Xi'an to Chungking that time, Dai had me go to meet him. That morning, when Dai, Mao Renfeng and I were having breakfast together, Dai spoke of Hu's recommending Ma with great displeasure, but out of regard for Hu's feelings he could only reluctantly agree, and on the surface put on a great show of welcome. When I brought Ma back and Dai saw that Ma had come alone, without even an aide or a guard, he told me to pick a smart guard for him, specified that one of the very best pistols be taken from his private collection to give to Ma, and had another brand-new German twenty-shot Mauser given to the guard, along with much other equipment for Ma. I of course understood well that this was not favour to Ma but regard for Hu's face. Not long after Ma took up the command, Dai finally dismissed him and replaced him with Zhou Weilong, a man he had cultivated himself.

Between Hu Zongnan and Dai Li there was indeed a state of holding nothing back from each other. Hu once sent Dai Li sixty or seventy female students he had recruited in Changsha under the name of the Central Military Academy's Seventh Branch, to be trained as agents at Dai Li's Linli special training class; he transferred several hundred armed troops, men and weapons together, to the Juntong's operatives' corps, and sent several dozen cavalry horses to the special-police class besides. This is only a small part of what I know. Dai Li, for his part, besides sending Hu luxury goods bought abroad, regularly gave gifts on Hu's behalf to various dignitaries at Chungking, drawing personal connections tighter for Hu. At every festival or on these dignitaries' birthdays — men like He Yingqin, Qian Dajun, Lin Wei and Yu Jishi — besides preparing a gift of his own, Dai had me prepare one for Hu as well. Dai was very careful: in sending gifts to a dignitary on Hu's behalf, he always cautioned me to see that the two gifts were not exactly alike, lest others perceive they came from one hand. The things sent out in Hu's name were often slightly higher in value than what Dai sent. Only after they had been sent would Dai wire, or in a long-distance call with Hu mention it, so that Hu could pick up the thread. The money advanced Dai always paid out of his own pocket; Hu was never seen to repay it, nor was it charged under Juntong expenses. In any case both men had inexhaustible large sums of income of murky origin, and neither reckoned such trifles.

The most lavish "gift" Dai gave Hu must be reckoned Hu's wife, Ye Xiadi! On Ye Xiadi's cultivation Dai spent, one may say, no little thought: besides sending her to America for "gilding," he introduced her, after her return, to a professorship at Guanghua University, so that she could take the fine title of "university professor" and be the better able to help Hu in his political activities later. Feeling that the "di" in Ye Xiadi was a touch vulgar, Dai changed the character 弟 to 翟, close in sound but far more elegant on paper. After the victory, Dai also readied a residence for them in Nanking, everything furnished with articles of the highest grade. Unexpectedly, this house later gave rise to an unforeseen dispute. The plot it stood on had belonged to Liu Jianqun, one of the Nationalist chiefs; when the National Government moved back to Nanking, Liu, knowing a traitor had put up a Western-style house on his land, had assumed he could take back land and house together, and, beside himself with delight, prepared to enjoy the windfall. To his surprise, on inquiry, the house had not only been requisitioned by Dai Li a step ahead of him but given to Hu Zongnan to live in. Knowing these two were more formidable than he and that he could not seize it back by force, he sought people everywhere to plead his case. Dai, being busy, took no notice of it. When Dai Li died, Liu could not wait — he set his wife on to take the children and make a great scene at the gate day after day, sleeping there and playing the shameless nuisance. Mao Renfeng, judging that Liu Jianqun too was not a man to be trifled with, had me find Hu another house and give this one up.

Both Hu Zongnan and Dai Li were consumed by ambition, and even in their dreams thought of becoming Chiang Kai-shek's successor. In my years of contact with them I felt that Hu was the more arrogant of the two, and Dai the more sinister. Once I had the chance to hear them hold forth for several hours, which deepened this impression. That time the American secret-service chief Miles had given Dai Li a twenty-line automatic telephone exchange, and Dai at once had me install an automatic telephone in every room of his Zengjiayan mansion, the bathroom included, so that, bathing two or three times a day, he could make his calls even soaking in the tub. I took a senior technician from the General Affairs Department's telephone section round each room to survey and design the installation, while Dai and Hu talked freely in a sitting room outside the bedroom. Moving in and out of these rooms, I heard them for better than two hours off and on. Without the least concealment they were reckoning how to divide the realm between them in future. Hu urged Dai to do his utmost to control the political and economic departments and prepare plenty of men for that side. Dai flattered Hu extravagantly, saying his future military development was beyond measure. And Hu himself gave arrogant voice to the notion that in ruling the military, "who but I?"

Hearing these exchanges, I thought at the time that if Chiang Kai-shek were to die, a great and bloody struggle for power to the death was bound to break out within the Nationalist Party, and that if in the end only these two were left, they would still have to fight it out once more before it was over — never would one really confine himself to the military and the other rest content with politics alone, the two existing side by side. For Hu at that time was running not only the military but politics as well: wherever his military power reached, he seized political power too, and even the secret-service organization run by this most intimate friend of his, he had set up a version of on his own.

Hu Zongnan was then deputy commander-in-chief of the First War Zone, and the second department doing secret-service work in his deputy-commander's office was staffed not by Dai Li's men but by his intimate Liu Qingzeng as its head. In those days almost every other unit had its second department controlled by the Juntong, but Hu would not agree to have Dai Li arrange it uniformly, and ran a separate set of his own. Besides Liu Qingzeng, Hu Zongnan's secret-service organization had several other minor chiefs, men like Liu Dajun, all cultivated by himself. From the time Hu was commander of the First Army, Liu Qingzeng had been chief of the second section in the staff department and had all along run that secret-service organization for him. In those days Chiang Kai-shek forbade any army or military organ of the "Central" system to have its own secret-service system; if Dai Li learned of one, he would move heaven and earth to take it over. But Hu, trading on his relationship with Dai, could be the exception.

Since the Juntong agents all knew the two men's relationship was out of the ordinary, none dared suggest to Dai that it be unified; behind his back, though, there was no little grumbling, to the effect that on this point Hu still did not trust the Juntong. Yet Dai Li still did his utmost to introduce Hu to many wireless-communications technicians and sent him many of the radio sets the Americans had given the Juntong to use. This branch of Hu's work he kept in his own hands for years, not handing it to the Juntong nor letting his agents join the Juntong organization. After the victory, when the deputy-commander's office was abolished and Hu became director of the Xi'an Pacification Headquarters, the second department had the Juntong agent Jin Shuyun appointed as its head by the Second Bureau of the Ministry of National Defence; Hu could only transfer Liu Qingzeng to be a major-general senior staff officer, still keeping his own secret-service organization, run by Liu and the others, unmerged with the Juntong's.

Though Dai Li on the surface exalted Hu Zongnan and let him unify the military, he did not relax his own work on that side and schemed by every means to grasp troops as well. Besides greatly building up armed secret-service forces, he cultivated military talent in quantity. In 1944 Dai Li set up a senior-staff detachment within the intelligence-staff class run by the Second Bureau of the Ministry of Military Operations, which the Juntong controlled. At the same time he recommended a batch of senior Juntong agents — Wei Daming, Yue Zhuyuan, Ruan Qingyuan, Xie Ligong, Wang Yixin and several dozen others — for training at the general-officers' class of the Army University, or the general-officers' class of the Infantry Specialist School, or the advanced instruction class of the Military Academy, and the like. Doing this, he could on one hand let his subordinates acquire officer's qualifications, and on the other draw military cadres to the Juntong. The military-intelligence department head Bao Zhihong, the deputy heads Du Kui and Hu Pinghan, the section chief Ma Qingjiang and others of the time were all pulled into the Juntong after graduating from the Army University.

In grasping troops, however, Dai Li got no help from Hu Zongnan. Dai once asked Hu to give him Fan Hanjie to be commander of the tax-police regiment; Hu never agreed. When Dai wanted someone to command the Loyal and Patriotic Army, Hu recommended Ma Zhichao, who had originally followed Dai. Conversely, men who had originally worked in the Juntong and shown themselves active in anti-Communism, Hu did his utmost to take in and pull over. Liang Ganqiao had followed Dai for years, but because he was zealous in anti-Communism he joined Hu's staff as commissioner of Yaoxian, specially to carry on the criminal work of blockading Yenan and the like. And Zhao Longwen, the most active favourite at Dai Li's side, who before the war had been Hangzhou police chief, was also asked for by Hu to become a counsellor in the "brain trust" around him.

In short, on questions bearing on their vital interests and their own futures, the two men were not truly of one heart and mind, but often at odds, each with his own arrangements and calculations. While Chiang Kai-shek was still alive, the field for each to develop in was still large, and on questions that did not touch fundamental interests they could collude very closely, working hand in glove.

Before his subordinates Dai Li was forever doing his utmost to sing Hu's praises. Every time he came back from an inspection tour of the Northwest, he would openly heap praise on Hu at the memorial-week assembly, saying how capable "Commandant Hu" was in governing his army, how crack his troops, how fine their equipment, how zealous he was in anti-Communism — as though, without Hu holding the Northwest, the Communists would come pouring down to Chungking, and the Chiang dynasty depended wholly on Hu's protection.

Dai Li not only praised Hu Zongnan before the agents but also puffed him up to his American masters on Hu's behalf. I remember that the first time he accompanied Miles on an inspection tour of the Northwest, as usual he called a few of us together to breakfast before setting off, and arranged the work for his absence in passing. That time he spent a long while introducing Hu's character to Miles — how important he was at Chiang Kai-shek's side, that the troops he held were China's crack forces, his role in anti-Communism and Communist-prevention, and a whole pile besides. Miles nodded steadily as he listened and said he was glad to go and see for himself.

When Miles went back to America to recuperate for illness around 1944, and the SACO American chief of staff Bei Leli was acting deputy director, Hu came from Xi'an to Chungking one time, and Dai again specially accompanied Hu on a tour of SACO. When Dai introduced Hu to Bei Leli, he gave Hu another great puffing-up. Standing beside them, I saw that when Bei Leli, having heard Dai's introduction, once more gripped Hu's hand tightly, Hu's air of self-satisfied elation was such that even some of the American agents looking on turned away to laugh in secret.

Hu Zongnan too had his own ways of currying favour with his American masters. On his tour of SACO, whenever he saw an American he would take the initiative to go over and shake hands warmly, waiting for Dai Li and Bei Leli to introduce him. Some of the American agents knew him and were happy to deal with him. He praised everything of the Americans' without exception, offered every American agent his regards, and lauded them all round. Sure enough, after he left, many of the interpreters reported that the American agents had "a very good impression" of Hu. Dai naturally had to tell Hu all this, giving him a few more fits of self-satisfied wild laughter.

Wherever Dai Li went, he had to tell Hu beforehand, and he kept up constant contact with Hu. In exchanging telegrams with Hu he used several sets of cipher books; besides the ordinary one there was a set for "personal decipherment" that he carried on him at all times. What was called "personal decipherment" was in fact not deciphered by themselves but by someone still more trusted on their behalf, the aim being to let as few people as possible understand the questions they discussed. Once Dai Li decided in great haste to go to Guiyang and was a little late in telling Hu. The day after Dai left, Hu sent him a personally-decipherable telegram which the deciphering section of the Juntong's confidential group could not make out — because Dai had taken the personal cipher book away with him. So some of the unspeakable things between them not even Mao Renfeng could then fully know.

In the telegrams they exchanged daily, Dai Li kept Hu informed at all times of every problem arising within the reactionary camp, so that on certain questions they could take a common line, or study things together. The methods they used in currying favour with Chiang Kai-shek were almost identical: besides doing their utmost, in anti-Communist, anti-people work and in clearing away Chiang Kai-shek's rivals, to fall in with Chiang's mind, both worked the inside track and vied to handle Chiang Kai-shek's private affairs and become his trusted men. In this both were equally successful. The two of them agreed to hand Chiang Kai-shek's other wife, Chen Jieru, and his younger son Chiang Wei-kuo to Hu Zongnan's care, and to give Chiang's own elder brother to Dai Li to settle, which further increased their intimate ties with Chiang Kai-shek.

In the spring of 1946 Dai Li was killed in a plane crash. On getting the news, Hu was stricken with grief and hurried to Nanking, going in person to No. 357 Zhongshan Road to offer condolences. I and several senior agents rushed there to attend to things. Entering and seeing Dai's memorial portrait, Hu's tears streamed down; he ran at once into the mourning hall and wept bitterly over the coffin. He wept a long while before asking us about how Dai had been killed. There were many rumours abroad at the time, and Hu had heard not a few, so he asked us what had really happened. We told him that repeated investigation from many sides had confirmed no one had done him harm — it was truly a matter of the weather, the pilot carelessly striking a mountain. He had meant to go and see the place where Dai was killed, but since no car could go straight there and one had to walk many footpaths, he did not go in the end. I gave him a set of photographs of the recovery of Dai's body after his death; he took them with tears, walked twice more round the coffin, and only then left, reluctant to part.

After Dai Li died, Mao Renfeng inherited Dai's mantle, and toward Hu he was not only as before but relied on him all the more. At the time the Juntong was under attack from every quarter, and Hu did his utmost to support it, again and again speaking well of it before Chiang Kai-shek. Many agents then were grateful for such conduct, holding that he had always cherished the Juntong. Mao Renfeng respected Hu greatly, and just as in Dai Li's lifetime kept reporting to him regularly on affairs at Nanking, though not with as many telegrams as in Dai Li's day.

In the autumn or winter of 1947 I went to Xi'an to see Hu, over the matter of the Juntong's tens of thousands of sacks of flour in Xi'an. How this flour had come to be I can no longer recall. At the time Jin Shuyun, head of the second department of the Xi'an Pacification Headquarters, and Hou Zhengbang, chief of the Bureau of Secrets Preservation's Shaanxi station, were quarrelling irreconcilably over seizing this flour, and it had finally reached the Bureau. Mao Renfeng telegraphed Jin Shuyun to hand it over, and Jin would not accept it either. So Mao wrote a letter in his own hand for me to take to Hu. On seeing the letter Hu at once sent for Jin, ordered him to hand it over to me to dispose of, and stated that the Pacification Headquarters did not want the stuff. In this way I was able to sell it off smoothly.

While I was in Xi'an dealing with the flour, the Bureau's action-department head Ye Xiangzhi was there rounding up the Northwest Communist underground organization. Among the leads sorted out, it was found that among Hu Zongnan's secretary and the head of his Northwest news agency were Communist Party members who had been active for years. This was no small matter. Hu held military and civil power in the Northwest, sent not a single soldier even against the Japanese, but bent all his strength against the Communists; and now to find Communists operating in secret at his very side made Hu lose the most face — it was something Chiang Kai-shek could not but give him a sharp scolding for.

Ye Xiangzhi and I lodged together at the China Travel Service guest house in Xi'an, and one midnight he woke me, saying this bore greatly on Hu Zongnan's reputation, and asked how it should be handled. I suggested he ask Mao Renfeng's instructions at once, reckoning an answer could be had within a day. The next day Mao Renfeng wired back that since it touched on Hu's subordinates, Hu should first be given a detailed report, and the relevant case files could all be sent for him to see.

Hu was indeed thunderstruck by this. This man who set such store by face, on hearing there were Communists among his own intimates, turned green with rage. He at once decided to handle personally all the several men implicated among his subordinates, telling Ye Xiangzhi not to concern himself; even in reporting to Chiang Kai-shek, these few men should be listed separately, on no account letting Chiang Kai-shek know. That evening Ye Xiangzhi and I were both invited to dine at his home, and the moment he raised the matter he felt boundless pain, that he should never have imagined there were Communists at his side and gone so long without discovering it. In a self-mocking tone he said to us that because he had devoted himself wholly to grasping the army, he had all along paid no good attention to such things and had been too negligent on this side. He said that in the past he had not even been clear that Fan Changjiang was a Communist, until he recommended Fan to Chiang Kai-shek to go to America and was scolded by Chiang Kai-shek for not even knowing the man was a Communist. Hearing this, we could only say: "The commandant is too busy — where would he find so much time to attend to these things? This is because our work has not been done well." That at least gave him a way down.

Fearing Ye Xiangzhi would talk, he was especially warm toward Ye. In talking with us he kept coming back to his relationship with Dai Li, afraid we might forget it for a moment. When Ye Xiangzhi was preparing to hurry to Lanzhou to round up the Communist underground organization discovered there, Hu Zongnan at once lent Ye his own small private plane to fly to Lanzhou; unexpectedly the plane developed trouble at Pingliang and nearly killed Ye. Hearing this, Hu was greatly alarmed and at once had the air force stationed at Xi'an arrange for another plane to take Ye from Pingliang on to Lanzhou.

In the spring of 1949, when I saw Mao Renfeng in Shanghai, he told me to find time to go to Xi'an once, to bring Ye Xiadi to live in Kunming, and to find Hu a handsome house in Kunming. Because Hu Zongnan and Chen Cheng had always been on bad terms, Hu did not intend to send Ye Xiadi to Taiwan, and Mao Renfeng too hoped she could live in Kunming. I put it off until the summer of that year before going to Xi'an to see Hu, telling him I had readied a house for him in Kunming and hoped he would send Ye there to live. Seeing that the whole situation was by then past saving, and that there would be no place of refuge on the mainland, he indicated he would not send his family to Kunming for the present but to Taiwan after all. That time he questioned me at length about conditions in Yunnan; though he gave no definite word of sending troops there at once, he coveted the place greatly, holding it could be retreated to or held, far better than the Northwest. From the tone of his talk he had already lost confidence that Xi'an could be held, for all that he was still full of "I have methods" before me. His depressed state of mind could be seen from a passage of his talk that time.

He was much dissatisfied that in the Huaihai campaign the Chiang army's commanding generals could not cooperate, so that they were beaten piecemeal and utterly routed. I rashly asked him whether Xi'an might not be in trouble. His manner in answering me was still very proud, but he did say one thing: "So long as the friendly armies don't drag at my heels, there are methods." He boasted, too, that of all the Chiang army's generals he understood the Communists best, that none could match him. He said he had fought the Communists at close quarters for a dozen years and drawn from it a set of lessons, and his methods were cleverer than the Communists'. But less than a month after I left Xi'an, I saw that Xi'an was liberated in only a day or two, and he fled in haste to Hanchung.

After the victory I went twice to Xi'an, and saw with my own eyes and heard how some of his senior officers — men like Sheng Wen, Yuan Pu, Wang Zhen, Dong Zhao — spent their whole days in dissipation, whoring and gambling wildly. Out of curiosity I once asked some of the Juntong men in charge in Xi'an why Hu did not put such corruption in order. This was because Dai Li had always bragged that Hu governed his army with the utmost strictness and set an example in his own person, which was what had prompted my question. They answered me: "Commandant Hu has always boasted how fine his subordinates are; who would dare go and tell him the ugly things these men do? Do it clumsily and you'd run into a rebuff, so people are glad not to bother, and everyone can be happy together, all sweetness and harmony." They also told me that Hu had posted agents to watch his own subordinates, so he could not but know these things, and simply kept one eye open and one shut: so long as the matter was not exposed, he let it slide.

The last time I met Hu, taking my leave, he told me to keep him informed of conditions in Yunnan at all times. After I returned to Yunnan, since I had not arranged a cipher with him, I only had Mao Renfeng send one telegram, asking him to forward it to Hu, reporting to him on Yunnan's condition through the summer and autumn; but I got no reply.

In September 1949, when Mao Renfeng was staying at my house in Kunming, he more than once sighed that Hu Zongnan had not heeded his advice to send two or three armies into Yunnan, or Yunnan could have become the Juntong's own world. For Mao Renfeng, wanting to kill a great batch of progressives in Kunming, had met with Lu Han's refusal and delay and felt very put out. When Hu Zongnan was driven out of Shaanxi by the People's Liberation Army and fled to the Sichuan-Shaanxi border, Mao Renfeng wired him again explaining Yunnan's situation and urging him to detach part of his forces and hurry there so as to secure Yunnan. I too pinned great hopes on this and asked Mao repeatedly for Hu's reply. Only in early October did Mao tell me Hu had wired back that he had for the present no suitable troops to detach, but promised he would certainly try to send troops to Yunnan. Mao also enjoined me to keep it secret; but right up to the eve of Yunnan's peaceful liberation, not a shadow of his troops was ever seen.

The above is part of what I came to know of Hu Zongnan and the Juntong agents. As for his relations with certain other Juntong chiefs, such as Zheng Jiemin and Tang Zong, I saw only their ordinary dealings and do not know of any special relationship between them.
