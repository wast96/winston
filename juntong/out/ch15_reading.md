## The Kunming Fifteenth of July

### How the Student Patriotic Movement Was Suppressed

On 15 July 1948, Nationalist troops and police in Kunming suppressed the student patriotic movement, shooting more than thirty students dead or wounded, arresting over three hundred, and holding them for more than three months. I was then chief of the Yunnan station of the National Defence Ministry's Bureau of Secrets Preservation, the Nationalist secret-service organ, and one of the chief authors and perpetrators of this bloody case. Recalling that monstrous crime, I am to this day overcome with shame and remorse. Let me set down here, as far as I know it, how the events of that day came about.

After the victory over Japan, the student patriotic movement in Kunming ran very high, forever mounting large demonstrations and protesting the Nationalist government's various reactionary measures. The authorities loathed it and again and again put it down with cruel, bloody force, yet not only could not stop it — it grew fiercer by the day. Soon after I took up the Yunnan station post, the Yunnan Garrison commander-in-chief He Shaozhou told me how things stood in the province. What he found most intractable, he said, was that there was no way to deal with the students: too light a hand and they were not afraid, too heavy a hand and one risked antagonizing every quarter. What he resented most was that Yunnan's civil administration would not coordinate closely with him against the students. He complained that the provincial education department shirked its duty, and that the provincial chairman Lu Han dragged his feet, so that he could not do as he pleased and the student tide could not be put down. He very much hoped the Yunnan station would strengthen its work here — to sabotage from within, to get a grip on the student-union organization, and to ferret out those in it with Communist ties. Every student disturbance, he held, was directed by underground Communist workers; otherwise it could never have been mobilized on such a scale, so organized and so planned. If it were merely some students making trouble, he would have no difficulty handling it. So the Yunnan station's main task, he believed, was to make these connections clear and, taking the firewood from under the cauldron, arrest the few leaders linked to the Communists and go on to break the Yunnan Communist underground — that would settle the problem for good.

I quite agreed with this approach at the time and set about strengthening the Yunnan station's student-movement work. In this period the station had only two people on it: the student-movement director Yang Wenzhi and Zhang Fashun, a professional agent-student in the politics department of Yunnan University for the Kunming group. Yang Wenzhi had only ever gathered general information through the teaching staff of a few schools, so I directed him to use those staff connections to recruit a batch of students directly in the schools that were forever making trouble, and to use the clerical staff and workmen of the schools to watch the students' movements and search their studies secretly during class, so as to find leads from every side. Zhang Fashun, a student at Yunnan University, worked only on his own, so I directed him to make more use of fellow provincials and close classmates to set up little reading groups and the like, to draw reliable classmates into formal membership in the secret-service organization with a stipend from the station, and not to do as the agents of the Garrison Headquarters' intelligence and inspection sections did — fixing only on the "fierce Zhang Feis" who made the loudest noise and shouted the hardest, but paying special attention to the "Zhuge Liangs," who said little in the ordinary way and quietly set the "fierce Zhang Feis" on.

At the same time I laid it down that the Kunming group's work should centre on the student movement. Besides having the group leader Luo Ruowei develop leads directly in the schools through relatives, friends and old classmates, I assigned a woman member, Wang Jinlin, to work solely on student-movement activity. Beyond this, the few precincts of the Kunming police bureau near the school districts — public secret-service organs the station controlled — were given the same task; in particular I told Zhou Boxian, chief of the police criminal-investigation corps, to use the basketball and volleyball teams and other athletic bodies the corps had organized to draw in a batch of students and work the student movement for the corps. Through these new arrangements the station's student-movement intelligence improved in quantity and quality, but as for controlling the student tide and running down leads to the Communist underground workers, there was never any way.

In June the students of Kunming again massed more than thirty thousand strong in a great march against American support of Japan. After the march they gathered on the lawn before the American consulate to demonstrate, shouting slogans and handing in a letter of protest, until the consulate staff, frightened, shut the iron gate fast and dared not come out to answer. The students also pasted slogans in Chinese and English outside the consulate gate. He Shaozhou sent out a large force of troops and police, but even so could not disperse them.

That night He Shaozhou called us to a meeting at his home. Those present were the Kunming police chief Wang Wei, the thirteenth military-police regiment commander Peng Jinren, the Garrison Headquarters inspection chief Ruan Gengsheng, the intelligence chief Liu Jianyi, and the political-work chief Liu Shanshu. After hearing several men report on the day's march, He said in great anger: "What a disgrace! So many of us and we could not stop this one march. Do you know — the students go to the American consulate to protest, and the Americans protest to me. That is as galling as a slap in the face."

After the meeting the others left, but He Shaozhou specially had me stay to talk it over with him. We worked out how to put the matter to the Yunnan Special Joint Conference for discussion, to force the province's Nationalist party headquarters and provincial government to fall in step with us and take part in armed suppression of the Kunming student tide, so that no one would drag his feet or make cold remarks from the sidelines. Because the students' parents mostly had dealings with the province's party, government and army people, there was a fear that some, out of personal feeling, would oppose the use of force. He Shaozhou felt that unless the students were shown some colour, things could not go on in peace. I too felt that armed suppression carried out together with sabotage from within could do much to clear away the Kunming student tide, and I agreed that we should take concerted action for one great crackdown.

The Yunnan Special Joint Conference, because its convener, the provincial chairman Lu Han, had never much cared for it, had played little part in the past, but it still had to meet once a month or every other month. In early July, after I got the notice of the meeting sent under Lu Han's alias, I first talked with He Shaozhou, and we decided to raise at this meeting the question of armed suppression of the student tide, and to ask He to take the lead in bringing it up. He too thought the moment right and that the chance should not be missed.

Those at this meeting were Lu Han; the provincial-government secretary-general Zhu Jingxuan; the civil-affairs chief Yang Wenqing; the education chief Wang Zheng; the construction chief Long Tiyao; the police-affairs chief Li Yuzhen; He Shaozhou and his deputy commander-in-chief and chief of staff Ma Ying; the Yunnan Security Command chief of staff Xie Chongwen; the CC Clique's man in charge in Yunnan, Zha Zongfan; and me — some ten-odd in all. Besides the usual relaying of the central Special Joint Conference's directive telegrams, we reported on and discussed the current problems in Yunnan.

As it happened, the central directive of that meeting touched on some matters bearing on the prevention of student disturbances in various places. He Shaozhou then reported at some length on the recent anti-American march and the demonstration before the consulate, and said: "To prevent any such incident from recurring, we should carry out a sweep of Yunnan University, the Teachers' College, Nanjing Middle School and other schools." He stressed again and again that this student tide was not simply a matter of students making trouble, that there was certainly Communist manipulation behind it, and that if it were not checked in time it might at any moment grow into student rioting in the city.

After this alarmist speech of He Shaozhou's, all present agreed to a planned sweep, to strike hard at the swelling student tide. The education chief Wang Zheng in particular felt there was no better course, for He had rebuked the education department in his speech, holding that the office in charge of education was too irresponsible and grew ever more indulgent of the students. In the end He Shaozhou volunteered to take charge of the work himself, asking the provincial government, the Security Command, the CC Clique's bureau and the Bureau of Secrets Preservation and other units to assist.

On the morning of about 8 or 9 July, He Shaozhou invited me to a meeting at the Garrison Headquarters. Those present were the police chief, the military-police regiment commander, and the section chiefs of the Garrison Headquarters. He reported on the recent Kunming student tide, explained that the Special Joint Conference had agreed he should take charge of a sweep, and asked each unit concerned to supply blacklists at once. The day after the meeting I sent him the information the station held on some student activity in a few schools, together with a list of the student-union leaders. On the afternoon of the 10th he had me go to his home again to discuss the sweep, and it was decided to begin the action on the night of the 11th; he also asked me to keep supplying him with relevant material.

On the afternoon of the 11th He Shaozhou assembled plainclothes agents of the inspection section and the criminal-police corps, two companies of military police, two squadrons of the police security corps, and a company of the Headquarters' agent battalion, and after ten at night surrounded the students and dormitories of Yunnan University and the Teachers' College, arresting over thirty students and sending them to the Headquarters' judge-advocate lockup, where the judge-advocate Ding Longkai interrogated them in person through the night. This sudden sweep started the students from their sleep at midnight with no time to resist; but apart from a few of the most active being taken, some important leaders of the Kunming student union were not caught, nor were important documents found. The next morning I telephoned He to ask how it had gone; he was not satisfied and meant to carry out a more thorough sweep.

News that the Garrison Headquarters had arrested a great number of students in the dead of night quickly spread through Kunming. The parents of the arrested and the students of many schools at once organized themselves, appealing on every side for the arrested students' rescue. The provincial assembly held an emergency session, hoping the matter would soon be cleared up and the innocent students arrested released at once, lest public anger be roused. He Shaozhou paid no heed to any of this and insisted on pressing the suppression further.

From the 12th, to guard against another sweep by the troops and police, the students of the various schools gathered in numbers at Yunnan University, standing watch by night in turns, reinforcing the gaps in the surrounding walls, and blocking the gate for self-defence. Though they were now cut off within and without, the doings of the more than ten thousand students massed at Yunnan University still reached the agents' ears at any time through some of the clerical staff. The students' feeling was extremely high, their hatred of the reactionary authorities' persecution fiercer still. All day in the campus they carried on every kind of activity against the Nationalists; angry poems and protest essays were pasted all over the school, and their impassioned singing could be heard across the walls.

On receiving reports of this sort, He Shaozhou slandered it as the students' preparation for a great uprising in Kunming, which could not be put down except by greater force. On the afternoon of the 14th he assembled all the troops, police, military police and agents he could move in Kunming, meaning to storm Yunnan University at one blow, carry out a still larger sweep, and disperse the massed students entirely. That night I sat with He Shaozhou at the Garrison Headquarters awaiting news. He found these students too much of a headache and swore that the Nationalists' men in charge of education were all good-for-nothings, raising students only to oppose their own side. He wanted to propose closing these schools, lest the Communists make use of them.

This attack on Yunnan University was in the dead of night; I did not see it with my own eyes and heard of it only by telephone. When the great force of troops and police reached the school, the students on night watch spotted them and at once struck the alarm bells; the students, startled from sleep, rose to resist. These unarmed university and middle-school students were beaten savagely with rifle butts and clubs; unable to hold out, they were soon overrun and the troops broke through the gate. Fighting as they fell back, the students were mostly cut off by the reactionary troops and police who poured in over the walls from every side. Some retreated to the classrooms, dormitories and dining halls and were placed under guard; a portion fell all the way back to Huize Hall, the largest building at Yunnan University, and held out there to the last, so that the great force of troops and police ringed the building round and round.

Just at daybreak, as He Shaozhou and I were taking breakfast, we suddenly heard a burst of gunfire. He hurriedly had his aide telephone to ask what had happened, and presently the answer came: because the students, falling back from the ground floor to the second, had fought back with bricks and tiles, the troops and police had fired to overawe them. Anxious to see for myself, I hurried to the scene. On the several roads near the university I saw armed troops and police standing thick, all traffic cut off. Of the onlookers there were close to a thousand, many of them parents wanting to go in to see their children, all barred from entering. Some old people were weeping and crying aloud; everywhere was the sound of the crowd's anger.

When I reached the front of Huize Hall, I saw the police chief Wang Wei, the Garrison Headquarters inspection chief Ruan Gengsheng, and Liu Jianyi and Liu Shanshu directing the troops and police in their attack on the students in the building. By now more than three hundred students had fallen back from the lower floors all the way to the top of the fourth, and had torn down the stairs, resolved not to come down.

On receiving the report, He Shaozhou came at once in person. Having asked how things stood, he said to think of another way. I suggested to him that the students' parents be let in to call them down. He agreed, and let in a great crowd of parents who had been waiting at the school gate. These old people called their children's names in hoarse voices, but not one student answered; after about an hour, not one had come down. He Shaozhou had the police chief Wang Wei hold up to the fourth floor a length of white cloth on which was written in large characters "those who come down of their own accord will not be pursued," and had the fire brigade's aerial ladder raised to the roof of the fourth floor, ready to force an entry. Not only did the students not come down — they tore off the stone balustrade of the flat roof and flung down the bricks. Spotting He Shaozhou, they threw the bricks at him. One brick struck He on the left hand, and Wang Wei hastily took the steel helmet off a fire officer and put it on He, and escorted him to rest on the second floor. He then ordered the troops and police to fire on the students.

At this point the Teachers' College dean, Zha, the Yunnan University president Xiong Qinglai, and the heads of several other middle schools all hurried up, and with one voice asked that such methods not be used on the students, the school heads being willing to find a way to resolve it. He Shaozhou said fiercely: "Have you a way to call them all down at once?" Seeing how unreasonable he was, none of the school heads dared guarantee it could be done, and they fell silent. He then ordered Wang Wei, Peng Jinren and the rest to clear out first the students penned in the classrooms, dormitories and dining halls, to pick out and take away the leaders of the Kunming student union and the heads of the several school unions, and to drive the rest out of the school; as for the more than three hundred who would not come down, a portion of the force was to keep the building surrounded and see how long they could hold out.

After He Shaozhou left, the military police and agents, sorting through the students penned in various places, searched most of a day and could not find more than a few of the blacklisted. Since most of the student-union backbone had fled to the fourth floor, and the students shielded one another and would not give them up, only a few known to the agents from before were pointed out on the spot and seized, while the rest were driven out of the school. Of the arrested students, those whose names I remember are Wen Zongjiang, Bai Mingshun, Wang Youzhen, Fu Jinde, Dong Deping and Jin Weihe.

The same day, the military police besieging Nanjing Middle School tried to climb the wall into the school's office building, but the students were prepared and they could not get in. The military police then used ladders to break in through the windows on all sides, only to have the students block the windows. Four military police climbed onto the roof, meaning to lift the tiles and enter the building; one of them lost his footing and fell from the roof, dying on the spot. Seeing a man fall to his death, the regiment commander ordered firing to threaten the students, but they held to the struggle. The next day a great force of military police charged up from every side, wounding scores of students and arresting over thirty, who were later held together with the students taken at Yunnan University at Nanjing Middle School for "disciplinary training."

After the incident, all Kunming was thrown into turmoil, the whole city uneasy, the people's hatred of the authorities for using such bloody means against patriotic students at its very extreme; everywhere one heard the cry "save the children." The parents of some of the students besieged on the fourth floor of Yunnan University wept and cried outside the school gate day and night, begging everyone for mercy and seeking news. Some progressive papers too raised their reproaches. But He Shaozhou still insisted that the ringleading students must be arrested.

The two sides held out to the 16th with no result. I went to Yunnan University once more that day. Though the students had been besieged on the roof a whole day without food, they still posted lookouts on every side against the military police and prevented them from climbing up. While I was discussing what to do with Wang Wei, Liu Shanshu, Ruan Gengsheng and the rest, I noticed the students pouring water down from the building. I knew a man could hold out a day or two without eating, but not so easily without water, and so proposed that if the students were to be forced down, the running water should be cut off. All heard it and readily agreed, and Wang Wei had the firemen shut the water main to the roof. Presently, sure enough, a clamour came from the roof: "The water's gone! The water's gone!" But this vicious device too took no effect; still no one came down, and another day and night passed like this.

On the morning of the 17th, the Yunnan provincial chairman Lu Han himself went to Yunnan University, set up a loudspeaker below the building, and in a tone of warm concern urged the besieged students not to hold out any longer. Hearing Lu's voice, the students all gathered at the edge of the building to listen. At last Lu gave his guarantee that they would not be punished, saying that to hold out like this was to their own harm; that there had been nothing so very serious about it, but that if it went on developing because they would not heed good advice, the consequences would be unthinkable. When Lu had finished, he called out again and again for the students to come down at once. By now I too had hurried over. Seeing that the students still gave no sign, Lu once more spoke a few earnest words. In his speech he said: "If you still will not listen to me, I shall have nothing more to do with you!" With that he left the loudspeaker and made to get into his car.

At this a shout went up among the students: "Chairman, don't go — we'll come down! We'll listen to you!" Hearing the shout, Lu turned back and stood below the building. Now the blocked door to the fourth floor was opened, and the police standing below also called out "They're coming down!" Lu stood below and watched the students come down in single file. Some of the lightly wounded were helped along by classmates; the gravely wounded could only be got down carried on others' backs. Lu ordered only that Wang Wei and the school authorities quickly get the students breakfast and tea, and enjoined that the gravely wounded be sent to hospital first and the lightly wounded have their wounds dressed. Some of the students who had been shot, though they had bound their wounds with clothing, were still bleeding, their clothes soaked with blood. Once down, they rested apart in several classrooms, every one of them worn to exhaustion.

Wang Wei and I went up to the fourth floor and saw that there were still several sacks of rice. From this it was plain that, had Lu Han not come forward to call them down, they would have held out several days more. No sooner had Lu gone than He Shaozhou hurried up. Seeing me, he said angrily: "He" — meaning Lu Han — "plays the good man and leaves us to be the villains. I fear nothing; but his promising not to pursue the matter will not come so cheap. We must still find a way to settle this problem thoroughly."

That afternoon He Shaozhou ordered this batch of students taken by truck to Nanjing Middle School and held in confinement, under the name of "long-term training" for the outside world. He designated the political-work chief Liu Shanshu to take charge, on the one hand carrying out military drill and, with emphasis, fascist poisoning of their minds, and on the other using mutual denunciation and bought informers to screen out the progressive students. He hoped to make clear the background of some of the Kunming student-union leaders, so as to break the Communist underground in Yunnan. And so in this concentration camp in another guise, denunciation and screening went on constantly in the form of discussion meetings. The Bureau of Secrets Preservation, the CC Clique's bureau, the Garrison Headquarters, the military police, the police and other secret-service organs sent men to work at it without cease. But according to the reports of the station's student-movement director Yang Wenzhi and the agents of the Kunming group, the students held together very tightly and it was far from easy to reach the further aim of breaking the Communist organization. He Shaozhou too was much vexed by this; and as, one after another, several more of the ten-odd gravely wounded students died, the reproaches on every side forced him to give up his scheme of holding this batch of students indefinitely, and after three months of "training" it was declared at an end. Beyond rousing the broad masses to greater fury, this bloody case of savage suppression of the Kunming student patriotic movement gained the reactionary authorities nothing, and had to be let drop.
