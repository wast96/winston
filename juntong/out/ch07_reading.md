## The Murder of General Yang Hucheng

### Home to Fight Japan, Detained by Chiang's Trick

After the Xi'an Incident, Chiang Kai-shek was released and returned to Nanking, where he turned his face about at once and refused to acknowledge a single promise he had made at Xi'an. He hated Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng to the marrow: Zhang he placed under arrest, and Yang he forced to resign his posts and go abroad on an "inspection tour." Yang Hucheng could only take his wife Xie Baozhen, his young son Zhengzhong and his aides on a tour through Europe, staying for a period in Switzerland. Then came the July 7th Incident, when the Communist Party and the whole people stood together for united resistance to Japan, for national salvation and survival, and firmly against Chiang Kai-shek's policy of selling out the country and capitulating. Hearing this news in Europe, Yang judged that Chiang Kai-shek might now honour his old promises, and careless of his own safety he resolutely gathered his family and sailed home to share the national crisis. But Chiang Kai-shek had never for a moment forgotten the grudge of his detention at Xi'an, and still set private scores above everything. The moment word came that Yang had started for home, he summoned the Juntong chief Dai Li to Nanchang by wire and gave him his instructions face to face. Dai Li returned to Wuhan and at once ordered the Juntong detachment captain Li Jiajie to pick out more than twenty plain-clothes guards from the detachment; Dai Li called in and looked over every man by name, and Li Jiajie then led them ahead to Nanchang to make the arrangements. Chiang Kai-shek added a company of military police besides, to share the work of escorting and guarding Yang and to forestall any accident.

By the time Yang Hucheng reached his homeland, the fighting of August 13th at Shanghai had long since broken out, and the reactionary Nationalist government was in headlong flight toward Wuhan. On reaching Hong Kong, Yang received a telegram from Chiang Kai-shek and went first to Wuhan, where Dai Li met him at Wuchang. Chiang Kai-shek then gave out, falsely, that he would receive him at Nanchang, so Dai Li accompanied Yang Hucheng there from Hankou by special plane; and on arrival Yang was shut up under guard in a villa belonging to Xiong Shihui on Baihuazhou in Nanchang. Only then did he understand that he had walked into Chiang Kai-shek's trap. His indignation knew no bounds, but there was nothing to be done. Xiong Shihui's villa was a detached little Western-style house; the inner watch was kept entirely by Juntong agents, the outer ring of sentries by military police, and the precautions were uncommonly thorough. What Chiang Kai-shek had feared most was that Yang, once home, would make first for the Northwest, where it would be hard to lay hands on him; hence the decision to seize him at once. Yang's wife, son and aides had already gone back to Xi'an, but Chiang would not let even them alone: they too were presently lured to Nanchang and imprisoned with him.

In December 1937, when Nanking fell, Chiang Kai-shek ordered Dai Li, before leaving Nanchang himself, to move Yang, his wife and his son to the rear. They travelled in a special car on the Zhejiang-Jiangxi railway through Zhuzhou to Changsha, closely guarded the whole way, boarding and leaving the train only by night. Yang lived for a while at the Zhu family garden in the eastern suburbs of Changsha, and was then taken under escort to Taohuaping in Yiyang, where he was lodged in a village house near the temporary prison the Juntong kept there. At the evacuation of Wuhan the whole family was moved on again, to Guizhou. On the last night before they left Yiyang, the Juntong murdered the twenty-odd progressive figures then imprisoned at Yiyang, people who had been moved there in the withdrawals from Nanking, Nanchang and Wuhan. According to Li Jiajie, then captain of the detachment guarding Yang, Yang heard the shots and the screams near by and did not sleep the whole night, believing they meant to kill him there. He held his little boy and said: "If I die, who will there be to look after you?" How could he have dreamed that when Chiang Kai-shek did finally have him killed, even the child would fall victim to the same murderous hands? Setting out at first light next day, he saw the agents burying the victims' bodies near by; he said not one word, and climbed into the car carrying a grief past measuring. After they reached Xifeng county in Guizhou, Yang Hucheng and his family lived for a period in the detention house the Juntong kept at Yanglangba. That detention house later became the Juntong's Xifeng prison, a concentration camp with room for three or four hundred, reserved for imprisoning Communists, progressives, and Juntong agents who had broken Juntong discipline. Luo Shiwen and Che Yaoxian of the Communist Party's Sichuan provincial committee, the Party members Zhang Weilin, Zhang Luping, Feng Chuanqing and Yang Guang, and many others besides were held there, as were the democratic figures Ma Yinchu and Huang Xiansheng. Two or three years in the place left a man so broken he could not manage half a li on his own feet. The Juntong's own agents shuddered at the mention of that prison; what its persecution of the revolutionaries was like may be imagined!

### Prison Life at Xifeng

In 1939 Dai Li came to Xifeng. Judging that Yanglangba lay too close to the highway, where something might go wrong, he ordered the agents to find another place. They discovered Xuantian Cave, on the top of a high mountain a dozen li and more from the Xifeng county town; Dai Li climbed up to look the ground over himself, and decided Yang should be moved there. As soon as the buildings put up near by to house the agent detachment and the military police were finished, Yang was transferred.

Xuantian Cave is a great natural cavern, upwards of ten zhang high, holding a Daoist temple inside, with a single large mouth for going in and out — which made the guarding of it supremely convenient. The agents drove the priests out and seized the place. Standing inside the cave, the temple never sees sunlight from one year's end to the next, and the damp is beyond anything. The Yang family, used to the dry air of the Northwest, took badly to such surroundings, and with their spirits low as well they were constantly falling ill. By now Yang could see that Chiang Kai-shek had no thought of releasing him any time soon, and planning for the long term, he asked again and again to be allowed to put up a house outside the cave at his own expense and live there. The captain of his guard detachment, Li Jiajie, a man from Yanjin in Yunnan and greedy for money past all curing, heard that Yang was willing to pay for the building himself and pressed the request on Dai Li for all he was worth, until at last it was approved. Yang very happily produced four hundred American dollars and handed them to Li Jiajie to manage the work. Li first skimmed himself a profit on the changing of the dollars, then scamped the work and stinted the materials, so that the house came out wretchedly crude. Yang was furious at it, but once more there was nothing to be done, and he made the best of things.

Chiang Kai-shek repeatedly enjoined Dai Li to take no chances in the guarding of Yang. In 1940 Dai went to Xifeng again to see to the dispositions in person. Besides the plain-clothes agents keeping close watch on all four sides of Yang's dwelling, the military police outside were posted in two further belts. The company headquarters sat on the high ground of the hill behind, commanding both the rear slope and Yang's house; the Juntong detachment sat at the mouth of the uphill path in front. By day the sentry posts stood at distances where each could see the next clearly; at night the ring drew in tighter still, and the watches were kept by beating them. The instrument was a section of big nan bamboo cut with two slots, like the bamboo clappers the cities once used for the night watch, which gives out a great noise when struck with a wooden baton. Every night, at set intervals, the first post struck a few beats; the instant the sound fell, the second post had to take it up, and so it went round, one after another, and if a single post failed to sound, the duty man went at once to find out why. From nightfall until the next dawn, the clack of the watch rang through those high hills and deep valleys.

By day Yang could walk about a little outside his door. What he loved best was to walk out to a spur a few dozen paces from the house and gaze at the highway that ran through Xifeng: no outsider came up that mountain the whole year round, and only from there could he watch the cars pass and repass on the road. After nightfall he was not allowed out of doors at all, and could stir only inside the house.

I was then head of the General Affairs Department at the Juntong bureau, and Dai Li often sent me to look in on Zhang Xueliang, imprisoned at Tongzi in Guizhou, and Yang Hucheng at Xifeng, to see how they were living and bring them a few things to eat and use as I came. Dai Li at that time arranged considerably better daily treatment for Zhang Xueliang, because Zhang's ties with T. V. Soong ran deep and Dai took special care of him on that account; moreover, up to 1944 Zhang's wife Yu Fengzhi and Zhao Yidi (that is, Fourth Miss Zhao) took turns keeping him company, each in her year off going to America for a rest and always coming back with a good many things, so his life was somewhat the easier. As for Yang Hucheng, Chiang Kai-shek hated him with a particular hatred, and no relief from outside ever reached him. What money he had of his own he could not well bring out and use; the fabi was falling in value by the day, and the agents through whose hands things passed skimmed their oil off everything, so that his life was genuinely hard. Even getting a new suit of padded cotton clothes was no small matter. Every time I went to see him he would pour out his hardships to me behind the guards' backs. What sympathy had I for him in those days? I merely made the proper noises and told him to be patient. Even when the Juntong now and then adjusted the schedule of Yang's maintenance, it never held good more than a few days; the fabi went on falling, and it was short of this and out of that day after day, so that the small business of daily life was a standing torment to him.

Yang was much concerned in those days for the education of the young son he kept beside him. The Juntong would not let the boy go out to school, for fear word would leak; so Yang asked many times to be allowed to buy an encyclopaedia (or the Wanyou Wenku) for the boy to read, and in the end this was permitted. It pleased him greatly, and he often took the volumes up himself to pass the time. Whenever I talked with him, he would speak only of the things he had seen travelling in Europe, and never let one word fall about the Xi'an Incident. Once, when the old story of how he and Li Huchen held Xi'an happened to come up, he at once declined to carry the subject further. What I admired in him was his memory, and the whole-souled attention he gave when anyone read to him. In conversation he could recall with perfect clarity where and when he had seen a thing; and where something in another man's talk was doubtful or against the facts, he could point it out, item by item, once the man had finished.

Around 1941 Yang's wife bore another child, a girl. It brought him no small comfort of spirit; but the passing solace soon turned to grief. The Juntong held that his wife, Xie Baozhen, was progressive in her thinking, and not only watched her words and doings with particular closeness but made a practice of goading her; and after years of one galling thing upon another, her mind began, little by little, to give way. She loathed the agents — even Dai Li, when he came, she would rebuke in fury. This only fed the agents' resentment and spite, and they set themselves to finding new ways to torment her, giving her scarcely any of the care that was her due even after her confinement. Once at a meal, just as she was finding the coarse food nearly impossible to swallow, the detachment captain Li Jiajie happened in to see them, and she confronted him with it. Li answered her insolently; in her fury she flung the bowl in her hand at him. Li saw how things stood and took to his heels, and on getting back he reported that she was gravely deranged and should be separated from Yang, lest she disturb Yang's life. The Juntong approved Li's proposal and forced them apart: she was to go back and live alone inside Xuantian Cave. Only at Yang's repeated pleading did they agree instead that she should live in the house he had built outside, while Yang himself moved into the damp and dark of the cave. So husband and wife were pulled asunder. Yang grieved and raged at it; but in such circumstances, what was there to do? Under this treatment his wife's mind gave way further and further; she cried out and railed the day long, and the agents, not one of whom dared face her, kept her shut in her room all day and would not let her out. Whenever Yang heard her cries, not daring to go and comfort her, he could only weep where no one saw, in misery past telling.

In the winter of 1941 or the spring of 1942, Dai Li came to see Yang, and Yang, at the end of what could be borne, asked him to replace Li Jiajie, who had made him suffer too much. Dai could only consent, and on returning to Chungking he sent Gong Guoyan to relieve Li as captain, making Zhang Jingfu, till then the detachment's medical officer, deputy captain as well. Dai Li took the view that Li Jiajie's constant reporting on the Yangs had been guard work of real merit, and promoted him colonel and deputy chief of the guard section of the Central Training Corps, to act in the chief's post Dai himself held concurrently.

Gong Guoyan, a Zhejiang man, was an agent out of Dai Li's Hangzhou special training class, and Dai had always counted him loyal and dependable. In outward manner he was milder with people than Li; in substance he was every bit as venomous and sly. Zhang Jingfu, from Hebei, was an agent out of Dai Li's Linli special training class in Hunan; he had studied medicine for some years but had next to no experience at the bedside, and beyond squeezing his oil out of the medicine bills there was no illness he could cure. When Yang or his wife fell ill they did not send for Zhang Jingfu, but usually asked the medical officer of the Juntong's Xifeng training class to come and treat them, with a Chinese physician called in once in a while. Around 1944 her condition took a turn for the better, and only then was Yang permitted to move out of the cave and live with her again.

To the arrangements already described, Dai Li added one more care: he particularly instructed Zhou Yanghao (Yangzhai), head of the Juntong's Xifeng prison, to go over regularly and look in. Zhou was from Dai's own little home district, and a secret-service chieftain of the utmost cruelty and violence; Dai trusted him greatly, and had warm praise for Zhou's whole close-woven system for controlling the prisoners at Xifeng. The detachment guarding Yang answered directly to the Juntong's judicial department and to the operatives' corps, but it was placed under Zhou's supervision as well: one more layer of relations, the better to guard against some mishap over the long years. Zhou went often to keep Yang company over small-stakes mahjong, Gong Guoyan always in the room, and Yang, whose heart was never in it, lost every time. Even with all this in place Dai Li was not yet easy, so he reached out and took the office of Xifeng county magistrate itself, putting in the Juntong agents Deng Kuangyuan, Xu Yuyi and Chen Guozhen as magistrates one after another. With that, every organ of the locality was gathered into the hands of Juntong agents, all made to serve the work of guarding Yang. The Juntong at Xifeng then had its special training class, its prison and its depots, and on top of these the detachment guarding Yang, the military police, the radio station and the electrical-machine works; had anyone but a Juntong agent held the magistracy, he would have seen the very gates of the county government pulled down by those fiends. The common people who lived near those agent posts cried to heaven and had nowhere to turn; they could only look on while agents with pistols in their belts rode roughshod over everything and stopped at nothing. Even the drivers whose routes lay through Xifeng went with their hearts in their mouths: any car on the highway had to stop the instant an agent raised a hand, and if it was slow in stopping, there came the crack of a shot; if it still failed to take the hint, the second round put a hole through the windscreen.

### The Moves after the Victory

When the news of Japan's surrender reached Xifeng, Yang, his wife and his son leapt up for joy at the hearing of it. But when Yang recalled how he had hurried back from Europe with a breast full of patriotic ardour to share the national crisis, and had then not only done not one stroke of work in the whole war but spent it imprisoned by Chiang Kai-shek for eight years, his grief and anger overflowed. He believed now that Chiang would release him; and yet he feared that in the flush of victory Chiang might forget a man still shut away among desolate mountains and deep valleys, and several times he took up his pen to write to Chiang and to Yu Youren, asking not to be forgotten, asking to be let out soon. For demanding that Chiang resist Japan and stop fighting the civil war, he had held Chiang half a month; for that he had been imprisoned eight years; surely, he thought, so old a score could be written off at last. He would finish a letter and tear it up, write and tear up again, hoping all the while that Chiang would set him free. In the summer of 1946 the Juntong wound up all its establishments at Xifeng and merged them into Chungking, and it was decided that Yang too should be moved to Chungking.

When Yang learned he was to return to Chungking, his joy and hope were past all telling. But the moment he arrived, he was imprisoned in a single-storey house at Yangjiashan, inside the Sino-American Special Technical Cooperative Organization compound under Geleshan. Now every hope was gone, and he passed his days in long sighs. His wife was angrier still, and forever crying out that Chiang Kai-shek had no heart in his body and that the agents were not human beings; Yang, knowing this could only bring worse upon them, was constantly restraining her. Eight years of torment had broken her health lower and lower. The agents hated her: when she fell ill they not only gave her no treatment but found pretexts to goad her further. In 1947 she died, her breast still full of unspent anger. Yang's grief was terrible. He set the box of his wife's ashes at his own pillow, and passed the days holding his small daughter, the old man's tears streaming down, his appetite falling away. His voice had once carried tremendously — at wine, at the finger-game, it rang beyond the room; now his speech turned low and dull, and the flesh fell from him. When these reports reached Chiang Kai-shek's ear, he always allowed himself a satisfied smile. Before long Yang developed a severe gallstone condition, and only in 1948 was he permitted to be taken for surgery to the "Four-One Hospital" the Juntong had run near the SACO compound — by then handed over to the Central Hospital and renamed its Shaci branch. The surgeons took a whole handful of stones out of his gallbladder: the fruit of years of drinking the mineral-laden spring water at Xuantian Cave in Xifeng. When he had mended a little he was moved back to the old place. Through this period his mood was restless in the extreme, and he often wrangled with Gong Guoyan over small matters of daily life. Once when I went to see him he was very low. I proposed a stroll near by; he would not go, and had me sit and talk with him instead. Gong Guoyan told me afterwards that Yang had once walked round behind Yangjiashan and come upon a grave, with the four characters "The Abode of Rest of the Yangs" cut into the stone railing before it. He said with great feeling: "How is the coincidence so exact? One character's difference from my own name." The words were barely out when he looked up: on the ornamental column beside the grave were carved the words "dragon coiled, tiger crouching." The scene went home. He gave a long sigh: "My name is all here already. I live in this place today; that I shall die in it is past doubting." After that he was most unwilling to walk beyond his door, unwilling to set eyes on the grave that carried his name.

### The Killing, Before and After

In January 1949, Chiang Kai-shek — having gambled away in the three great campaigns, Liaoshen, Pingjin and Huaihai, the whole capital he had spent so many years building up for use against the Communists and the people — could do nothing else, and announced his retirement.

When Li Zongren took the stage as acting president, he hoped to gull the people awhile with a show of sham democracy and drag out the regime's last gasp; so he proclaimed a release of political prisoners, and ordered by name the immediate release of Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng. When the Chungking *Central Daily News* printed this, Gong Guoyan, who had been told nothing beforehand, did not know what to do. He dared not let Yang learn of it, and held back that day's paper. Yang read the papers every day and followed the state of the country; he knew things must be moving — if Chiang Kai-shek had retired, perhaps his case was no longer in Chiang's hands. Missing that one day's paper made him suspicious, and he kept after Gong demanding it, until Gong had no choice but to let him see it. Yang laughed aloud: "So this day has come at last!" From then on he held himself ready to leave at any hour, believing nothing further stood in the way.

Li Zongren had given the order in the acting president's name; but whatever the retired Chiang Kai-shek had not consented to still could not be done, and no one accepted the order. Mao Renfeng had by then led the agents of the Bureau of Secrets Preservation (the Juntong's name after its reorganization) out of Nanking to offices in Shanghai, and simply ignored the whole business. The acting president, seeing his order go unhonoured, telegraphed the mayor of Chungking, Yang Sen, directing him to release Yang Hucheng. Yang Sen, Chiang Kai-shek's faithful running dog and the best of friends with Mao Renfeng, was hardly going to do Li Zongren's bidding: he gave out that Mao Renfeng was away from Chungking, and that he himself did not know where Yang Hucheng was held. The Chungking papers then carried the banner headline "WHERE IS MAO RENFENG?" — the gist being that according to Mayor Yang, since Mao Renfeng could not be found and no one knew where Yang Hucheng was, the acting president's order could not be executed. In fact Yang Sen not only had long known exactly where Yang Hucheng was, but was seeing him regularly. And fearing that delay would breed change, it was Yang Sen who proposed that Yang Hucheng be moved elsewhere for the time being.

Zhou Yanghao, by then head of the Bureau's public-property management group at Chungking (he had been transferred there when the Xifeng prison closed), telephoned Mao Renfeng long-distance for instructions, speaking the local dialect of Jiangshan county, Zhejiang; Mao asked Chiang Kai-shek, and it was decided to move Yang's imprisonment to Guizhou. When Gong Guoyan, Xu Yuanju (Yuanfei) and the others put the move to him, Yang burst out in anger: "The acting president has ordered me released. Why are you still keeping me locked up? I refuse to go — if I am to die, I will die here!" Gong argued hard and coaxed soft, to no effect, and quarrelled with him over it repeatedly. In the end Xu Yuanju asked Zhou Yanghao to come and talk him round. Zhou set out the position over and over: not going was simply not open to him, for without Chiang Kai-shek's word no one else's counted for anything; let him bear it yet a little. After holding out one more day Yang agreed to go to Guizhou, but laid down three conditions to be settled first: that Gong Guoyan be removed, who over the years had tormented him quite enough; that his secretary Song Qiyun and Song's wife be brought from the other detention house to go with him; and that Zhou Yanghao escort him to Guizhou himself. All three were approved by Mao Renfeng. When the detachment captain Gong Guoyan was transferred out, there was posted in his place a man fiercer than Gong: Zhang Hu, warden of the Baigongguan detention house at the Sino-American Cooperative Organization. Song Qiyun had been Yang's secretary for many years, and had later been imprisoned by Chiang Kai-shek together with his wife, always kept apart from Yang. In prison Song's wife had borne a boy, a year or two older than Yang's little girl. Now, at Yang's request, the Songs and their child were to travel with him to Guizhou. That loyal secretary had never once, in all those years, changed his bearing toward Yang.

When everything was ready, Zhou Yanghao hurried the whole party off to a house at Qilin Cave, under Qianling Mountain outside Guiyang. The spot is one of Guiyang's beauty places; Dai Li, to please Chiang Kai-shek, had built a mansion for him there, though in fact Chiang Kai-shek never once stayed in it and Dai Li had the whole enjoyment of it himself — the Juntong in those days called it the Qilin Cave mansion. Here Yang lived another seven months and more. He read the papers every day with the closest attention, following the people's army as it went from victory to victory in the war of liberation. He grew warier than ever, never letting fall an attitude on the situation, for fear of feeding Chiang Kai-shek's grudge against him. As for the acting president's order of release, it had long since become waste paper, and nobody spoke of it again.

In August 1949 Chiang Kai-shek came from Taiwan to Chungking. The hatred and disgust of the people of Sichuan for him had truly reached the limit, and he sensed it himself. To dress the thing over, the Juntong and the reactionary party organizations together turned out every secret-society and gang organization and scraped up some ten thousand souls to welcome him, while the reactionary press blared that a hundred thousand had lined the roads. After he reached Chungking his temper was sour in the extreme; but his appetite for killing, as the reactionary armies went down to rout on every front, grew only the more unbridled. More than once he said to Mao Renfeng: "Today's defeat comes of killing too few in the old days, and leaving people who oppose us alive. It has cost us dearly!" So when Mao Renfeng asked his pleasure concerning Yang Hucheng — whether he should be moved on to Taiwan — he answered without a breath of hesitation: "What is he kept for? He should have been killed long ago!" But he ended by instructing Mao Renfeng that Yang should be brought back to Chungking for it, and secretly, with no outsider to know. As Mao Renfeng told me afterwards, at the same time as he ordered Yang's murder, Chiang Kai-shek gave him directions for disposing of the two-hundred-odd revolutionaries held in the two concentration camps inside the SACO compound, Baigongguan and Zhazidong: "In the days when our power was what it was, these people would not surrender. Now that we are beaten in every quarter, are they going to come over to us? There is nothing for it but to kill them all together!" The great massacre that shocked the world — the successive mass killings of more than two hundred people in the SACO compound at Chungking on the eve of the liberation — was the proud masterpiece of this Chungking journey of Chiang Kai-shek's.

Mao Renfeng, his orders received, sent Zhou Yanghao to Guiyang again to fetch Yang. Before Zhou set out, Mao called together the southwest region chief Xu Yuanju, the Baigongguan warden Lu Jingqing, and Zhou himself to work through the method in detail, and it was settled that the killing should be done with knives, to avoid raising any alarm. Lu Jingqing went on to recommend for the work the Baigongguan head guard Yang Jinxing, the action-group chief of the Second Department of the Southwest Headquarters Xiong Xiang, and other executioners of name. The next day Yang and Xiong actually brought the sharpened daggers they had prepared for Mao Renfeng to see, and the place was fixed: the "Dai Memorial Hall" on Songlinpo inside the SACO compound, being the most secluded spot of all. This "Dai Memorial Hall" had begun as one more house Dai Li built during the war, on the pretext of sheltering Chiang Kai-shek from air raids — in practice one more pleasure-house fitted out for Dai's own use — on the mid-slope of Little Geleshan at the east of the SACO compound, closed round on every side by pine woods, deeply quiet, where in the ordinary way scarcely a soul could come. After Dai Li's death, the agents renamed it the Dai Memorial Hall and used it for his rites. When Zhou Yanghao reached Guiyang he told Yang the old story: Chiang Kai-shek had come to Chungking and wished to see him. Yang had been deceived too many times to believe it now. Zhou offered his congratulations — this time there was real hope. Yang knew what he knew: from the day he came home, Chiang Kai-shek had never once received him; now, with the whole country on the eve of liberation, was Chiang going to let him go? So he met it all with perfect composure. Before starting out, Zhou Yanghao reported to Mao Renfeng the hour they would reach Chungking; and Mao ordered that on the day before (this was about the end of August or the beginning of September 1949) a company of the Traffic Police Corps should ring Songlinpo tight about and let no one into the area.

When the car carrying Yang reached Songkan, on the border of Guizhou and Sichuan, at noon the next day, Zhou Yanghao saw that the light was still high — to cross on the Chungking ferry in daylight was to risk being seen — and persuaded Yang to sleep out the afternoon in the little inn at Songkan, while word went ahead to Chungking to make all ready. They left Songkan only at four in the afternoon, and it was dark when they reached Chungking. Mao Renfeng had had Yang Jinxing hold the ferry ready and wait to take them over the river, then ride with the cars to Songlinpo. Zhou Yanghao himself went home to Yangjiashan in the SACO compound, to wait for the news.

When Yang left the car, two agents, half supporting him, walked him up the three hundred and more stone steps to the Dai Memorial Hall. His son Yang Zhengzhong, carrying his mother's ashes before him in both hands, was gripped between two more agents and taken up with him. Song Qiyun and his wife, with the two children, Yang's and the Songs', were lodged in the flat-roofed rooms by the garage, the whole three-hundred-odd steps below. When father and son gained the top, the agents led them apart, to the main rooms on the right hand and the left; and the whole arrangement filled Yang with foreboding. What the agents themselves most feared at that moment was resistance from Yang's son, a young man in his twenties, and they had decided to deal with him first. The instant he stepped through the door, an agent posted behind it drove a blade into his chest. He screamed the one word "Father!" — and as he struggled, the agents brought him down with several thrusts more. Yang Hucheng heard his son's cry and knew in his heart that something was wrong. In that scene of the mountain rain about to break and the wind filling the tower, the old gentleman (as the agents had always called him), desperate for his son, turned to go and see; and as his head came round, the executioner's blade was already in his belly. He gave one great cry and fell where he stood. The killers dealt each of them several strokes more to make an end, judged the thing done, and made off down the hill.

When they walked into the room of Song Qiyun and Xu Linxia, the dripping blades still in their hands, husband and wife took one look at those murderous faces and knew there was no escaping death. Song's wife begged the killers to spare the two children. The killers, without one shred of humanity, said not a word and closed in. The two children, eight or nine years old, only just down from the car and still deep in their play, saw what was before them and clung fast to each other. When the Songs had been driven back against the wall with nowhere left to stand, the killers sprang the last step and struck them blow upon blow full in the chest. At that the children shrieked and burst out crying; the killers, even as they drove their knives into the Songs, roared at them that crying was forbidden. The children choked their sobs back down and held each other the tighter. Then the killers, their work on the grown ones done, drew their daggers from the bodies and turned on the children. As the first child took a thrust that ran from his back clean through to his breast, the other at once threw himself upon him and held him; and a second killer, with a snarling laugh, served him the same. Song's wife had not yet gone. No sound would come from her, but she fixed them with eyes wide with a fury past all limit, her teeth clenched hard, and watched her son Sensen and his small friend being butchered. The killers, to sharpen the agony of the dying, dealt her several strokes more at the last. When the killing was over they buried the six bodies in haste near by, and went back to their masters to claim the credit. The valuables in the small leather case Yang had carried with him they divided up as prize money. The face of General Yang Hucheng's corpse was doused by the agents with nitric acid before he was buried inside a raised flower bed; the other bodies were buried close about.

The killers who took part in the slaughter — Zhang Hu, Zhang Jingfu, Chen Baoqi and the rest, more than twenty men — were all posted afterwards to Yunnan, where I assigned them their further work. As they reported in, every one of them recounted the business to me over and over, always in tones of the highest satisfaction, vying with one another in boasting of their "results." When Mao Renfeng spoke to me of the affair later, he said: "The Old Man" — Chiang Kai-shek — "is very pleased with how clean and neat a piece of work this was."

In the winter of 1944, when the Japanese bandits had taken Dushan and were preparing to drive on Guiyang, I went with Dai Li to Guiyang to salvage supplies. One noon He Yingqin came to Dai Li's quarters at Qilin Cave in Guiyang, talked awhile, and stayed to lunch. While the three of us were still at table, Gong Guoyan, captain of the detachment guarding General Yang Hucheng, arrived in Guiyang to ask Dai for instructions, and Dai had him wait in the sitting room. The moment He Yingqin was gone, Dai told me to bring Gong Guoyan to his office. Gong asked Dai when Yang Hucheng might be moved. Dai gave a short laugh and said: "At a moment as tight as this, when I cannot even free the trucks to move supplies, who has attention to spare for them? If the Japanese push on — the moment you hear Guiyang has fallen and the enemy is moving on Chungking, you can finish off the whole Yang Hucheng family in the confusion. There will be no need to keep them on." After Gong Guoyan had gone, I asked Dai whether Zhang Xueliang, at Tongzi, ought to be moved out first. He answered without so much as a pause for thought: "Come to that, he is settled the same way. When we cannot look after our own selves, we are forced to simple methods with people like these!" By good fortune the Japanese army came no further; otherwise Generals Zhang and Yang would both have been killed in the Chiang army's retreat.
