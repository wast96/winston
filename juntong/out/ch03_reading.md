# Juntong Crimes in Chungking during the War of Resistance

Not long after the war of resistance began, Chiang Kai-shek led that government of his in flight to Chungking. This city, styled the wartime auxiliary capital, was not only the political centre of the Kuomintang government but also the seat of the Juntong's bureau headquarters. During the war years the Juntong operatives concentrated in Chungking numbered more than twenty thousand at their height, while the peripheral elements, and the men the field operatives used as "second-string runners" (that is, the assistants the field men employed), came to more than twenty thousand besides. All day long they were engaged in criminal activities against the Communists and against the people.

Chiang Kai-shek's purpose in setting up the Juntong was principally to use it against the Communist Party, to suppress the people and to clear away those who were not of his own faction. Once he had been forced to concede the Communist Party's legal standing, he set still greater store by what a secret-service organization like the Juntong could do for him. So it was that during the war the Juntong expanded without restraint, and Dai Li, the Juntong's chief, stood ever higher in his favour. The Juntong of those years not only carried out faithfully Chiang Kai-shek's consistent policy of putting anti-Communism before resistance to Japan; it was at the same time the faithful executor of his policy of "saving the nation by a curved path," of collusion with the Japanese and their puppets and of joint action with them against the Communists. Here I shall give only a general account of the Juntong's part in carrying out Chiang Kai-shek's policy of turning the gun inward. The various criminal activities bound up with its collaboration with the Japanese and the puppet regimes I intend to write up later, when I come to other materials.

When Dai Li formally took charge of the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Military Affairs Commission in 1938, it had at first only four departments and two offices inside, with a headquarters staff of no more than a hundred-odd and fewer than two thousand men in the field. After the flight to Chungking the inside establishment grew to ten departments, with more than ten offices, districts and sections ranking equal to a department, and several committees besides; the headquarters staff reached more than a thousand and the field strength rose to more than fifty thousand. In the Chungking area alone it ran, one after another, more than thirty training classes for operatives of every description. The training class for special communications personnel, for instance, which handled postal and telegraphic censorship, and the training class for intelligence staff officers, which dealt in military intelligence, each took four or five hundred men on average and each was run six or seven times over. Short courses averaging about two hundred men a time were run more than ten times. Other classes were run at least twice. These operative-students, numbering in the tens of thousands and trained under Dai Li's personal direction, all became core elements of the Juntong soon enough, and from here they were distributed to every region of the country.

Dai Li meant to arrange Chungking as tightly as a net spread over heaven and earth, but those disciples and disciples' disciples of his fell far short of realizing his ideal, and so the machinery kept multiplying, one bed built on top of another. Even so the work was full of holes, and there was a constant sense of attending to one thing at the cost of another; he was forever taking Chiang Kai-shek's abuse for it, and the people's resentment ran higher still.

In those days every person entering Chungking from outside, whether he came by air, by water or overland, first ran up against the disagreeable faces of the inspection men and had to be put to some trouble by them. Letters you posted from Chungking, and the letters and telegrams sent to you from elsewhere, all had to pass first under the eye of an operative. As for a telephone number thought worth listening to, you had only to be connected and someone would cut into the receiver to eavesdrop on your conversation. Staying in a hotel, batch after batch of inspection men would disturb you until you could not sleep in peace and would question you until you were sick of it. Walking into the Queen's Hotel at Huixianqiao in the central district, the largest Chinese and Western restaurant there, you would be sure to see one of its proprietors looking you over with shifty eyes: this was the big Juntong operative Xu Zhongwu. He kept not only the restaurant but a dance hall at the mouth of Datong Street as well, where the waiters and the dance hostesses all had to report to him and gather material on the customers for him. Among the proprietors of the well-known Sichuan restaurant Xinweiyu on Zourong Road and of the Weiyu on Minsheng Road was Shen Xifeng, deputy commander of the detective brigade. Next door to the Xinweiyu stood another famous Sichuan house, the Kaigegui, which although it had been got up with shares raised by Li Yueyang, a graduate of the first Whampoa class, and was a place the senior Kuomintang officers frequented, was by then thoroughly of a piece with the Juntong and had become one of the districts of operative activity. The proprietors of the other restaurants were for the most part hand in glove with the operatives and kept in touch with them. The Hongyunlou, opened on Linsen Road by Zheng Hongfu of the Qingyutang, and the Maili Western restaurant he ran near Douyou Street, were as convenient to the operatives as premises of their own. Even among the staff of the Victory Mansions, the largest establishment in Chungking at that time, the operatives had "second-stringers" coming and going regularly. A beef house outside Tongyuan Gate, knocked together out of two thatched rooms, became a place the operatives watched, because so many well-known figures of the cultural world used to go there.

If a person of any social standing arrived in Chungking and found himself surrounded by a crowd of newspapermen, there would always be a good number of false reporters mixed in among them, asking you this and that in the hope of getting something out of you. If you fell ill and wanted a doctor, someone was certain to tell you that Zhang Jianzhai, the famous Nanking physician at Shihuishi, stood first among the practitioners of Chinese medicine. Not only were there operatives about him coming and going all day long; when he took his opium there would be someone to keep him company and chat and pick up what he could. Chen Xunzhai, who stood a little below Zhang, had fewer patients crowding in on him, but he was equally an adviser and a volunteer informant of the Inspectorate. Zhang So-and-so, celebrated for practising Western as well as Chinese medicine, also accepted a retainer from the Inspectorate and became its medical adviser.

Of all the doctors in Chungking at that time the most conspicuous was Wei Guangcai, the Nanking eye specialist who lived on Zhonglieci Street. He not only served the operatives as a volunteer informant; he paid out of his own pocket to have one of them buy him a pistol, which he wore at his waist all day long. Whenever the operatives went out on a search or an arrest, he hoped to go along in person. He had only to be called and he would abandon the patients in his consulting room, say a word about an emergency call, and follow after them in high spirits. The operatives took him for an amateur and generally gave him nothing to do but guard a front or back door or watch over a prisoner, and he was very glad to take it. Coming back from a job, he would put his hand in his pocket again and stand the operatives a meal at a restaurant.

To collect photographs of passengers travelling by air, the Juntong opened the Feihong Photographic Studio on the road beside the Shanhuba airfield in the city, run by the Juntong photographer Wang Wenzhao with his assistant Hou Feipeng. Later the business was enlarged, and when identity cards were issued to the citizens of Chungking the studio advertised loudly with "ready the same day, low prices" and the like, often taking trade from the other studios at no profit to itself. Once a cheap photograph of that kind had been taken, the files of the secret service gained a little more material.

The auction houses, which flourished in Chungking during the war, the Juntong also seized the chance to control. Fang Yuan, deputy head of the Chungking economic intelligence group, which the Juntong directed itself, opened the Anli Auction House next door to Chuanyan Yili on Bao'an Road, as cover for his work and as an open occupation in which he could keep up with others in the trade. The other overt secret-service bodies, such as the Inspectorate's detective brigade, had a hand in the auction houses too. Ostensibly they were guarding against stolen goods being slipped in among the lots; in reality what they attended to was the names, standing and addresses of the people consigning goods for sale, so that when someone had to be investigated the connection could be used to get near him. So far as I know, the larger houses, the Junyi on Minsheng Road and the Dalu on Zourong Road among them, all had dealings with the operatives; otherwise the operatives would seize on any pretext to ruin the proprietors and leave them with no way to climb down.

The more prudent sort of person in those days did not dare call often on his friends without cause, because every office had counter-espionage men trained by the Juntong keeping watch on their own colleagues, and a careless moment could bring an undeserved calamity down not only on oneself but often on one's relatives and friends as well. These trained "peripheral elements" would be suspended from duty if they had nothing to show for their work, so acting against conscience was a common thing with them: once in six months or a year they had to find something to keep their rice bowls filled. And when you walked into a school to look for someone, batch after batch of professional student operatives would contrive to find out what you and your relatives and friends had talked about.

A suspect who had drawn the operatives' attention would find, wherever he went, that someone was looking at him differently from the rest. Some men noticed that whenever they went out there was always somebody following in the shadows, and when they wanted to shake off these phantoms they supposed they had only to seize a chance to step smartly aboard a bus just pulling away, leaving the operative behind, and they would be safe. What nobody reckoned on was that the men who spent all day on the buses checking or selling tickets were of the same fraternity. When you jumped nimbly down again, pleased with yourself, just as the bus was moving off, the inspector or the conductor would point you out to another group of operatives near the stop, and the "tailing" would go on. In short, in the Chungking of those days, if there was the least thing progressive in your words or your conduct, then wherever you went you would meet these plague gods coming to make trouble for you.

By rights, with precautions as close as these in the auxiliary capital during the war against Japan, very few traitors should have been able to slip in, and one should have heard regularly of cases in which they were caught. In fact the traitors great and small who came to Chungking from Shanghai, Nanking, Wuhan and the other occupied areas never stopped coming, and not one genuine traitor was ever taken in the net. Meanwhile the Juntong's prisons, perpetually overcrowded, held thousands upon thousands of Communists and patriots who genuinely stood by the resistance to Japan. This is the most concrete proof there is that the Juntong of those years faithfully carried out Chiang Kai-shek's policy of turning the gun inward, and grew all the more anti-Communist and anti-people in doing it.

I was in Chungking for more than six years, and in that time I served as commander of the detective brigade of the Chungking municipal police, as inspector-general and then deputy head of the Inspectorate of the Chungking Garrison Command, and as head of the general affairs department at Juntong bureau headquarters. In what follows I shall speak only briefly of the organization and the activities of some of the Juntong's units in Chungking, from which it can be seen what criminal activities the Juntong really carried on in the auxiliary capital during the war.

## The Inspectorate of the Chungking Garrison Command

The Inspectorate of the Garrison Command was the largest overt secret-service body the Juntong controlled in Chungking during the war against Japan. Its organization reached into all thirteen counties of the garrison district, and although its establishment came to only some five hundred men, the assistants each field man employed ran to a hundred or more for some and twenty or thirty even for the fewest. Its privileges exceeded those of the garrison commander-in-chief himself, and most of the Juntong's many criminal activities in Chungking were carried out through it.

The office was set up in 1939, having grown out of the Inspectorate of the Wuhan Garrison Headquarters after that body moved to Chungking. Nominally it was a unit of the Garrison Command; in reality it was entirely under Juntong control, all its personnel matters being handled by the Juntong's personnel department, and the garrison commander-in-chief could not so much as enquire into them. Liu Zhi and Wang Zanxu, who held the post of commander-in-chief in turn, both knew very well how things stood and left everything about the office alone, because Chiang Kai-shek had told them himself that it was to be handed over to Dai Li entire.

When Liu Zhi had been in Honan he had a secret-service staff of his own, and when the Inspectorate was formed he was anxious to place these hundred-odd men in it, but Dai Li refused him. Liu himself thought nothing of it; his wife, however, was thoroughly put out, because these were the men who had helped her make a good deal of money in Honan. Having no other course, Liu Zhi set up a separate detective squad inside the Garrison Command to accommodate them. The Juntong at once made representations to him and would not permit a second organization of the same kind. He had then to reduce the men and attach them to the staff department as an investigation section. But the Inspectorate would not let it go: it required Liu Zhi to abolish the investigation section, and announced publicly that it would arrest these men and forbid them to operate outside, until Liu Zhi was driven to find some other way of placing them. Liu's wife went to the commander-in-chief's office and made a great scene, pointing at Liu Zhi and telling him he was utterly useless, that he was commander-in-chief and could not decide even a small matter like this for himself. Liu Zhi explained again and again that the Juntong was an organization not to be provoked, and others explained the consequences to her repeatedly as well, before she understood that Chiang Kai-shek had a whole set of men as vicious as this about him, and only then did she moderate a little the manner she had brought from Honan.

About 1942 Guo Jiqiao became deputy commander-in-chief of the garrison. He was famous for being overbearing, and from the outset he meant to gather all authority into his own hands. Liu Zhi, much put out, deliberately let him run into a wall and said nothing to him about the Inspectorate. Sure enough, Guo Jiqiao set about enquiring into the office's work. Hearing on one occasion that the Inspectorate had arrested several men near Tongyuan Gate and had never reported it to the Garrison Command, he sent for Tao Yishan, then head of the office, to call him to account. Tao told him very politely that it had been done on the orders of the Juntong bureau. Guo still wanted to know the circumstances, so Tao invited him to go and ask the Juntong. Guo sprang up in a rage, and was on the point of losing his temper at Tao when Tao, without a word, picked up his hat and walked out. After that Guo telephoned the Inspectorate several more times on similar matters, and when his calls came to me I told him bluntly that in business assigned by the Juntong bureau the regulations allowed the commander-in-chief and his deputy to be bypassed, and that this had the Generalissimo's approval. He still talked a little officialese at me, but there was nothing he could do about it. Afterwards others advised him not to invite snubs, and he stopped enquiring into the office's affairs.

From the day it was founded the office did not sit with the Garrison Command but had premises of its own inside the Chungking vocational school at Shihuishi. This utterly vicious secret-service body spent its days seizing men and beating them, with prisoners marched in and out daily, and no one passing the place failed to look at it askance; some would rather go round by another road than walk past it at all.

Besides carrying out most of the Juntong bureau's field work in Chungking, the office was also where Dai Li tested the abilities of his cadres and kept and seasoned his core operatives. The deputy head he appointed each time was always the man intended to succeed as head. When Zhao Shirui was relieved, for instance, the deputy head Tao Yishan took over; when Tao left, the deputy head Liao Gongshao succeeded him. After Liao left, since I was being transferred to be head of the general affairs department of the Juntong bureau, the vacancy was again filled by He Longqing, the other deputy head under Tao. Partly this was for fear that a new head unfamiliar with the work would readily make a mess of it, so that he had to pass through the deputy's stage first; and partly it was Dai Li's consistent method of handling men, deliberately setting head and deputy at odds and keeping each suspicious of the other, which made them easier to control and kept them from colluding to deceive him. Besides this, when a core Juntong operative had got into some trouble outside and the case was one that might or might not be dealt with under the law, Dai Li, disposing of it according to his own liking, would often post the man to this office to be looked over for a time; and men who could not be placed anywhere else for the moment were sent here as probationers too. When I was inspector-general there were three deputy inspectors-general who had been heads of Inspectorates elsewhere and had been recalled to be "looked over" after some small trouble. Zuo Ming, head of the Inspectorate of the Hanchung Garrison Headquarters, for instance, had heard Dai Li mention that Hu Zongnan was preparing to recommend Zhu Shaozhou, the Hanchung garrison commander, to Chiang Kai-shek as governor of Shensi; wanting to ingratiate himself with Zhu, Zuo Ming let the news out and was denounced for it. Dai Li had meant to punish him for divulging secrets, but Zhu Shaozhou interceded and he was transferred to Chungking to be watched instead. Huang Jiachi, head of the Inspectorate of the Tungting Lake Garrison Headquarters, was on very good terms with the commander Huo Kuizhang; Huo was Chen Cheng's man, Dai had always disliked Chen, and fearing that Huang would follow Huo onto Chen's line he transferred him to Chungking to be watched too. Guo Zongyao, head of the Inspectorate of the Yuanling Garrison Headquarters, was suspected of corruption on insufficient evidence, and he too was transferred to Chungking. Dai Li posted all three to the Inspectorate as deputy inspectors-general and told me to watch their words and conduct closely. I let them know privately what Dai was about, and they all set to work in dead earnest. Before long Huang Jiachi was promoted to head the anti-smuggling department of Kweichow province, Zuo Ming went out to be a chief of police, and Guo Zongyao was moved to the Juntong as a secretary. The result was that a great many of those posted to the Inspectorate hoped that after a spell there Dai Li would employ them more highly still, and there was not one who did not work with extraordinary zeal. Everyone was exceptionally keen, and so the work of the Inspectorate was always at that pitch of tension, with all of them looking for something to do in order to please Dai Li. Whenever a matter of any weight came up, the head of the office, the deputy head, the inspector-general and the deputy inspectors-general all fell over one another to direct the arrangements in person, since the least showing of a result would reach the ears of Dai Li and the other big Juntong operatives at once. I understood this knack very well myself at the time. As inspector-general I travelled round all thirteen counties to inspect the work of every inspection post and picket, partly to learn the situation so as to have it in hand, and partly with the idea of taking the road less trodden, where anything I found I could deal with myself, rather than compete with others in the city of Chungking. Once I was deputy head of the office I stayed in it all day and took no part in pointless socializing, because trouble was likeliest to break out after hours: not only could my subordinates reach me at any time, but whenever Dai Li telephoned after hours to ask about something it was generally I who took the call. Whatever Dai Li asked about, I could report to him in detail on the telephone straight away, without looking up the files or ringing back later. Dai Li was an impatient man and was very pleased with this, and it was chiefly by these means that I was transferred to his side as head of the general affairs department while still in my twenties.

The Inspectorate had four sections.

The First Section was in charge of general affairs and had three sub-sections under it for administration, personnel and records. Its section heads were Ji Xianxun and Wang Qiupan in turn, and its deputy head was Wang Shusheng, who was not a Juntong man.

The Second Section was the operational one, responsible for intelligence, action, counter-espionage, air inspection and the rest, and it was the key unit. Wang Xisheng and Song Tingjun served in turn as its head; Wang Yixin, Zhang Shangyu and Jie Mengyu served as deputy heads.

The Third Section was the telecommunications monitoring section, headed in turn by Xiao Maoru and Zha Suizhi. This section was directed straight by the Juntong's telecommunications department, and its forty-odd staff, men such as Huang Qin, Mao Hongming, Lan Tianyou, Yang Xuanwu and Zhao Zhongling, were all selected and posted by the telecommunications department's personnel assessment sub-section, unlike the personnel of the other units, who were assigned by the Juntong's personnel department. The section's work fell into an inside and a field branch. One task of the inside branch was to control the sale and distribution of wireless equipment throughout the garrison district: any unit wishing to buy equipment of this kind had first to apply, and only after examination and approval could it take a purchase permit to a shop and buy. Shops, wherever they bought such goods from, had to go through this unit first, or have the crime of "supplying the enemy" fastened on them and go to prison. The other task of the inside branch was detection. On the third floor of the Inspectorate stood several small detector sets, used solely to listen in on every wireless transmission in Chungking. Every wireless station established in Chungking at that time had to register, item by item, its type, call sign and wavelength and the stations it communicated with regularly. Nor was that all: this section listened all day to check whether these stations' traffic matched what had been registered, and at the least suspicious point it judged there to be something wrong, and besides an open check by the field men there would be covert monitoring on the air. The main weight of their work, however, was intercepting the telegrams passing to and from the Communist Party office, the *Xinhua Daily* and similar bodies, which were sent to the Juntong telecommunications department's technical research office to be deciphered. Although they worked day and night, and although it was always said openly that the object was to prevent the Japanese and their puppets setting up stations in Chungking, in six or seven years they never uncovered or detected a single Japanese or puppet station. The stations communicating with the Japanese and puppets at Nanking, Shanghai and Wuhan were not under these men's supervision at all: that work was done by the Juntong bureau's own central wireless station, so where was anything to be uncovered? The section's field work was the inspection of wireless transmitters and receivers. Installing a set to listen to broadcasts was not the simple matter it is today. Apart from certain dignitaries and privileged persons, who might listen freely, ordinary people were not permitted a set with short wave. A citizen's receiver had first to be registered here, and after a permit had been obtained the set still had to be carried in for inspection, any short-wave portion being cut out or sealed. If a man reconnected it himself after the cutting and sealing, then whether or not he had listened to Yenan or to Shanghai or Nanking, the charge of "secretly listening to enemy propaganda" was fastened on him and he went to prison. Although the Juntong ran a transmitter at that time expressly to jam the Yenan broadcasts, there were still people who listened in secret. The section's operatives would often burst in without warning and take a household's wireless apart to inspect it, and if they found anything at all suspicious about the cut and sealed portion the owner was carried off on the spot. Sometimes they practised deliberate extortion, purposely leaving something undone when the set was brought in for cutting and sealing, and calling at the house to make trouble some time later. Some could settle it by paying a little; others paid and went to prison unjustly all the same.

The Fourth Section was the judicial section, headed in turn by Xu Zhongqi and Hu Zao. This section came directly under the Juntong bureau's judicial department, its personnel being posted by arrangement between that department and the personnel department, and in the handling of cases it took its orders from the judicial department, in which the head of the Inspectorate had no say. This was particularly so while Xu Zhongqi was section head: being a man from Dai Li's own district, he paid no attention to the head of the office at all, and whenever anything came up he would go straight to the Juntong bureau for instructions and mention it to the head of the office afterwards if he happened to feel like it. Otherwise a man held in the Inspectorate might be taken away without the head of the office knowing. Since the Inspectorate's detention house came under this section, the section head could produce prisoners and confine them, and so long as he had the judicial department's instructions he need not ask whether the head of the office agreed. A head of the Inspectorate faced with this generally had nothing for it but a wry smile.

The Inspectorate had three groups besides. The chief of these was the party and government reconnaissance group, headed in turn by Cao Wandao, Yi Xiaofu, Jing Zhijian and Lei Hechun, with more than thirty members. It was quartered near the Taiyanggou market, having its offices outside because it was inconvenient for these operatives, who went about disguised among the democratic parties and every class of society, to be seen coming and going at the Inspectorate. Its task was party and government intelligence proper, the surveillance of progressive figures, and any intelligence the Juntong wanted re-checked. The group was also in direct touch with the Juntong's party and government intelligence department, and amounted to that department's field unit. At that time there were several hundred organizations and individuals whom Chiang Kai-shek had ordered watched, and once the Juntong had its orders it parcelled them out to its units in Chungking. To the Chinese Communist Party office, the Eighth Route Army office and the *Xinhua Daily* men were assigned specially to watch. Song Qingling, Shi Liang, Shen Junru and others were made over to the Inspectorate to watch. The group had only some thirty men in all, and to keep several organizations and people numbering in the hundreds under constant watch it could not possibly be spread far enough. The only way was to depend on informers and on buying inside lines, and to use each man's relatives and friends to help carry it on.

For watching the Communist Party office at Zengjiayan they used the proprietor of a small teahouse opened nearby, a man who was at that time either the head of the ward or the head of the tithing, I no longer remember clearly which. In the ordinary way they sent someone daily to see this man and find out how matters had stood that day; when new arrivals came from Yenan, or on an anniversary of some kind, or while the People's Political Council was sitting, several more men had to be added day by day. Sometimes they would keep watch nearby disguised as pedlars and the like, paying particular attention to those going in and out. What most vexed the operatives was that they were never clear who the people going in actually were. The rule at that time was that a guest whom Vice-Chairman Zhou Enlai saw out in person need not be tailed; a guest seen out by Vice-Chairman Zhou's secretaries or others might or might not be tailed; but a man who pushed the door open and let himself in, and pulled it shut behind him on the way out, was to be tailed, since this showed him to be one of the household.

Watching Hongyan Village at Hualongqiao was harder still, because so many people came and went there, and it was impossible to make out who mattered and who did not, most of them being unknown even by name. In this district the Inspectorate used a two-tier arrangement: besides the party and government group, the Xiaolongkan inspection post kept an inspection picket at Hualongqiao, with several men living nearby whose sole business this was. Duan Chutian, the picket commander, once found a workman inside the *Xinhua Daily* whom he took at the time for an excellent inside line; but the man was taken off the work before long, and no connection inside was ever found again.

For watching Guo Moruo, Tian Han, Cao Yu and others they often used a small beef house outside Tongyuan Gate. I went to look at that beef house once myself. Shi Liang was living at Youzhuang in Zaozilanya at the time, and several big Juntong operatives lived in the same compound; their male and female servants did the work, taking the chance their acquaintance with the Shi family's servants gave them to pick up what they could.

In the way of intelligence work this party and government reconnaissance group could accomplish nothing much, since its men had no standing in society and the friends they made were all of a shabby sort. Among the democratic parties of the day they could reach no further than the middling and lower members of the Youth Party and the Democratic Socialist Party, and little was expected of them, because for work at the upper levels the Juntong did not rely on them at all. Apart from a body of men Dai Li handled directly himself, the Juntong also employed people of the design-committee sort for the work, and the Chungking special district had several dozen couriers of its own working on the democratic parties, of which I shall write separately below. What the Juntong wanted of the party and government group was that it should do the watching and the re-checking well, since much of what came in had reached Dai Li, or the Juntong, or the Chungking special district, from democratic figures of the upper level or from other quarters, and those informants could not establish the particulars clearly. On vague, half-true questions the Juntong could not well press these informants directly, for fear of giving offence, and had to set the Inspectorate's party and government group to make a re-check and establish how far the intelligence was true. So there were at that time several such items to be verified and answered almost every day, sometimes several dozen. Some carried a time limit, and these were mostly matters intended for report to Chiang Kai-shek, or things sent down by the Sixth Group of the Attendants' Office. Whenever an urgent item of this kind came in with a day fixed for the answer, the more important ones had a supervisor from the supervision office attached to see it through.

On one occasion, before the People's Political Council met, several Kuomintang councillors were regularly meeting and mixing with Communist councillors, and had reported it to Chiang Kai-shek neither before nor after. Chiang at once ordered the Juntong to find out. The Juntong gave the Inspectorate two days to report back, and kept pressing for an answer before the two days were up, so that the Inspectorate sent out even its deputy inspectors-general, and only after three or four days running did it get the matter clear. What had happened was that whoever wrote the report had confused the names of several of the men Chiang Kai-shek had designated as being in contact with the Communist representatives, putting Zhang's hat on Li's head, so that even Chiang Kai-shek had been thrown into a needless panic.

The party and government group's work had its defined limits, but any operative who drilled into whatever crack he found in the matter of opposing the Communists and the Soviet Union was sure of encouragement and commendation from above. The Inspectorate had an external affairs reconnaissance group which dealt exclusively in intelligence on foreigners; yet when Xiao Zhijian of the party and government group was deputy group head, having a fellow townsman employed at the office of the Soviet trade delegation, he refused to hand the connection over to the external affairs group, and the Juntong agreed that he should keep it, adding a little to his special allowance each month so that he could get from this townsman what intelligence he could about the Soviet personnel in Chungking.

The Inspectorate's external affairs reconnaissance group, although nominally one of its units and housed in the rear building within the compound, was in fact directed straight by the Juntong bureau, and the Inspectorate could not enquire into its personnel or its funds, still less into its work. Kong Jie and Wu Runqi served in turn as its head, and Dai Li further appointed Liu Hui, head of the external affairs section of the Chungking municipal police, to serve concurrently as deputy head, so as to bring the external affairs work of Chungking under one hand. The group also went by cover names, using Bi Gongchou and then Bi Kongyin at different times, and calling itself the Bi Residence to the outside world. It had a back door, so that one could come and go without passing the Inspectorate's front gate.

The external affairs reconnaissance group's work centred on watching the activities in Chungking of the Soviet embassy, TASS and the Soviet trade delegation, and on the Chinese who had dealings with them. Ordinarily this was done in secret, though at times it operated openly.

Some of the senior couriers under direct Juntong control who worked on external affairs, men such as Wei Jingmeng of the International Publicity Department, were also put in touch through this group so that they might work in concert. Juntong operatives then employed in the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, such as Zeng Guangxun, head of the passport section, and many others who had working relations with the Juntong, were all in close touch with it. When Xu Nianzeng of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs went to Cairo as minister, he specially asked for the transfer of Gu Yihua, a member of this group, to be his secretary, so as to keep up close communication with the Juntong.

The Inspectorate's third group was the social reconnaissance group, whose head, Qi Yulin, did not change for several years. Of all the groups this was the one the head of the Inspectorate could most fully control and direct. It had no direct connection with the Juntong bureau, being responsible specifically for public order and chiefly for cases of banditry and theft; in reality, though, its business was to do the searching and the seizing for the Inspectorate. The arrests of political and criminal prisoners were all carried out by this group, and when political prisoners were tortured under interrogation its members were called in to assist. Its work differed in kind from that of the other two groups: someone had to be on duty at the office in rotation all day, ready to turn out at any moment.

This social reconnaissance group had fifty or sixty men, most of them drifters risen out of the secret societies and the Paoko, with a very small number of graduates of the Juntong's special training classes, and a proportion transferred from the Wuhan Inspectorate. The men who came from Wuhan knew the thieves and pickpockets who had fled from Wuhan to Chungking quite well. The detective squad of the Chungking police, though it too was in Juntong hands at that time, was forever falling out with the Inspectorate over the competition for business. When the gang of robbers and pickpockets from Wuhan committed a crime in Chungking, in particular, the police detective squad could not clear the case, and even when it found a lead the Inspectorate would quietly shield the men. Conversely, when the Inspectorate wanted to arrest one of the local thieves the police squad had in hand, the squad's men would often obstruct it, and the two sides kept taking the quarrel up to the Juntong bureau.

Of all of them the Inspectorate did the people the most harm and the greatest. I heard more than one person say: "Let a man serve a single year as an inspector, and to take him out and shoot him would be no injustice at all." When I was inspector-general I regularly dealt with cases of this group's inspectors running wild outside. I reported the circumstances to Dai Li more than once, but Dai Li held that these men knew the ground and had done a good deal for the Juntong in hunting down and seizing prisoners in Chungking. So long as no great trouble blew up, Dai Li said, it was enough to deal with the individual when an individual got into a scrape.

The lengths to which these men went in doing evil may fairly be called shocking. I shall give only one of them as an example, a lieutenant inspector named Che Zhongguang, and the general run of it can be seen from him. While searching hotel rooms this man came upon a young woman who had come from the Northeast to Chungking to join a relative, had failed to find them, and had run up a large bill for board and lodging. On the pretext of helping her settle her difficulties he raped her, and afterwards forced her into prostitution and split her nightly earnings with her. She later met a traveller from the Northeast and poured out her story to him, and the traveller laid a complaint with the Inspectorate. I went myself to look into it and the facts were as stated; but the head of the Inspectorate would not agree to punish Che Zhongguang and even spoke up for him, because Che was very good at finding women and pimped regularly for many of the big operatives. This same Che Zhongguang afterwards took up with the proprietress of a small biscuit factory in Ranjiaxiang, and went so far as to poison her husband with a drug and seize the factory for himself.

Besides the Inspectorate, the bodies inspecting hotels and inns in Chungking at that time included the police, the detective squad and the military police. They passed through the hotels and inns in turn like shuttles, one arriving as another left, until travellers could get no peace even in the middle of the night. Only in the closing period of the war was a unified inspection group formed, with officers deputed from each body acting jointly, and the abuses grew somewhat fewer.

The reason the army, the police, the military police and the rest of them competed for the business of searching travellers was that it gave them their chance at extortion. Some would bring opium or pistol cartridges in secretly and, under cover of the search, slip them quietly into a traveller's luggage to plant the evidence and then extort him; and if he would not oblige they took him away. In seizing a man these secret-service bodies used no procedure of any kind: whoever they wanted, they could take.

Hotels and inns ran some degree of risk when this sort of thing happened on their premises, and so they had their own set of precautions. In those days the military police wore uniform on a search, and whenever they appeared at a hotel door the waiter would call out loudly and deliberately, "Is Mr. Wang Sixin at home?" or "There is a visitor for Mr. Wang!" These shouts were the signal, so that guests who happened to be smoking opium, or with a prostitute, or gambling, could make their preparations. The traditional form of the character *xian*, for the military police, is written with a roof radical over the three elements *wang*, *si* and *xin*, and so they took it apart and called it out as a name. When it was plain-clothes operatives who came, there was nothing for it but to find some way of warning the other guests once the search had already begun. In some hotels the staff were in league with the operatives, which was aiding the tyrant in his cruelty, and the two sides divided the spoils.

These operatives who searched the hotels actually had the power at that time to shoot a traveller dead, and on one occasion the business ended in the joke of their shooting one of "their own." The man was a Juntong operative named Xie Zhipan who had fled from Shanghai to Chungking, a younger brother of the big Juntong operative Xie Ligong. He was a relative of Tang Shaoyi, and the Juntong had made use of his connection with Tang to have Tang assassinated; escaping from Shanghai afterwards his nerves had been strung up too tightly, and in Chungking he was still jumping at shadows, sitting in his hotel all day and not daring to go out. The operatives searching the hotel grew suspicious and climbed up at the window to look in; he fired at once, the operatives returned fire immediately, and only after a wild volley had shot him dead did they discover that they had shot one of "their own" by mistake.

The operatives of the social reconnaissance group were not supposed to concern themselves with political matters, but running wild outside they would meddle in anything. On one occasion the Cathay Cinema was staging a play directed by Ying Yunwei, and having been given a few tickets too few they went to make trouble. Xia Yunhu, the Cathay's manager, produced the licence issued by the government for them to examine, and still it would not do. They pretended to have received a secret report that the actors' lines differed from the version passed by the censors and were meant to satirize the government, and on that ground they forbade the performance. The house was full by then and still they would not allow the curtain to be raised, until Ying Yunwei was at his wits' end; he pleaded with them himself and even that was no use. I happened to be at the theatre that evening, and seeing that twenty minutes and more had passed with no sign of a start I went out to ask what the matter was, only to find Ying Yunwei, streaming with sweat, talking to Zhang, Yan and Wang, members of this group. One question told me they were making difficulties on purpose, since matters of this sort were no business of theirs at all. These few men could hardly read a character between them, let alone remember what the lines had been. So I said that I had come specially to listen and see whether there was anything wrong with it, and told them to go back. With that the curtain was at last raised.

The Inspectorate also had an operatives' squad of more than thirty men under Ji Lü. These were originally employed only on guard duty and in watching prisoners, and were all on a private soldier's pay. But every one of them had a suit of plain clothes, and once off duty they went about the city doing as they pleased, sometimes following behind the inspectors to serve as their strong-arm men, and did evil at no lower a rate than the inspectors themselves.

The Inspectorate's field units comprised more than ten inspection posts and more than thirty inspection pickets. The powers of these posts were exceptionally wide: they could search travellers at will, search dwellings, detain vehicles and boats and seal up warehouses. Although the Military Affairs Commission had a Unified Land and Water Transport Inspection Department at the time, with Dai Li holding the headship concurrently, and although other bodies were forbidden to set up inspection machinery of their own, the military police being brought under it as well, the Inspectorate was nevertheless permitted to keep every one of its inspection establishments.

The water inspection post then quartered above the sugar guild at Chaotianmen searched boats and passengers coming and going, and the Unified Land and Water Transport Inspection Department kept a water inspection post as well, the two units performing the same duty. One would judge a boat fit to sail and the other say not yet; one would order her held and the other agree to let her go. That the two posts wrangled and fell out mattered less than that the men responsible aboard the steamers were left with no idea whom to obey, being afraid to offend either side. Worse used still were the passengers, packed aboard, stifling in summer and perishing with cold in winter, waiting for one lot to finish its formalities before the next lot began. Dai Li knew all this perfectly well and did nothing to stop it; he tacitly approved. He reckoned that with each keeping a check on the other there would be less trouble, and that since it was the passengers who suffered in any case, it was no concern of his.

Most striking of all, in places where the Unified Inspection Department kept no post or picket, the Inspectorate would go and set one up on its own, and Dai Li did not suppress it. Had a unit outside the Juntong behaved so he would never have allowed it. The Lianglukou inspection post set a picket on the bank at Huangshaxi expressly to search the wooden boats plying the Szechwan reach of the river, every one of which had to come alongside to be searched as it passed. When a boat running downstream did not know there was an inspection post there and failed to heave to, they would fire to bring her up; and if the shots were not heard they would fire directly at the boat, or send a small craft after her and hold boat and men together for ten days or half a month. On the pretext of searching boats and passengers entering and leaving the city they practised all manner of extortion on the cargo boats, repeatedly ordering a whole boatload of goods unloaded and examined piece by piece; and yet, a little money paid over, and the business was at an end. The cargo boats that went up and down knew the whole routine. Whatever they were carrying they had to give the men something, or be held up. On one occasion, because a boat had paid no "tribute", the operatives at the picket pushed a merchant travelling with the cargo into the water and drowned him; and although the others aboard saw the outrage with their own eyes, they all gave written testimony that the merchant had "lost his footing and fallen in." When I went to inspect this picket even I was taken aback, for there were no more than five or six men there and half a room was piled with oranges and pomelos. They told me at the time that these had been left in their keeping, but on enquiry it turned out that every boat gave them a few catties, and a day or two was enough to accumulate that much. Besides distributing part of it to the Inspectorate's staff from sub-section head downwards, they sent half to the inspection post. Everyone regarded it as a perquisite that went with the job, and in time it was taken as a matter of course.

The south bank inspection post at that time, besides keeping a picket at Haitangxi to search passengers boarding and leaving the motor buses, kept another at Yipinchang which shared the searching of vehicles and passengers with the Unified Inspection Department's own Yipinchang post. They set up a further picket at Huangjueya to watch certain progressive figures from the Northeast who lived there. Qingmuguan was then the first gate on the road from Chungking to the northwest, and since Communist Party and Eighth Route Army personnel passed through it regularly, the Inspectorate placed a post even in that hamlet of a few dozen households, sharing the searching with the Unified Inspection Department's Qingmuguan post. This post also set a picket at Beibei, charged specifically with watching the progressive teachers and students in the colleges there, and when the need arose it would carry out searches of travellers at the Beibei bus station and steamer wharf as well.

While I was at the Inspectorate I went twice to inspect the Jiangjin post, chiefly because Chen Duxiu was living there. Let me say something here in passing about Chen Duxiu's situation. Before Chen was arrested Chiang Kai-shek had put up a reward of a hundred thousand dollars for his capture; only after the arrest was it fully understood that he had become an oppositionist within the Communist Party, and there was some regret and a feeling that he should not have been taken. The man who sold Chen Duxiu, one Xie, afterwards served as a Juntong operative, and people would often mock him by saying that in selling Chen Duxiu he had done the Kuomintang no service but had done the Communist Party a good turn.

After his arrest Chen Duxiu was held a long while in the model prison at Soochow. The Kuomintang tried to make use of him, and once the war against Japan began, fearing that Soochow was unsafe for him, they sent him far away to the deep rear. Before he left, many senior Kuomintang officials in Nanking saw him and offered him some consolation. He lived at Jiangjin afterwards and was comparatively free in his movements. What the Jiangjin post chiefly did was watch the visitors who went to see him; towards Chen himself it was fairly civil. Before I went to see him Dai Li instructed me that I might find out how he was living but was on no account to provoke him, since keeping him would still be of great use.

When I went to Jiangjin to see Chen Duxiu the head of the inspection post, Li Jiechuan, always went with me. Once he knew who I was, a conversation would run to an hour or two. He held that the Communist Party ought to conduct its activities under Kuomintang leadership; that until conditions were ripe the Chinese Communist Party could not act independently and should attach itself to the Kuomintang. He was especially unstinting in praise of Chiang Kai-shek, holding that without such a man to lead the whole country's resistance China could only perish at Japan's hands. All this talk of Chen's struck me as strange at the time. There was a middle school at Jiangjin that had moved to the rear from Anhwei, and some of its teaching staff used to go and listen to him talk. He did indeed have a considerable numbing effect on these people, and no wonder he could earn the commendation of the reactionaries and the operatives.

The second time I talked with Chen Duxiu he laid into the Communist Party's leaders with still greater vigour, as though he alone were fit to lead the Chinese Communist Party and not one of the rest was right about anything. What he said was of great interest to a good many people as stubborn as I was myself. Every time I passed on to Dai Li what he had said to me, Dai too praised him as the most clear-sighted and outstanding talent in the Communist Party. For these reasons a good many Kuomintang officials went to call on him regularly during his years at Jiangjin, and everyone who talked with him came away with a good impression.

During the war of resistance, when the Kuomintang and the Communists were co-operating, the Inspectorate acted upon Chiang Kai-shek's intentions and carried out the anti-Communist policy without wavering. Although it did not dare at that time to arrest Communists who were in Chungking openly, for years it did its utmost to hunt down and seize those engaged in underground work. Whenever the Inspectorate had a Communist case in hand it grew particularly tense and threw everything into it. On one occasion Cheng Chuanluo, head of the Nanchuan inspection post, reported secretly that a Communist had got work at an air force machine plant in Nanchuan and, though found out and dismissed, was still active there. Liu Guoxian, a deputy inspector-general, at once took six or seven operatives, went by night to bring him back, and after a brief questioning sent him on to the Juntong bureau to be dealt with. Xu So-and-so, head of the Fuling inspection post (who later changed his name, also to Xu), broke two Communist cases. Among those arrested was a woman teacher surnamed Fu, the younger sister of a secretary to John Leighton Stuart, whom Xu raped. Dai Li had long before issued an express order forbidding operatives to marry during the war, and not a few Juntong men were sentenced to imprisonment for breaking it, yet Xu alone was permitted. The reason was that he had induced a woman Communist to leave the Party publicly, which not only offset his offence but merited a reward. Within the city of Chungking there were five or six arrests of Communists, at the Chuqimen wharf, at Cuntan in Jiangbei and elsewhere, and in every case they were sent to the Juntong bureau at once. Democratic figures were arrested in numbers too. One democratic figure secretly arrested below the Wenfeng Pagoda on the south bank, whose name I have now forgotten, was beaten black and blue all over by the operatives at the Inspectorate before being sent on to the Juntong bureau.

Besides opposing the Communists and the people, the Inspectorate's other important duty was protecting the Four Great Families and the high dignitaries of the Kuomintang. When Tao Yishan was head of the office he knew that Chiang Kai-shek frequently went across to the south bank from Chungking by motor launch, and besides regularly posting inspectors to give covert protection at the ferry landing Chiang used, he often went to perform this duty in person. Once, when Chiang Kai-shek crossed the river in haste during a night raid by enemy aircraft, Tao ran down to the landing to direct the guard arrangements himself. H. H. Kung had a villa on the hilltop at Huxiaokou in Nanwenquan where banquets and dances were regularly held, and the south bank inspection post placed a picket there expressly to act as guard.

In those days the Kung family's aides, plain-clothes guards, chauffeurs and the rest all wore a badge bearing a single character, and the sedan-chair bearers had the same character on their jackets in white on blue, the size of a bowl, so that at a glance one knew them for retainers of the household of Kong Yongzhi, that is H. H. Kung. This pack of servants, ravening as wolves and tigers, traded on their master's power to run outside the law, beating and cursing people at every turn. Some sightseers, not knowing that a kinsman of the imperial house of this kind lived in that beautiful and celebrated spot, were fond of taking photographs there. When the servants noticed, they would immediately order every negative exposed, and even set about the offenders with blows and abuse. On one occasion Song Xilian, deputy director of training of the Central Training Corps, was photographed there with several senior officers, in full uniform and wearing a lieutenant-general's collar badges, and even he was stopped by them.

Some sightseers were regularly denounced by these servants as traitors said to be photographing bombing targets, and beyond the beating and cursing were locked up as well. This inspection picket was the hound of these great houses, bullying the common people as it pleased; anyone the Kung family sent over was taken into custody without a word, whether he had done anything irregular or not. H. H. Kung's second daughter gave several dances for the American forces, and as it happened to be a time of drought, with the water low at Huaxi and the small hydro-electric station at Nanwenquan not generating power enough, she ordered the supply cut off to every shop and household in the town and the whole of it diverted to her house. The families who had electric light were forced to sit without it, while up on the hill the Kung house blazed with light. People who knew the Kungs were giving a dance cursed Miss Kung the Second behind her back, and when the servants overheard it there was trouble. Some, overheard by the servants, were seized without a word and sent to the inspection picket. I happened to be on leave at Nanwenquan that day, and the picket commander Zhang Zhongheng came for instructions: the Kung household had sent over several men and wanted him to take them to Chungking and lock them up, and what was he to do? It occurred to me that even our head of office had to do as the Kungs said, and I could not make trouble for him; so I said there was no vehicle to escort them that night and the picket had no room to hold them, and asked whether they might not be punished on the spot as a warning, to stop them talking wildly again. Zhang Zhongheng went and asked H. H. Kung's chief bodyguard, obtained his agreement, and gave each man a beating on the palms and released him. Angry as these men were at being beaten, they knew it was a light disposal, since once they had been taken into the city there was no telling what they might have suffered.

The Inspectorate's operatives lorded it over the people and gave themselves airs, yet before the rich and powerful they had to bow and scrape. I saw with my own eyes the disgraceful scene Tao Yishan made before Kong Lingjun, that is Miss Kung the Second, and I have not forgotten it to this day. One day Tao was driving himself, taking me and several inspectors by night to Baishiyi airfield to deal with an urgent case. As our car passed near Shandong, a saloon came at us head-on from the opposite direction without dipping its headlamps. Tao signalled again and again by flashing his lights, and the other car took no notice. Tao was a habitual bully who would never give way to anyone on the road, and naturally he would not stand for this; he at once put his own headlamps full on and drove straight at them. When the two cars were nose to nose he sprang out to give the other party a dressing-down. Sitting in the front I saw it all clearly: the driver of the other car was a young person in men's clothes with a cap worn askew, and only when she spoke did we know it was a woman. Tilting her head slightly back, she said to Tao in a tone of the utmost arrogance, "I thought it must be somebody of consequence to behave like this, and it turns out to be nothing but a petty major-general!" Tao flushed scarlet with rage and was on the point of exploding when the car of Tang Yi, the Chungking chief of police, happened to come up and stop alongside. Tang sprang down, saluted her with the greatest deference and introduced her to Tao at once: "This is Miss Kung the Second!" She gave a snort and asked Tang, "Who is this fellow?" When Tang had explained, she said to him in what was almost a tone of command, "I have business today and no time to deal with him. Bring him to see me tomorrow!" Tao came back to the car and did mutter a defiant word or two, but when Tang urged him to clear the road quickly he changed places with me and told me to drive. He sat beside me and said not a word the whole way. None of us in fact knew this notorious person by sight at the time, since she had not long come to Chungking from Hong Kong. Next day Tang did indeed come to invite Tao to go with him and apologize to her. Afterwards a good many people called Tao a spineless creature, but he came back as though nothing had happened.

In the autumn of 1941, when enemy aircraft were bombing Chungking regularly, I had just risen at daybreak one morning when Zheng Jiemin, the Juntong's number two, came through the rain to the Inspectorate, drew a letter from inside his coat and handed it to me, saying: "Somebody has actually written a letter abusing Madame" - meaning Song Meiling. "Madame read it and lost her temper completely. You have three days to break the case, and whoever wrote this letter is to be arrested and severely dealt with!" Naturally I undertook to carry out the order.

As soon as Zheng Jiemin had gone I called men in to study it. It proved to be an anonymous letter, the substance of which was this: the writer was a small tradesman who had subscribed several times to the aviation fund for national salvation, in the hope that the government would buy aircraft to resist Japan. He had heard that the aircraft bought were so decrepit as to be unusable and that the money had gone into the pocket of the principal person responsible, Song Meiling, secretary-general of the Aviation Commission, with the result that enemy aircraft came daily and there was no way of resisting them. The small shop he had run for years had been bombed to nothing and his children killed in the bombing, and he wrote in bitter hatred to vent his grievance.

How the letter came into Song Meiling's hands I do not know, but she gave orders that the writer be arrested and severely dealt with. After careful study we thought the proprietor of a bombed-out shop at Huangjiayakou highly suspect, only it was not clear where the family had moved after the bombing; half a day's searching brought in four men connected with it. Repeated interrogation under torture and comparison of handwriting proved beyond doubt that they had written it. Their reason for writing was simple and exactly as the letter said: indignation.

Before the three days were up Zheng Jiemin came twice in person to ask. He reported the outcome to Song Meiling with great satisfaction, and she directed that they be dealt with severely. The judge advocate's department of the Garrison Command sentenced one of the writers to fifteen years' imprisonment, and the other three, on a charge of conspiracy, to somewhat lighter terms. Because the Inspectorate had stated that the case was "assigned personally by Madame Chiang," judgment came very quickly. After the case closed Zheng Jiemin took me one day to see Song Meiling and report how it had been handled and the sentences passed. She was pleased to hear it and at once produced a pair of gold Parker pens for me as a reward for handling the case. Prison terms for four men, more than ten years of unjust imprisonment, exchanged for a pair of fountain pens and a few words of commendation. That is enough to show how loyally the operatives served as hounds for the great houses.

From the day it was founded the Inspectorate made the clearing of traitors out of Chungking its pretext, and every incoming head of the office declared publicly that this was his first task. Yet in several years it not only never arrested a single genuine traitor but was secretly protecting them, and even had traitor operatives serving as deputy head and head. Liao Gongshao, deputy head during Tao Yishan's headship and Tao's successor as head, and Ye Yansu, the chief secretary Liao recommended while he was head, were both notorious traitors. Liao Gongshao had been director of the South China bureau of the political security bureau of Wang Jingwei's government; his uncle Liao Yuewan was head of the Chiahsing branch of the Hangchow bureau of the puppet political security bureau; his father Liao Jiaju and his younger brother Liao Gongzhu also held office under the puppet government. A whole household of traitors, and yet he had Dai Li's confidence and was given charge of security in the wartime auxiliary capital. What people found least intelligible was that while Liao Gongshao was head of the office he recommended Ye Yansu, who had served as a traitor county magistrate, to be chief secretary. Dai Li's trust in Liao at that time far exceeded his trust in Tao, and one could not help finding this extremely odd.

When I was inspector-general, Liao arrived in Chungking and took over from He Longqing as deputy head. He was constantly escorting strange visitors who spoke with Kiangsu and Chekiang accents in to see Dai Li. At first Tao Yishan was afraid that Liao was playing some trick on him and meant to snatch the headship; but later, when Liao had become head of the office and I was deputy, he still went regularly with these furtive visitors to see Dai Li and Mao Renfeng, and this began to strike me as odd. Afterwards, when I was transferred into the Juntong bureau as head of the general affairs department and had occasion to receive such visitors myself, I learned that they came from Nanking, Shanghai and other places, and were representatives sent by Ren Yuandao, Zhou Fohai and other arch-traitors. If out-and-out traitors like these could go in to see Dai Li escorted by the head of the Inspectorate, who would dare lay a finger on them? So it was that through all the years of the war Chungking bristled with troops and police and swarmed with operatives, and yet.

The thing is plain enough: it was not that there were no traitors in Chungking, but that a traitor arriving in Chungking was an honoured guest of the security authorities, so that naturally nobody could arrest him. Yet every day a number of so-called suspected traitors were in fact picked up. It is truly laughable. During an enemy raid someone who felt his shelter unsafe and wanted to move might hurry along a silent and empty street, and if the troops or police or operatives stopped him and searched him and found on him anything they chose to think suspicious, he could hardly escape a prosecution, and often enough was left wronged with nowhere to appeal. It was said that a mirror used to flash sunlight into the sky could mark out bombing targets for enemy aircraft. On that basis, if a man were taken with witnesses against him he had no way of clearing himself; add a confession beaten out of him, and in the end there was nothing for it but a court martial. By this the security authorities could apply to their superiors for commendation. and could boast to the common people that they had killed another "traitor." There were others who, sheltering at night from a raid warning that had gone on a long time with no enemy aircraft coming, would take a quiet draw on a cigarette, and this too could be treated as ironclad proof of a traitor signalling targets. In short, any careless act or word, or anything faintly taboo carried by accident, brought with it the risk at any moment of being denounced as a traitor. How else, in the wartime auxiliary capital, with so many troops and police and operatives, could they have accounted for themselves without catching a few "traitors"? So a number of bewildered scapegoats went on crying out that they were wronged right up to the execution ground, while the real traitors sat flushed with wine in the vermilion gates of the powerful, raising their cups and drinking deep.

In handling cases of this kind I also asked some friends in the air force about it. They held that smoke raised by day and fires lit by night could indeed mark out a bombing target, but that the reflection off a small shaving mirror, or the glow of a cigarette, was very hard indeed to make out from a bomber at altitude. Still, when a "traitor" had been laboriously seized by the men below, and the men above were demanding a scapegoat daily, then even knowing it to be unjust one had to extract a confession by torture, and nobody dared risk his own prospects to say a fair word.

I remember one occasion when a young couple who had fled from south of the Yangtze to Chungking were sheltering from an air-raid warning with a small child among the graves near the copper-mint arsenal on the south bank. A thermos flask they had with them was broken by accident, and the child was playing with the broken liner, on which the sunlight struck and threw off streaks of reflected light. It happened that the enemy aircraft dropped several bombs near this arsenal that day, and operatives of the south bank inspection post were sheltering nearby. Searching about, they saw these shining objects beside the couple and concluded without hearing a word that the two had been signalling targets to the enemy aircraft. When they came to search them, moreover, they found on the child's neck a red cord threaded through three copper coins hanging at his chest, and at that they considered the case proved by witness and exhibit alike. When the post commander Cao Wandao sent the three of them in to the Inspectorate, the woman explained again and again that the coins on the child were purely a superstition of their home district and that the thermos had genuinely been broken by accident, nor had they any notion that enemy aircraft would come and bomb there. But the operatives of the south bank post insisted they had seen with their own eyes that the couple broke the flask only as the aircraft came overhead, using the liner as a mirror to direct them onto the arsenal, and that they had already admitted the fact, which was in truth a confession beaten out of them. Before long the Garrison Command telephoned to enquire, and since the Juntong bureau did not want cases of this kind I had no choice but to send the three of them on to the Garrison Command on the strength of the south bank post's report. Within ten days the couple were shot as traitors. In those few years there is no telling how many people were shot in that bewildered way.

There was another kind of case, however. The operatives found a suspicious man on the front line at Sandouping and shadowed him all the way from Hupeh to Chungking. This man, one Wang, was the young proprietor of Wang Yuxia, a very large southern-goods and confectionery shop in Hankow, and had been sent by the Japanese to operate in Chungking. But no sooner had he reached Chungking than he went straight to see Wei Daming, head of the Juntong's telecommunications department. Seeing this, the Inspectorate's operatives at once lifted their surveillance and let him move about Chungking as freely as he pleased, and nobody dared lay a finger on him again.

Because their showing against the Communists was poor, Dai Li was forever sending for the Inspectorate's men to give them a dressing-down. The chief problem at that time was that there was no way of stopping the publication of the *Xinhua Daily* in Chungking. Chiang Kai-shek hated that paper to the bone and had always hoped to use secret-service methods to limit the force it exerted among the people. Every time Dai Li sent for us to discuss the matter, the several of us would look at one another blankly with nothing to say, and he would curse us for good-for-nothings who would not do their utmost to accomplish the task the "Leader" had entrusted to us.

In fact everyone in the Inspectorate, from the inspectors up to the head of the office, regarded that paper as a "mortal enemy." Every time we set inspectors on to have their assistants beat up the newsboys, we supposed that next day no paper would be delivered; and next day, walking into the office, it was the first thing we saw. It came out earlier than the other papers and had its own men to deliver it. The Inspectorate got nearly all the other papers free as complimentary copies; this was the only one it paid a subscription for, and every officer from section head upwards had one. On one occasion Tao Yishan boasted in front of Dai Li that he had hit on an excellent method for keeping it off the streets of Chungking altogether. What he had done was to station inspectors at each of the main road junctions with a crowd of hired roughs, who besides beating the newsboys as usual took every paper out of their hands. But while he was on the telephone reporting to Dai Li, the orderly brought the paper in. In his fury he beat the man and chased him out. He had barely put the receiver down when Dai Li's call came, and Tao Yishan's face went white. I was beside him, and he told me under his breath to take it and say he had just gone out. I took Dai's call, and sure enough the same thing had happened at his end: he had still received that day's paper, only a little later than usual. Dai Li's cursing down the telephone was so loud that Tao, sitting beside me, almost began to shake. In the end Dai still made us go to him and gave us a severe dressing-down face to face.

In 1942 Dai Li felt that the office's strength in Chungking was still not sufficient, and set up a reconnaissance brigade under it, appointing Wang Huiyun as brigade commander and Liu Yao as his deputy, and seizing a hotel on Minsheng Road for the brigade headquarters. The brigade had more than two hundred men, the greater part transferred in from the Juntong and a few introduced by the Inspectorate's own operatives.

Dai Li's purpose in forming this brigade was fear that a rising might break out in Chungking with no force at hand to put it down, since the Inspectorate, in an emergency, could turn out no more than two hundred-odd men all told. Called a reconnaissance brigade, it in fact kept most of its men waiting at headquarters in full kit for orders, only a small part going out on operations, and at night it regularly maintained about a hundred men ready to move at any moment. Dai Li would telephone constantly to ask how many men could be assembled at once; we would suppose some emergency had arisen, and it would turn out that he was merely checking. He lived in a state of continual apprehension in those days, terrified that the people of Chungking would mount a demonstration or the like under Communist leadership and that there would be no time to deal with it. All this he had explained to Tao Yishan and me quite plainly before the brigade was formed, though at the Juntong's memorial-week assemblies afterwards he put it differently. There he made it sound very well: the fear was that the Japanese would take advantage of an air raid, when everyone had gone down into the shelters, to send airborne troops to seize Chungking, and that the brigade's men had therefore to be ready at all times to meet an emergency of that sort.

Every man in this brigade was issued a good pistol and made to practise regularly with live ammunition. Dai Li later laid down that men were to be sent by turns into the city daily to patrol in secret, and that when there was a public gathering in particular they were to turn out in force and keep it under covert observation. Dai Li always felt that there were still too few operatives in Chungking; by his intention the brigade should have been expanded to more than three hundred. But the conditions of selection were fairly strict and men could not be taken on casually, and although the Juntong's training classes turned out students by the several thousand every year, there were still not enough to go round, so its establishment was never filled.

## The Detective Brigade of the Chungking Municipal Police

The detective brigade of the Chungking municipal police was formed in October 1941 by enlarging that bureau's detective squad. When Dai Li transferred me to be commander of the detective brigade he wrote a letter in his own hand to Tang Yi, then chief of police, explaining that I had formerly been head of the Inspectorate of the Changde Garrison Headquarters, that I was now being transferred to Chungking to take over the post of the former squad commander Pu Gang, and that he hoped the detective squad might be expanded into a brigade so as to strengthen detective work in the city of Chungking. On receiving the letter Tang told me at once that since Dai Li had decided to convert the detective squad into a detective brigade he would certainly comply, and would apply immediately for a change in the existing establishment. Feng Yukun, then director of the police administration department of the Ministry of the Interior, which supervised police work throughout the country, was a Juntong operative, and that department too was in Juntong hands; so at the same time as he wrote to Tang, Dai Li also wrote to him. explaining that he wished to change the arrangement, and Feng assented on the telephone with a string of yesses and undertook to see it done. So when Tang Yi's report went up through the Chungking municipal government, in less than a week I received my commission as brigade commander. That was about as high a pitch of efficiency as anything reached in the reactionary government of the day, and it shows too that once Dai Li had decided a matter an establishment could be altered on the instant.

My taking over was a novel business indeed. Pu Gang went through the formal handover with me not in his office but in a teahouse at the gate of the detective squad at Lailongbu, Fuzichi. The teahouse had been opened by a retired member of the squad, and Pu Gang and his many sub-squad commanders met there daily. Several of the best seats were permanently reserved for them, and even when empty were not sold to other customers, so that the men could sit down at any time. Since the teahouse stood at the squad's gate, its proprietor was a former member of the squad, and the men who came daily were the squad's own officers, the place had become the best there was for "talking business" with the detective squad. The proprietor became their go-between, and tea was not his principal trade. His main income was not from selling tea but from the "sideline" by which he took his share and grew rich. For after the detective squad came under Juntong control, neither Xu Zhongqi, who was of the Juntong, nor Tang Yi, who was not, enquired into that unit's work at all.

The choice of chief of police in wartime Chungking was an entertaining matter. Xu Zhongqi and Tang Yi each held the post twice: Xu came down and handed it to Tang, Tang came down and handed it back to Xu, Xu came down again and handed it once more to Tang, as though no third man could be found. The reason behind it was simple enough. There were plenty of others contending for it, but their turn never came, because Xu was a Juntong man who besides Dai's backing had a line of his own straight to Chiang Kai-shek, and above all curried favour with H. H. Kung, going to great lengths to please Kung's second daughter Kong Lingjun. Tang, though not of the Juntong, had always been close to Dai Li, and had besides the support of the Szechwan warlord Wang Zanxu; and taking the Chiang and Kung line as Xu did, he could be appointed term after term. Each time they changed places, apart from bringing a chief secretary of his own, the incoming man kept on the head of the supervision department, the head of the judicial section and the rest.

The detective squad, however, was not wholly in the hands of the Juntong bureau but was worked by the Juntong's Chungking special district, the squad commander, and later the brigade commander, serving concurrently as a group head of that district and doing social intelligence and action work on its behalf. Only for such work as watching the Communist Party office and the *Xinhua Daily* did the Juntong make unified arrangements. So this detective squad could exploit the gaps to arrest people as it pleased and practise extortion. The teahouse became the place where terms were settled, the money passed with one hand and the prisoner released with the other.

When I went to take over, Pu Gang stood at the door and hailed me, and I mistakenly supposed he was being very civil in coming out to wait for me. Only when he had pulled me inside the teahouse and sat me down did I understand what it was really about. I felt at the time that such a setting was most unsuitable for a conversation, and he then agreed to go to his office, where he introduced some of the squad's officers to me.

The squad's internal establishment had been very simple: apart from the deputy commander Shen Xifeng there was only a clerk and a few copyists and orderlies, and the clerk did everything, interrogations included. Generally, though, the sub-squad commanders seized men and questioned them themselves, and the clerk merely recorded the statement and signed a recommendation. The files were in complete disorder. When I first came and watched those sub-squad commanders questioning thieves and pickpockets, they talked to them entirely in the cant of the trade, "shop talk," and the other side answered fluently, and only at the end did they come to the case. Asked how many times he had been in before, a man would readily admit which jobs were his and what had become of the goods, speaking quite frankly without trying to shift the blame, and showing no fear whatever. The questioning went like a chat about household matters; there was no need for torture at all, and it did not resemble an interrogation.

Once I tried changing the method and questioned a thief in the ordinary way. He protested his innocence over and over, flatly denied stealing anything from anyone, and made himself out to be a pitiable honest man. Naturally I did not believe him, but I had no way of getting the truth out of him; and just as I was left with no way to climb down, an old member of the squad came in to see me on some business, took one look at this self-declared honest man and said at once, "So-and-so, how is it you have come unstuck again?" At that the man changed his tune and made a full confession, and the matter was cleared up on the spot. He was a habitual thief and had not been wronged at all.

After becoming brigade commander I set about enlarging and reorganizing the detective brigade. It seemed to me that a detective brigade in the seat of the wartime auxiliary capital ought not to be satisfied with social intelligence and the management of thieves and pickpockets. I understood Dai Li's cast of mind and what the Juntong wanted: so long as one could think of a method and put the request, support was certain. Questions of establishment or of law I did not consider at all at the time. Nor did I consult the deputy commander Shen Xifeng much, since Dai Li did not trust him and I thought nothing of him either. I set about the reorganization entirely on the pattern I had worked with and seen in Shanghai, beginning with brigade headquarters, and asked the Juntong bureau's personnel office for a batch of graduates of the Linli special training class. The personnel office was at that time run almost entirely by Linli men, and as I had been an instructor of that class I was on good terms with them. This request was met at once. First a clerical office was set up at brigade headquarters with Deng Yifu, of the Linli class, as its head; the existing clerk, Wang Chengjun, not being a Juntong man, was left in place as clerk of that office for the time being, so that Deng could get the situation clear before replacing him. For an officer commanding a Juntong field unit at that time could not transfer a Juntong operative except through the Juntong bureau, whereas a man who was not of the Juntong could be replaced at will.

At brigade headquarters I created three new sub-sections, for supervision, judicial matters and general affairs, with the Linli men Shao Hua, Li Xiang and Ling Junba as their heads. I also asked the Juntong bureau to transfer part of its police-dog room, men and dogs alike, to the brigade, with Wang Shilu, head of the bureau's police-dog room, holding the same post in the brigade concurrently. From the police bureau I asked for part of the fingerprint room's staff to come to the brigade as well.

I adjusted the field units too. I gathered up the dozen or so men of better education and formed a directly attached squad charged solely with party and government intelligence, with Li Qiaoyi as its commander. Li was at that time a sworn brother of Shi Xiaoxian, a leading Paoko figure in Chungking. The Lan Society, the Paoko fraternity Shi organized and led, numbered among its members a good many people of the Kuomintang, the Democratic Socialist Party and the Youth Party, and others besides from banking and commerce, and lawyers and doctors. Li was the Lan Society's steward and his social connections were complex. Some of the other men, Zhang Kedong among them, had ties with the press, some had dealings with the teachers and students of the colleges, and some had friends in cultural circles. After talking with these men individually over a few days I had a fair idea of them, and so decided to pick this group out. I decided to pick this group out and let them play a more important part, no longer handling cases of theft and pickpocketing but devoting themselves to gathering the party and government intelligence the Juntong wanted. I understood very well that there were fewer pickings in this work than in the others, so I raised their allowances a little, explained to them that results would bring a special bonus or promotion, and told them that in future the brigade would treat them particularly favourably in the distribution of bonuses.

I raised every sub-squad under the detective squad to a company and every group to a platoon, which delighted the officers of all the units. So as not to break the continuity of the work I left the existing commanders essentially unchanged and did not alter the districts in their charge. The First Company kept He Yukun as its commander, responsible for the city district. The Third kept Huang Junliang, responsible for the south bank. The Fourth kept Jiang Rushan, responsible for the Jiangbei district. The Fifth kept Li Wenyuan, responsible for the area from Hualongqiao to Shapingba and Ciqikou. Only the Second Company was changed, to the Linli man Tao Siqing, responsible for the lower city district, because that district held, besides the Juntong bureau's own headquarters, Dai Li's residence at Zengjiayan and Chiang Kai-shek's at Shangqingsi. and the Communist Party's office at Zengjiayan as well. It was the key district of the day, and had therefore to be put in the charge of my most trusted student. Not only must nothing go wrong in it; the work of watching the Communist Party office had to be carried further. The Fifth Company's ground was also a key district at that time, since the Juntong's office at the Ciqikou silk filature and Hongyan Village in the Xiaolongkan sub-district both needed particular attention.

I wrote this plan out myself and took it straight to Dai Li for his instructions. He was delighted when he had read it and said over and over, "Very good, very good." What pleased him most was my request to form a directly attached squad working solely on party and government intelligence. He wrote a letter there and then in his own hand to Tang Yi, the chief of police, telling him to act on my plan. As I was taking my leave he reminded me to go through the formalities at the Juntong bureau first, so that the bureau should not be left out of the loop. I therefore took the simplest course and sent his letter and my plan together to Mao Renfeng, so as not to approach Tang Yi directly myself. Within three days it had all been carried out. I then drew up a set of regulations, and had an informers' box put up at the gate of brigade headquarters, in which the public were invited to denounce unlawful conduct by the men outside; it was opened daily by me in person.

After taking over I moved into brigade headquarters, and being busy learning the work and the personnel I had not yet found time to look into the detention house one saw on entering, which could hold a hundred men and was packed with prisoners. One night I was woken in the small hours by a burst of crying and the sound of blows and curses. Getting up at once to look, I found the "old prisoners" in the detention house beating and cursing a very young "new prisoner." The warders came up then to report to me, saying the new boy had been beaten because he would not fall in with the old prisoners' arrangements. The warder urged me to go back to bed and let him settle the matter.

I thought it very odd at the time that a temporary detention house of this kind should have "old prisoners" at all, since by the regulations then in force ordinary criminal offenders and those charged under the police regulations had to be dealt with within fixed periods and ought not to be held long in a detention house. I decided to find out what was really going on, and after the better part of a night I had at least the beginnings of it. The detention house was subject to no legal restriction whatever: a man was held as long as they pleased. The so-called old prisoners were men held over long periods who had become the greater and lesser bosses of the place. The oldest of them had "lived" there more than two years, and having gradually established his "position" had become its chief. Under him were three lesser bosses of about a year's standing who helped him bully the newcomers. Any who dared disobey were beaten and cursed on the spot or refused food and sleep. Prisoners were searched as a matter of course on entering the detention house and everything of value taken from them, to be "kept" for them by brigade headquarters. Anything that escaped the search this boss appropriated, forbidding the prisoner to say a word about it. As for the food and clothing sent in by a new prisoner's family, half had to be given him as "tribute" first. So living there he had a larger income than the ordinary civil servant of the day. This comfortable post was not easily come by, and having it he had no intention of leaving, and went to all lengths to ingratiate himself with the men of the brigade so that his case should not be disposed of. What charge he had been arrested on not even the warder could say. I had the man in charge of the files look the case up, and there was no case on record. When I examined this "old prisoner's" belongings I found that besides three or four very good quilts he had a small leather case, and opening it startled even me. Inside were not only a thick wad of banknotes but American dollars, small pieces of gold, several watches and seven or eight gold pens, all squeezed out of the other prisoners. And this old prisoner's "estate" was only what remained after he had paid his "tribute" to the warders, for in two years and more several thousand prisoners had passed through, he had stripped them clean, and he could stand in for the warders in managing them besides. So nobody wanted him sent away, and he for his part regarded the place as his paradise, where he could do as he liked and range unchecked within his little world.

That evening I sorted out from the near hundred men more than forty who had been held over a month without being dealt with. Of these I kept back only the dozen or so arrested on political suspicion, for further investigation; the rest, the thieves and pickpockets whose cases were outstanding, together with that chief and his lesser bosses, I sent next day to the judicial section of the police bureau to be dealt with. These political prisoners were mostly students and workers. Some of the students had been active at school and had said a few words against the National Government, or were fond of reading the *Xinhua Daily* and had spoken well of the Communist Party. Some of the workers had complained in their factories that prices were soaring and wages too low to keep a family on, and had wanted the proprietor to raise their wages; some had grumbled and cursed the reactionary government. In short they had all been marked down as gravely suspect on insufficient evidence.

I did not dare take it on myself to dispose of these people lightly, so I asked the Chungking special district. They could think of nothing either, and in the end an application was made to the Juntong bureau, which sent a judicial officer to examine them; he took away four students and two workers whose cases were more serious and left the rest for further investigation. I was not content at that time merely to catch thieves and pickpockets: I always wanted to lay hands on some Communists. So I kept a tight grip on the directly attached squad's work, sending for the men regularly and telling them face to face how they were to proceed. On one occasion intelligence came in that a Communist cell had been found in a small ironworks near Cuntan in Jiangbei; I took men there overnight to search and seize, and arrested three workers under grave suspicion, from whose homes several mimeographed documents were taken. For this the Juntong bureau gave us a commendation. Successive brigade commanders after me all attached great importance to this squad's work, which gradually widened until it became a small Juntong intelligence unit in Chungking.

Of the successive commanders of the detective brigade, I held the post for a little over four months, and after me it was held by Xu Zhongwu, Li Lianfu, Tan Rongzhang and others. The squad's work, which had always been confined to theft, gradually turned into Juntong secret-service work, and it competed with the Inspectorate in watching the Communist leaders in Chungking, in party and government intelligence, and in all manner of activity directed at cultural circles.

The dark dealings of this detective brigade were beyond counting, and I shall speak of them only briefly here. They seized and confined men at will. In arresting political prisoners they would take them away denounced as thieves, and would even bring a thief along on purpose to identify them, alleging that they were of the fraternity or had once received stolen goods. In this way a political prisoner against whom there was not a shred of evidence could be carried off with perfect convenience, while the habitual thieves themselves were left undisturbed. When an ordinary household lost something, no amount of appealing to the detective squad would get the case solved; and even if it were solved, the goods could not be got back to their owner. But when a household of power and influence was robbed, the detective squad would recover the goods very quickly; and if the thief happened to be a man down from the lower Yangtze who had not joined a gang in Chungking, the squad would require the other thieves to make the value good.

I handled a case of this kind myself. A youth named Wang, who had left school, fled from Shanghai to Chungking, and failing to find the relative he had come to join and able to find no relief anywhere, took a desperate course and stole a few goods from a shop in the banking district; he was seized on the spot and handed over to the detective squad. One of the men looked into it, judged him a first offender, dealt with him leniently and released him. The youth afterwards found a post as a primary school teacher, and one day, crossing at the steamer wharf, he ran into the very man who had arrested him. The man invited him to a teahouse nearby and asked how "business" had been of late. Not understanding what the man was driving at, the youth said that he was not in trade but teaching at a primary school. Taking this for ingratitude, the man gave him a slap to sharpen his wits and made him take his coat off. The youth naturally would not stand for it, the two of them fell to quarrelling, and when the man wanted to take him away he was not afraid either, so that the quarrel went on all the way to brigade headquarters. I sent someone to his school to establish the facts, and the school stood surety and took him away. Only then did I understand that once an ordinary thief had been arrested and the men knew him by sight, they would ask him for money and goods whenever they met him afterwards, and if he would not agree they could arrest him again at any time. Every man's pocket was stuffed with outstanding theft cases, and he had only to fasten one of them on a man at random to land him in a prosecution. Sometimes, if the man would not admit it, they could put another thief up to swearing he had been an accomplice, and in the end right and wrong could not be told apart and he had no way of clearing himself.

The really accomplished habitual thieves the men were reluctant to arrest, because leaving them at large meant a larger share of the spoils, and the bigger and more numerous the cases the bigger the share. On a case that did not absolutely have to be solved they would simply seize a few scapegoats to make up the number. Newcomers driven to a desperate course were the likeliest of all to be taken. In my four months and more with the detective brigade, nine out of ten of the thieves arrested were novices, whereas an old hand was recognized by the men the moment he came through the door, and they could tell you at once that this was one who "ate match money" (picking a pocket being like feeling for a match), or one who "gathered dew money" (a thief who went in door to door at daybreak), or one who "ate the lamp flower" (who stole at night after the lamps were lit). They sorted them into their kinds and were never once wrong.

The detective squad's handling of stolen property was darker still. When ordinary people lost money or goods and reported it, brigade headquarters would allocate the case by district to the company responsible for the ground, and nine times in ten it went like a stone into the sea. In the one or two cases that were solved, cash was docked as a matter of course and most of it never recovered, and the owner still had to hand over some tenths of the value "voluntarily", which is to say under compulsion, as a reward before he could collect his property. If he failed to take the hint and would not part with a hair, misfortunes came in pairs: the men would find some further pretext and call on him at any hour. There were servants of the great and powerful who, though their masters had lost nothing at all, would hide something deliberately, take their master's visiting card to brigade headquarters to report a theft, and set a time limit for solving it. When I first met with this I did not know what to do about it. One day the aide-de-camp of Dai Chuanxian, president of the Kuomintang's Examination Yuan, came to see me with Dai's visiting card, saying that an antique porcelain vase had been stolen from the president's drawing room and that I was to find some means at once of clearing the case and recovering it. I sent two old hands to look at it, and they came back and told me it was probably a thief within the household, or else the aide making mischief on purpose. They had experience of cases of this kind, so I handed it to them. Some days later the aide came again to press me, and after I had talked it over with the two old hands they went and negotiated with him directly, and the end of it was that a little money changed hands and the matter was dropped. Later, when I had become head of the general affairs department at the Juntong, Dai Li told me to take a letter of his and ask Dai Chuanxian to write something out for him. I mentioned the vase missing from his drawing room, as though by the way, meaning to ingratiate myself a little by pointing out that I had handled the case for him. To my surprise he denied outright that any such thing had happened, and pointing to the vase in his reception room said it was there every day and had never been lost. He then sent for the aide and questioned him, and the aide said haltingly that it had been missing one morning but had been found in its place again by noon. Only then did I believe that the two old hands had guessed quite right, and that it had been the servants of those great houses making mischief all along.

In those days the detective brigade caught several, or a dozen or more, thieves every day, and a good deal of cash and goods could be taken off them. Juntong operatives all understood this arrangement, and many of them came to me regularly to ask for things. They would go and look over the stolen-property store first, and for whatever took their fancy would fill out a declaration under some false name, saying they had lost that article at such a place on such a date, the date being put a few days before the case was solved. I would pass the slip down, and the men below would endorse it "case solved, article recovered, recommend restitution", and I would minute it "approved", and the thing passed into the operative's hands without the least trouble. If the real owner got wind of it and enquired, we could still see it out, because the formalities were complete and the goods had genuinely been collected by their "owner". In my four months and more with the detective brigade I did this sort of thing against my conscience more than ten times, mostly for section and sub-section officers of the Juntong bureau, and what they asked for was always the best of it.

Of the great quantity of stolen property taken from thieves and pickpockets, apart from what the operatives appropriated, anything unclaimed after about half a year could be treated as the squad's own property, applied to bonuses or a reserve fund and disposed of by the squad itself; a small part was sent on with the case to the judicial section of the police bureau. That section's methods were much the same as the squad's: it always found some way to make the goods its own. Xu Zhongwu, in his half-year and more as brigade commander, pulled down the detective brigade's old premises and put up a two-storey building, and the money came out of this stolen property. Many people said behind their hands at the time: a thief who steals goes to prison, while the detective brigade builds a house with money a thief stole and gets commended for it.

I was brigade commander for a little over four months before being moved to the Inspectorate as inspector-general, and the transfer came about, it must be said, absurdly. One day Tang Yi's residence on Cangping Street was burgled and an eiderdown quilt and some clothes taken: the thieves had actually got into the house of the chief of police. It was the joke of the moment and the talk of the whole town. When Tang Yi telephoned to ask me over and discuss it, we were both extremely embarrassed. I charged the First Company to do its utmost to clear the case, since the compensation method could not be used here and the goods themselves had to be recovered. I threatened several sub-squad commanders that if the case were not solved in three days I would lock them up.

Sure enough, in under three days the goods were recovered without the loss of a single article and two men brought in as well. I questioned them myself. Both thieves were full of regret, saying they had not made sure it was the chief's residence and would never dare steal there again. I then followed the newly drawn-up procedure and sent the owner a printed notice asking him to come and identify his property and complete the formalities of collection. To my surprise nobody came for two or three days after the notice went out, so I sent a reminder. That same afternoon one of Tang Yi's adopted sons came storming into my office in a fury. His view was that when the chief's house had lost something, not to send men round with it promptly but to serve a notice as though on any ordinary member of the public was grossly discourteous, and he insisted I deliver it in person. My blood was up at the time and I was in no mood for that sort of thing; trading on my Juntong connections, I quarrelled back at him. Seeing that I would not give way, he put his hand inside his coat to feel for something. Taking him to be reaching for a pistol I made ready to have it out with him, and what he pulled out turned out to be one of Tang Yi's visiting cards, which he told me to look at and say who this was. He had probably been in the habit of frightening people with that card, and had not reckoned on my throwing it on the floor and treading on it. He then overturned my desk, and I gave him several punches without ceremony and pushed him out of the door. The moment he had gone I ordered the handover to be prepared and went straight off to see Dai Li. When Dai had heard me out he reproved me only lightly, agreed there and then to my resigning, and told me to make ready to go and work at the Inspectorate. That is how I left the detective brigade. Yet the Chungking scandal-sheets of the day said I had been promoted for my excellent record, which heaven knows was not so.

A year later I was transferred to the Juntong bureau, but I had not forgotten the brigade's work. I once put it to Dai Li that besides using the brigade for political work we ought to strengthen its part in clearing up theft in Chungking. He answered me without the least interest: "Do you imagine we want to control this organization simply in order to catch thieves and pickpockets for other people?" From what Dai Li said it is clear that he wanted these overt bodies for the convenience of the Juntong's work, and that anything of benefit to the townspeople he was not much inclined to do.

This brigade had between a hundred and seventy and a hundred and eighty regular members, but the contacts it worked came to nearly a thousand. Of basic Juntong operatives there were only a dozen or so; Dai Li did his utmost to work these people for the Juntong without wishing to take the rest into the Juntong organization. Only a very few, with his permission, were sent for a month or two to a short training course run by the Juntong so as to become his students, and only then were they allowed to become members and go through the formalities of joining. The great majority of the rest were simply worked by the Juntong and laboured on its behalf.

## The Juntong Bureau's Chungking Special District

What directed the field operations of the Juntong bureau's operatives in Chungking was the bureau's Chungking special district, the Yu special district for short. It had grown out of the Juntong's former Chungking station and was a fairly large organization, with the Wanxian station, the Luzhou station and others beneath it, directing the work of several dozen counties in eastern Szechwan; only from Neijiang westwards did responsibility pass to the Chuan-Kang district, seated at Chengtu. Here I shall speak only of some of its activities in Chungking.

The heads of the Yu special district were, in succession, Jiang Shaomo, Tu Shoumei, Zhu Xiaogu and Ye Xiangzhi, Jiang Shaomo holding the post twice. The district office was at Ciju in the old street of Chungking. Before the liberation of Chungking that building was converted into the offices of the Second Department of the Southwest Military and Political Headquarters.

The Yu special district had forty or fifty senior intelligence men directed by the head of the district in person, with a liaison officer to keep in regular touch with them. Apart from a few who were senior officials in the various ministries and commissions of the government of the day, most were members of the democratic parties, or people connected with the Communist Party, or renegades from it. Besides using them for intelligence on the democratic parties, the Juntong hoped still more to obtain intelligence on the Communist Party through them. Work directed at the Communists was at that time the key task among key tasks, yet it never attained the object the Juntong had set itself. Much ingenuity was spent in gathering in renegades and left-wing figures, and the result was always disappointment.

The Yu special district directed five or six groups in the Chungking area, divided into the upper city, lower city, western suburbs, south bank and Jiangbei groups. The key one was the western suburbs group, aimed chiefly at the Communist Party office at Hongyan Village and at the *Xinhua Daily*, and its office was at Hualongqiao. I remember that when Gan Lizuo was head of the western suburbs group he was forever complaining bitterly that the task was too heavy and the men too few, that no results could be shown for the work, and that he was constantly being reproached for it.

This group had a dozen or so members. The hope had originally been to work their way step by step into the Communist Party office and the *Xinhua Daily*; but although they lived close by, they could never think how to do it. They then adopted the method of "drawing people out", and that too cost a great deal of thought for nothing. In the end they had nothing but the routine of watching and tailing to give them a thread to follow. They sent operatives daily to keep watch on Hongyan Village, and dared not go too close, knowing that the Communist Party office and the other units there were on their guard, and there being a rumour that the office meant to seize secretly any operative bold enough to come reconnoitring nearby.

While I was head of the general affairs department at the Juntong bureau, Liu Tinggen, head of the general affairs sub-section of the Yu special district, came to me once to draw several pistols, and I refused him. By the Juntong bureau's regulations field operatives were not permitted to carry arms; only those on overt duties might. This was because a field operative had to do his utmost not to expose his identity, which was the only way he could operate, and carrying a pistol would give away the target. Liu said at the time that it was the western suburbs group, afraid that the people at the Communist Party office would move against them, who had asked for a few pistols to keep at the group office for self-defence. I answered him: "It would be best of all if the people at the Communist Party office came and seized us, or fired on our men. They need only do that once and we should have ample grounds for searching their office. Issuing a few pistols is quite unnecessary, and if one went off by accident it would cause trouble instead."

Although I dealt with the matter in that way, I was not altogether easy about it afterwards. One day I took the opportunity to report it to Dai Li and see what his view was. He was very dissatisfied with the Yu special district at that time, holding that its men could not conceal their identities properly and behaved as though their one fear were that nobody should know them for Juntong men, so that it was hard for them to penetrate the various classes of society and the whole meaning of secret work was lost. He not only approved my refusing them arms but cursed them for good-for-nothings. If they exposed themselves and got shot dead, he said, he would be ashamed to acknowledge them as Juntong men. He told me to pass this on to the head of the Yu special district and to give the matter particular attention.

I then put my own idea to him: if the Communist Party office killed or wounded one of our men, we should have a pretext for searching it. To my surprise he was extremely angry when he heard it and reproved me: "You are talking nonsense! Do you imagine methods like that will achieve our object in the struggle with the Communist Party? A man killed is killed for nothing, and a man wounded has it coming to him! You are not to scheme along those lines." He went on at me for a long while, holding that this notion of mine was too naive and showed no political sense. Finally he said that the people at the Communist Party office would never adopt such methods at all. His view was that if an operative went blundering in regardless, they would detain him, make representations to us, publish it openly in the newspapers and shame him, and that this would be far harder to deal with than having one of our men killed. Hearing that the matter was as serious as this, I went at once to see Jiang Shaomo, then head of the district, explained the position and instructed them to pay particular attention to it.

Jiang Shaomo told me that the Yu special district's work never opened out, chiefly because it lacked threads leading to the Communist Party and so could show no results. He was twice head of the district, and at first, by making use of individual right-wingers within the democratic parties, he could still learn something about the Communists at second hand. Later such people grew fewer and fewer, while the Juntong pressed for deeper penetration still, so that the means available grew ever scarcer.

In intelligence work the Yu special district had rather more resources than the other units. Many overseas Chinese returning home, for instance, who had been to Zengjiayan to meet the Communist leaders, often did not understand that Chiang Kai-shek co-operated with the Communist Party only in appearance while in reality opposing the Communists and the people, and so would talk freely about what had been said in their conversations with the Communist leaders. This was a great deal better than merely standing at the gate watching people come and go every day without so much as learning their names. At the same time every group had several operatives who could work their way into one or another of the democratic parties, and could penetrate the various progressive bodies with relative ease. So of the units doing intelligence work in Chungking at that time, the Yu special district may be counted the one with the most resources.

## The Operatives' Corps

The Juntong bureau's operatives' corps had grown out of the pre-war operatives' squad, enlarged and reorganized. Before the war the operatives' squad had all been in plain clothes; only when the war began was it converted into an armed force. These troops Dai Li obtained one by one from his friends Hu Zongnan, Tang Enbo, Tang Shengming and others. In 1938, when Tang Shengming was garrison commander at Changde, Dai Li passed through the town, took a fancy at first sight to Tang's operatives' company, and carried it off men and weapons together to Chungking. That is a fact I saw with my own eyes.

The operatives' corps also went at various times under the names of the Second Patrol Corps of the Military Affairs Commission's Unified Land and Water Transport Inspection Department, and the Fifth Operatives' Regiment of the Military Affairs Commission, but within the Juntong it was always called the operatives' corps. Those who served as its commander or as regimental commander included Zhang Ye, Wang Zhaohuai, Yang Qingzhi, Zhang Fubang, Wang Chunhui and Zhou Wenxin.

The operatives' regiments of the Kuomintang's forces and military organs were generally employed on guard duty above all, but this operatives' regiment of the Juntong's really did carry on secret-service work. It had three armed battalions and a plain-clothes company. Apart from a portion employed on guard at Juntong offices and at the residences of Dai Li, T. V. Soong and others, most served as warders in the detention houses and prisons, and it was these men who carried out the Juntong's killings. The guarding of Communists held by the Juntong, such as Mr. Liao Chengzhi and General Ye Ting, was also done by operatives of this corps, and the operatives' squads that guarded Generals Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng were detached from it too.

Besides the clerical, adjutant and quartermaster offices that any regiment has, this corps had an action group whose particular business was arrests and torture. Although more than a thousand people worked at Juntong bureau headquarters at that time, they were all inside staff doing clerical work, and when the Juntong wanted a man seized it was the action group that was sent. They could search for and seize members of the public openly and at will, and Juntong operatives who broke the rules were handed to them for confinement as well. The operatives called this group the "Embroidered Uniform Guard" behind its back. Overbearing and insufferable as the operatives were before the common people, at the sight of these men of the Embroidered Uniform Guard, ravening as wolves and tigers, coming to deal with them, their legs would turn to water. I saw with my own eyes Dai Li send these men to arrest Tao Yishan, head of the Chungking Inspectorate. This Tao Yishan, a devil in the eyes of the people of Chungking, went white in the face the moment he saw them and could not get his words out. The operatives knew better than anyone that the calamity such a visit brought was not to be resisted and did not bear thinking about.

The corps' detention house was more blood-curdling still. Juntong people generally called it the "Elementary School", Baigongguan being the "Middle School" and the Xifeng concentration camp the "University", because it was the first gate into the Juntong's prisons. The Juntong's interrogations began here, and every kind of instrument was to be had. The detention house was the responsibility of the action group, and to walk into the office of its head, Cheng Yongming, was to be set shaking: the walls were hung with fetters and manacles of every description, and with whips and rods on which the blood was not yet dry, everything one could think of. Their favourite occupation was studying with great concentration which instrument suited which sort of person, which injured the skin without injuring the bone, and which injured the bone without injuring the skin.

At that time I taught operational technique in many of the Juntong's training classes, and this group's head and a good many of its members were my students, so I often went there to hear the practical experience they had gained in carrying out these criminal activities, and used it to enrich my teaching material. I was then, like them, a man who had lost his humanity. In the "invention" of many new instruments of torture I had a hand in the discussion of most. Within a few days a conclusion could be reached about how well any of these things worked, because there was opportunity to try them out almost daily.

This corps was quartered throughout in the Hunan-Hupeh guild hall at Wanglongmen on Linsen Road. In those days many people in Chungking, at the mere sound of the three characters "Wanglongmen", thought at once of that body's brutality, and there was not one who did not hate and fear it. When the corps' operatives were running outside the law and were asked what organization they belonged to, they would answer in an overbearing tone, "Wanglongmen." On the strength of those four characters they could go to the theatre without buying a ticket and travel by cart or boat without paying, and the licensed and unlicensed brothels and the opium dens, hearing that they came from there, groaned inwardly while having to put on a smiling face to welcome them. They were fiercer than the ordinary overt secret-service bodies, being a unit directly under the Juntong bureau, and even many operatives had to give way to them a little, so that they grew fiercer and fiercer and nobody dared provoke them.

The only cinema in Chungking, at Yanwuting, whose manager was Zhou Boxun, supposed that with the Kuomintang Central Propaganda Department's film studio behind it, it need not defer to them and could take little notice of any "Wanglongmen men." The upshot was that the cinema was smashed up by them several times running and had in the end to acknowledge their privileges. They often boasted at that time, "Chungking was won by us!" I was then regularly duty officer at the Juntong bureau, and many reactionary organs would complain to the bureau about these men making trouble outside. I always told them to take it to the Inspectorate or the police bureau, since those overt secret-service units would shield them and the matter generally came to nothing.

In 1943 Chiang Kai-shek decided to have General Ye Ting brought from Kweilin to Chungking, and at first he was confined in the residence of this regiment's commander at 22 Wanglongmen. Later, because of the daily air raids, he was moved to Baigongguan; and afterwards, when Baigongguan was made quarters for the American operatives of SACO, he was moved again to the Honglu factory to the southeast of SACO. Later still Chen Cheng had General Ye Ting brought to the headquarters of the Sixth War Zone at Enshi in Hupeh to be talked into surrender, and failing in that sent him back, and he was confined here again. This was not far from the place where Mr. Liao Chengzhi was held, and throughout it was operatives of this corps who guarded them.

## The Special Censorship Department of the Military Affairs Commission

The Special Censorship Department of the General Office of the Military Affairs Commission did a great deal in the way of criminal activity against the Communists and the people during the war against Japan, but ordinary people knew nothing of it. This was because, unlike the Inspectorate, the detective brigade and similar bodies, it did not persecute people directly all day long, but worked its mischief in the dark.

The department was quartered at Qingdeli in Lailong Lane, near Fuzichi in a busy quarter of Chungking. Nothing in its outward appearance suggested an active anti-Communist and anti-people secret-service body, for nobody was ever arrested there and it had no detention house or the like; only about a hundred people worked in it, and it looked like any ordinary office. Yet the bodies it directed were engaged all day in criminal activity that would not bear the light.

From 1941 until victory in the war, the head of this department was Li Xiaobai, a favourite about Dai Li and head of the Juntong bureau's personnel office. The department had sections for general affairs, postal and telegraphic censorship, counter-espionage and so on, whose heads were Zhong Gongxun, Zhou Jingdun, Yang Bing-somebody and others. Being on good personal terms with Li Xiaobai I went there often to see him, and so learned something of how the department stood.

About 1942 this department was renamed the Postal and Air Censorship Department of the General Office of the Military Affairs Commission, and the air censorship work of the various regions was merged into it as well. People outside understood this very little at the time, and even many Juntong operatives were unclear about it. The department's existing work was busy enough already, and air censorship at Chungking and elsewhere had always been directed by the Inspectorate; if it was to be unified at all, then by rights it could only have been merged into the Military Affairs Commission's Unified Land and Water Transport Inspection Department. Dai Li was head of that department himself, and a single order in his hand could have converted it into a unified land, water and air transport inspection department. That instead it was merged into a body which never showed itself in the ordinary way struck a good many people as odd.

The air censorship posts of that time were the fattest berths of all the inspection posts. A man had only to become an inspector at one to have the best watches, gold pens and Western suits come his way at once, for these men could control air tickets and deal on the black market, and above all they had dealings all day with the great speculators who travelled by air on business and with the flying men who ran contraband, so that there was no easier way to grow rich. Those they mixed with were high officials, or high officials' wives and concubines, and not a few air censors were regularly asked for by name by the powerful and transferred at their request. As a short road to office and fortune, nothing surpassed it.

Before the question of which body the air censorship posts belonged to was settled, both the Inspectorate and the Land and Water Transport Inspection Department wanted them for themselves. Having served in the Inspectorate I wanted them attached there too, since it would have made it more convenient to get friends and relatives onto aircraft, or to have goods bought abroad and local products brought from various places; and I took an opportunity to suggest to Dai Li that they be left with the Inspectorate. The result was a rebuff. He said only, "What do you know about it?" and I did not dare raise it again. That roused my curiosity, and when I had the chance I asked around. It turned out that Dai Li wanted to settle the minds of the postal and telegraphic censorship staff by giving them a means of improving their circumstances. Of the overt organs the Juntong controlled at that time, the anti-smuggling administration, the freight bureau, the transport inspection department, the Inspectorate, the police bureau and the rest were all rich in sources of revenue and each had its privileges, whereas the postal and telegraphic censorship department was a "clear-water yamen." In their own phrase they were "picking bones out of waste paper", for however good their results, what they turned up was not fat meat and there were no pickings in it, so they were never content in the work.

The Chungking postal censorship post was the largest of them, on a special establishment with about two hundred men and ample funds; yet even its head, Liu Zhipan, asked repeatedly to be transferred, from which the feelings of the rest may be imagined. For even if they intercepted a letter of the greatest value they had to hand it to another body to act on and had no further part in it, and even when a case was broken they could receive only a small share of the reward. Yet the Juntong set particular store by this work and could not do without it. So that those engaged in it should get something too, the air censorship posts of the various regions were merged into this department. There were of course other reasons, bearing on relations within and without, but what I understood to be the main one, though it was never formally announced, was this.

I went to see how the postal censorship post worked. Walking in, one saw progressive books and periodicals of every kind heaped up like a small hill, and bundled copies of the *Xinhua Daily*. When the post office handed registered and express letters over to the operatives for examination, they were checked over one by one and returned in the same number afterwards; insured letters the operatives were made to open on the spot, since valuables might be stolen or exchanged by an inspector and add to the post office's difficulties. When an operative found a suspicious letter he took it back to be assessed and studied by the examiners; in most cases the copyists of the clerical office transcribed the suspect passage or the whole of it, and only the few judged important were photographed. Ordinary letters, however, the operatives could carry off in bulk to examine, and so the originals were frequently confiscated. While examining ordinary letters some operatives would quietly keep back small articles people had sent, handkerchiefs, scenic photographs, photographs of women. Some of the women inspectors were fondest of reading other people's love letters, which they treated as an amusement.

The post's way of opening letters was very simple: generally they were eased open with a small bone knife or the like, or held over steam for a moment and lifted. Letters sealed with egg white were said to be hard to open, but they had a method for those too. They kept chemicals of the paper-pulp and fibre sort, and having cut the envelope open with a small knife and taken the letter out, they made good the cut with the pulp after reading it and pressed it lightly with an iron, so that no trace could be seen.

The letters passing through the post office each day then ran into the tens of thousands, and the operatives could not possibly open them all, so they could only examine selectively. At the head of the register of what had to be examined they placed the Communist Party's organs in Chungking and many of its leaders. While Vice-Chairman Zhou Enlai was in Chungking, letters came for him from every quarter almost daily, most of them expressing respect for Zhou Enlai, or for the Communist Party, or for Chairman Mao; some came from people persecuted by the Kuomintang, asking him to right their wrongs. Letters of this kind were mostly detained, and those carrying a name and address were sent to the Juntong bureau to be dealt with, the writer being watched if he got off lightly and arrested if he did not. Those with no address were commonly thrown into the wastepaper basket and burned day by day, and never reached Vice-Chairman Zhou Enlai at all. After victory in the war, when Chairman Mao went to Chungking, still more letters were written to him from every quarter, and of those that reached his hands there can hardly have been more than one or two in a hundred; the rest were destroyed by these operatives in the same way.

Second on their register came the many democratic figures. A great many letters were written to Mr. Guo Moruo from all quarters, and most of them met the same fate. Then there were letters from people of the universities and of cultural circles, which also had to be examined. Besides these standing priorities, the Juntong bureau would send down from time to time lists of revolutionary figures or suspects to be watched, and for these the letters and telegrams passing to and fro were examined without exception, whatever was found being reported to the bureau at once.

Certain progressive papers and periodicals they impounded. The *Xinhua Daily*, for instance, was not held back in its entirety every day when it went out to the provinces; they judged by whether the articles and news carried that day were too damaging to the Kuomintang. If it would pass muster they did not detain it, since to detain it daily would have brought a protest from the paper to the post office, and the post office would have been placed in a difficult position.

As for ordinary letters, and books and periodicals sent unregistered, any inspector could hold them back at will, and every man's shelves were crammed with the magazines, books and papers he liked to read. Besides reading them themselves they had plenty to give away to friends and relatives, and in my years in Chungking I was constantly given quantities of such things.

In 1944 Dai Li wanted to collect a hundred thousand thread-bound volumes, and the postal censorship post thereupon fastened the label of "prohibited publications" on thread-bound books passing through the post and impounded them for presentation to the Juntong. A good many people who had waited a long while without receiving such things enquired at the post office, were told they had been impounded, and fell to cursing, while the post office staff could only smile wryly. The post and telegraph offices could do nothing themselves with these operatives, who would listen to no reason, and there was constant friction between them, since the examinations wasted time and hampered their work. So far as I understood it, many of the post and telegraph office staff took no little trouble over this and did what they could, but could find no way of getting what they wanted.

From 1943 the Special Censorship Department added a telephone monitoring group in Chungking. To install equipment supplied by the American imperialists of SACO, I took an American imperialist operative who specialized in telephone tapping to look over the air-raid shelter housing the exchange at the Chungking telephone office. Having made his inspection, this expert offered Dai Li a number of suggestions, all of which were adopted. From then on, many telephones in Chungking that had been marked down for attention had somebody listening in at any hour.

The dozen or so members of this telephone tapping group were all women. Apart from several switchboard operators transferred from the telephone squad of the Juntong's general affairs department, the rest were women operatives who had graduated from the Juntong's special training classes. Dai Li thought highly of their work, and fearing they might grow discontented told me to take particular care of their living conditions and welfare; to those of weaker health I was to issue some of the various vitamin pills the American imperialists of SACO had given the Juntong. I gave them some instructions about their work and encouraged them to do their utmost and find material in the telephone calls.

After a period of this work Li Xiaobai, head of the Special Censorship Department, put a request to Dai Li for several recording machines to be added. In tapping a suspect call the important points on both sides had to be taken down properly in writing, and these operatives were not equal to it and could set down only the barest outline. Dai Li thereupon asked the American imperialists to help the Juntong with ten recorders. I was busy afterwards and do not know whether the recorders ever arrived; I know only that Miles agreed readily enough. In those days, whenever Dai Li asked for something needed for opposing the Communists and the people, the American imperialists could always meet the request quickly.

The postal and telegraphic censorship posts really did count for a great deal in the Juntong's secret-service work in Chungking, and among the mass of material they sent to the bureau daily a good many threads were found. Many of the cases the Juntong bureau passed to the overt secret-service organs in Chungking and elsewhere for re-checking, reconnaissance and surveillance arose out of that material. When the Inspectorate arrested a political prisoner, then even though no evidence at all had been found in the search and seizure, this material would be produced once he had been sent on to the Juntong and used as matter for his interrogation.

The post sometimes caused the Juntong bureau trouble too, and often made Dai Li fly into a rage. On one occasion a newly arrived operative opened a letter of Chen Lifu's out of curiosity. His hands being unpractised, Chen on receiving the letter saw the marks of its having been opened and read, and made representations to Dai Li. Dai investigated, found it to be so, and had to go and apologize to Chen and confine the inspector for several months.

The letters of these powerful men could not be tampered with lightly; and yet leaving them alone caused trouble as well, for a good many people at that time hated them, and having no other way of venting it would write an anonymous letter to relieve their feelings. Chiang Kai-shek's Attendants' Office regularly received letters of this kind. Others, such as T. V. Soong and H. H. Kung, were often enough abused in letters too. When that happened, besides requiring Dai Li to find out who had abused them and have the writer arrested and punished, their intimates would frequently complain to Dai Li about it, asking why such letters should be allowed through the post at all. Li Xiaobai grumbled to me more than once: "Great men are hard to serve. Open their letters and we are punished; leave their letters unread, and when they take abuse we are blamed for that too. One really does not know what to do."

The Special Censorship Department's other main line of work was counter-espionage. About 1940, after the departments of the Kuomintang government had all moved to Chungking, Dai Li proposed to Chiang Kai-shek that to prevent the enemy and the puppets and the Communist Party from planting men in these organs for intelligence work, a body of reliable people should be drawn from every unit and trained, so as to increase the Juntong's eyes and ears. Chiang Kai-shek praised it as an excellent method, and a secret order was issued in his name directing the head of every unit to select intimates whom they themselves considered reliable and hand them to Dai Li to be trained and organized. This counter-espionage organization was given the name of the training class for secret supervisory personnel, though in practice its main business was always guarding against the Communist Party.

This class was quartered in the Wanshou Palace of the Kiangsi guild hall at 82 Ganjiang Street. Two courses were run, of two or three hundred men each, most of them confidential secretaries or officers of section-head rank belonging to the heads of the central organs, the yuan, ministries and commissions. These heads had their own calculations and were glad enough to send men they themselves considered reliable to be trained, this being a great deal safer than having a secret-service body recruit people inside their offices in secret, so that most of them did not object to the arrangement. The class taught chiefly the rudiments of secret-service work, how to look out for and distinguish a suspect, and the various ways of keeping in touch with Juntong operatives.

The Special Censorship Department's counter-espionage section also kept many liaison stations in Chungking, with men detailed to keep in touch with those who had been trained. These people generally served without pay, the Juntong giving them no emoluments and a bonus only when their work produced results. Some who had been trained were not enthusiastic about the work, though a proportion showed themselves keen, being very willing to deal with the operatives and watching every person in their own units in earnest. But I heard from Dai Li's own mouth that he was very dissatisfied with their work. He held that the training was too short, only about a month, that these people had patrons of their own and had no need to depend on the Juntong, and that as they served without pay they treated some things as optional. Still, there were one or two such people in every unit at the time, and that was of some use to the Juntong's operations.

## The Unified Land and Water Transport Inspection Department

This department's predecessor was the supervision department of the Military Affairs Commission's Transport Control Bureau. The bureau was set up about 1939 with He Chengjun as its director and Zeng Yangfu as head of the supervision department, which was responsible for directing the supervision and inspection of transport in the areas under Chiang's control. From the outset Chiang Kai-shek wanted Zeng Yangfu to co-operate with the Juntong, so Dai Li held the deputy headship concurrently. The department's establishment was drafted by Zeng Yangfu, and its scale was accordingly very large. Zeng Yangfu's style was quite unlike Dai Li's: he had no interest in this headship and found a mere department head unsatisfying, so in drawing up the establishment he did it on the pattern of a bureau. Below the department were the general affairs and operations groups, below the groups sections, and below the sections sub-sections. The department had a chief secretary, with branch departments in the southeast and northwest and inspection posts in the various regions. Dai Li at that time wanted only to grasp the real power and the machinery, and being junior in standing he was never willing to be too conspicuous in drawing up any unit's establishment. When the Transport Control Bureau was abolished, Chiang Kai-shek judged that this one department should be kept, and it was converted into the Military Affairs Commission's Unified Land and Water Transport Inspection Department with Dai Li named as its head.

When Dai Li took charge of the department he transferred Zhang Yanyuan, head of the guard and inspection group of the Southwest Transport Office, to be deputy head and manage it for him. Zhang was one of Dai Li's "Ten-Man Team", and the head of the operations group was likewise one of the Ten, Hu Tianqiu. The rest of the staff remained as before. The head and deputy head of this department were both lieutenant-generals, the group heads and branch department heads major-generals, and the section heads and the heads of the various inspection posts generally full colonels, only a very few being lieutenant-colonels: the ranks throughout were very high. Here I shall speak only of the activities of the three inspection posts in Chungking and of one bus supervision office.

The inspection post commanding Yipinchang to the south of Chungking was then the most important gate on the overland road to Chungking from the southeast, from central China and from Yunnan and Kweichow. Its head, Wei Xian, held the post for several years. Not only could he issue proclamations of his own; he had a good many circulars printed and distributed as well. Whenever a motor vehicle came through he would as a matter of course hand over a circular, and besides the many regulations of the unified inspection department he added rules of his own which all vehicles passing were required to observe. Many public and private vehicles passing that place found it particularly troublesome. But Dai Li commended him to his face every time he came through, and so he grew more and more overbearing. When Dai Li was to pass that way in the ordinary course, Zhang Yanyuan, getting word of it, would always tell him secretly beforehand so that he could make ready. So while Dai Li's car was still a long way off he would be standing with a green flag in his hand, ordering the barrier raised for Dai's car to sweep through. Yet Dai always liked to stop and take the opportunity of an inspection. The man certainly had his methods: the post was always kept in perfect order and every inspector smartly turned out, which naturally pleased Dai.

Although everyone spoke ill of him, Dai Li's confidence in him never wavered and he was not moved for years, and there was a reason for it. Once Dai Li was hurrying back to Chungking from Kweiyang and passed this post by night, nobody having been told beforehand. Dai's driver, accustomed to charging through, would not stop for inspection as required, and when the inspectors held him up he flew into a rage. Wei Xian was on inspection duty; the night being dark he could not make out that Dai Li was in the car, and fearing the driver was passing himself off, he still ordered it stopped. The driver shouted, "You would dare stop the boss's car!" Wei answered him without ceremony: "I am inspecting on the boss's orders. He laid down the rule himself, and he of all people should keep it. Whoever it is, I shall inspect!" On hearing this Dai got down from the car at once, told him to proceed by the proper formalities, and commended him particularly. When the story got about, vehicles passing were more careful still, knowing this fellow was not to be provoked.

Smuggled goods coming from Kunming, the southeast, Kwangtung and Kwangsi were detected and confiscated by him here almost daily. When I was at the Juntong bureau I regularly sent men to the warehouse where this post stored confiscated goods to check over what the Juntong needed, and if a single formality had not been properly completed he would not let anything be taken away. I disliked him too and complained of him to Dai Li, but Dai answered me: "It is precisely this kind of cadre we need, who shows no favour and does not much understand the ways of the world. Whatever his other faults, that alone makes him worth having." For that reason he worked harder still. When vehicles were inspected here, at the least irregularity he would often have a whole load taken off, sometimes even the tyres removed, and a delay of half a day or a day was common. The hotels and eating-houses of the place did a brisk trade, and this had something to do with it. In his years there he brought down a chorus of complaint upon himself, and drivers and travellers alike called the place behind his back the "gate of hell."

The Qingmuguan inspection post, set up on the highway leading to the northwest, paid particular attention to young people going from Chungking to the northwest. Chiang Kai-shek was afraid of people making their way to Yenan and expressly told Dai Li to examine and detain them in earnest. Anyone bound for the northwest whose papers gave the least cause for doubt was turned back if he got off lightly and detained if he did not.

This post was under orders to make particular difficulties for the officers and men of the Eighth Route Army. Even when generals of divisional rank of the Eighteenth Army Group came through, they were often subjected to trouble made without cause, searched on any pretext, with the full repertoire of the operative-hooligan played out on them. Whenever Dai Li, Mao Renfeng and the rest heard a report of Communist personnel being obstructed they were delighted, and so the operatives on inspection duty grew more and more shameless. Once, when several generals coming from Yenan passed through Qingmuguan in heavy rain, the inspectors deliberately made them get down and stand in the rain while the search was carried out slowly, so that the men were soaked to the skin. When the big operatives heard of it they roared with laughter.

The inspection post near Chaotianmen was headed by Wang Xingguo. Its chief business was searching passengers and goods. Students and teachers travelling to Jiangjin, Beibei and other places were frequently held up by them. On one occasion a professor was carrying several works of Lu Xun's with red covers, and at the sight of them they wanted to take him away without hearing a word. Only after many bystanders had testified that these books had been published before the war and that the Kuomintang had permitted their circulation did they finally refrain from taking the man; the several red-covered books, however, were impounded.

In 1944 this department also put its hand into the Chungking municipal bus company, judging that this would be of great convenience to them, and set up a supervision office for the purpose. The head of the supervision office was Shi Zhenjiang, and its premises were inside the Lianglukou bus station. Besides using this body to supervise the bus company's employees, the operatives took advantage of working aboard the buses to carry on tailing and surveillance. At the same time the operatives held free passes and priority tickets on a permanent footing and enjoyed special treatment. As they never paid a fare in any case, these free passes were given to their friends and relatives to use. A privately owned bus company running from Chungking to Ciqikou was reduced to bitter complaint at the time, since of a busload setting out nearly half would be operatives travelling without paying and their dependants.

Carrying the signboard of the Military Affairs Commission, the Unified Transport Inspection Department "unified" under itself the inspection bodies previously set up by local organs and the military police; the military police were attached to each inspection post and took its orders, and the inspection machinery of the local military and police organs was compelled to disband. Yet where units connected with the Juntong were concerned, Dai Li asked for no unification at all. He held that an additional layer of inspection meant an additional show of strength and made things more thorough, so this unifying work went only to unify others and not itself. While these lawless inspection men made trouble for people everywhere, Dai Li regularly praised these departments' work at the bureau's business meetings, holding that in the control of transport the Juntong now had considerable strength and considerable resources.

Over these years the department did indeed detect a good deal of contraband and prohibited goods and confiscated a great deal; but no small quantity was returned again by Dai Li's order. Great merchants with connections in Chiang Kai-shek's Attendants' Office would get somebody there to put in a word with him, or work through Song Meiling, or plead with him through Du Yuesheng and others, and however large the matter it could be settled. There was in any case no superior above him to make trouble for him, and he disposed of things as he pleased. Once the Juntong bureau needed a quantity of parts for older motor vehicles, and I sent men to sort out a consignment of confiscated goods from this department's warehouse and had it brought back. Supposing the goods already forfeit, I put some of them to use; and then somebody got an intimate of T. V. Soong's to intercede with Dai Li, and he actually agreed to return them. The inspection department sent to reclaim them from me, and being unable to produce them I explained to Dai that the bureau had used them. In the ordinary way he was always pleased when I schemed to get things in from wherever I could, and often said of me that I was like a good steward; this time he flew into a rage and insisted I hand them back, and that whatever had been used should be bought and returned. The reason was simply that Soong's weight was behind it.

The largest case the inspection department handled openly in those years was the smuggling case of Lin Shiliang, head of the transport department of the Central Trust. Lin was an intimate of H. H. Kung's, and had he not presumed on his backing and refused to defer, but instead had a word put to Dai Li beforehand through the right people, the affair would never have ended as it did. It was said outside that Dai Li insisted on Lin Shiliang's execution for the sake of the reward on that consignment, which is quite untrue. The Juntong of those days thought nothing at all of a reward of that size. The real point was that Lin Shiliang was contemptuous of people and supposed that with H. H. Kung behind him he need fear nobody. Once the matter had been forced into the open, to ask a man as jealous of his face as Dai Li to give way was something he would not do on any terms. Governing so many subordinates, he could not have justified himself to them if he had been told openly not to proceed against Lin; that is why he held out to the end. Had contact been made with him early, he would never have been so genuinely impartial.

## The Anti-Japanese Traitor-Elimination Corps

The Anti-Japanese Traitor-Elimination Corps, called the Anti-Traitor Corps for short within the Juntong, was a peripheral secret-service organization of the Juntong in the colleges. It began to be organized after the Mukden Incident in the universities and colleges of Tientsin, Peiping and elsewhere. Its heads were Zeng Ze and then Zhu Zongliang. Its members were chiefly college students, and besides the sons and daughters of great bureaucrats, great landlords and capitalists there were a good many children of notorious traitors among them.

While this corps was operating in north China it did some work in the way of struggle against the enemy and the puppets and in intelligence. After the Marco Polo Bridge Incident a large part of its members went south under Zhu Zongliang and made their way by stages to Szechwan, whereupon its character changed entirely and they became the Juntong's professional student operatives in the various schools. The Anti-Traitor Corps became a unit directly under Juntong control whose sole business was secret-service work among students. Its offices at that time were on the floor below the Juntong training department's office in the Wanshou Palace at 82 Ganjiang Street, where six or seven people worked. Dai Li thought highly of the corps and regularly sent for Zhu Zongliang to give him instructions in person and learn how matters stood.

In the autumn of 1942 I went with Dai Li to Chengtu, and Zhu Zongliang went along too. Dai Li set aside a great deal of time, having Zhu gather the Anti-Traitor Corps students in Chengtu every evening and receiving them in batches to learn how matters stood in the various schools, and dining and dancing with them besides.

Not every member of the Anti-Traitor Corps joined the Juntong's secret-service organization; the Juntong merely used these people to carry on secret-service work in the schools. Dai Li said quite frankly that the "traitor" formerly to be rooted out had been the traitor to China, whereas the "traitor" now to be rooted out was the traitor within, a term the reactionaries used to insult the Communist Party, so that the responsibility was greater and the task harder to accomplish. What they did in the schools at that time was partly intelligence and partly wrecking. They drew students in under the cloak of resisting Japan and saving the country, and contrived to infiltrate progressive bodies of every kind. Against student associations in the schools that were genuinely patriotic and anti-Chiang they struck covertly, and where necessary called in the Juntong's overt secret-service organs to persecute them. Where a member's family was in financial difficulty the Juntong would give him a little help. The others generally drew no fixed emoluments, but the Juntong would meet their difficulties for them when they had any.

Besides the Anti-Traitor Corps, the Juntong sent many operatives directly into the various colleges. I remember that among the schools operatives were planted in were Fudan University, the drama college (first at Jiangjin, later moved to Beibei), the music college and the accountancy college, and there were many others I can no longer recall. Every summer vacation Dai Li called these people back and formed a summer training class to put them through a course. Since these professional students had always passed themselves off as progressives in their schools, some of the progressive books and periodicals the Juntong took at the postal censorship post were given to them to read when necessary, so that they could pick up a few of the new progressive terms. People who did not know what they were were often taken in by their progressive vocabulary. But Dai Li also knew that young people readily take up Communist ideas, and that a moment's carelessness could turn the pretence into the real thing, and that was why he used the summer vacation to put them through a course.

Each intake of professional students came to more than a hundred. These people carried on secret-service work in their several schools without coming under the Anti-Traitor Corps' direction and without any lateral contact with the corps' students; this was the Juntong's two-tier organization in the schools at that time. Even into the Army University and the Central Training Corps, which the Kuomintang government controlled very closely, the Juntong had to send men to operate. The Army University investigation group, for instance, existed solely to do secret-service work there. Its head, Zou Lufu, regularly reported the words and conduct of the university's teachers and students to the Juntong, and recruited cadres for the Juntong in the school besides: not a few Juntong men who were Army University graduates were brought in that way.

The Central Training Corps put central and local cadres through courses by rotation, and its intake was extremely mixed. Chiang Kai-shek used the opportunity to strengthen the indoctrination of his own inner-circle cadres, and it was also a means of winning over local cadres and the miscellaneous forces, so that men of every description were there. When the corps was first set up it was decided that the guard group should be handed to the Juntong, with Dai Li himself as its head at first and Li Jiajie and Zhang Ye succeeding him later. The group had more than thirty operatives, who kept covert watch on the words and conduct of those under training. Chiang Kai-shek set great store by the intelligence from this quarter, and the operatives' confidential reports became material for his assessment of cadres, so that what a man said during his course could affect his prospects for good or ill.

Chiang Kai-shek and the chief figures of the Four Great Families attended the Central Training Corps at every intake. They understood perfectly well that they had too many enemies at that time, and for their own safety had to rely on operatives to protect them. Whenever Chiang Kai-shek, T. V. Soong, H. H. Kung and the rest went to speak, the operatives were packed close about them on every side, guarding at every moment against a possible move. Below the platform ran a row of small windows through which the operatives looked out and could see clearly what every member of the audience did. These men gripped loaded pistols, ready at any instant to fire on anyone attempting harm to their masters. The man on the platform might speak at his ease, but below the platform they were in the greatest tension.

## The Guard and Inspection Department of the Ordnance Administration

To suppress the workers in the arsenals, the Juntong set up this guard and inspection department about 1940, with Zhang Shi and then Liao Zongze as its head and Yang Wei as commander of the guard corps. Before that each arsenal had its own guard battalion or guard squad, and only after the war against Japan began did the Juntong put its hand directly on this work. A great many arsenals had by then been moved from various places to Szechwan and Chungking, and Chiang Kai-shek was uneasy at having these workers, numbering in the tens of thousands, suddenly concentrated in Chungking. Being on fairly good personal terms with Zhang Shi I often went to his house, and heard him speak from time to time about this department's affairs.

The Kuomintang's Ordnance Administration was at that time Yu Dawei's domain, and Yu, presuming on Chiang Kai-shek's particular confidence in him, did not want anyone else putting a hand inside his sphere. While the arsenals had been scattered about the country the question had not been conspicuous, but with about half of them concentrated in the auxiliary capital during the war the case was altered. Chiang Kai-shek pointed out to Yu Dawei that if it were not carefully watched, and the Communists once penetrated and became active there, it would be impossible to regain control; and to guard against it in good time he told him to co-operate with Dai Li, handing over the work against the Communists to the Juntong while Yu remained responsible only for administration, technical matters and production. After Chiang Kai-shek had explained, Yu had no choice but to consent to letting the Juntong in. In the regulations for this department that he and Dai Li agreed on, it was stated openly that its purpose was to prevent the Communist Party from infiltrating the arsenals to build an organization and carry on activity.

Dai Li went about this task Chiang Kai-shek had handed him with unusual care. He picked Zhang Shi, formerly chief clerk of the Special Services Department, the Juntong's predecessor, to head it. He thought it over repeatedly, considering that Zhang, as a Communist renegade who had studied in the Soviet Union, had always had experience of dealing with the Communist Party, and so had Zhang Shi take part in drafting the organizational regulations as well. Zhang put forward a number of points at this stage which both Dai and Yu thought important. At first Dai and Yu had thought only of forming guard and inspection groups in each arsenal to supervise; Zhang, who understood something of how the Communists worked, argued that the operatives must be able to go right into the workshops and inspect them regularly, which would both prevent wrecking by the workers and give a means of getting close to them, so that they could be bought and watched. But there were far too few Juntong operatives who understood the technical side, and it was Zhang Shi again who found the way round it: going deep into the workshops under the name of safety inspection required no particular technical knowledge. This proposal duly won the approval of both Dai and Yu, and so a safety section was set up under the department and a safety sub-section in each arsenal's guard and inspection group, the object being to prevent the workers using any opportunity to wreck, while also affording direct dealings with them, which answered far better than watching from one side.

Yu Dawei's chief fear at the time was that the Juntong would enquire into the accounts of the Ordnance Administration and the arsenals. It was said that the Quartermaster Administration did not audit these accounts at all and that he settled them directly with Chiang Kai-shek. Dai Li, knowing what was on his mind, made clear that the operatives posted to the Ordnance Administration would take their orders from him and would not enquire into other matters, and so the department was set up quite smoothly. Below it were four sections: general affairs, inspection, safety and judicial. To prevent strikes and other progressive activity among the arsenal workers, the reactionaries at that time placed all workers on the footing of serving soldiers, forbade them to organize unions and forbade strikes. Anyone who disobeyed orders was "dealt with under military law", which meant prison if he got off lightly and shooting if he did not; and once military law had given judgment there was no appeal and no defence.

To bring the guard battalions and squads of the various arsenals under one direction, a guard corps headquarters was set up with the deputy head of the department, Yang Wei, as its commander. Unified direction though it was called, Dai Li in fact made some concessions here: the existing commanders of the arsenals' guard battalions were, without special reason, essentially left in place. Most of these men were fairly close confidants of the arsenal directors themselves, and to replace them at once would readily have caused resentment and hindered the work of setting up guard-inspection groups in the factories. So this corps headquarters existed in name only, which Yang Wei greatly resented. Dai urged Yang Wei to go slowly, telling him that there was no need to replace these battalion commanders and that the better course was to take them into the Juntong organization and draw them over one by one, which would serve the Juntong's work far better.

Dai Li knew how highly Chiang Kai-shek favoured Yu Dawei, and repeatedly enjoined Zhang Shi to contrive to keep on good terms with Yu and not to trespass on his authority. On one occasion Chen Changxi, a member of an arsenal's guard and inspection group, reported that a senior employee of that factory was suspected of being a Communist, and Dai Li, without establishing the facts, reported it to Chiang Kai-shek and had the man detained. When Yu Dawei afterwards made representations, Dai hurriedly released the man and had the informant Chen Changxi shot, by way of pleasing Yu Dawei. So there was never any friction between the two of them, and Yu did not find these operatives objectionable either.

The department's operatives were mostly graduates of the Juntong's various operative training classes, and a good many of them had been trained by Zhang Guotao, coming out of the special political training class devoted to dealing with the Communist Party. These students, whom Dai Li treated as treasures, could not be sent to work in the Communist liberated areas as Zhang Guotao's calculations had intended, so Dai Li settled for the next best thing and posted them to this department to work against the Communists.

Over several years the department arrested more than a hundred progressive workers and suspects. The reason for arrest was generally that a man had read the *Xinhua Daily*, or had cursed Chiang Kai-shek and the National Government behind their backs; but no Communist organization was ever found. Dai Li boasted that this was owing to the thoroughness of the operatives' precautions, and reported openly at the memorial-week assemblies that the department had excellent "results".

Many Juntong operatives at that time hoped to be transferred to this department, since the pay and conditions were good and the work not pressing; and as many arsenal directors understood the operatives' cast of mind and gave them a small allowance every month under one head or another, a special allowance or extra office expenses, both sides got on without trouble. Gong Xianfang, head of Juntong personnel, and Bao Zhihong, head of military intelligence, and other big operatives all sponged lodging in the staff quarters of the Egongyan arsenal.

I have a brother, Shen Yulong, who was head of a guard and inspection group at an arsenal on the south bank. I went to see him there several times and learned something of how the operatives controlled the arsenal workers. Their method was to ring the whole factory area about, setting up numerous inspection pickets on the main thoroughfares and posts everywhere, everyone entering or leaving the area having to produce papers. Workers who were livelier than most they had watched covertly, while buying over some of the more backward workers to serve as their informers, and eavesdropping on the workers' conversations whenever they gathered. The workers found the inspectors thoroughly objectionable and hateful and never wanted to have dealings with them. On one occasion an inspector had arrested a worker and had not yet sent him away when the other workers found out, surrounded the inspection post and insisted that he be released. Some of the factory's officers came out to reason with them and the workers would not listen, and in the end there was nothing for it but to release the man. He came out cursing loudly all the while, and they could do nothing about it. When they wanted to arrest anyone after that they could only move covertly under cover of night, and get him away in secret before he was noticed. Between the inspection department's men and the workers the attitude on both sides was always one of enmity.

## The Juntong Bureau's Headquarters and Its Activities in Chungking

The Juntong bureau's headquarters was the base from which the secret-service bodies spread densely over the areas under Chiang's control, the occupied areas, parts of the liberated areas and abroad were directed in their work. I intend to write of its activities as a whole separately. Here I shall speak only briefly of the various criminal activities it carried out directly within the one area of Chungking.

The Juntong did its harm to the revolution and its suppression of the people by pistols and prisons. During the war the American imperialists supplied weapons of every kind beyond counting, so that its supply was inexhaustible. Besides Baigongguan and Zhazidong inside SACO, the detention houses where the Juntong confined prisoners included the detention house of the operatives' corps at Wanglongmen, and those of the Inspectorate, the detective brigade, the Unified Transport Inspection Department, the military law enforcement office of the National General Mobilization Council, the anti-smuggling administration, the Ordnance Administration's inspection department, and the Ministry of the Interior's police corps. On a casual count there were as many as ten detention houses of that size. Smaller ones were more numerous still: the police sub-bureaus the Juntong controlled, the guard and inspection groups of the various arsenals, and the small detention house at the Honglu factory inside SACO where senior political prisoners were held.

The Juntong held the privilege of seizing, confining and killing at will, and guarded at the same time against others moving against it, so that the precautions around Juntong premises were particularly close. While the Juntong operative Xu Zhongqi was chief of the Chungking municipal police, the Juntong could of course control and work the whole force. Under Tang Yi it could control only part; but there were several units and sub-bureaus which the Juntong meant to control whoever was chief. The police bureau's judicial section and detective brigade it would not let go of. The head of the Sixth Sub-bureau at Luojiawan, where the Juntong bureau's headquarters stood, the head of the Eighth Sub-bureau at Xiaolongkan and Hualongqiao, and the heads and post commanders at Ciqikou, Tongjiaqiao and the site of SACO, all had to be under Juntong control. The head of the Sixth Sub-bureau, Du Chun, had to work for the Juntong most of the time.

The Juntong bureau took the registration of households near its headquarters in deadly earnest. Plain-clothes operatives of the operatives' corps seconded to serve at headquarters would regularly go with the household registration police to check them over, and any resident who gave the least cause for suspicion was closely interrogated; at the smallest discrepancy he was taken away at once, for it needed only some casual charge of being "suspected of spying on the secrets of bureau headquarters" to have him locked up.

Liu Tong, head of the Eighth Sub-bureau, was the Juntong's main force in watching the Communist Party office and the *Xinhua Daily* at Hongyan Village. Although the Juntong had five or six overt and secret establishments posted in that district, none was as convenient as the sub-bureau for the ground itself; using it for this criminal work served better than any other unit, and so that sub-bureau simply had to be kept in hand.

The police post at Tongjiaqiao in Ciqikou had under it the area of SACO and the Juntong's country offices, where several of Dai Li's country villas stood. This post was simply taken over entire by the plain-clothes operatives of the operatives' corps: the post commander, the police officers and the constables were operatives to a man.

The armed operatives guarding this district generally amounted to about two battalions, and even that Dai Li thought insufficient. He moved the Ministry of the Interior's police corps, which the Juntong controlled, to be stationed about Geleshan and Jingangpo behind SACO, keeping two battalions there permanently, commanding from the heights the highway leading to Chungking and covering the American and Chiang secret-service base below Geleshan. The corps commander was Ren Jianpeng, whom Dai Li often sent for to talk with. The operative police force he led also furnished the gate guards for the various foreign embassies and consulates in Chungking and the guards for the Kuomintang's civil organs, which was the best of covers for secret-service work.

Dai Li often boasted of how close the guard was in this district; but it may as well be taken for the fear of this murderous demon himself. For all the privileges the Juntong held and the tens of thousands of operatives and armed operatives it had, engaged all day in criminal activity against the Communists and the people, it was at the same time the thing most afraid of the Communist Party and of the people. The operatives in Chungking then were lawless and overbearing and constantly brought Dai Li trouble, and his whole apparatus of harsh "house law" could not restrain them, so he had to keep issuing warnings to them at the memorial-week assemblies. In a threatening tone he would say: "You carry on outside anyhow, and if the Communist Party gets hold of the evidence and publishes it in the *Xinhua Daily*, then it is your own bad luck, and I shall not concern myself whether you live or die." Hearing this the operatives were genuinely afraid, for they knew that once the *Xinhua Daily* exposed their crimes publicly Dai Li could not shield them and they would face an open trial. Dai Li frightening them with such words was often far more effective than his stamping and cursing.

On one occasion a secretary of the Eighth Route Army office was shot dead aboard a bus on his way into the city from Xiaolongkan. When Dai Li had the news his face went livid with anxiety and he told me to establish at once whether it had been done by Juntong operatives, and if so to seize whoever it was without exception and bring him to the Juntong bureau to be dealt with. He said over and over, "The Communist Party will certainly not let this pass. If our men did it, there is no telling how much trouble it will add." I enquired at once of several of the secret-service bodies, and all said they had not done it.

What had happened was this. A company of new recruits was marching near Xiaolongkan and had scattered to rest lying along the road, when a car belonging to the Communist Party office ran over one recruit's foot. The driver did not notice and drove on, whereupon the squad leader escorting the recruits raised his rifle and fired a shot at the car. The squad leader did not know either that this shot had killed a man inside; after the rest he marched on and got as far as Bishan before the Chungking security authorities sent men to bring him back. It was afterwards established that it had been unintentional, and Zhou Enlai did not require the squad leader's life for the killing. Only when the whole thing had been cleared up did Dai Li breathe again.

When Chairman Mao went to Chungking, Dai Li ordered the special guard group and the Inspectorate to give him close covert protection. This was for no other reason than that Dai Li was afraid some ruffian might seize the chance to make an attempt on Chairman Mao, and that if anything happened the reckoning would fall on the Juntong. So far from daring to do Chairman Mao any harm, Dai Li was in dread of anyone else acting wildly. The special guard group, whose sole business was the safety of Chiang Kai-shek, had also to take on the duty of protecting Chairman Mao covertly. One operative on guard at Dai Li's house said, "Why not take this chance and finish Chairman Mao off? A few years in prison and you would have earned great merit." When Dai Li heard of it he sent for the man at once and cursed him for a fool, took his pistol away and ordered him back to the operatives' corps, for fear he might lose his head and cause some great disaster.

In the so-called high-level intelligence and secret-service work the Juntong carried on in Chungking, Dai Li himself was often out in the field. Chiang Kai-shek wanted the Szechwan warlords won over, so Dai regularly found time to call on many Szechwanese generals, and often invited them home to dinner to cultivate their goodwill. Among those he made direct use of were Li Weiguo, a department director in the Ministry of Foreign Affairs; Wang Pengsheng, head of the International Affairs Research Institute; Lin Kesheng, director of the Army Medical Administration; Bei Zuyi of the banking world; and many of his good friends such as Wu Renshuo and He Shili. For high-level intelligence within the Kuomintang government, a good many of the Juntong bureau's design committee members did this kind of work too. Yu Zuobai, for instance, who had once been acting governor of Kwangsi and was closely connected with many anti-Chiang figures in Kwangtung and Kwangsi, and Wang Gupan, formerly commissioner of the capital police, were both willing to exert themselves a little for the Juntong. Du Yuesheng was living in Chungking at that time and his help to Dai Li was by no means small: besides handling the secret-society side for him, he did a good deal to put Dai Li in the way of useful connections and information.

The range of people Dai Li worked to do secret-service business for him was very wide. Some of it succeeded, and a good deal of it disappointed him or even made him indignant. A former subordinate of General Zhang Xueliang's, one Chen, was much in Dai's favour and held important posts in the Juntong throughout. But the former subordinates General Zhang introduced to him did not all serve him. Wang Huayi, once president of Northeastern University, and Wu Sai, who had been a brigade commander, had very little appetite for secret-service work, and even Dai Li's hope that they would supply intelligence on the Northeasterners in Chungking came to nothing. Dai often said behind their backs, "Some people take our money and do nothing for us, and some take not the least favour from us and help us with extraordinary energy." Of the Juntong's several hundred design committee members, nearly half took money and did no work for the Juntong. Dai Li nevertheless kept the whole set of them, hoping that one day he would use one of them once and recoup it all.

Among the people the Juntong worked directly there were individuals Dai Li thought thoroughly reliable who turned out contrary to his expectations, and his having them confined in a rage was common enough. The case I know best is Shen Xifeng. This man had joined the Communist Party while a student in the fourth Whampoa class, and during the war he kept the Yuxin Hotel at Xiaoliangzi in Chungking. He had long had dealings with progressive figures, and a good many young people bound for Yenan had assembled at his hotel. The premises the Eighth Route Army first used for its office on Jifang Street in Chungking were rented through his introduction. Not a few Juntong operatives were his classmates of the same intake, and supposing that he would have some means of getting Communist intelligence for the Juntong, they drew him in and made him a courier directly attached to the Yu special district. In the event he worked solely for the Juntong on social and economic intelligence. The Juntong hinted to him again and again, hoping he would put his whole strength into work on the Communist Party. To make him content and willing they even made him deputy commander of the detective brigade. After waiting two or three years and still not having what it wanted, the Juntong had Dai Li order him confined at the Inspectorate. I was working at the Inspectorate then, and was ordered to investigate whether he had used his Juntong connections to do any work for the Communist Party. Several months of investigation produced no evidence of it whatever, and he had to be released. The Juntong then set him to economic intelligence while watching his movements covertly, and never at any time discovered any dealings between him and the Communists. The Juntong did a good deal of business at a loss like that.

Whenever the People's Political Council was sitting, it was the Juntong's busiest and tensest time. The head of the party and government intelligence department would generally lead a temporary body formed for the work of each session. Chiang Kai-shek always wanted to know early what proposals every non-Kuomintang councillor would put before the session, what he was doing, and what he said outside it.

There was no shortage of people doing that work, but Dai Li kept a particularly tight grip on it, often saying that this was the critical moment for sharing the "Leader's" labours and cares. He always went out himself, getting in touch first with some of the council's officers so that operatives could be planted in the assembly's temporary body to do a little work there, and at the same time inviting the councillors connected with the Juntong to confer with him. Zhang Guotao came forward in the character of a Kiangsi provincial councillor to make connections for the Juntong. The Juntong had to report the intelligence obtained to Chiang Kai-shek in detail day by day, and the body was not dissolved until the session ended.

Before and after each session of the council, Dai Li always had me make several dozen serviceable motor cars ready for occasional use. The special reconnaissance group at 1 Shuixiangzi, directed by the Juntong itself, was particularly active too. Dai Li posted the youngest and prettiest of the Juntong's women operatives to this group as members: Ge Tianxuan, Chen Yunna, Chen Wen, Ruan Xiaolan, Liu Lingyan. He gave the group a large sum in special operating expenses as well, hoping to attain his object by money and beautiful women and the rest of it.

In controlling aviation at Chungking the Juntong already had its Chungking air censorship post, the Juntong operative Wang Yunsu was manager of the Shanhuba airfield, and the operatives Jian Pu and Xu Zhilin headed the air force political department and the air force investigation office respectively; yet Dai Li still thought this not close enough, and in 1943 added an air censorship section to the party and government intelligence department under Wang Fangnan, to control air tickets directly. Dai Li knew the full list of passengers flying in and out each day on the day itself, and the movements of people Chiang Kai-shek took an interest in had to be reported to him by Dai Li. In the same way Chungking's economic situation and the fluctuation of prices had to be reported to Chiang Kai-shek daily.

While I was in Chungking I heard a good many stories about Chiang Kai-shek's ignorance of prices. It was said that he once bought a felt hat at a hat shop in Lianglukou where the proprietor quoted him a price close to the pre-war figure, and that after Chiang Kai-shek had gone off with it well pleased, the municipal government promptly commended the shopkeeper and made his loss good. On another occasion Gu Zhenggang, the Minister of Social Affairs, took Chiang Kai-shek to dine at the Guanshengyuan and had the price board written up close to pre-war figures on purpose. Chiang Kai-shek looked at the bill of fare and nodded repeatedly: "Everyone complains that prices are soaring, when in fact the rise is quite limited." Stories of this kind were simply propaganda for Chiang Kai-shek, meant to show that it was not that he did not care about the people's livelihood but that he had been deceived by his subordinates, and to make people believe he was a good man. In fact the daily movement of prices and the ratios against pre-war figures were not only reported by the Juntong in great detail; Chiang Kai-shek saw them perfectly clearly.

The Juntong did its utmost to protect the safety of the Four Great Families, and above all Chiang Kai-shek's, every Juntong operative being required to discharge his duty of guarding the "Leader". Besides the escort guard group Dai Li posted to the Attendants' Office, which followed him all day, there was a special guard group of nearly two hundred graduates of the Central Military Academy. These men, picked and trained by Dai Li in person and led by Zhu Jinhua, were spread thickly along the roads Chiang Kai-shek travelled each day, on patrol. When Chiang Kai-shek went out he always looked to see whether special guards holding the *Central Daily News* in their left hands were posted along both sides of the road to protect him.

A plain-clothes guard platoon sent by the Juntong was stationed at T. V. Soong's house, a dozen or so operatives keeping the brother-in-law of the ruling house safe. H. H. Kung's household had a guard of its own, but Dai Li, to please him, still had to send operatives to keep watch nearby whenever a banquet or a dance was held at the Kung residence at Fanzhuang on Guofu Road, or at the Kung family's villa at Nanwenquan. When H. H. Kung held a great birthday banquet at the Broadcasting Building, Dai Li stood at the door as a greeter himself and sent a large body of operatives to protect the dignitaries who came to offer congratulations. As for the Chen brothers, though they had Zhongtong operatives to guard them, the Juntong always had men on hand to assist.

During the war against Japan the Juntong operatives in Chungking did every evil thing there was, more than can be written down. What I have learned is only a part of it, and only some of the surface of things. If all the crimes the Juntong operatives committed in Chungking at that time were gathered together, they would be more shocking still. The facts and dates given here rest on my own memory and errors are unavoidable; I would ask those who know the circumstances of that time to correct me.
