## The Juntong's Criminal Activities in Shanghai before the War

### Organization and Personnel

### Criminal Activities of Various Kinds

### Secret Killings

In the years before the war against Japan the Juntong always attached particular importance to work in the Shanghai area. The chief reason was that the Central Committee of the Chinese Communist Party had been based in Shanghai, directing the Party organization across the country in years of revolutionary struggle from there; the second was that the democratic parties, the progressive societies and figures opposed to Chiang Kai-shek used the foreign concessions in Shanghai as cover for anti-Chiang activity of every kind. When the Renaissance Society's Special Services Department — the Juntong's predecessor — was set up, the two great tasks Chiang Kai-shek gave it were to oppose the Communists and to clear away those who did not belong to his own faction, so as to make his dictatorship secure.

I left school and joined the Juntong in 1932, in Shanghai itself, where I served in turn as courier of the Shanghai district, head of an intelligence group and assistant secretary, and also held the posts of inspector and operations group head with the detective corps of the Songhu Garrison Command, until the withdrawal from Shanghai after the war began in 1937. So I know a good deal about the Juntong's organization and personnel in Shanghai in those years and about its criminal activities of every kind, and there is much in them I took part in myself. It was during this period that the Juntong's work in Shanghai was at its most savage and brutal, and it was because of the "results" he obtained in Shanghai in those years that Dai Li rose higher and higher in Chiang Kai-shek's favour.

Before the founding of the Renaissance Society's Special Services Department on 1 April 1932 — known to outsiders as the Special Services Department of the Military Affairs Commission — Dai Li had long since posted Weng Guanghui, Chen Zhiqiang, Wang Changyu and others to Shanghai to work there, and went to Shanghai constantly himself, though there was as yet no formal organization with a name. Only after 1932 was a Shanghai district set up, with Weng Guanghui as district chief. Weng was a graduate of the third Whampoa class, a Zhejiang man who had spent years in the navy. When he was district chief the organization was very simple: one secretary, one clerk, one general-duties man and one courier, and only three groups below them. The head of the First Group was Chen Zhiqiang, a Shanghai man, third Whampoa class, who had always worked among the gangs and the riff-raff of the city; the head of the Second Group was Wang Changyu, a Wenchang man from Qiongzhou in Guangdong, fifth Whampoa class; the head of the Third Group was Xu Zhaojun, a Sichuanese, third Whampoa class. At that time the agents in Shanghai, headquarters and field together, came to no more than thirty-odd, and their work was generally confined to intelligence. Chiang Kai-shek hoped these agents would get inside the Communist organization in Shanghai; in the event, they did not learn so much as a word about the Party Central Committee's move from Shanghai to the Jiangxi soviet area.

For years afterwards, in talking to his agents in Shanghai, Dai Li would freely admit that this was the Juntong's greatest failure. Weng Guanghui had not been district chief long when, meaning to send an important item of intelligence about the Communists direct to Chiang Kai-shek rather than through Dai Li, he was found out by Dai and detained, and removed from his post. He was replaced by Yu Lexing, an instructor at the Hangchow and Nanking special training classes, and the Shanghai district was enlarged into an East China district, with a Fourth Group and an operations group added. The whole staff of the Fourth Group were agents just out of the A course of the Hangchow special class; its head was Zhang Renyou, a Wenzhou man from Zhejiang, and its members were Cheng Linjiang, Ni Yongchao, Fang Yuanzhu, Ying Zhichun, Ying Huaibao, Shi Renchong and some ten others. The operations group was headed by Zhao Lijun with Wang Kequan as his deputy, and had Li Ada, Guo Decheng, Shi Tizhi and a dozen more as members, most of them habitual criminals who had killed and robbed.

This group answered to two masters: assassination work was directed by Dai Li and the head office in Nanking, while kidnapping and secret arrests in the Shanghai area came under the district chief; personnel and money likewise came direct from Nanking. In the autumn of 1932, when Yu Lexing was district chief, he went under the name of Jin Mingsan, his alias at the Special Services Department in Nanking being Yang Liuqing. Yu was a native of Liling in Hunan, a work-and-study student in France who had afterwards gone to the Soviet Union to study political security work, and Dai Li had high hopes of him. The East China district was in principle to direct the work of Shanghai, Zhejiang, Fujian and the Xuzhou-Haizhou area, but in fact none of the other units would accept the district's direction except the few groups in Shanghai, so the East China district was a district in name only, and before long it was changed back into the Shanghai special district, responsible for the Shanghai area alone. The district organization was still very simple: when there was a great deal of intelligence coming in, the district secretary Deng Zhiying and I had to help the clerk Chen Bage copy out the invisible-ink letters as well as the district chief.

The chemical fluid used at first was very simple, and only later became a little more elaborate; from 1934, when Nanking began sending a courier daily to collect the intelligence, copying in invisible ink and posting it stopped altogether. The principal members of the four intelligence groups at that time were all core agents, and their task was to find any means they could of getting inside Communist organizations and the organizations of the democratic parties, and of keeping watch on those anti-Chiang figures Chiang Kai-shek himself had marked down as "to be kept under surveillance." So the intelligence groups did not in fact produce a great deal of intelligence, and the main source was the "directly attached correspondents" the district ran itself. Most of these had been recommended to Dai Li or to the district chief from one quarter or another; some were still on trial and others had become correspondents proper. There were more than thirty such directly attached correspondents, all of them with settled public occupations and wide connections. Their secret-service work was at first a sideline and only gradually became their profession.

They were generally handled by the district chief, the secretary and the courier, and the great majority through the courier; those working inside the democratic parties or passing themselves off as progressives especially dreaded being seen to have dealings with agents, so contact with them was made in great secrecy. For its first few years the Shanghai special district had only a post-office box and no premises for meetings. Contact was generally made once a week, and anything special was written and posted to the box; when I was courier I had to go round several places every day to collect intelligence. When a directly attached correspondent had to meet the district chief in person, both sides made some preparation for it. The district chief was afraid they might prove unreliable and sell him, so when a meeting was arranged I would often pass on the arrangements in full and then take the man at short notice to a hotel room or to a park teahouse to wait for the chief.

Sometimes the place and time were changed more than once; sometimes, after a few words, a taxi would be called and the two would go somewhere else for a long talk. It was all made very mysterious. Some of the correspondents were themselves in a state of dread, afraid that if a meeting went badly they would be taken off to Nanking, so neither side wanted to meet often. Some of them saw the district chief only once in six months. Where the head office in Nanking thought a man played a particularly important part, Liang Ganqiao, Tang Zong, Zhang Shi and others who had served as secretary-general of the head office would come up from Nanking to talk to him, and on those occasions the mystery was thicker still. I not only had to get the two sides to their meeting on time but had to act as a temporary bodyguard as well, to prevent anything going wrong.

With correspondents the district reckoned fairly reliable there was none of the elaborate mystification used for men with party connections: besides the courier's going to them directly as a matter of routine, the district chief and secretary would often go and deal with them in person — though they never dared tell them where the district office was, or its telephone number.

Among the correspondents whose names and locations I still remember is Tao Yishan, a military instructor at the Wusong merchant marine school, who used the alias Tao Shiguan. He had been recommended to Dai Li by Pan Youqiang; being a Central Military Academy man with a public position, there was no risk in him, and he was made responsible for social intelligence in the Wusong area and for intelligence from the several schools there. There was another correspondent whose work was entirely among the students of Fudan University, named Chen Shaozong, also a Whampoa man, who had taught military subjects at Fudan. After he left that post Dai Li had him open the Jiabin Restaurant just outside the back gate of Fudan. He used his wife Wu Shubin's name as his alias. Because the restaurant drew a subsidy from the secret service its capital was larger than such places usually have. Chen knew a great many students and was willing to let them run up accounts, so business was brisk. The restaurant had two small rooms which, besides being used by students for dinners and parties, were lent to them for small meetings and discussions. Chen would then make bringing them tea his excuse for sending his daughter, a girl of a dozen or so, in and out to listen to what they said. When the students told him beforehand not to come in, that in itself said that something secret was under discussion, which made a still better lead to follow. He drew in a good many students, planting them in the various organizations within the university, and so produced a fair amount of intelligence.

In the end the whole of the Jiangwan area was put in his charge, and he even developed students at the nearby women's physical education college and the law and political science university to work for him. Whenever I went there I would buy meal tickets, slipping the district's instructions to him in among the banknotes I handed to his wife at the counter, and take back the intelligence he had written under cover of getting my change. Jinan University at Zhenru, which gave the most trouble of any at that time, had agents who ran a coffee shop nearby as cover, and a number of overseas Chinese students back from the South Seas were recruited as agents.

Although these students were still at their books, they already carried pistols about and were forever making trouble with them, pulling them out to threaten people. Later one of Yang Hu's concubines introduced a dozen of the overseas Chinese students of that university, Su Zhentong and Liu Guoqing among them, to Dai Li, and they were sent to the special training class at the Hangchow police academy and became core men of the Juntong. There were also liaison men whom various regional warlords had posted in Shanghai, and they too did work for the Juntong. In the cultural world, Cui Wanqiu, who edited the supplement *The Torch* for the Shanghai *Dawan Bao*, and Gao Gongbai, who had long been active in education, were both directly attached correspondents of the Shanghai district, and I was in contact with both. It was in the supplement Cui Wanqiu edited that Zhang Chunqiao, writing under the pen name Di Ke, published an article attacking Lu Xun, asking that Lu Xun's reply be sent through *The Torch*'s editorial office. Cui Wanqiu's several articles praising Lan Ping also appeared in that paper. Cui Wanqiu was living in the French Concession at the time, and I took him a subsidy of eighty yuan a month, with a bonus of a few dozen more from time to time. When I took him his money and collected his intelligence at his home, I saw Lan Ping there more than once, but Cui never introduced us.

If he had guests when I called he would always take me straight through to the box room at the back of the house; a hand-over one way, a hand-over the other, and I left. He was terrified of being found out, and I had no wish to be greeting his visitors. There were others besides — lawyers, professors, bank clerks, journalists, and idlers, hooligans and gang bosses of every description. In 1934 the post passed to Wu Naixian, a first-class Whampoa man who had earned Chiang Kai-shek's confidence by betraying Mr. Deng Yanda. Chiang Kai-shek and Dai Li hoped to use his old connections in the Provisional Action Committee of the Chinese Kuomintang — which the reactionaries called the Third Party — both to work at wrecking that party and, through a few degenerates within it, to get close to Communist organizations and plot the destruction of the Communist underground. The Third Party men he brought into the Juntong were not many, but they were thought a great deal of by the head office in Nanking. The head office's secretary-general came up to Shanghai several times to meet them, and their expenses were generous. I never went to where these men lived; they generally arranged matters by telephone directly with Wu Naixian, who would tell me where to go and wait for them to give me their intelligence. When I met them at the appointed place and hour I would often hand over their money at the same time. I talked very little with these particular correspondents, because Wu Naixian had told me over and over to say as little to them as possible; when we met, both sides were very polite, and the place was always a private room in a teahouse or restaurant.

They would as a matter of form stand me a meal, and often gave me the intelligence tucked inside an illustrated magazine or a novel. I was then only in my twenties and they were mostly men of thirty or forty, so they treated me rather as a younger brother and always warned me not to lose the things. I did courier work in the Shanghai district for nearly three years. From 1933, although I was already head of the French Concession intelligence group, I still doubled as courier and lived at the district office. Only in 1935, when I took on the post of inspector with the detective corps of the Songhu Garrison Command as well, did I give up the courier's job, and the head office in Nanking sent Wang Xiangqi as district courier in my place. Of the several dozen directly attached correspondents I dealt with as district courier, various as they were, not one was a Communist cadre, and only one or two belonged to organizations on the Communists' fringe. Every time Dai Li came to Shanghai he flew into a rage at the Shanghai district's inability to get inside the Communist organization or to draw out any important Communist. Once, having called the district chief, the secretary and several group heads to dinner at the Chahua Lou on Fourth Avenue, he said furiously: "Go on like this and our work will collapse. How is it that you cannot get inside a single Communist organization?"

Some of the secretaries and group heads the Shanghai district had had were Communist renegades, but once they changed sides their connections were broken and there was no way for them to develop anything further. This, the main work of all, was what Chiang Kai-shek and Dai Li were least satisfied with, and they always laid the blame on the agents of the Shanghai district at every level for not putting their whole strength into it. In my nearly six years in Shanghai, so far as I know, there was not an agent who did not try every means he could think of to get inside the Communist underground organization and to draw people out of it, and the results were very small — never as easy as with the various democratic parties, the progressive bodies and the anti-Chiang groups. So at the annual review of the year's work the Shanghai district always came in for a cursing from the head office and from Dai Li. Every year, at the beginning, a set of methods for dealing with the Communist organization would be drawn up, and not once was any of it ever carried out.

Dai Li was forever saying that with the Communists it was a struggle of life and death, and demanding strictly of every agent that he never forget this was the greatest of all threats from within; yet right up to the outbreak of war and my own departure from Shanghai, our work in that direction still showed no "results." For this the district chief, the secretary and the group heads were replaced over and over in the course of those few years. When Yu Lexing was district chief the district office was always inside the French Concession, at Route Lafayette, Route Vallon and Route Sœur Allegre in turn. The office's running costs and special expenses came to only two hundred yuan, and after the rent and the hotel rooms and restaurants for meetings with directly attached correspondents had taken a hundred-odd, there was not much left; it took several months even to save up for a decent camera.

The agents in those days were very timid, terrified that the Communists would retaliate against them, and apart from the few group heads who came and went, the ordinary members did not know where the district office was. They were afraid of being followed as they came and went, and there was not even a motor car. The only car was the operations group's, and it was not to be used at all except for kidnappings and assassinations. After Wu Naixian became district chief in 1934 the post of district secretary passed to Zhang Shi, whose style-name was Xingchen. Zhang was a Communist renegade, and Dai Li hoped he would prove more use in dealing with the Communists. A district inspector was added at the same time, Chen Ci, whose style-name was Zhiping — I am told he later became one of Chiang's ambassadors abroad — and the district's funds were raised to three hundred yuan. Wu Naixian and Zhang Shi were timider still, and moved the district office out of the French Concession to Laoximen, under Kuomintang rule; after that the office never went back into the concessions but shifted about in the neighbourhood of Laoximen. Since sanitary conditions in the Chinese city were very poor, however, Wu Naixian and Chen Ci themselves lived on Route Vallon in the French Concession, and nobody but the secretary and I dared be told where. After Zhang Shi became district secretary he was still no better able to get inside the Communist organization, but he had many suggestions about handling intelligence concerning the Communists and some insight into analysing it. Dai Li took to him and promoted him to be secretary-general of the head office in Nanking; his place was filled in turn by Xu Jin and Yao Zecong, and for a time I was made assistant secretary as well. Before long, as I had so many appointments already, Zheng Xiuyuan was appointed assistant secretary.

Early in 1935 Chiang Kai-shek approved the handing over to the Juntong of the detective corps of the Songhu Garrison Command, with Wu Naixian holding the command of it concurrently. From then on the Juntong's activities in Shanghai moved by degrees from the secret to the half-open: some of the arrest and surveillance work was handed to the detective corps, and political prisoners taken no longer all had to go to Nanking but could be held at the corps, where the first interrogations and the torture that went with them were carried out. About the autumn or winter of 1935 Wu Naixian was appointed by Chiang Kai-shek head of the police administration of the Nanking-Shanghai and Hangchow-Ningbo railways, and Wang Xinheng became chief of the Shanghai district with Qin Chengzhi as district secretary, while the command of the detective corps went to Weng Guanghui. From that point the Juntong had control of the police of both those railway lines.

Not only did every courier of the head office's communications section receive a permanent free second-class pass on the Nanking-Shanghai-Hangchow-Ningbo lines; the headquarters staff of the Shanghai district and several group heads travelled free on those lines as well. Prisoners sent from Shanghai to Nanking also had the railway police's help. Han Shangying, the police district officer stationed at Soochow, was assigned to assist the Shanghai district in escorting prisoners. After Wang Xinheng became district chief the staff, at headquarters and in the field, was larger than before, and Wang himself was particularly active. He was a Communist renegade who had studied at Sun Yat-sen University in Moscow. He was also a Ningbo man, long acquainted with the many Ningbo merchants of Shanghai, and he was on very close terms with Du Yuesheng, being one of the most welcome guests of Du's Green Gang association, the Hengshe. During his time as district chief he drew into the Juntong a good many renegades who had studied in the Soviet Union, Shanghai gang members, men of the Shanghai postal workers' union, and people from business and cultural circles. He left Shanghai only on the eve of the war, when Zhou Weilong succeeded him.

In this period the Shanghai district's organization was greatly enlarged: besides the original intelligence groups, sleeper groups were added in Hongkou, Zhabei and southern Shanghai. More than ten field investigation groups attached to the armies were added as well, the staff at headquarters and in the field grew from a hundred-odd to more than five hundred, and a great many wireless stations were set up. I do not propose to describe that period here, and shall leave it for other material. The basic organization of the Shanghai district before the war was its several intelligence groups. I took over from Xu Zhaojun as a group head in 1933; at first it was called the Second Group. The First Group was headed by Chen Zhiqiang, the Third by Wang Changyu, the Fourth by Zhang Renyou. After Wu Naixian became district chief the groups were made responsible by area: Chen for the Chinese city, myself for the French Concession, Wang for the International Settlement, Zhang for western Shanghai. Later, since there was no way of dividing up the lines of work the group members were pursuing, the system of division by area lapsed of itself and each went where he pleased to develop his connections, only the surveillance work being divided up.

Surveillance within the French Concession of Communist suspects, of members of the democratic parties and of anti-Chiang groups fell to my group, while the members themselves were not confined to any area in developing their own connections. I was a group head for three years and more, and though the agents I directed were only twenty-odd, each of them had anything from a few to a few dozen "eyes" — that is, assistants — working for him, and I too used a number of connections of my own for one activity and another. The activities of these group members at the time may be summarized as follows. Su Yeguang, a native of Xinhua in Hunan, had once been a Communist and had belonged to a democratic party as well. After he became an agent he worked exclusively on the democratic parties, and on the New Kuomintang too. Every district chief in turn hoped he would get inside the Communists' outer organizations and work his way in to the Party organization itself; in the event he wasted his efforts for years and never managed it.

His method, besides talking progressive talk at every turn, was to curse Chiang Kai-shek and the reactionaries loudly in company and to write colourless pieces for one paper and another expressing dissatisfaction with society as it was. These were the means by which he got close to progressive people and carried on his secret-service work. A good many were taken in by him at the time, though after a while the trick was seen through, so that he could never get in deep. Jia Jinbo, a Sichuanese, had dealings with people in publishing. His method was to feign sympathy with progressive opinion, constantly carrying books and papers the reactionaries had banned into the Chinese city for people, so as to win the trust of progressives; many who did not know what he was would leave progressive publications at his house for safe keeping. He spent his days full of indignation and ready to run risks for others, and besides being able to hand in progressive books, papers and leaflets as evidence of his work, he turned up a good many leads that way. Dai Li at that time attached great importance to the Life Bookshop and the Uchiyama Bookshop, and through Jia's connections an agent's son was introduced to the Life Bookshop as an apprentice, in the hope that he would lie low for a long time and gradually come to understand the bookshop's connections with the Communist organization, and Mr. Zou Taofen's activities.

After the war began I had no further contact with the man planted in the Life Bookshop. As for the Uchiyama Bookshop, because Mr. Lu Xun went there often, the agents always held that it was run by the Japanese Communist Party and were forever wanting to plant someone inside, but they never succeeded, and Jia Jinbo was reproached for it more than once. Mao Fangmei was a press photographer with the Shanghai *Chenbao*. He used his open position as a reporter for his secret-service work, and was at the same time responsible for keeping watch on progressive people in Shanghai journalism.

Documents the agents of the Shanghai district stole from the democratic parties were photographed by him and then returned. The "eyes" he used were mostly men working in journalism, and at every progressive gathering in Shanghai at that time there were such reporters mixed in doing secret-service work. Fan Guangzhen was a Chinese detective inspector who had served for years in the police of the French Concession. From constable to plain-clothes man — a *baodating*, as the Shanghai people call them — and then to inspector, he had spent his whole career in the French Concession, and not only knew it thoroughly but had a great many "eyes"; being besides a minor gang boss himself, his social connections were extremely tangled. Although he had joined the Juntong he was not willing to put his whole heart into its service, and he was more loyal to the French colonial authorities than to us. Dai Li understood that his way was to keep in with both sides, and that at need he would sooner sacrifice his Juntong connection and throw in his lot with the French, so nothing very strict was demanded of him: whatever he did was taken as enough, and he was generally used only when there was no help for it.

His intelligence was plentiful but very little of it was of use; and for all his tangled connections there was never a Communist lead to be followed up through him. He was used only to give quiet cover to the Juntong's kidnappings in the French Concession. Ruan Zhaohui, a sixth-class Whampoa man who had been in the same company as Dai Li, had been a courier at the head office in Nanking. Since Fan Guangzhen was unreliable, and since the Juntong had to have a core agent of its own inside the French police, Dai Li told Fan to pull strings and, with five hundred yuan in expenses, got him a post as a plain-clothes detective. Ordinary detectives in the French police were normally promoted from among the constables; he got the post without going through the several years as a constable. He was at once Fan Guangzhen's open assistant and his superior: in police work he took Fan's direction, and in Juntong work Fan took his. Since Fan did not care to have much to do with group heads or district chiefs, being afraid of being found out, the group head directed him through Ruan, telling Ruan from time to time what work he was to be given. Ruan himself had no social connections to speak of, but he made very good use of Fan's to carry on all his activities, and within a year almost all of Fan's connections had become Ruan's. These men could not get inside the Communists or the democratic parties, but they were of great convenience for keeping watch on progressive people living in the French Concession, and they could attend openly, or slip into, the various gatherings held there.

Zhou Xunzi, a Sichuanese, fourth Whampoa class, was at that time the liaison man the Sichuan warlords kept in Shanghai. After joining the Juntong he played a double game throughout, unwilling to offend the Sichuan warlords and wanting at the same time to draw close to Chiang Kai-shek's group. The place he chiefly frequented was the Yipinxiang Hotel, where Xie Wuliang and Zeng Tongyi were long-term residents. These two had lived at the Yipinxiang for years; the hotel changed hands several times and they never moved. They lived entirely on what the Sichuan warlords sent them, and the people who came and went there were mostly Sichuanese too. Fellow provincials together will talk about anything, and Zhou could pick up a good deal of information and many leads there, and after going about and checking a little himself, that became his intelligence. He took money from both sides, so he had at times to pass back to the warlords something of the central government's measures and opinions regarding Sichuan. Dai Li always held that he was unreliable and told me repeatedly to guard against him. Luo Hua was Zhou Xunzi's wife.

She was recruited into the Juntong mainly to keep watch on Zhou Xunzi. Her work consisted simply of reporting every day on what Zhou did. Within six months, from her daily and factual reports on her own husband's words and deeds and the friends who came to see him, the Shanghai district had only to check these against Zhou's own intelligence to discover that he had been deceiving them all along, and Dai Li ordered him detained and taken to Nanking to be imprisoned. Once in Nanking, unable to talk his way round the many specific questions the judge put to him, Zhou had no choice but to admit his fault. But it never once occurred to him that the material had come from his own wife. And after Zhou Xunzi was locked up, his wife Luo Hua was left completely in the dark, with no idea that she had destroyed her husband. It never entered her dreams that forty yuan a month would sell him. Once Zhou had been detained her work served no purpose either, so the Juntong suspended her and told her to resume her connection when she had found some line of work. Before long, under the successive blows of her husband's detention and having nothing to live on, her mind gave way, and the Juntong took no further notice of her; within the year she collapsed in the street and died. Some of the agents who knew what lay behind it were chilled by the sight, and from then on were watchful for any sign that the organization might be secretly using their own wives against them, often not daring to let their wives know what they did, so that husband and wife lived on their guard against one another. These are only a few examples.

Other members of the group, Zheng Gongxuan, Ma Longhai, Wang Manzhi and some ten more, either had a few leads and worked on the democratic parties or, having no lead at all, could only be given surveillance and check-up work, and I shall not list them one by one. Every group head at that time had also to do intelligence and other secret-service work himself.

In my own case, the alias I used outside was Chen Lun, or Chen Cang, and my open cover was as the Shanghai correspondent of the Hunan Xiangguang News Agency. I would go about "reporting" and taking photographs with a camera round my neck and a press card in my pocket. At a number of universities I used connections among fellow provincials and relatives to have them find things out for me, and when a suspicious person or a progressive organization was discovered, a little money would be spent to have them look into it more deeply. Of those who did most work for me over those years, the chief was Yao Mingda, a professor of history at Fudan University, and several of his students. He himself had opened a women's bookshop opposite the Paris Cinema on Route Pichon in the French Concession and ran a women's monthly, and the *Women's Monthly* he published sold mainly in the South Seas and in Fujian and Guangdong. I made use of that monthly's editor as well. For years she never understood what I really was. Any of her literary friends, and any of those who wrote regularly for the *Women's Monthly*, whom I judged to be of the smallest use to my work, I would find some way of getting to know and approaching, and without their realizing it they would do something for me. Afterwards, though I was also inspector and group head with the detective corps of the Garrison Command and constantly engaged in kidnapping, assassination and other criminal business, none of these people had the least idea of it, and I never let slip a word of it in front of them. My mother was a woman who kept a vegetarian diet and told her beads, and she stayed with me many times without knowing that her own son was engaged day after day in killing and injuring people. Once she was coming home from worship at the temple just as I was fleeing with several men from a bungled attempt to kidnap a Communist underground worker, and the car we were in ran straight into the rickshaw she was riding. Seeing that one of the men in the car looked like me, she called out my childhood name. I had to harden my heart and curse her in Shanghai dialect — "Are you blind?" — and only then did she stop calling. An agent will not only disown his own kin; he will curse his own mother. When I came home well dressed later, my mother told me at once that she had seen a man in shabby working clothes very like me, and had called to him and been cursed for it; I fobbed her off as best I could. When Dai Li heard of this afterwards, he praised me more than once for my presence of mind, in not giving myself away even in front of my own mother. In those days I could hardly meet a man without trying every way of making some use of him.

If he was of no use today, then tomorrow or the day after there would be some chance to use him once, and only then was I satisfied. Whenever I filled in a form about my social connections I would put down every relative and friend without exception, because sooner or later they would all become working connections of mine. I know something of the other groups too, in a general way.

Chen Zhiqiang's group, for instance, which had its office in Yucai Lane, Penglai Road, in the southern city, was responsible for work in the Chinese city. Chen was a minor gang boss and constantly used his position as an agent to protect disciples of his who manufactured red pills and morphine, taking a share of the profits, so that he had a motor car of his own and lived far better than the district chief. Dai Li knew perfectly well that he was using his privileges to line his pockets but could find no evidence; and since Chen also kept a firm grip on his work and both the quantity and the quality of his intelligence were good, Dai had to leave him alone. His work was chiefly social intelligence in Shanghai, and surveillance in the Chinese city. Some of his group members were district and station superintendents, section officers of the Shanghai municipal police, and corps and group commanders of the criminal investigation corps, along with vagrants and small traders. The Juntong did not yet control the Shanghai police, so it used Chen's connections to work them at second hand. The police commissioner Wen Hongju and the head of the criminal investigation corps Lu Ying were neither of them much inclined to help Dai Li, so Dai relied on Chen Zhiqiang to keep some part of the police force working for the Juntong. Later, when Cai Jinjun became commissioner, his connivance with Dai grew closer, and some posts at the police training school were made over to the Juntong, Dai recommending the district inspector Chen Zhiping to be its secretary and head of discipline, with Yang Huabo and Huang Jiachi as instructors. Dai several times wanted to bring Lu Ying into the Juntong so as to get hold of the criminal investigation corps, but Lu would never agree. By this time Chen Zhiqiang was growing bolder and bolder and Dai was much displeased with him; and though nothing could be pinned on him, in 1935 he was removed all the same and transferred to be head of the criminal investigation squad of the provincial capital police bureau in Shaanxi, his group being broken up and its members distributed among the others.

Wang Changyu's group was responsible for work in the International Settlement, but his group office was always in the French Concession near the Settlement boundary, chiefly because he was timid and afraid of trouble: the Settlement was rather disorderly and the French Concession a little better. He used as cover the character of an anti-Chiang man among the Whampoa graduates, spending his days cursing Chiang Kai-shek in front of his Whampoa fellows and complaining loudly that he had been given no work. When Whampoa men in office came to Shanghai and saw him, besides commiserating there were those who gave him money to live on and offered to find him a post. Others were completely taken in and chimed in with him — with the result that other people's anti-Chiang remarks all became his intelligence material. That was the method by which he worked on the anti-Chiang groups. Most of his group members were Cantonese living about Hongkou, some of them connected with the New Kuomintang and the Guangdong and Guangxi warlords, and a few of them planted in the democratic parties; he had "eyes" as well in Cantonese restaurants and wine shops such as the Xinya, the Sun Ya and the Dasanyuan, and in Cantonese department stores like the Sincere and the Wing On. Whenever I had to work in such places I would go to him for an introduction, and he would help with the surveillance and the watching. He worked very hard, and Dai Li thought a great deal of him. He was a group head from 1932 until the outbreak of war, when he left Shanghai. Zhang Renyou's group was responsible for the western district.

His office was always about Xiang'ancun Road and Seymour Road. His cover was the title of head of the Shanghai office of the *Wenzhou Daily*, and sometimes he passed himself off as the manager or assistant manager of some Wenzhou shop. As he was fresh out of the Hangchow special class he had few connections in Shanghai and his own activity was limited, and his members too were at first Hangchow students sent to Shanghai to gain practice, who could do no more than general social investigation and surveillance. Later Ying Huaibao and Shi Renchong were transferred away one after another, and a few men with lines of work of their own were taken on. Of the Hangchow students only one, Cheng Muxi, a Yima man, was willing to rough it, and lived a long time among the workers of the western district, so that he later came into contact with people in some of the Communists' outer organizations and had something to show for it. Afterwards Zhang Renyou, having several times borrowed money from the garrison commander Yang Hu on his own account and squandered it, was found out by Dai Li and dismissed, and Cheng Muxi took over the group, working exclusively on developing leads to the Communists; in 1935 it was through his connections that a district committee organization and the Jiangsu provincial committee organization were destroyed.

After Wang Xinheng became district chief two more intelligence groups were added. One was led by Liu Caiqing, and its men were again Hangchow graduates sent to Shanghai for practice, who gradually built up some working connections. The other was led by Ruan Yacheng and was made up mostly of gangsters and vagrants with a few traitors among them. After 1935, since I had taken on the command of the First Group of the Garrison Command's detective corps and was responsible for operations, I handed the intelligence group over to Su Yeguang, the membership remaining much as before. Once Wang was district chief the system of dividing responsibility by area was abolished altogether: wherever there was a line to be developed, the area did not matter. Since there was no lateral connection between groups, and only the group heads met one another with any frequency, the members never had dealings with each other at all, and it happened often enough that our own people worked on our own people, which made an absurd situation.

These group members always put on in company the "progressive" air of men dissatisfied with things as they were and opposed to Chiang Kai-shek's rule of darkness; and when two of them were working in the same place, the false zeal each displayed was conspicuous and drew attention easily. Since neither knew what the other was, and each had several different aliases, our own men worked on our own men without the district office noticing, and a great deal of time and money would go on it, until at the point of making the arrest, when the stolen documents and the secretly taken photographs were sent to the district office for a final decision, it was discovered that they were our own.

Once, over a directly attached correspondent named Ma, there was very nearly a catastrophe. Almost half a year went by, more than a thousand yuan in expenses was spent, over twenty men were put on it, and when the arrests were made in the end several of our own people were taken. Dai Li flew into a great rage over it. The man had originally been a Communist renegade. Because he understood the minds and the circumstances of both sides, he went on operating in society in the old way, and what he showed the world was more mysterious and more accomplished than the ordinary agent's. The agents of several groups all took a close interest in him, and he used this to get close to them and to work a deception both ways. He would hint, half deliberately, that he was an important Communist leader for East China. He was always making out that he was short of money, without ever asking the agents for any directly, with the result that he received a steady flow of help from them. When it was finally decided to make the arrests, Dai Li even reported it to Chiang Kai-shek; and only when the man had been taken, and several agents implicated with him, did the truth come out. Dai Li was angry and at the same time bitterly resentful, and cursed the responsible agents of the Shanghai district for a pack of fools. The district chief Wang Xinheng for his part blamed the man for not having reported truthfully to the district every day while he was carrying on this deception, so that true and false could not be told apart.

But in the end Dai Li decided to treat the false as true and let the mistake stand, and sent him to Nanking to be dealt with — which meant neither losing face before Chiang Kai-shek nor missing the credit. When I escorted this bogus head of the Communists' East China bureau to Nanking, Dai Li said to me: "The fellow actually dared use us as cover for his own activities. It is as well we found out early, or he would have cost us dear." I was one of those who knew the truth of it, and hearing him talk like that I understood that, things being as they were, I should have to stifle my conscience and let the man be sacrificed. It was as well that Communists the Juntong took were never handed to any other body for examination: they could be dealt with as we chose and nobody would ever ask. This renegade, when he was a Communist in earnest, had been arrested and not killed; and it never occurred to him that having sold his own soul and become an agent, he would be treated as a Communist and put to death.

After this lesson Dai Li laid it down that agents must report in detail on any new lead they found and on anyone they met while posing as progressives, so as to stop anything of the kind happening again. Of the open secret-service bodies the Shanghai district controlled and made use of, much the most important was the detective corps of the Songhu Garrison Command. When this corps was handed over to the Juntong, the first commander, Wu Naixian, went to take it up single-handed; only when the second commander, Weng Guanghui, had gone in did Dai Li send myself, Cheng Muxi, Lin Zhijiang and Ni Yongchao in as inspectors.

The deputy commander Peng Bowei was left where he was throughout: he was Yang Hu's man, and had for years run the seamen's union for Yang, and a good many of the inspectors owed their posts to his introduction. The Juntong's way was to take hold of it by degrees, edging people out little by little and recruiting them one by one, so as to use as many of the existing staff as possible in the Juntong's service. Only in 1935, when Wang Zhaohuai had succeeded Weng Guanghui as commander and the situation had gradually been made out, was it decided to squeeze out Peng Bowei, who was not much inclined to do as he was told, and to promote the head of the First Group, Yang Fengqi, to be deputy commander, with the head of the Second Group, Xu Pengfei, doubling as staff officer of the corps; only then was the First Group taken over, with myself as its head, and a large body of Juntong men — Jiang Zhijun, Su Zhentong, Liu Yao, Zhong Diren, Yao Kairu and a dozen more — sent in along with me. This was because, immediately after the takeover, the chief fear was that the old hands would be uneasy and cause trouble: it was after all an open public-security body which had to keep order in the locality, and besides, some of the old hands had long had close ties with the police of the British and French concessions, which made arresting people in the concessions very convenient, so it would not do to take everything over at once.

The other method was to recruit the old staff into the Juntong one by one, until the whole was in hand and everything was of one colour. Before long the former inspectors Zhu Youxin, Wang Kaiming, Liu Guangqi, Chen Jinbiao, Xia Jinhai, Lin Jieqing and Chen Bangguo had all become Juntong men one after another, and the Juntong could then use the corps freely and boldly for open and half-open activities of every kind in Shanghai — arresting, kidnapping, interrogating and imprisoning Communists and democratic figures.

Of the other open bodies that could be got hold of — the police administration of the Nanking-Shanghai and Hangchow-Ningbo railways, the police training school of the Shanghai municipal police, the guard and inspection office of the China Merchants Steam Navigation Company — none was of much help to the Shanghai district's work; and as both the railway police administration and the China Merchants inspection office came under the head office, the Shanghai district could only make occasional use of them, for help with communications and with escorting prisoners, and as cover at need. The Juntong paid great attention to the schools of Shanghai at that time, but the only one it could get hold of and use was the Miqin Middle School run by Yang Hu, whose principal Guan Suzhi was also used by the Juntong. A few of the school's staff, such as the head of general affairs Tan Liangpu, were agents Dai Li had sent in. Since the school's chairman was that notorious butcher Yang Hu, using it for operations was of no use: no progressive organization would ever carry on open activity there, and there was nothing for it but to rely on Guan Suzhi personally to do a little work for the Juntong in secret under cover of his position as principal.

As for the Sanji Wireless Training Institute which the Juntong ran on Route Lafayette in the French Concession, that existed solely to recruit wireless operators for the Juntong. Its dealings with the Shanghai district were purely administrative, and Dai Li would not allow agents to use the school for any other activity, for fear of affecting the recruitment of wireless personnel. Although I had dealings with some of the agents at that school, I never dared use them for work, or even as cover. Although the Special Services Department was at that time nominally part of the Renaissance Society, it was directed throughout by Chiang Kai-shek personally and had little to do with the Society, so that the Shanghai district and the Society's Shanghai organization could not work as one. The head of the Renaissance Society in Shanghai at that time, Liu Binghuo, and his assistant Niu Peijiang, were at loggerheads over power and profit in the most disgraceful fashion. In his eagerness to squeeze Niu Peijiang out, Liu Binghuo was forever spreading slanders about him to all comers, saying how badly Niu behaved and that in order to seize the leadership in Shanghai for himself Niu was preparing to have him assassinated; and Niu retaliated on Liu in the same way. This dog-eat-dog struggle of theirs, open and covert, also became a subject of Shanghai district intelligence, and was regularly reported to the head office.

Several times instructions came down from Chiang Kai-shek himself for the Shanghai district to make a thorough investigation. Yet the Shanghai district never mentioned any of this to Liu Binghuo and the rest. When Xu Jin, whose style-name was Andeng, a second-rank Renaissance Society official in Shanghai whom the Society had recommended to Dai Li, came to the Shanghai district as secretary, the district chief Wu Naixian warned him again and again that on no account must anything of the district's investigations into the Society be let out, to avoid unnecessary trouble over personalities. Most of the core agents of the Shanghai district at that time were required to join the Renaissance Society, though ordinary group members were not necessarily required to. Even after joining, the cells they were formed into still kept the Juntong men together and did not mix them with outsiders.

I was in a cell with several group heads, together with the Juntong men working at the police training school, Huang Jiachi and Yang Huabo. What each of us actually did was never reported at cell meetings, and as for the Society's instruction that we should report developments as they arose, that was never once complied with; later we stopped holding cell meetings at all. These men were nominally members of the Renaissance Society and of the Revolutionary Youth Comrades' Association, yet they never performed a single duty for that organization — chiefly because the criminal business they were engaged in was already many times worse than anything an ordinary member of the Society did. Before the war, the agents working for the Juntong in Shanghai were paid rather better than the ordinary reactionary officer, and had bonuses and special allowances besides. Members whose work showed nothing outstanding could draw only a fixed living allowance. Apart from the directly attached correspondents, who had living allowances of a hundred yuan and upwards, the group members mostly had from thirty or forty up to a hundred, and a group head had a further hundred in special allowance and a rent allowance. So the ordinary members constantly found their outgoings exceeding their income, and were always racking their brains for ways of earning a bonus. I spoke to Dai Li several times about the members' living allowances being too small, and his answer was always: "We are doing revolutionary work. We cannot talk about comfort; we ought to live hard." After he had said this high-sounding stuff a good many times, he did once let something of his real meaning show. On the occasion when Zhang Renyou was found out for borrowing money from Yang Hu, Dai sent for me and cursed me for having known beforehand and not reported it.

When I again raised the point that the money really was too little to live on, he said something to me like this — I cannot recall the exact words, only the sense: if the living allowance is generous, some men will grow comfortable and stop working hard, so we give only enough to keep body and soul together; that way a man must exert himself and find a way to earn bonuses to make up the difference. The better the work, the bigger the bonus, and results show themselves most readily; work badly and he gets nothing, which saves a large fixed outlay.

He held this to be the best way of raising the standard of the work. But it also produced the other thing: agents used the opportunities their positions gave them to line their pockets. At that time the agents of the Shanghai district carried no documents at all, apart from a very few who had badges of the Military Affairs Commission or the General Staff or the Military Magazine Society. At first it was not easy to extort or embezzle; but once the detective corps had been taken over, those doing secret work came to know the corps' agents and the two made common cause in the business. Agents who held public posts were better off, since they drew their living allowance as usual and had the income of the post as well, so a great many agents hoped for a public post as a way of getting more money. Some agents on secret work were eager for a public post because it meant not only drawing pay from both sides but a good deal more besides in extras. Units like the Garrison Command's detective corps and the police bureau's criminal investigation corps were especially convenient for making money. I remember that when I had just been posted to the detective corps as inspector, on my first morning there I put some books and papers into a drawer and found a red paper envelope in it with three characters written on the outside: "Kindly accept." I opened it and found two hundred yuan in cash.

### Kidnapping

Surprised, I hurried to ask the commander Weng Guanghui what it meant. He laughed and said he did not know. I then demanded of the orderly who looked after the office who had put the thing in my drawer. He answered me quite unconcernedly: "Somebody has sent you money. If you do not want it, that is that. What is there to lose your temper about?" The next day I opened the drawer again and the money was gone. I told Dai Li about it afterwards. He asked me why I had not taken it. I said: "Take a man's money and you must take away his troubles; from then on I should have to do as others told me." He was delighted and praised me. After that nobody ever put anything in my desk again. Lin Zhijiang's desk faced mine, and I often saw him open his drawer and, smiling, transfer the contents to his pocket. Of the Juntong's crimes in Shanghai in this period the chief was assassination.

Apart from the killings of Yang Xingfo and Shi Liangcai, which I have written of separately, the most numerous and the most terrible were the killings of Communists secretly arrested in Shanghai at that time. Because these martyrs who were murdered were not public figures like Yang Xingfo and Shi Liangcai but nameless heroes engaged in underground revolutionary work, I have never been able to recall their names. In some cases their names were not known even then: it was known only that they were Communists, and nobody ever troubled to find out whether the name given was real or false — they were killed and that was the end of it. Now, when I should like to say something of the noblest qualities of these martyrs who died so bravely, and of deeds shining enough to be sung and wept over, I cannot even establish a single name. Since liberation a good many people have spoken of the appalling crime of the Juntong's destroying the bodies of the revolutionaries it slaughtered with chemicals, and I did once see this inhuman practice with my own eyes. As for the pits of chemical solution that people have pointed to since liberation at places like the SACO compound in Chungking during the war, as the vats in which agents dissolved the bodies of those they killed, from what I know these are probably a mistaken report.

In areas under their own control the Juntong's agents, having slaughtered revolutionaries, generally either buried them afterwards or buried them alive without killing them first, and were unwilling to spend a great deal of time and money on destroying the bodies with chemicals. If it really became necessary — as after the killing of Luo Shiwen and Che Yaoxian, the secretaries of the Communist Party's Sichuan provincial committee — cremation was used. In those areas they could do as they pleased and there was no need to go to such lengths. It was only when Communists were secretly arrested inside the Shanghai concessions, where they were awkward to move and where a body could not be disposed of after a killing, that the method of destroying the body and the traces was adopted, both to avoid the attention of the concession police authorities and to make sure nothing leaked out. Dai Li's reason for using such savage methods against Communists, as I came to understand from my years at his side afterwards, was mainly that the Communists were not like other progressive people, with whom killing one could warn a hundred, or at least frighten the rest. The killing of Yang Xingfo, for instance, did not frighten Madame Sun Yat-sen, the leading figure of the League for the Protection of Civil Rights. After the killing of Shi Liangcai, although many in Shanghai journalism saw that it was a planned political assassination, none dared denounce the reactionary authorities publicly with the righteous severity Madame Sun had shown after Yang Xingfo's death; it silenced them like cicadas in cold weather.

### Raids and Arrests

To the reactionary authorities killings of that sort had many advantages, but against the Communists they had no effect: however many were killed, the other Party members were not frightened but only strengthened in their revolutionary resolve. At the same time, the Juntong's killing people everywhere caused great public revulsion, so the method of secret killing was used, which left other Communists in ignorance of what had happened and made them readier to relax their guard. Individual Party members who changed sides after arrest would even ask, in conversation, whether so-and-so had been arrested and had turned.

That gave the Juntong's agents a further useful instrument of deception and enticement. So there were reasons for using these barbarous and cruel methods on Party members who would not renounce their principles after arrest. The one occasion on which I saw with my own eyes a Communist's body destroyed with "bone-dissolving fluid" was in the autumn or winter of 1933 or 1934. At that time I was both the Shanghai district's courier and an intelligence group head, so I could be about among the agents constantly and nobody would suspect me.

One afternoon, having to take an important document to the head of the operations group, Zhao Lijun, I went first to his lodgings and, not finding him, went on to his group office. That was a place other agents were not allowed to go, and I myself went only occasionally when I had to see the head or his deputy. When I had slipped in at the back door I found Zhao Lijun and several of his men extremely busy. The moment Zhao saw me he asked whether I had time to spare. When I answered that I had nothing to do but find him, he asked me to stay.

A little later I learned that a Communist kidnapped in secret the day before was to be killed, and a chemical solution tried for the first time to destroy the body. I was curious, and wanted to see how this so-called bone-dissolving fluid worked, so I stayed.

I never asked the name of the man who had been seized; I knew only that he was a Communist. After his abduction he had been utterly firm, unmoved by anything, and had shouted and cursed them. Afraid of disturbing the neighbours, the agents had knocked him senseless. When he came to he was as unchanged as before, and was knocked senseless again, then bound hand and foot with a towel stuffed into his mouth. Zhao Lijun decided to carry out Nanking's instruction — that a man seized who showed no sign whatever of being shaken or turned was to be killed in secret and his body destroyed. This man became the agents' experiment. Afterwards Zhao Lijun told me the solution would not do: it was troublesome and caused a great deal of bother, and he was against using the method again. From that time I neither saw nor heard of the agents using bone-dissolving fluid to destroy a body. Before long they turned to another method, the one that produced the "trunk murders" which occurred so often in the Shanghai concessions before the war.

This cruel method was used not merely several times; the Juntong afterwards collected material on it and made it into teaching matter, as a way of "shifting the corpse and the blame" after a killing in special circumstances. The method was to beat the victim to death, dismember the body, and pack it in a trunk. Sometimes the trunk was taken out by car and dumped in some remote and empty place; sometimes it was carried out to the street, a rickshaw hired, and the trunk taken to the railway station or to some hotel. When a rickshaw man took such a trunk, the man seeing him off would follow for a stretch and then slip away. Reaching the appointed place and finding that no owner had followed, the rickshaw man would sometimes quietly take it home, thinking his fortune made — and on opening it find a corpse, and more often than not end up in court and unable to clear himself. These ruthless methods of the agents' were always praised by Dai Li as clean, neat and properly handled.

In slaughtering revolutionaries the agents used other methods as well. They would knock the victim senseless or kill him, put him in a sack and throw him into the Whangpoo. Sometimes they would lay a false trail: having killed a man in what was really a political assassination, they would deliberately cut off the genitals and put them in the dead man's mouth, so that it would be taken for a killing over a woman or over a rape. Besides these inhuman ways of killing Communists, the agents did things baser still, below the level of the beasts. Young women Communists were commonly raped in turn and then butchered. When Dai Li heard of it, so far from stopping it, he regarded it as a kind of "encouragement" for his operations men. To an operations man who was on the timid side they would cut out the victim's heart and liver, cook them and make him eat them, telling him that eating a man's heart would give him courage. The number of Communists whom the Juntong assassinated in Shanghai before the war by these most savage and brutal methods was more than even Zhao Lijun could remember. This ferocious butcher enjoyed Dai Li's particular favour throughout.

### The Punishments I Received

Early in the war, having fled from Shanghai to Chungking, he was appointed by Dai Li head of the Juntong's operations section, and soon promoted to be major-general training commissioner of the First War Zone. Killing being second nature to him, he came into conflict with Zhongtong agents in Honan and went so far as to arrest six men in secret — an administrative inspector of the Zhongtong, a middle-school principal and others — and bury them alive. When the Zhongtong afterwards established the proof of it and Chen Lifu and the rest complained to Chiang Kai-shek and demanded severe punishment, Dai Li still tried to shield him; and it was only because Chiang Kai-shek wanted the Zhongtong's agents to go on exerting themselves against the Communists and the people that the decision was taken to have Zhao shot. This butcher who had killed for Chiang Kai-shek for more than ten years was in the end killed by his own master. Dai Li, besides weeping bitterly several times, never afterwards passed through Chengtu without going to the Juntong cemetery at Longquanyi to pay his respects at the grave. More common than assassination in the Shanghai concessions before the war was kidnapping, which was dignified at the time with the name of "secret arrest" and was in fact political abduction. Besides Communists, who were the principal object, many democratic figures and members of anti-Chiang groups were dealt with this way and taken to Nanking or secretly killed. This criminal work was at first carried out by the operations group under Zhao Lijun.

After 1935, when I had taken the public post with the Garrison Command's detective corps, most of the kidnapping fell to me. The agents began by using the methods of the Shanghai kidnapping gangs — several of them threatening a man with pistols and dragging him bodily into a car. This often led to unexpected trouble, the hardest part being that once a man had been dragged as far as the car he would throw his head back and struggle with all his might rather than get in, and even with an agent gripping him on either side and a pistol against his back he was not easily pushed through the door. Dai Li was always cursing us for clumsy fools with no ideas. So I and the men then under me — Su Zhentong, Zhu Youxin, Wang Kaiming, Chen Bangguo and a dozen others — took to studying the matter constantly and improving our methods of kidnapping. We had open positions and permits to carry pistols in both the British and French concessions, so we could go about the concessions armed all day; and if anything went wrong we could turn a kidnapping into an open arrest. When we had first worked out a technique for getting a man into a car, Dai Li praised us highly.

It was in fact quite simple. When the man taken — the "meat ticket" — had been brought up to the car, one man inside would have the door already open; of the two agents holding him, one would press a pistol into the small of his back while the other struck him hard in the belly, at which he would naturally double up, which is exactly the posture for getting into a car. Immediately another agent would press down on his upper body so that he could not throw his head back again, and the man inside would pull — and so the "meat ticket" could be pushed into the car very quickly. It was often a matter of a hair's breadth at the critical moment, with every second to be fought for, so we practised it hard whenever we had the chance. In the end we had the thing down so exactly that even if the man screamed and shouted we needed only a few minutes to get him into the car. Often the constables nearby had not even had time to blow a whistle before we were away in a cloud of dust. Seeing that we were willing to work at it, Dai Li urged us to go further and study the "soft" methods, so that we could kidnap in the crowded main streets in front of everyone and not be confined to out-of-the-way districts.

Under constant encouragement in money and in praise, the agents gradually found a number of new tricks, and we added several more methods which we used regularly and which answered well. For instance, we would first find out the route the intended victim regularly took, drive the car up and park it nearby, and when he came within a short distance of it one agent would cover his eyes from behind with both hands, pressing hard with the thumbs into the hollows below the ears so that he could not break free, while another gripped both his hands, and the two would laugh loudly and say, "Now you will never guess who this is!" Passers-by took it for horseplay, and before the man had made out what was happening he had been half dragged and half pushed into the car; by the time he realized and wanted to shout it was too late. Sometimes we could bring a man in without a car at all, hiring a rickshaw on the spot.

For that a length of rubber tubing was prepared with iron shot packed into one end; an agent would strike the man hard on the head from behind and instantly make off. When the man had been knocked down, another agent would come up pretending to be a relative or friend, holding him up, shouting for someone to catch the assailant, and playing the good Samaritan by calling a rickshaw — or hiring a car nearby — to rush him to hospital; once he was in the car or the rickshaw the destination would be changed on the way, on the pretext of taking him home first, and so the man was easily brought in. Once, using this method on a painter, we ran into one of his pupils, and the agents promptly told him to go and tell the family to come to the Guangci Hospital. The student was of course willing, and so was got rid of without difficulty. Since the agents spent their whole time devising wickedness of this kind, the methods multiplied. Once I took two men to reconnoitre Chen Liu'an, the head of the agents the Guangxi clique kept in Shanghai, meaning to kidnap him, and happened to meet him coming out of his house with a large briefcase, walking towards a hire-car garage. We at once adopted an improvised method. We were all in plain clothes. I told my two assistants to wait in the street and ran quickly to the garage to call a car, so that the garage took me for one of Chen's household; at the same time I helped the driver give the car a wipe down, so that Chen took me for the garage's man. When Chen came up to hire the car I hurried to open the door for him and got in beside the driver myself.

The car had barely started when I told the driver to stop for a moment, and my two assistants sprang in on either side; under three pistols he was taken to the detective corps. When the driver saw it was a military establishment he was too frightened to speak of it, and a few dollars extra settled him. Naturally, using someone else's car for a kidnapping sometimes failed. In kidnapping Mr. Liu Luyin at the Yangtze Hotel on Third Avenue in the International Settlement, his wife noticed and began to scream, and Mr. Liu very quickly turned a somersault from the back into the front and took possession of the driver's seat so that the agents could not drive off. The constables came running up, and we had no choice but to turn the kidnapping into an open arrest. By the beginning of 1937 the agents' methods of kidnapping had grown more sinister and more vicious still. We trained two women agents to help. This method was not restricted by locality and could be used in any crowded street. It used the loose relations between men and women common in the old society as cover for a criminal act, and was particularly effective against men who dressed well, lived in style and could not easily be taken by other means. It could be used anywhere except at the victim's own home. The woman agent would pose as the man's mistress; the moment they met she would seize hold of him, weeping and making a scene and insisting on taking him back with her. Faced with this sudden entanglement, the man would certainly not cry kidnapping at once, but would begin by explaining that she had mistaken him for someone else. She would then set up a louder outcry, calling him heartless, saying he had thrown her over for another and so on, while pulling him hard towards the car; and a second woman agent would join in from the side, urging him to go back with her. At this point one or two male agents would step out of the crowd of onlookers and open with: "What, quarrelling again? Quarrelling in the street is not decent. Better go home, all of you, and talk it over properly."

All these people would with one voice fix the affair in the onlookers' minds as a private quarrel between a man and a woman, so that nobody would see it for a kidnapping. Within a few minutes this knot of agents would have hauled and shoved the "meat ticket" into the car, leaving him with a mouth and no way to use it. Once he was in the car everything was settled. The agents used this method three times in the busiest parts of the British and French concessions and met no difficulty; it was only when the war began that they stopped. Besides the constant kidnappings in the concessions, such things were done in the areas under the reactionary government's own rule as well, though generally only against progressive workers in the factories and progressive students at the universities. Making an open arrest inside a factory was liable to be obstructed by the workers, and sometimes ended in a fight, so that the man was not taken, word got out, and the connections were moved at once, so that no further arrests could be made. So arrest was always carried out by secret abduction, and interrogation under torture followed immediately. For such secret arrests — that is, kidnappings — contact was usually made beforehand with the management or with the officials of a yellow union, who supplied the photograph and the connections, and information about where the man commonly went; sometimes, even on a night shift, the management or the union would send the intended victim out on some pretext and quietly assist the agents, so that once he was away from the factory or from any crowd of workers he could be taken.

### Tortures of Various Kinds

In kidnapping — that is, secretly arresting — progressive students at a university, no contact was made with the school authorities, because when a student disappeared his parents would make a scene and demand him of the school. Nor did the agents make open arrests at a school, since the students would come swarming and the man would be hard to remove. The usual method was to have a professional student inside the school supply a lead, or deliberately invite the man out, so as to give the agents their opportunity. Individual Communists and democratic figures living in the Chinese city were generally not kidnapped but seized in a raid late at night. That way the man could not only be taken but documentary evidence seized as well. Over those years the political prisoners taken by open arrest of this kind were a great many more than those secretly kidnapped, and I shall mention only the most important of them here. About 1935 a Communist underground member who had been arrested and had changed sides, having betrayed several of his comrades, wanted to go further and destroy the higher leading body, and spent his days going about everywhere. In the end the underground Party organization carried out a disciplinary execution: he was shot twice on the waste ground near Xiaoyou Lane at Zikawei in the French Concession, and because he was not hit in a vital place he did not die. Dai Li happened to be in Shanghai; learning of this, he instructed the Shanghai district and the detective corps to use this renegade's life to destroy the Shanghai underground organization. He would not let him be taken to a hospital in the Chinese city but insisted on his being put in a small hospital in a quiet part of the western French Concession. At the same time he had a tabloid print a story saying that a killing over a woman had taken place on the waste ground at Zikawei, that the victim had been hit twice, that after emergency treatment at such-and-such a hospital his life was out of danger, and that he would be discharged in a few days. Dai Li reckoned that the man, not having been killed, would hate the Communists still more and would certainly seek revenge; and on the other side, that the underground organization would certainly send someone to finish the discipline.

If they came again, a lead would be found and the destruction could be widened. He assigned a dozen agents to watch in secret, ready to follow, and put one inside the hospital as well. What Dai Li decided was that there should be no arrest on the spot: they were to wait until the renegade had been shot dead and then follow the men separately. Since the concession police were constantly stopping people in the street and searching them, a Communist would not go about armed, and they would certainly go back by separate ways; and it would be enough to keep behind one of them to find the secret establishment.

I too was assigned to take several agents and watch nearby, and for three or four days nothing whatever stirred. My men disguised themselves severally as rickshaw pullers, as small traders, or as cyclists pretending to mend a breakdown, and all of them were well covered. This renegade lying in the hospital did not know that the Juntong was prepared to sacrifice his life to destroy the Party organization, and kept submitting written requests to be allowed out with his wounds to look for connections — partly to earn merit while the chance lasted and partly to be revenged. Dai Li flatly refused and told him to lie in bed all day and rest. On about the fifth or sixth day, in the afternoon, two men did indeed come into the hospital with fruit and food to see the renegade. The two or three agents keeping watch nearby saw it and were instantly on edge, and at once made all their several preparations. The two had not been in long when two shots were heard, and in a moment they came running out and made off by different ways. The agents waiting outside the hospital, on bicycles, on rickshaws and on foot, all set off together in pursuit. Of the dozen agents, two were too slow to keep up and took a tram back, and halfway there ran into one of the men, which is the only reason he was not "shaken off." The rest, after going round in circles, could not find the men they were following, and I had to take them back to face punishment.

Dai Li, having received the report of the agent posted in the hospital and learned that the renegade had received his second sentence and had been shot dead on the spot with a bullet in the head, hurried to the detective corps to hear the results of the pursuit. One look at me coming back with several men, crestfallen, told him we had lost them; he was so angry that he stamped and swore, and set a time limit within which the men's whereabouts must be discovered, on pain of severe punishment. Having cursed us once he was not satisfied, and a little later cursed me and several of the agents all over again. Just as Dai Li, still in a fury, was about to leave, a telephone call came suddenly from the two agents who had kept up, saying that one of the men who had fired had been followed to a jeweller's shop on Route Frelupt in the French Concession, and that he had gone up by the back door and had been a long time without coming out. Dai at once ordered a raid in concert with the French concession police. The deputy commander of the detective corps Yang Fengqi, the group head Xu Pengfei and I led out almost the whole corps and, with the Chinese detective inspector of the French police, went straight for the jeweller's, where sure enough six pistols, several dozen rounds and several hundred yuan in cash were found on the first floor, and three men and one woman arrested, one of the men being the one who had fired at the hospital.

The French concession police authorities, the case having occurred in the French Concession, wanted to conduct the interrogation first; Dai Li took the view that so long as Communists had been caught, an establishment destroyed and weapons seized, it did no harm to let the French police interrogate them, provided any new lead was reported to the detective corps at once. I am told that the prisoners admitted only to belonging to an organization that existed to discipline renegades within the Communist Party — a "dog-beating corps," which beat renegades only and did not go in for assassinating the upper ranks of the Kuomintang ruling group. These loyal and unyielding Party members gave nothing else away, and in the end all of them were handed over to Nanking and put to death. In the years I was in Shanghai the open arrest of Communists and the destruction of underground Party organizations never let up. The case of Joseph Walden, the head of the Comintern's chief intelligence branch for China, which caused a sensation both in China and abroad — Dai Li, reporting his success to Chiang Kai-shek, always described him as the man in charge for the Far East — was an arrest by Juntong agents. The lead in this case was found by the Juntong's Hupeh station and the case finally broken in Shanghai. The Hupeh station first arrested a Communist suspect named Guan Zhaonan and interrogated him for a long time without result. Later the head office in Nanking sent the judicial officer Zhou Yanghao to Hankow to interrogate him, and Guan Zhaonan agreed to try writing a letter to Shanghai asking the organization to send someone to make contact with him.

Before long Shanghai did indeed send a man named Lu Dubu to Hankow, and he was arrested on arrival. After several rounds of torture Lu Dubu gave up the method by which contact was made with the organization in Shanghai; but he was responsible only for external liaison and knew nothing of the internal arrangements. Dai Li at once decided to send Lu to Shanghai, and hurried there himself to direct the arrangements.

Lu Dubu was handed over on arrival to the agents of the detective corps, and following his method of contact with the organization a room was first taken at the East Asia Hotel on Nanking Road and Lu then wrote a letter to a designated post-office box, the agents waiting in the room for whoever should come. Next day Lu's elder brother Lu Haifang arrived on time; he pushed the door open, saw nobody he knew, and turned and fled. The agents on watch were at that moment dozing, and only started up and gave chase at the sound of the door, without having got a clear look at him. I was outside with two agents, and seeing him push the door and go, I knew he had realized, and followed close. Going downstairs, he turned about a few times in the crowd at the Sincere department store and was lost. Knowing it was useless to search there, I told one assistant to look inside while the other and I went separately to watch the small streets leading out at the back. After a while I saw him come hurrying out of a side door, and went after him at once. In his haste he slipped and fell; I ran up and pinned him, and he twisted round and bit me on the wrist. I struck him hard in the mouth with the butt of my pistol, knocking out his front teeth, and only then got one cuff on him and the other on myself, so that he could not break away.

When my assistant came up we took him together to the Old Cha police station nearby, and that same morning he was handed over by the International Settlement police to the detective corps. This wretch who had wormed his way into the ranks of the revolution and clung to life at any cost, seeing the array of instruments brought out to interrogate him, did not even wait for them to be used: after a round of Dai Li's inducements he agreed at once to give up his superior. He was in a great hurry about it, asking to be taken immediately to a quiet street near the French Park in the French Concession to wait for his superior, saying that if the meeting were missed there would be no way of making contact again. Dai Li, to be safe, had the French concession police notified to assist. That afternoon, in heavy rain, I and two agents took this renegade — who had turned faster than any Communist I had ever seen arrested — and drove, half believing him, to the appointed place to wait. At the hour he had named, exactly four o'clock, a European of no great height came walking up in a raincoat and carrying an umbrella.

The moment the renegade said, in astonished delight, the three words "That is him," my two assistants were out of the car and had seized the European. At this Lu Haifang begged me to drive off at once, being unwilling to ride in the same car with him. Another car waiting nearby came up, and I took Lu Haifang away first. I asked him why he was still afraid of this foreigner. He said, reddening: "Better he should not know me yet; it will be more convenient later."

The French concession police, seeing that the man arrested was a European with not a scrap of evidence on him, would not agree to hand him over at once, and he had to be kept in their custody for the time being. According to the renegade Lu Haifang, this foreigner was one of the Comintern's responsible men, and he himself was the foreigner's English secretary; he knew only that his name was Walden and nothing else whatever. Lu could meet Walden only once or twice a week, the place being fixed each time and always notified by Walden beforehand, so he did not know his address or anyone connected with him. At dusk that day Lu Haifang asked to go and fetch his wife, saying that she too was a Communist and that he was afraid the organization would force her to leave, after which she could never be found. I took him there once more. But no sooner had we set foot in his house than we saw his wife burning documents. At the sight of Lu Haifang walking in with me, still well dressed and talking and laughing as though nothing had happened, this admirable woman Communist turned on him at once in the sternest terms. To this day I have not forgotten those righteous words of hers. In a voice of the utmost fury she said to Lu Haifang: "So you are not dead? You have the face to come and see me like this? I have no face to look at you, you renegade!" He was thrown into utter confusion and explained himself over and over; I too urged her from the side to be sensible and to come away with us and avoid trouble. She looked at me with a glance of the deepest contempt and said: "Unless you arrest me, I shall not go with you of my own accord as he has." Seeing her so firm, I knew persuasion was useless, and supposing it to be a momentary passion, thought she would be different if I came another day. As Lu and I left I heard several more cries of "Shameless renegade! Shameless beast!" I went back and reported this to Dai Li, suggesting that we fetch her in a day or two. Hearing it, he told me at once to arrest her immediately. As I had been busy all day and had not eaten, I went to have a meal first. When I got there again, apart from a great heap of paper ash and things flung about the floor, she was gone. I came back empty-handed and was preparing for a cursing when Dai Li, so far from losing his temper, said nothing — a big fish had been caught and only a small one had slipped the net.

Being in a good humour, he gave me a lesson instead. He thought I understood far too little of the character of Communists: that between father and son, husband and wife, it was not as it was in the Kuomintang, and that one must never be careless on the strength of a personal relationship. He told me to pay particular attention to such things in future. I was not discouraged, and had agents watch the place for three days more; she never came back. From the next day Lu Haifang went on giving up people he had had working relations with, among them, of those I arrested and whose names I can recall, the film actress ⸻. She was at that time starring in a picture called *The Goddess of Freedom*, and having arrested her the agents jeered to her face that the goddess of freedom was not free today. She was held upstairs at the detective corps and given privileged treatment, and the Shanghai district chief Wang Xinheng and the detective corps commander Wang Zhaohuai would go and chat with her and ask her to sing. Dai Li had in fact been meaning to trifle with her himself; discovering that his subordinates had got in first, he put on a serious face and gave them a long lecture, saying they would be seduced by the Communists and it would affect their work. I too had been talking to her every day, and after that did not dare seek her out casually. Another of those arrested, Yuan ⸻, also known as Yuan ⸻ ⸻, was a left-wing writer whose books and articles were all seized along with him, and he too was given privileged treatment. There was also a woman called Cheng ⸻, otherwise Cheng ⸻ ⸻, nicknamed the Black Peony, from Wanxian in Sichuan, who had studied in Germany. The agents were astonished, and much taken aback, that Cheng ⸻ should be a Communist at all. She had previously been on close terms with Yang Hu and Chen Qun, the chief butchers of the Shanghai massacre of 12 April, who had sent her to Germany, and after her return she had been very friendly with a concubine from Wanxian belonging to the Sichuan warlord Wang Lingji.

When we went to the address Lu Haifang gave to arrest her, she was not there. Wang Lingji was then living in retirement in Shanghai and she was hiding in his house, and afterwards fled to her old home at Soochow. She had shielded an important Communist, Liu ⸻ ⸻, whom Lu Haifang had given up, and got him clear of the agents' clutches, while she herself had not managed to get away in time and was arrested. The rest of those Lu gave up I did not arrest myself, and I cannot recall their names. After Walden's arrest he would not speak at all; not only was his address never found, but neither his nationality nor his real name was ever learned, for when he was taken he had no document of any kind on him, not even the foreign residence certificate a foreigner in those days had at least to carry. Once Lu Haifang had said that Walden was a responsible man, the Chinese and Western detectives of both the British and French concessions, together with the police bureau's criminal investigation corps, the Garrison Command's detective corps and the agents of the Shanghai district, were mobilized almost to the last man to investigate his address, his activities and his connections, so as to trace his organization further.

These thousands of Chinese and Western detectives and agents, with their tens of thousands of "eyes" and assistants, turned the whole city of Shanghai upside down, and got precisely nothing. Dai Li cursed them for a pack of fools and Chiang Kai-shek cursed Dai Li for a useless fellow; and after nearly a month of uproar there was still no way of establishing so much as where Walden had lived. Under interrogation not one word could be got out of him, and the newspapers could only call it "the case of the strange foreigner." Once I took Lu Haifang to court to give evidence, and Walden, seeing Lu at a distance walking towards the court, could not contain himself and spoke in court for the first time, saying one word to Lu in English: "Renegade." At this Lu could not even stand; his legs gave way and he knelt on the floor.

I forced the renegade again and again to go up, but seeing Walden standing there without moving a muscle his legs shook continuously, and he begged me to have him shot at once rather than make him go up and testify again. I kicked him and it made no difference; I had to take him away. Later, when Lu was an agent in the Juntong, I asked him why he had been so frightened that day. He said that in all his life he had never seen a man so terrifying. Although there was neither evidence nor confession against Walden, the concession authorities nevertheless agreed to hand him over to Hankow to be dealt with there, for having called Lu Haifang a renegade: those two words were enough to establish that he was a Communist. On the journey the agents watched him with the greatest curiosity to see whether he would speak or not. But the moment he was aboard the ship he chatted freely with the agents who knew English. Someone asked him why he had not spoken under interrogation. His answer is one nobody could forget. He said: "For a Communist to offer explanations under his enemy's interrogation is a sign of weakness, a plea for mercy. To give an account of anything of one's own to the enemy — even one's name or one's address — is the most contemptible conduct of a renegade, because these are matters that are not to be discussed with an enemy."

### The Methods of Escorting Prisoners

Of course, what he talked about with the agents on the ship was no more than eating and drinking and such daily matters. When they tried to learn anything further about him they were generally met with a rebuke. Only after liberation, on seeing material provided by others, did I learn that this strange foreigner's real name was Lawrence. In setting down this material about my own criminal anti-Communist past, I have not forgotten the several punishments I received in the course of struggling with Communists. More than once I was very nearly killed, for taking a Communist, whether openly or in secret, was never an easy thing. They resisted with every ounce of strength they had, and only the rarest of them submitted to be taken quietly. In that struggle of life and death I was beaten more than once. All these years later I still have a lingering fear of those martyrs who fought back so bravely. They never stopped hitting back until they were entirely powerless to resist, and I took a good many fists and boots. To tell the truth, towards the end I grew more and more fearful and cautious, and whenever I heard we were going to take a Communist my nerves were always on edge until I got back safely. The few scars on my body today, which do me no credit at all, are the marks of the just punishment I received.

In lecturing at the Juntong's many training classes I would never bring out these discreditable things of my own as teaching material, but liked to talk only of what I thought my finest "results." One evening in the summer of 1935 I was ordered to take a dozen agents to Jiangwan to raid a meeting of Communists. We had barely got near when we were spotted, and they at once scattered and ran. One Party member, whose task was to cover the rest, stayed at the mouth of the passage and did not go, covering the others' escape. As I rushed forward with two agents there was a sudden shot, and it was as though I had been punched in the left breast. I knew I was hit, but as it was a small pistol and the range fairly long it had not gone through. I looked down and saw blood coming from my chest, so I lifted my jacket to keep the blood from soaking through it, and quickly leaned against the wall to direct my assistants forward. I knew that if I did not hold out for a moment my assistants would come to look after me first, which would give the other side the chance to keep firing; so I could only set my teeth and bear it. The agents took it that I had been frightened by the shot, and hesitated a moment themselves; only after a long chase did they wound him in the leg with a shot. He fell, but went on firing, and was only taken when his ammunition was gone. Seeing me sitting on the ground bleeding heavily, he said to me in a tone of the deepest regret: "If I had known I could hit you, you running dog, I should have given you a few more." Because that one man had discharged his duty of covering and guarding, all the rest got away safely. I was taken to the Paulun Hospital, where a small Browning bullet was taken out from between two ribs on my left side; and although I was out of hospital in a week, I was left with a scar that marks my crimes for life.

In the winter of the same year I took two agents to Caojiadu to arrest a Communist who worked in literature. We had thought beforehand that he was a literary man and were not on our guard against him or taking him seriously enough, and as a result all three of us very nearly lost our lives. We had first reconnoitred his movements and knew that he usually came home very late, so we decided to make the arrest before dawn, having quietly arranged beforehand with his sub-landlord to act for us inside. We knocked softly, the door was opened, and we went straight up to his third-floor room before waking him. He opened his door, saw at a glance how things stood, politely asked us to sit down, and agreed to come with us as soon as he had dressed. Unhurried as he was, the agents nevertheless searched him and went through his clothes before letting him put them on. Three of us against one — we were quite easy about it; and as he showed not the least sign of resistance, we put our pistols away, meaning to be civil so as to get him to talk sooner.

A moment later he asked to take a hat from behind the door and put it on before coming with us, and we all thought it was going smoothly. Suddenly he pulled a hand grenade out of a felt hat, pulled the pin out very fast, and his manner changed at once to a grim one: "Move," he said, "and we all go together!" That shout struck the three of us like a thunderclap. The agents all knew what such a bomb could do: with a fast fuse it would explode the instant he let go, and exploding in a room of that size, all four of us would be dead or badly hurt with not one escaping. I for one had no wish to die like that, and would rather let him escape and be punished for it than take the risk. I knew that in such a case he was willing to trade one man for three enemies. Holding the bomb high with the pin out, he came steadily closer to us, and I could only put on a smile and tell him that anything could be talked over and there was no need for this. He was not listening; he simply went on warning us that if anyone dared reach for a pistol it would go off at once. I told my two assistants not to move and did not dare draw myself, hoping for a chance to snatch the thing from his hand and throw it out of the window. He edged slowly to the door and suddenly switched off the light. At the sound that followed, all three of us thought he had thrown the bomb down and each threw himself flat, almost in a panic. When I got my torch out and shone it, the man was gone and the door was shut. I switched the light on at once, but the door had been bolted from outside. We beat on it and shouted for the sub-landlord to come up and open it, and asked him — the man had long since gone. We blamed the sub-landlord for not stopping him, and his answer was enough to make one laugh and cry at once. "Three of you with pistols could not hold him," he said, "and you expect me, bare-handed, to dare stop him?" By the time we got out and went several ways to look for him, the streets were full of workers going on the early shift and coming off the night shift, and there was no finding him.

Afterwards, in training agent-students, although I never told the whole of this shameful business, I always warned them that in making an arrest nothing the prisoner wore or used should be handed to him directly, but must be carefully examined first and then given to him; otherwise there might be danger to life at any moment. As for the various forms of resistance I met with in those years when raiding for Communists, being beaten and bitten was almost an everyday matter, and I shall not go into it in detail.

Besides what has been described above, some renegades who went about destroying Party organizations met with punishment too, and renegades who had been years in the Juntong often had to live in fear. Wang Kequan, the deputy head of the operations group, is an example. After changing sides he did the underground Party organization no small harm, and was still not satisfied: he was forever going about with a pistol at his belt, and whenever he met a Party member he had known he would try to drag him down with him by persuasion or by force, and if a man would not be tempted he would arrest and imprison him. Once he went alone to the factory district in Pudong and met two Party members he had known before, both of whom had once worked under him. He was quite confident of being able to draw them into the Juntong; instead he was lured out into the open country and beaten half to death, and his pistol taken. If a police patrol had not passed nearby and saved him he would have been killed then and there, and from that time he never dared go about alone again.

In those days suspicious persons were constantly to be seen watching and reconnoitring outside the doors of the senior agents living in the concessions; and even near the detective corps at the Baiyun Temple on Fangxie Road in the Chinese city — the Maoshan Temple, as Shanghai people generally call it — three or four Communists who had come to reconnoitre were arrested at one time or another. Because people were being brought in there day after day, and a good many loyal and unyielding Party members were held there, there were often people investigating in the neighbourhood. Even in that zone of white terror the agents lived in constant fear, often taking every bush and tree for an enemy soldier, afraid of Communist reprisals.

Besides its main tasks of dealing with the Communists, the Juntong had other business in Shanghai. In the autumn of 1935, for instance, an assistant of one of the Shanghai district's intelligence men found out the whereabouts of the assassin, and of T.V. Soong's driver, in the case before the fighting of 28 January in which Soong had been shot at and missed at Shanghai North Station and his secretary Tang Yulu killed in his place. Dai Li was extremely interested in this new lead in a case so many years old, and instructed at once that men must be sent to bring them in, since it would please T.V. Soong.

I was ordered to go with Cheng Muxi, taking this informer, and we reconnoitred at Shi'erwei in Yizheng county, then at Guazhou and Taixing, and finally arrested one of the men who had fired at Soong at Yancheng and Soong's driver at Yangchow. This man had left Shanghai after the failed attempt and was a squad leader in the Yancheng security force. As our informer knew him, we hired a small boat and waited by the creek, and the moment the informer had lured him aboard we took him. After he had been sent to the county government and given a round of torture, he stated that at the time of the attempt on Soong they had been in touch with Soong's driver, which was how they knew Soong's movements. After the attempt the driver had fled too, and had been repairing bicycles and the like in northern Kiangsu; we caught him afterwards at Yangchow.

When we had brought the two back to Shanghai, Dai Li at once had a photograph of Soong's driver sent to Soong to look at, and he identified him as the driver who had fled after the incident. T.V. Soong was very pleased about it, and had Dai Li take me to his house to see him. He signed a cheque for five hundred yuan on the spot as a reward for me. I had barely taken it when Dai Li gave me a look, and I returned it to him most respectfully and firmly refused to accept. Dai said at once: "Protecting the minister's safety is our duty; we dare not accept a reward." Soong was much pleased to hear it. When we had bowed ourselves out, Dai Li gave me, Cheng Muxi and the informer five hundred yuan each.

When the four great families and the mass of wealthy grandees came from Nanking to Shanghai every Saturday for their shameless debauches, the Juntong protected them; and of the people openly arrested and searched over those years for posing any threat whatever to their safety, those I handled personally were not fewer than ten cases. A man had only to walk twice past the gate of one of these people's houses or villas, or glance a few times too often at them getting into or out of a car, to be called a suspected assassin; and once arrested, by the time it was established that he was entirely innocent he had spent months for nothing in a dark cell, and even on release never dared complain openly, but had on the contrary to spend a sum of money thanking these lawless agents for their "enlightened" mercy before the business could be settled. Of such examples there are more than can be counted, and I shall not give more here.

There was also the case of the attempt on Wang Jingwei's life at Nanking in November 1935, in which he was wounded and not killed: after Hua Kezhi, the head of the Chenguang News Agency who had planned it, and Zhang Yuhua and the rest had fled to Shanghai, it was again the agents of the Shanghai district and the detective corps who arrested them openly. Apart from Hua Kezhi, who got away, Zhang Yuhua, the wife of the assassin Sun Fengming and several important suspects were all taken.

I have already dealt with this in the material I wrote about Dai Li, and shall not repeat it here. Besides the criminal activities described above, the agents of the Shanghai district committed many other despicable outrages which are hateful to think of. They planted bombs several times at the Life Bookshop, and a number of readers were injured. The Life Bookshop was then in a single-storey building at the corner of Route Tenant de la Tour and Route Vallon in the French Concession; as its *Life Weekly* and other publications carried progressive opinions and were very popular with readers, a great many people came in to buy. Chiang Kai-shek and Dai Li detested the shop but, since it was in the concession, could not simply close it down, and had to resort to base tricks to disrupt it. The method was to wrap a contact-fuse explosive in newspaper, in a parcel much like a package of ordinary books; an agent would carry it into the shop, put it somewhere out of the way, quietly pull the pin and leave at once. From then on, the moment anyone touched it, it would explode. Some readers, seeing such a parcel, would take it for something another customer had forgotten and hand it in to the shop; sometimes a member of the staff would move it while tidying up, and the bomb would explode, and people standing nearby reading or buying, and the shop's staff, would be injured. The Juntong could not yet make time bombs, and it was not convenient to throw grenades openly, so this method was used. Yet though it was done two or three times, so far from frightening the people in the shop, the readers grew more numerous than ever. This small bookshop in a district that was not a commercial one drew still more interest and attention from readers after being bombed once and then again. This result, entirely contrary to Dai Li's expectation, obliged him to order the practice stopped, and to send men to work their way into the shop instead, so as to learn its affairs thoroughly and destroy it from within. That scheme lasted until the war with Japan began. Once the Juntong had control of the detective corps as an open secret-service body, there was scarcely any evil it did not do.

### Surveillance

Nothing it did was any concern even of the Garrison Command; and although in Yang Hu's time as commander he and Dai Li were hand in glove, he could not interfere in that unit's work either. I remember that when I took several agents to kidnap Mr. Liu Luyin and failed, so that we had to turn it into an open arrest in the detective corps' name, even the commanding officer knew nothing about it beforehand. When it had happened, he was found in the middle of the night and asked to sign a warrant after the event, and had no choice but to do it. The detective corps became simply a field operations unit of the Shanghai district, and the commander was only approached when something could not be dealt with. Sometimes, for convenience, and particularly when disposing of innocent people who had been wrongly arrested, the judge advocate's office was brought in to act. The judge advocate at that time, Lu Xishi, and the corps commander Wang Zhaohuai, were both disciples of Du Yuesheng and were on especially close terms. When the detective corps had beaten a suspect until there was not a whole piece of skin on him, he would at the end be sent to the judge advocate's office to be released on bail. Lu himself never objected, but the staff of the office were forever grumbling behind his back that they were "carrying the coffin" for the detective corps — in Shanghai speech, cleaning up after somebody else — and that there was no sense in it.

Of those arrested by that detective corps, even the agents who guarded them used to say that men walked in on their own legs but seldom walked out on them. In a small courtyard on the right of the entrance there were three cells, their bars made of timbers as thick as a rice bowl. Once a prisoner was inside, every movement he made could be seen clearly. On the floor in winter there were only a few heaps of straw; in summer the mosquitoes and bedbugs came in armies. Those held there were forbidden visitors and letters without exception, and wore what they had been arrested in and nothing else, receiving no help of any kind from relatives or friends. Some people knew perfectly well that a relation of theirs was held there, and were told, when they came to ask, that he was not.

A political prisoner was never allowed to see anyone there. The torture room was behind the cells, and of the Communists, members of the democratic parties and suspects arrested, scarcely one escaped torture at the first hearing, apart from the very few who broke under the strain and turned on the spot. There were no interrogators or judges here: the first hearing was generally conducted by a group head or an inspector, anyone of importance by the deputy commander or the commander, and anyone of particular importance by the district chief, or sometimes by Dai Li himself. In interrogation, three words out of order and it was "String him up." The corps' guard squad, besides its guard duties, existed to help with the torture.

The instruments were laid out in the interrogation room, and two hemp ropes for stringing men up hung from the beam. The mildest form was to tie the hands behind the back, bind the two thumbs together, and at the shout of "Up" to haul on the rope so that the prisoner was lifted from the floor. At first he would be left with his toes just touching, and the rope hitched over a fixed hook while the questioning went on. Within a few minutes, however strong the man, the sweat would be pouring off him. If he still would not talk, the shout was "Haul" — and now the whole body hung clear, the entire weight on the two thumbs, and in a minute or two he would be soaked through and in unbearable pain. The interrogating agents would sit comfortably at the side smoking and watching him struggle in his agony. If he still did not speak, a man of weaker constitution would often faint from the pain. The agents would then let the rope down and throw a basin of cold water over his head to bring him round slowly. A basin of cold water does not matter much in summer; but in winter, with the whole of his upper clothing soaked, nobody cared whether he was cold or not, and there was neither dry clothing to change into nor a fire to dry at — he had simply to sit until it dried on him. Some while later it would be done a second time, and that alone was enough to be going on with.

But there were many crueller punishments still. If stringing up did not answer, the second was the "stir-fried spare ribs." For this the prisoner was first strung up with his toes touching the ground, and sometimes his feet tied down as well, to prevent his kicking the agent doing the work. On one occasion, interrogating a young Communist, the agent who was both questioning and applying the torture was kicked hard several times by this Party member who would die rather than yield. The man was of course beaten to death afterwards, and the agents, taught by that lesson, were more careful and guarded against resistance from then on. The torture called "stir-fried spare ribs" was this: the prisoner's back was set against the wall and his clothing opened so that he could not draw back, and the agent, wearing thick coarse gloves, would press down slowly and hard on his ribs so as to compress the organs inside, and then work the hands up and down. The pressure alone was pain enough; the rubbing up and down was past all bearing. But it would not make him faint at once, which was convenient for the questioning; and once a man had been "fried" in this fashion he was not only in pain at the time but often unable to bear a touch on the chest for a month or two, and where the rubbing had been severe, in pain for years.

If that too failed to answer, the next stage was pouring cold water into the nostrils, and the "tiger's bench" and the "treading on the pole." For the water, a tap was turned on and the jet directed straight into the prisoner's nose and mouth. It is worse than drowning; a little longer and either bloody water comes out of the nose and lungs or the man faints away. As for the tiger's bench and the treading on the pole, it was common enough for these to cripple a man for life. To force revolutionaries to give up fresh leads the agents were forever devising ways to torment their bodies. After one set of these and then another, a man who did not die was left badly injured. And even when a man had given some account of things, or genuinely had nothing new to give, an agent conducting the questioning who happened to be out of temper would put him through a round or two all the same; that was a commonplace.

With women revolutionaries under interrogation the agents had a wider range still. They questioned and gratified their animal appetites at the same time. Besides the tortures described above, there were needles pushed under the nipples, bamboo splinters driven under the fingernails, whipping of the genitals with switches, and stripping the clothes off to shame them, with the agents standing round to enjoy it. Once, when a woman Communist's clothes were being stripped off to threaten her, she was roused to the last degree of fury and demanded of the agents: "Have you no mothers, no sisters? Are you human beings at all? What you are doing is below the level of the beasts!" But curse as she might, the agents held to their purpose and stripped her all the same. At the last she gave a burst of wild laughter and said: "I am not afraid of death — do you think I am afraid of this?" In the end, after enduring every kind of torture and humiliation, she died in that dark little women's lock-up, without a single word betrayed.

In sending political prisoners from Shanghai to Nanking, apart from a very few sent specially by car, the train was generally used. The method of escorting was peculiar too: handcuffs and leg-irons were rarely used, and the principle was to cover the prisoner's face by some simple disguise so that he could not be recognized at a glance — half the face or the eyes wrapped in bandages, for instance, or a pair of large dark glasses. The most inhuman device was to fill a shoe with drawing pins, so that the man being escorted could not put one foot to the ground and so could not run, and had to play the cripple, held up by agents as he got on and off the train. Wearing such a shoe, a man who was careless and trod on that foot had the pins, fixed to a stiff card, driven into his sole, and by the time he got off the train the blood would have filled the shoe; and he was not allowed to cry out. With that method one or two men were enough for an escort.

Since prisoners were being sent to Nanking constantly, the methods of escort multiplied. The usual time was the night express, boarding the sleeping car only as the train was about to leave, because on one occasion a political prisoner had used a visit to the lavatory to jump from the window. After that, political prisoners being escorted were given nothing to drink all the day they travelled, and once on the train were not allowed to relieve themselves even if they genuinely needed to, but had to hold out until Nanking.

That single night was hard indeed to get through. Agents working in the Shanghai district were themselves at risk of being sent to prison in Nanking at any time. Some were thought to be slack in their work or disloyal to the Juntong, others had corrupt private lives and misbehaved outside; the serious cases were escorted by men sent specially, and for lighter ones a "clever method" was used. For instance, A would plainly be sent to escort B, but B would deliberately be told to keep secret watch on A as far as Nanking. On arrival A would produce a letter of introduction and hand B over to the agents who had come to the station, leaving B bewildered. When two were to be sent, both A and B would be given letters of introduction and told to watch one another secretly as far as Nanking. Since they had no lateral dealings in the ordinary way, they did not know each other and did not know what they themselves had done wrong; and on arrival both would be taken away together.

This made some of the agents live in dread of the organization too, never knowing what day they might go to prison. In my few years in Shanghai the political prisoners I escorted to Nanking came to fifty or sixty, and the agents I escorted to more than twenty.

Some of these, after being moved from one place to another several times, were shut up for a decade and released only after the victory over Japan, and almost all of them were tormented into invalids or cripples. Another of the Shanghai district's main tasks over those years was keeping watch on progressive people and anti-Chiang elements in Shanghai, and almost every field agent took a hand in it. Why any particular person was under surveillance the ordinary agent did not necessarily know. Sometimes even the group head understood only in outline, and for the most part the real background was withheld; and if anything was disclosed it was a deception, the man being deliberately described as a traitor, or vaguely labelled a "counter-revolutionary" or a "reactionary."

Yet the real traitors were never touched — indeed the agents made common cause with them. About the beginning of 1937 a man named Guo was introduced to the detective corps as an inspector, the commander Wang Zhaohuai having asked Dai Li and obtained his approval. Afterwards everyone gradually came to understand that he had long been doing intelligence work for the Japanese consulate; but nobody dared say that working for the Japanese made a man a traitor, and he was on the contrary honoured with the title of "Japan hand," which he took as a distinction. Wang Zhaohuai was delighted to have got hold of such a man, and sent him on every negotiation with the Japanese. Whereas those who were regularly called traitors were, as I came to know later, democratic figures or anti-Chiang men.

During my time as head of the French Concession group, most of those my group kept watch on were well-known figures in public life, and as it would not do to call them traitors, they had to be called "counter-revolutionaries." Among those who were under surveillance without a break for years was Madame Sun Yat-sen, whom Chiang Kai-shek had personally directed should be watched constantly. All we knew at the time was that Madame Sun opposed Chiang Kai-shek and sympathized with the Communists and other democratic and progressive bodies, that she wanted to overthrow the Kuomintang dictatorship, and that she used her position to make difficulties for the government.

So Madame Sun was a thorn in the reactionaries' side; yet the agents could not conveniently move against her directly, and the method of long-term surveillance was adopted instead, chiefly to see who had dealings with her — those who could be dealt with being dealt with by the agents, and those who could not being reported upward. In the summer of 1933 the agents assassinated Mr. Yang Xingfo in Shanghai. This was on the one hand to intimidate Madame Sun and on the other a blow at the progressive people around her, an attempt to stop progressives having anything to do with her. The French Concession group was also instructed by Dai Li to find some way of planting a man inside Madame Sun's house. I first sent a woman agent to get to know the maidservant in the Sun household. This woman agent, disguised as a woman living nearby, struck up an acquaintance with her, but was very quickly found out: being careless in her work, she was noticed while sounding the maid about Madame Sun, and within a few days the maid would have nothing more to do with her. There was still one opening to be used, however, in that while they were acquainted the woman agent had learned that the maid had just divorced a hooligan of a husband, so I sent a male agent to court her instead. This agent was then only in his twenties, near enough in age to Madame Sun's maid, and good-looking besides.

For some reason I do not know, although they went about together for more than a month, and although this male agent never once asked her anything about the Sun household, in the end she refused him and would have nothing further to do with him. For all the tricks the agents produced, none of it had any effect; and yet Chiang Kai-shek would not give up, and still ordered long-term surveillance of Madame Sun, with a weekly surveillance report to be made and sent to Nanking. The Shanghai district accordingly assigned two agents to watch near the house, mornings and afternoons, noting particularly who went in and out; where people came by car the number was taken down, where they were known the name was noted, and the times of arrival and departure recorded. Busy as he was, Chiang Kai-shek never relaxed about this; but the agents doing the watching, as time went on, grew slack, and would sometimes simply enter on the weekly report the names of a few regular visitors, or pick up a little from a neighbour's servant and let that do. Since the rule was to watch only near the house and not to follow her out, what could be learned was very limited.

The agents also telephoned and wrote to Madame Sun from time to time to insult and frighten her, and all this did was to deepen her hatred and contempt for the reactionaries. Once, after the assassination of Yang Xingfo, Dai Li asked me whether Madame Sun's attitude had changed. I said it was as it had always been. He told me to give some thought to a way of dealing with her.

I considered it for a long time and worked out a plan, which I reported to Dai Li. He listened carefully, thought well of it, and told me to make my preparations and carry it out the moment he had put it to Chiang Kai-shek. My scheme was to ram the car Madame Sun rode in with another car and injure her badly, so that she could not be active any more. I had worked it out carefully: the thing should be done on a street inside the concessions. The method was to replace the windscreen of our own car with safety glass that would not shatter easily — at that time it was said that only Germany made such glass — and for the agent driving to wear a bulletproof vest to protect his chest from serious injury. When Madame Sun went out by car, the agent would drive behind her, and when the lights changed and her car stopped, he would ram the rear of it hard. Only when her car had come to a full stop would the impact have enough force to injure those inside; ramming a car in motion, since it too is going forward, does not easily cause serious injury even if it connects. Dai Li thought the method a good one, but who was to do the ramming? I said at the time that I was willing to go, and he was very glad that I should, since I was a good driver and there would be no danger of anything leaking out — it was the safest and surest arrangement. Not long afterwards, while I was still working out how to keep my own head, chest, hands and knees from serious injury in the collision, and waiting only for Dai Li's decision to carry it out, he came to Shanghai and I asked him when it was to be done. He gave a wry smile and said that Chiang Kai-shek thought the method excellent, but was concerned that if she were killed or too badly hurt, Soong Mei-ling and T.V. Soong would make trouble. So I was to keep my preparations ready and on no account act without his order. When the war with Japan came, the plan was never carried out.

At one time the agents also plotted to sabotage the car Madame Sun rode in; when Dai Li heard of it he gave instructions that this was not to be done — though he did not stop the anonymous letters and the telephone calls made to harass her.

After 1933 the agents kept Mr. Xue Duqiu under long-term surveillance and investigation, the reason being that he had resigned as a member of the Kuomintang Central Committee and of the National Government and gone to Shanghai to practise as a lawyer, which had greatly angered Chiang Kai-shek. On top of that he had, at the time of the Japanese advance on Shanhaiguan, proposed that the whole nation unite to resist Japan, which ran counter to Chiang Kai-shek's will and angered him further. On going to Shanghai he was welcomed by a number of well-known men of the business world, which drew the reactionaries' attention: they held that in resigning office he must have some other design, and one that would harm them. The agents had originally been preparing to do away with him, and afterwards, for what reason I do not know, were told only to watch and investigate. His chambers were then on an upper floor of a large building on Avenue Edward VII and his home at Jingu Village on Route Père Robert, both in the French Concession, so my little group had constantly to report on the surveillance and investigation of him. Sometimes agents would call on him as though on business; when progressives were arrested they would pose as such a person's relatives and ask him to help without a fee. Every time Mr. Xue indignantly agreed or expressed sympathy, that was the best possible intelligence material for the agents, and became strong evidence of his dissatisfaction with the government.

Again, when Mr. Fang Yiying was running the Anti-Japanese League in Shanghai, that too displeased Chiang Kai-shek, and besides sending men to watch him, the agent Su Yeguang was sent to investigate his words and doings on the strength of coming from the same province. There was also Mr. Huang Yanpei and the Chinese Vocational Education Society he directed, which was at the corner of Route Voyron and Route Vallon in the French Concession, so that the agents used the French Park opposite for the surveillance. The Life Bookshop run by Mr. Zou Taofen was naturally an object of surveillance and investigation as well.

As for those whom we were told from time to time to watch, they were beyond counting, and I shall not list more here. Surveillance was then the heaviest burden on every group, almost every agent having several people to watch. Each group at that time had only a dozen or so core agents; and though every agent had a good many friends and relatives he could use, they were still not enough to watch so many people at once, so the less important objects of surveillance were not watched regularly but looked in on every few days to see whether they had moved. Chiang Kai-shek and Dai Li thought a great deal of this work and were forever threatening people with "Put him under surveillance!" In fact very little came of it and it did little good; all it produced was a kind of terror and a general feeling of disgust.

The agents' surveillance of Communist underground workers at that time was very ineffective and Dai Li reproached them for it constantly, because Party members were highly vigilant and not casual like the people described above. My little group several times concentrated its strength on watching several Communist suspects at Xiaoyou Lane in Zikawei and at Dapuqiao, with no result whatever — we did not even establish when the people we were watching had moved away. Dai Li personally ordered the Shanghai district to arrest a Communist leader of the Shanghai underground named Chen — I heard later that this was General Chen Geng — of whom all that was known was that he was a first-class Whampoa graduate, and a photograph was even obtained. Wu Naixian, being himself a first-class Whampoa man, took the field in person, and in the end we never so much as saw his shadow.

After the Xi'an Incident, when Vice-Chairman Zhou Enlai went to Shanghai and stayed at the Xinya Hotel on North Sichuan Road, the Shanghai district concentrated a large body of agents on watching and following him, and Dai Li directed the arrangements himself. Although Zhou went out every day, in the end nothing whatever was discovered of his contacts with the Communist underground in Shanghai. Every theatre, shop and restaurant Zhou Enlai had been to was investigated and studied for a long time afterwards, again with no result whatever; and Dai Li's cursing the agents of the Shanghai district for a pack of fools and good-for-nothings did not help.

Before the war, because the Juntong followed Chiang Kai-shek's will and put its whole strength into dealing with the Communists, the democratic parties and anti-Chiang elements, it paid no attention whatever to Japan, the real and principal enemy. Only after the Marco Polo Bridge Incident did it set about work against Japan in a scramble, hurriedly setting up sleeper groups in Hongkou and Zhabei and groups at Wusong and Jiangwan. By then Zhou Weilong had succeeded Wang Xinheng as district chief, and Dai Li hurried up from Nanking to make the arrangements, calling a meeting of a dozen of the Shanghai core agents and cadres at his residence near Fenglin Bridge in the French Concession. The first thing he proposed was that a sleeper group be set up at once inside the Japanese-controlled area of Hongkou, with a wireless set and about ten members. Zhou Weilong proposed two men on the spot to head it, but both said their identities had long been known and they could not establish themselves there, and refused to go. When my name was raised third, I thought that as I was constantly engaged in operations I was not suited to lying low. But before I could open my mouth Dai Li agreed at once, and encouraged me: "You are a Hunan man and have never been afraid to die. You had better go." I had no choice but to agree.

Next day I set about finding men, and by dragging in a brother of mine who had come to Shanghai to see me, and several old friends, I made up eight in all. The wireless operator assigned, Gu Shenghu, was brave enough. Zhou Weilong's alias at the time was Ren Zhong, the Shanghai district's alias was Ren Zhiyuan, and my alias was changed to Du Kang. Before setting out I asked Dai Li whether we were still to pay attention to the Communists at the same time, since only a few days before I had seen the detective corps arresting a dozen Communist suspects at Shiliupu and in Pudong under the name of rounding up traitors and locking them up. Although Kuomintang-Communist cooperation was publicly acknowledged at the time, persecution of the Communists had never stopped for a moment; it was only rather more secret than before. Dai Li answered me that I need not go out of my way to work on the Communists, but that if I came across anything I should still do something about it. The Hongkou group had not been established a month before the whole of it was driven out by the Japanese. Gu Shenghu and I packed a small wireless set into a child's pushchair and pushed his baby, just a year old, out along with it. Zhou Weilong wanted me to go back and re-establish it, but there was no getting in again by any means. By then there was not one agent in the whole of Hongkou who could keep a footing there — this being the result of years of concentrating our whole strength against the Communists and the people, so that when the great enemy stood before us there was not the slightest preparation against the real enemy of the nation.
