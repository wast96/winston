## Juntong Operations in Kunming on the Eve of Yunnan's Liberation

On December 9, 1949, Yunnan declared its peaceful liberation. I was then chief of the Yunnan provincial station of the Nationalist secret-service organ, the Ministry of National Defence's Bureau of Secrets Preservation (the name the Juntong bureau took after its reorganization), and I was one of the chief plotters and participants in the Chiang Kai-shek clique's death-throes and its various schemes on the eve of Yunnan's peaceful liberation. To preserve truthfully a little historical material, I set down first what I could grasp within the range of my own activity at the time, leaving the rest to those concerned to give a full account.

### The People's Democratic Movement Rising, the Secret Services' Plots Rampant

Back during the war against Japan, Kunming became a democratic bastion of the great rear, and the people of Yunnan, long under the influence of democratic and patriotic thinking, saw the anti-Japanese, anti-Chiang movement rise steadily. Although after the victory Chiang Kai-shek disposed of Long Yun and placed the whole of Yunnan under his reign of terror, and with the most barbarous and cruel bloody-massacre policy suppressed the Kunming students' patriotic movement many times, and assassinated the two staunch democratic fighters Li Gongpu and Wen Yiduo, the people of Yunnan were not cowed; the progressive anti-Chiang forces rose higher day by day.

At that time the people's armed forces in many districts of Yunnan grew ever stronger, keeping the Chiang forces of the Yunnan clique stationed there run off their feet, able to hold only a few of the larger cities and a few communication lines, and quite helpless against "the other side of the mountains" (as the Yunnan people then called the liberated areas). Yu Chengwan, commander of the Nationalist 26th Army stationed at Kunming, was at his wits' end and daily asked the Centre for more troops. Yu Chengwan sent a division to guard over a hundred kilometres of the Xukun railway from Kunming through Zhanyi, and even so it was constantly sabotaged by the people's armed forces. The division commander Shi Butian said openly: "I ought not to be called Shi Butian [Stone Mends Heaven] but Shi Bulu [Stone Mends Road]. I cannot even keep up with mending this road day after day — where would I find time to mend heaven?" Chiang Kai-shek was resolved to hold Yunnan, the most convenient base for reaching abroad to obtain imperialist aid; but with defeats on every front and the crack Yunnan-clique forces he relied on for anti-Communism gambled clean away, he now had no strength to spare. In the spring of 1949, after the People's Liberation Army crossed the Yangtze and liberated Nanking, the people of Yunnan longed impatiently to be liberated the sooner and cast off the Chiang clique's dark rule. By then the Yunnan Garrison Headquarters that Chiang Kai-shek had posted directly to suppress the people had been abolished at Lu Han's request, its commander-in-chief He Shaozhou transferred away, and military and civil power in Yunnan gathered in Lu Han's one person. Chiang Kai-shek meant to govern Yunnan with a Yunnanese and settle the region for the time being; but in fact, having no adequate troops to send, he had in many respects to defer to Lu Han.

Before this, the local power represented by Lu Han and the central power represented by He Shaozhou had been at loggerheads in Yunnan. He Shaozhou was a first-class Whampoa graduate, trusted by Chiang Kai-shek and, being He Yingqin's adopted son (He Yingqin having no son of his own, he made this nephew his son), always arrogant and overbearing; he looked down on Lu Han as a Yi general and wished to squeeze Lu out and take the Yunnan governorship himself. So while he and Lu Han were civil on the surface, behind Lu's back he was forever sneering and cursing at him. This "young lord" of the Nationalist government had very much the airs of a rich man's son, spending his whole days at hunting or tennis or cards or women. His "three sports" had their friends — the heads of the various central organs at Kunming, some foreign consuls there, Yunnan military and civil men disaffected or out of favour with Lu Han, and some big merchants — who played along with him while helping him scheme against Lu Han.

The moment I reached Yunnan I threw in with He Shaozhou and helped him with his anti-Lu Han activity. As I too was fond of hunting and tennis, I quickly became one of his "two sports" friends. He Shaozhou was a man of no political head, bent only on rising in office and getting rich. Soon after reaching Yunnan he made a great fortune, chiefly in the opium trade. Opium bought for ten thousand yuan in Yunnan could be sold for a million in Shanghai, and for more in Hong Kong. Given his position and connections then, one such deal a month brought in an astonishing income. Newly arrived and without a house of his own, he borrowed a fairly large garden villa from the old Yunnan warlord Liu Zhilu, and still found it not good enough. A few months into his post as garrison commander he resolved to build a luxurious residence with several thousand taels of gold, meaning to settle in Yunnan for the long term; but no sooner was the house finished, before he had even moved in, than he was transferred away. In his time as garrison commander he not only cruelly suppressed the Yunnan people's democratic movement and the students' patriotic movement but was himself a demon who would shoot people dead at whim.

I often went hunting with him at places tens or a hundred-odd kilometres from Kunming. Once my car had a little trouble and he asked me into his. On the road I heard him with my own ears order the driver not to swerve for people or livestock on the highway and send the car over a cliff. I asked why. He said: "Haven't you seen the cars overturned on the mountain roads? Nine times in ten it's because they swerved to avoid people and drove into the gully. Don't swerve — drive straight through, and you avoid the danger of overturning." I said: "But wouldn't that kill or hurt the people and livestock on the road?" He laughed: "Is our life the important one, or theirs?"

When we went hunting we always liked to ride in an open car, so we could fire at any bird we spotted along the highway. Once he bought a brand-new sedan and, wanting to test its performance on a long run, asked me to go hunting with him near Songming in the new car. Coming back, with night falling, he was anxious to hurry home and kept urging the driver faster. On a narrow side road we came up behind a ten-wheel military truck that deliberately would not yield, driving down the centre of the road; horn as we might, it would not give way. The driver several times tried to force past, and the military truck's driver, just as the small car was about to pass, deliberately edged left and nearly forced us into the gully, so we dared not force past again. He Shaozhou cursed furiously in the car, and later, reaching a wide spot, the small car shot past. He Shaozhou then had the driver stop the car crosswise in the road, and the military truck behind had to stop too. Since our escort car with the guards had not yet caught up, and He Shaozhou, I and one of his aides were all in hunting clothes, the military driver took us for a few rich merchants and demanded of our driver why he blocked the way. He Shaozhou at once jumped down, drew his pistol, and roared: "I'll have your life!" I thought he only meant to scare the driver, and never imagined he really fired a shot at the man and wounded his arm. Seeing this, the military driver turned and ran for the roadside woods; He Shaozhou chased and fired again, missed, and the man vanished into the trees.

When the guards caught up, He Shaozhou wanted them to go and seize the man, but with night falling he came back fuming. Home at once, he had his aide telephone for the colonel of that motor-transport regiment and gave him a dressing-down. On my asking, it turned out the colonel was an old driver of his uncle He Yingqin's, promoted step by step to colonel, so he was utterly deferential and swallowed the humiliation. As I left, I advised him not to do such things again. He said carelessly that this time the fellow was lucky, that he had spared his life. He added, quite pleased, that once on a Guangxi highway he had met such a situation and killed the driver with two shots. Because he did every evil in Yunnan, he made the whole place seethe with anger. After his transfer he hated Lu Han bitterly, and besides scattering everywhere, in the name of Yunnanese abroad, handbills attacking Lu Han, he had several hundred sent to me and asked me to distribute them for him in Yunnan. To keep from spoiling my relations with Lu Han, I destroyed them all.

While the Garrison Headquarters existed, the Bureau's Yunnan station, on getting intelligence of local people's armed activity, could ask the Garrison Headquarters to send troops at once to attack, or ask them to come forward and arrest suspects; and if the wrong person was seized it did not matter. Not only did He Shaozhou himself strongly support my activities in Yunnan, but the Garrison Headquarters' judge-advocate, Li Longkai, was a man I had used at the start of the war when I was inspectorate head of the Changde Garrison Headquarters in Hunan, and whatever I told him to do he would do. After the Garrison Headquarters was abolished, though I could still carry out tasks through the open secret-service organs the Yunnan station led — the Yunnan provincial police department, the Kunming police bureau and its criminal-police corps, and the provincial government's investigation office — these bodies were all under Lu Han's jurisdiction and had to take his direction.

Once a hand-crank wireless transceiver of the Yunnan local people's armed force, the Yun-Gui-Qian Border Region Column, broke down, and men were sent to Kunming to repair it. After the Juntong Kunming group secretly reported this to me, I decided to arrest the men when they came to fetch the set. About a week later, the set just repaired, I notified the criminal-police corps to have some men ready to make an arrest. I did not tell the corps the task or the place, for fear of a leak, saying only that the Kunming group's men would lead them there at the time. On the day, sure enough the men were seized together with the machine. Under interrogation the man admitted he was of the "Border Column." Wanting to trace the lead further, to learn more about the "Border Column," I did not send him to Chungking.

Another time, an XXX, a former subordinate of Lu Han's who had been mayor of Yingkou, was taken alive when Yingkou was liberated, and released after a period of re-education by the PLA. Returning to Kunming, he spread word of the liberation policies — how the PLA treated prisoners well and so on. I had the Kunming police chief Wang Wei detain him. The next day Wang came to see me, saying Lu Han had telephoned to demand why XXX had been arrested and ordered his immediate release. Wang answered that it was I who had wanted him held, and Lu Han said no more. Wang hoped I would explain this to Lu, to spare him difficulty. I went at once to see Lu and told him XXX was spreading Communist policy and disturbing the morale of the troops and people, so I had detained him. Lu said this man had a tiresome mouth and loved to talk wildly, and agreed to hold him a while. A few days later I got a letter in Mao Renfeng's own hand, saying he had arranged with Hu Zongnan for Hu to agree to send his wife, the Juntong agent Ye Xiadi, to Kunming rather than Taiwan (Hu being on bad terms with Chen Cheng), and that he would detach an army or two to strengthen the Centre's force in Yunnan. Mao wanted me to go to Xi'an in person to see Hu Zongnan, explain Yunnan's situation and importance, urge Hu to set up a base in the Southwest, and bring Ye Xiadi to Kunming on the way. On getting the letter I set out the next day. Before leaving, I reckoned Lu Han might force Wang Wei to release XXX and the "Border Column" radio men. The secret-service organs then led by the Bureau's Yunnan station and not under Lu Han's jurisdiction were the Dian-Yue railway police department, the fourth-district highway engineering bureau's police section, the fourth transport office's police-inspection group, the 53rd Arsenal's police-inspection group, and the guard corps. I meant to move XXX and the "Border Column" radio men from the police bureau to these units and imprison them for the time being; but for lack of time, and thinking Lu Han might not learn of it once I had left Kunming, I did not transfer them. Later I learned that the moment I set out, Lu Han ordered Wang Wei to release XXX and the "Border Column" men, machine and all, at once. On my return from the Northwest I went to see Lu Han, and he put it off as a proposal of the provincial assembly, released without waiting to consult me on my return. Of course I would not fall out with him over this; I only reported the matter in full to Mao Renfeng, who also agreed I should not quarrel with Lu Han over it.

After the Garrison Headquarters was abolished and He Shaozhou left Yunnan, Lu Han, toward the Yunnan people's democratic movement, reversed the harsh suppression He Shaozhou had used and adopted a mild attitude, tacitly permitting and supporting it. By the summer of 1949 some progressive papers in Kunming were repeatedly demanding openly the expulsion of the Nationalist central government's organs from Yunnan and of the Chiang Kai-shek clique's agents, and constantly denouncing in earnest many of the Nationalists' perverse reactionary policies and decrees.

The people of Yunnan also refused in numbers to use the gold-yuan notes the Nationalists issued, using instead the "half-open" hard coin that had circulated in Yunnan before in place of the gold-yuan note. Several Southwest provinces, under Yunnan's influence, switched to silver dollars and silver small-coin. The Nationalist Ministry of Finance, seizing on this, squeezed the people again by bringing out its stock of nickel subsidiary coin to circulate as silver dollars. It issued an order restoring the long-abolished nickel coin at ten jiao to the silver dollar. Before this decision was published, senior officials of the Finance Ministry and the Central Bank, learning of it, colluded with merchants to buy up the nickel coin scattered among the people everywhere. In a few days a few became overnight fortunes at huge profit, while the many were badly cheated, selling their hoarded nickel coin cheap at a few jiao or even a few fen a catty. Then the people refused the nickel coin too, and the Nationalist government could only devalue again the nickel coin whose use it had ordered restored only days before, stirring up worse chaos.

On February 11, 1949, the Yunnan *Central Daily News* suddenly printed a report that counterfeits of the purple fifty-yuan gold-yuan notes printed by the Centre had been discovered. At the same time the Central Bank in Kunming announced that fifty-yuan gold-yuan notes, genuine or false, would be suspended from use. At this all the shops of course refused them. The next day was the Lantern Festival, and the great mass of citizens holding such notes, wanting to buy something and unable to spend them, thronged the Central Bank in Kunming to ask to exchange them, but the bank would not even open for business. The crowd grew and grew, and in a while over two thousand had gathered at the door shouting for it to open, and the bank paid no heed. In their fury someone cried out to smash it, and a great many people tore off the doors and windows, rushed in, and threw the account books and the like into the street. The reactionaries' troops and police rushed in to suppress them, firing into the air to disperse the crowd on one hand and seizing people wildly on the other, and those seized were shot on the spot one after another. Later, when many local gentry and provincial-assembly members repeatedly interceded, the several dozen others arrested were handed to military justice. The innocent citizens killed this time — Zhang Chang'an, Liu Daixue, Li Xuecheng, Wang Cai, Xu Ji and others — came to twenty-one in all. Though the gold-yuan-note tumult subsided for the time, it stirred still greater popular anger.

No few progressive members of the Yunnan assembly not only raised in session their reproaches of many reactionary policies and decrees, and supported progressive opinion's demand to expel the Nationalists' organs, troops and agents from Yunnan, but also openly and ceaselessly attacked the Chiang Kai-shek clique's every doing. The Bureau's Yunnan station reported all this to Taiwan as it came. Especially after the Yunnan Garrison Headquarters was abolished, Lu Han carried out no more large-scale attacks on the Yunnan people's armed forces, and some local security corps kept a mutual non-aggression with the people's armed forces led by Zhu Jiabi, Zhuang Tian and others, which drew Taiwan's great attention. But Taiwan judged it not yet the moment to settle the Yunnan problem by force and remove Lu Han, and decided to withdraw the Centre's tax, audit and secret-service organs at Kunming.

In mid-July 1949, the Bureau's Yunnan station got Mao Renfeng's telegraphed order to withdraw the Yunnan station, the Yunnan radio sub-station, some already half-open local organizations, and individual agents whose cover was blown, together with the Bureau's Traffic Police secret-service forces in Yunnan, to Chungking; but the station chief Shen Zui was directed to remain at Kunming for the present and decide otherwise as the situation developed. I went at once to see Lu Han and reported this. He approved Taiwan's arrangement, holding that the people had indeed long hated the Juntong agents' various activities in Kunming to the marrow, and that their withdrawal could ease the people's feeling somewhat. Lu Han also said there was decidedly no problem in my staying at Kunming for the time, and hoped I would keep in constant touch with him.

In late July 1949 the Bureau's Yunnan station formally announced its withdrawal. In fact the station organization was shrunk, by the method of cutting the weak and keeping the strong, and a few able men moved into my home. After the Yunnan sub-station withdrew, a small wireless set for communication with Taiwan and Chungking was left, installed downstairs in my house. The so-called abolition of the Kunming group likewise only cancelled the group office and withdrew the agents whose cover was blown; those not blown, especially the several placed at Lu Han's side, such as the registry chief Wang Wenbin (alias Lin Zihua) and the secretary Jiang XX, not only did not leave but stepped up their activity and were told to recruit new agents. Of the county groups, only the three at Kaiyuan, Mengzi and Hekou were abolished; the organizations and radio sets at Zhaotong, Baoshan, Cheli, Fohai, Longchuan and elsewhere were never touched. Though some armed secret-service forces were withdrawn, the leadership only changed the medicine while keeping the same broth. Tian Dongyun, department head of the Dian-Chuan and Dian-Yue railway police, was directed to lead all the armed agents in Yunnan to Chungking, the two railways' police department still headed by the Juntong agent Su Zixiu. The fourth-district highway engineering bureau's guard-inspection group and the fourth transport office's police section, though a part of the traffic-police forces was moved, kept as their heads the Juntong agents Luo Gang and Zhang Zhen. The 53rd Arsenal's police-inspection group, being far out in the suburbs, was not touched at all, only its head changed. The best-manned open secret-service organ, the Kunming police bureau's criminal-police corps, not only had no one moved and stayed under Zhou Boxian but even took some of the corps' most diehard elements, He Yungui and others, into the Juntong organization. Nominally handed over but in fact still under Juntong internal control were the Yunnan police-security department, the Yunnan provincial government's investigation office and the Kunming police bureau. On the surface a great batch of agents seemed truly withdrawn; in fact the moment they left, Mao Renfeng at once sent the Bureau's undercover-placement section chief and Southwest special-district undercover-placement commissioner Ren Hongchuan, who reached Kunming in early August, and had me help him set up in Kunming an undercover group and two undercover radio sets. The group's main members, besides the head Ding Xiang and the members Wang Wenbin, Ma XX and others, all Yunnan-station agents whose cover was intact, included several doctors recruited. For instance the physician Ming Shaoqi, wanting to open a clinic on Zhonghua Road in Kunming but short of money, was introduced by his relative the Juntong agent Li Ruifeng, and given a thousand hard-coin by the Juntong on condition he join the Kunming undercover group, work for the Juntong after liberation, and shelter an agent in his clinic. Through secret-society ties too, the agent Jiang Yi and others found for the group Ma XX, an internal-medicine head at the hospital run by the Kunming Christian church. The group's radio operator Zheng Yuhe was chosen and sent from the Chungking sub-station. Then, in case the Kunming undercover group ran into trouble and the Kunming work could not continue, a double layer was arranged: a so-called independent undercover set was set up, with the operator Feng Zefen chosen and sent from Chungking as its head, doubling as intelligence officer, forming no lateral tie with the Kunming undercover group and communicating only directly with Taiwan — such an agent was then called an all-round intelligence officer. Chiang Kai-shek's directions on the Yunnan problem Mao Renfeng later told me to my face. Chiang held that Yunnan's democratic movement was no cause for worry, that the moment was not yet ripe, that Lu Han was a little unreliable but would not act at once. This was because, on one hand, the PLA was still far from Yunnan and could not help him, so rash action could not hold; and on the other, Lu Han's own force was limited, only a dozen ill-equipped security corps, whereas the central army's Yu Chengwan 26th Army was stationed just in the Kunming suburbs, enough to control Kunming, and Li Mi's 8th Army had already moved into Yunnan, and with Liu Bolong's 89th Army on the Guizhou-Yunnan border, these three armies' strength was more than enough to deal with Lu Han. So the main thing was to keep Lu Han from contacting the Communists and from dealings with Long Yun, while winning him over as far as possible and avoiding provoking him, and at the same time keeping him in some dread so he dared not act rashly. So it was decided to use terror: to remove first the several men at Lu's side who were progressive in thinking and could influence him politically.

From early August to early September 1949, I received in succession three coded telegrams from Mao Renfeng in his personal cipher, ordering me to assassinate at once in Kunming General Yang Jie and another head of the Yunnan Revolutionary Committee of the Kuomintang, Professor Chen Fuguang, along with the Yunnan provincial civil-affairs commissioner An Enpu, the Yunnan Security Command chief of staff Xie Chongwen, and the Yunnan security brigade commander Long Zehui — five men in all. For some had great influence on Lu Han's political line, and some were Lu's intimates. On getting this order I did not, for many reasons, carry it out in earnest. Mao Renfeng wired repeatedly to reproach me, explaining again and again that removing these few men first would greatly help turn the Yunnan situation and suppress the democratic movement; but I always put it off as difficult and being worked on.

In fact it would have been fairly easy then to assassinate Yang Jie and Chen Fuguang in Kunming, for my house at No. 51 Jingyuan New Village, Sanjieqiao, was diagonally across from Yang Jie's residence. Though Yang had a small jeep, he seldom used it to get about. His household was only an aide, an odd-job man, a driver and a foster-daughter. I knew his movements well. He dined almost every day at the home of the great Yunnan capitalist, the "tuo-tea king" Yan Xiecheng. The Yans could lay on several banquets at any time, and even a plain meal ran to a dozen dishes. I too went there sometimes, and meeting Yang Jie always liked to chat with him. Leaving home each day he passed a large lawn across from my house, and when my children were playing there he always liked to stop and fondle them, so we were well acquainted. I had meant to ambush him in a small lane leading to that lawn when he came home late at night from some engagement.

I found two action men and worked it out with them, and they too thought killing Yang Jie easy — the moment I decided, they would act at once. But when I was talking with them, my mother happened to be on the balcony outside my office and heard the whole conversation. The moment the two action men left she came in, furious, and pointing at my head said: "I have always taught you that a man may hold no office but must be a human being. If you will not heed me and go killing again, I will break off being your mother." This unexpected turn left me no choice but to put off the killing of Yang Jie planned for the next day.

The Revolutionary Committee head in Yunnan, Chen Fuguang, lived in the Jiatang New Village residential area behind my house, its gate on the road, without even a wall. Though the Yunnan station had withdrawn, among the agents left were Dai Jiyong, Yang Wenzhi and several others who had always done assassination work. Mao Renfeng also sent to Kunming two men who did assassination work for the Bureau, Wang XX and Qin Jingchuan. Qin was an executioner the Juntong had cultivated for years, and I too had done such work in the Juntong for years and served as instructor in it at many agent training classes, so Mao Renfeng thought there would be no problem. My reason for holding back was mainly fear I could not get away, for the moment these men were assassinated it would beyond doubt be guessed I had done it. If I first went to Zhanyi or Qujing and then had them act, there was no certainty, and it might rather beat the grass and startle the snake. The whole situation was then developing fast, the PLA sweeping toward the Southwest like a broken bamboo, plainly to reach Yunnan and Guizhou before long. I knew well that the handful of beaten troops the Nationalists had left were no match for the Communists, and killing a few men would not save the losing game. In that situation I was rather disheartened, and with my mother's words besides, I weighed personal interest heavily and let many favourable chances slip. Later Mao Renfeng severely reproached me for not assassinating Yang Jie in Kunming and letting him flee to Hong Kong, blaming me for slackness in carrying out orders. As for An Enpu, Xie Chongwen and Long Zehui, I had still greater misgivings: on one hand I feared that killing them would only enrage Lu Han, endangering my own life and perhaps making the Yunnan situation change faster still, so that in trying to hold Yunnan I would lose it the sooner; on the other, all three were serving officials who went about by car with guards at home, so they were harder to get at. Even so, on getting the second order I did make some dispositions, for Juntong discipline was strict and repeated failure to carry out orders drew severe punishment. Especially then, Mao Renfeng, to save the dying game, might at any time shoot a senior agent to overawe the wavering within. Much as I was unwilling a hundred and twenty times over, I could not but make some arrangements.

After the Yunnan station withdrew, its outer organizations still existed, the one with some strength being the "Jie Society" led by Li Jiajie, deputy head of the fourth transport office and commander of its transport corps. This secret-society organization's members were mostly the fourth transport office's drivers and transport workers. Those who joined were mostly lawless men who smuggled, trafficked drugs and evaded customs and tax, and no few were habitual bandits who robbed and killed on the Dian-Burma highway.

I was especially displeased with An Enpu, with whom I had never got on personally. To carry out the order, I had Li Jiajie pick from his men four sure hands at assassination, gave each a US-made automatic pistol, ten taels of gold as expenses in advance, and promised each ten taels more once it was done. Li Jiajie also lent them his own car, meaning to strike on a road An Enpu often travelled. As these men could easily get close to An's driver, the plan was first to buy the driver over, have him deliberately break down once on some quiet road, and the men following behind could assassinate An at once.

Besides, the ruffians led by the Hong Gang organization Wushengshan, controlled by the Juntong agent Yu Qian, were desperate men who did every evil, and I had him pick a few to work on murdering Xie Chongwen. Fearing trouble when they acted, I told them to tell me the day before they decided to strike, so that I could at the same time arrange to kill Yang Jie and Chen Fuguang and make ready in good time to flee Kunming. I knew that the killing of either An or Xie would make Lu Han turn on me at once and arrest or kill me, so I had to plan ahead.

As for Long Zehui, I did not mean to kill him then: on one hand he and I were on good personal terms, and on the other, being Lu's kinsman, killing him would not only bring me into head-on conflict with Lu but drive Lu the more firmly toward the Communists and into hatred of the Nationalists and Chiang Kai-shek. In replying to Mao Renfeng's second order I said only that I was preparing to kill the four, Yang, Chen, An and Xie.

Mao Renfeng wired a third time to press me to act quickly, and I had just severally summoned the several chosen assassins and decided to proceed simultaneously when I suddenly got intelligence that Lu Han was preparing to go to Chungking to see Chiang Kai-shek, so I notified each overnight to hold off. For if a pistol went off in Kunming, Lu Han might not go to Chungking, and I could not then bear that responsibility. Weighing the two, getting Lu to go and see Chiang Kai-shek early was the more important. So before liberation there was no second bloody case in Kunming after the 1947 assassinations of Li Gongpu and Wen Yiduo. Before Lu went to Chungking to see Chiang, I stayed in Kunming ostensibly to keep in touch with Lu for the time, but in fact my activity in this period was more intense than in any before. The radio set in touch with Taiwan was downstairs in my house, and we kept contact day and night. Anything happening in Kunming, especially Lu Han's every word and act, once such intelligence could be got, reached Taiwan minutes later. The heads of some open secret-service organs at Kunming held with me, almost every day or two, a "open-secret conference" (that is, a joint Juntong meeting of the Yunnan station's secret leading units and the open secret-service units), exchanging intelligence, studying countermeasures, and passing on Taiwan's directions. These conferences too were more secret and tense than before. Besides, I personally had to keep in constant touch with the 26th Army commander Yu Chengwan and the 8th Army commander Li Mi stationed at Kunming. Li Mi then lived at the home of the Yunnan national capitalist Wang Shaoyan; it was inconvenient to go there to talk, and his seeking me out was too conspicuous, so we always arranged by phone first and met on some out-of-the-way road, talking in the car. Li Mi had then borrowed an old sedan from Lu Han, with a movable thick glass partition between the front and back seats which, raised, kept even the driver in front from hearing clearly. Sometimes, to talk better, he got into my car and I drove out to the suburbs myself, where we could speak without reserve. Li Mi's ambition then was to take Lu Han's place in Yunnan; being a Yunnanese, and Chiang Kai-shek having sent him back to Yunnan with just this in view, on the surface he was very respectful to Lu while at bottom forever wanting to use me to report to Taiwan more intelligence of Lu's unreliability, so as to get his wish the sooner.

I was then very suspicious of a young concubine Yu Chengwan had newly taken, and had her investigated and watched. As Yu Chengwan told it, introducing this concubine of murky origin, she had been found for him by the famous novelist Zhang Henshui. It happened that during the war Yu commanded the 57th Division defending Changde city in Hunan; his division's code name was "Tiger Braves," and all officers and men wore armbands with those two characters. Zhang Henshui had been to Changde and had some dealings with Yu, and wrote a novel, *Long Live the Tiger Braves*, on Yu Chengwan's defence of Changde. This Suzhou young lady, having read the book, so admired Yu that she resolved, come what may, to give herself to the "tiger-brave hero" Zhang Henshui's pen had drawn, willingly becoming his concubine and living with him in secret. I was very suspicious of her, fearing she bore some political mission to work on Yu Chengwan, so I secretly had her watched and told Yu's deputy commander Peng Zuoxi to keep an eye on her and watch for any activity of hers at army headquarters. Peng Zuoxi, then very much wanting the command, did not relax on this task. I encouraged him: the moment he found anything, tell me at once for timely handling, and the command might yet be his.

Besides such sowing-of-discord secret-service work, I sometimes had to do some work of reconciliation. Since Li Mi returned to Kunming, Yu Chengwan had been out of sorts. For Yu wanted to take charge of Yunnan's military side, no matter who was governor, while Li wanted both military and civil power in one hand; each had his own calculation and his own backers, so the two were outwardly civil but inwardly at odds, mutually suspicious. I was very anxious over this, fearing their failure to cooperate would affect the whole situation. To bring them to an understanding I spent no little breath. Right up to when both were later detained by Lu Han and lodged together, though they became sworn brothers pledged to live and die together, on questions of personal interest each still had his own calculation.

At the time Taiwan was much dissatisfied with the Yunnan station's intelligence work, and I had to spend time daily on it myself. My centre for this was the Lexiang Restaurant that Yu Qian ran on Huguo Road. A batch of so-called well-informed people connected with me came there daily to play cards, smoke opium and eat and drink — chiefly the Guishan district administrative inspector Jiang Zixiao, Long Yun's former secretary Liu Gongdu, the Kunming chamber-of-commerce head Deng Hefeng, the postal-remittance savings-bureau manager Li Shaoyang, the *Morning Post* publisher Wang Gongxu, the physician Wang Zihan and others. Though they could only pick up hearsay, I could get some of Lu's more trusted men to confirm and supplement it — chiefly the provincial-government secretary-general Zhu Jingxuan and Lu's aide-de-camp chief Zhu Ziying.

Zhu Jingxuan and Zhu Ziying had always been close to the Juntong and knew I sought them with a purpose. Zhu Ziying, though Lu's intimate, had no political head. Knowing he was well acquainted with Lu's daily life, I never asked him about Lu directly but always chatted in a tone of concern for Lu, and from it got no little intelligence of Lu. He once let fall to me something of Lu Han's dealings with the "Border Column." Once Lu Han had him deliver weapons to aid the "Border Column," and he dared not tell me before going, only after returning. I said: had you told me first I would not have stopped you; in future tell me anything first, and I will never make things hard for you.

Besides, I set going the Juntong senior agent Qiu Kaiji, a former police-security commissioner, and the acting chairman of the Nationalist Yunnan party headquarters, Pei Cunfan, to use their past ties with Lu and their Kunming high-society connections to find things I needed. These intense and frequent activities were all, without doubt, aimed at Lu Han. At the same time I watched for Long Yun's possibly directing his old subordinates in Kunming from Hong Kong. What Chiang Kai-shek most feared was Lu and Long working together, and he hoped to deepen the rift between them. He especially feared that General Yang Jie would use his former position as head of the Army University to draw in senior Nationalist officers, take part in unofficial military activity and influence Lu Han, so he was resolved to remove him. At the time Chiang Kai-shek on the surface agreed to withdraw the Centre's organs and secret-service organizations from Yunnan to ease the Yunnan people's anti-Chiang feeling, but in fact he prepared to retreat in order to advance, detaching some troops to Yunnan to strengthen his control. He sought only to steady the Yunnan situation for a time, so that not only could he yield to Lu's demands but even defer to him, waiting only until he had force enough to drive Lu out in one stroke and place Yunnan wholly under his control. Lu Han was well aware of Chiang Kai-shek's ways too. So Lu, on the pretext of local unrest, raised the slogan of protecting the region and its people, and taking advantage of Chiang Kai-shek's inability to spare more troops for Yunnan and his wish to keep Yunnan as a rear, kept asking Chiang Kai-shek to build up the local armed force. Chiang Kai-shek, constrained by the situation, could only agree to expand Lu's security corps into two armies. By the rules, Nationalist armies all had to have Juntong secret-service organizations — an army group's investigation office or intelligence department, an army's investigation group or the second section of its staff department, a division's liaison staff officers — and the units could not set up secret-service organizations of their own. When Chiang Kai-shek agreed to Lu Han's expanding the security corps into the 93rd and 74th Armies, I went to Lu to ask to set up Juntong secret-service organizations in these two army headquarters. Lu, unable to oppose it outright, put it off, saying the headquarters were newly formed, the commanders decided but many personnel matters not yet settled, and one could wait a little to send men. I knew perfectly well he was refusing, but at that time could not press it.

Before long the Kunming group reported to me that the 93rd Army commander Long Zehui had set up a secret-service organization, headed by a Yunnanese named Li Shirong who had once worked in the Juntong. Li Shirong was working to draw over agents he had once had ties with, to do secret-service work against the Centre for him, his chief targets being me and the Yunnan station and the 26th and 8th Armies. The Kunming group proposed I arrest this man and secretly put him to death or send him to Chungking, and that if Lu Han or Long Zehui asked for him, I could tell them openly and justly that as a Juntong man this was enforcing Juntong discipline.

After considering it I decided not only not to arrest him but to instruct the Kunming group to let him draw men to work for him, so as to learn his situation. For arresting or killing him would only make them find someone else, and I would then be the more in the dark; better to turn the plot to account and let him draw men in. What I most watched for was whether they would take up assassination; if not, if they only did a little intelligence, I could fabricate something half-true and half-false to give them and win their trust. The Kunming group, on my instruction, had the two drawn-over agents join that organization and report their activity to me at all times, so I knew their situation. As no preparation for assassination was found, I never disturbed them.

Just then I found someone drawing over the radio operator set up in my house. This operator had been a lieutenant; after I placed him with his wife and child in my house I promoted him captain. Both he and his wife were Yunnanese with many relatives in Kunming. A relative of his working in the Yunnan forces drew him to Long Zehui's unit and promoted him major and radio-station head. By the rules a Juntong man could not leave the Juntong organization to take another post, so I could not be courteous. When I had the commission appointing him major searched out, I arrested him at once and sent him secretly to Chungking for imprisonment. On the eve of the great Chungking massacre, the judge in charge of clearing the Baigongguan prisoners wired to ask how to deal with this man; I wired back to imprison him a while as punishment, and so he was not killed in the massacre.

After Shanghai and Nanking were liberated in succession, the whole picture of the Nationalists' collapse grew clearer, and no few senior Nationalist officials sought to make some tie with the Communists early so as to find a way out after liberation. A bad element named Zhang XX, taking advantage of this, passed himself off as some Communist commissioner and carried on activity in Kunming. Some of Lu Han's senior aides prized this like treasure, holding it an honour to meet him once. This fellow put on airs and mystified, and individual department heads and chiefs of staff under Lu Han went to call on him most respectfully. He got whatever he asked for, and later even asked Lu Han for a battalion to command, which was granted. Hearing this I laughed to myself, for Zhang had drawn in a mixed lot to pose as his Communist subordinates, and among them were outer elements or operatives of Yunnan-station agents. They had long reported this to me, and I was waiting to watch the joke. Yet my mood then was heavy, for from the analysis of this affair one could see that Lu Han and several of his subordinates did have the intention and resolve to turn against Chiang and go over to the Communists; otherwise they would not have been so hungry as to swallow a bad man's bait.

The most laughable thing was that one evening Lu Han's provincial-government secretary-general Zhu Jingxuan (not one of Lu's intimates, being formerly of the Wang Jingwei set, and so on good terms with me) telephoned me to come to his house at once, saying he had urgent business to discuss. I reckoned it might be this very matter. For I had intelligence that Lu Han's intimates would not let Zhu Jingxuan take part in the contact with Zhang XX, so he wished to use me against them. I thought that if he had met Zhang, he might keep it from me. On reaching his house he ceremoniously had my guard sit at his front door, saying he would not even let his own household upstairs. He led me to the room where he smoked opium, lay down on the opium couch, and while working his opium pellet told me: Communist organizations were already active in Kunming, Lu Han had sent a representative with their men to Sichuan to make contact — did I know? I said I had long heard of it and had not meant to disturb them. He then suggested to me that a regiment be brought from the 26th Army to surround them, so as to disarm the battalion protecting them and take them all in one net. At last I said with a smile that there was no need for me to send men to arrest them — sooner or later those who had called on Zhang XX would seize them. Before long, sure enough, Lu Han found Zhang XX was posing as a Communist and had them arrested. Though this was a great joke on the eve of Yunnan's liberation, it still showed the Yunnan people's longing for liberation.

### The Fitfully Bright and Dark Political Situation

In August 1949 Chiang Kai-shek, the better to handle the Southwest problem himself, dreaming that his personal "prestige" could hold this last patch of Southwest soil as a base for Nationalist anti-Communism and for winning American aid in strength, flew from Taiwan to Chungking to take charge. Some senior Nationalist officials held that in the past eight years of war Chungking had throughout been able to serve as the great rear — not only because the terrain was strong and troops and grain easily replenished, but because these places were familiar and easily controlled — so now they pinned their last hope on this Southwest corner.

This was Chiang Kai-shek's first return, since leaving Chungking after the victory, to the old lair where he had hidden seven or eight years during the war. The general run of senior Nationalist officials knew well that the Chungking people, at Chiang Kai-shek's second coming, would only hate him the more and by no means welcome him. For of the many things he had promised the Sichuan people in the past, such as building the Cheng-Yu railway, not one had been fulfilled. To show they still revered Chiang Kai-shek, the Chungking mayor Yang Sen and others set the Chungking gang ruffians and Paoge chiefs — Tian Desheng, Shi Xiaoxian, Tang Shaowu, Feng Shizhu and the like — to turn out their disciples and brethren for a welcome rally said to be a hundred thousand gang members strong, meeting this Green-Gang-bred old man at the airfield, and staged a farce.

What Chiang Kai-shek then found most thorny and gave most attention was, of course, the Yunnan problem. What most vexed him was that he could no longer spare adequate troops for his plan, and in the end could only adopt a so-called carrot-and-stick, favour-and-force policy toward Yunnan. And this time the "force" was pitifully weak: he could only order the fairly intact 26th Army to surround Kunming, and have Li Mi — whose whole corps had been wiped out at Huaihai and who had escaped the encirclement alone — bring the newly patched-together 8th Army, together with the 89th Army stationed in Guizhou (commander Liu Bolong), pressing back toward Kunming. At the same time he blockaded Kunming's air links with the outside, withdrawing even the few battered transport and passenger planes from Kunming, striking a pose to force Lu Han into line.

Lu Han did indeed rack his brains facing this. From his intimate aide-de-camp chief Zhu Ziying I learned that Lu spent several nights on end in painful thought and worry, the cigarette butts piling up in the ashtray, from which one can see how hard he laboured to meet the situation. Especially when Chiang Kai-shek's special plane to fetch Lu Han to Chungking stood on the field waiting to take off, Lu, it is said, hardly slept the night. Not to go might well mean a fight, and a fight was far from sure, and Yunnan would suffer the ravages of war; to declare an uprising at once — the PLA was still a good way from Yunnan and could not immediately support him; but to go to Chungking risked Chiang Kai-shek turning on him. Everyone knew this was a man who could say any fine thing and do any foul thing. While Lu Han wavered undecided, Chiang Kai-shek, to relieve his misgivings, at last specially sent Chiang Ching-kuo to Kunming to urge him in person.

When Chiang Ching-kuo reached Kunming, he happened on a large-scale "anti-Chiang, anti-American" demonstration by over thirty thousand Kunming students and people. He asked me again and again: "Will Yunnan blow up at once?" I reassured him repeatedly that no trouble would come at once, and that if it truly reached the point of no return I would not wait in Kunming to be a prisoner. What he most feared was that the airfield could not be held and there would be no way to flee by plane. I told him the 26th Army still had a few tanks, and if anything happened they could at least send him out of Yunnan to Guizhou by tank, which set his mind at ease.

September 5 was Kunming's tensest day. Early that morning Yu Chengwan sent an aide to fetch me to his army headquarters. By then the agents left in Kunming all carried the two different badges of the 26th Army and the Yunnan Security Command, so that if the two sides clashed they could tell 26th Army troops they were of the 26th Army headquarters and tell Yunnan security corps they were of the Security Command's aide-de-camp office. When I reached the 26th Army headquarters, I learned that from September 3 the army's officers and men had all been barred from leaving, the few small guns polished clean, their covers already off, their muzzles pointed straight at Lu Han's Wuhuashan office, the shells carried out case by case, the troops fully ready for battle, war liable to break out at a touch.

The whole Kunming populace too was uneasy, a rush to buy up firewood, rice and vegetables breaking out in the markets, every household making ready lest, once the shooting began, nothing could be bought. I had the criminal-police corps head Zhou Boxian sit at Zhu Ziying's house waiting for news, Zhou being Zhu's brother-in-law and they on the closest terms. Only on September 6, when Zhou reported by phone that Lu Han had decided to set out for Chungking, did I breathe easier. The people's frantic tension eased somewhat, but Lu's subordinates now worried that once Chiang Kai-shek detained Lu at Chungking there would be chaos in Kunming. So in the three days of Lu's Chungking trip, things eased on the surface while at bottom grew busier. The long-distance line from Chungking to Kunming constantly reported Lu's situation there. The 26th Army's battle readiness did not change, and the security corps still held the key points, not relaxing their watch.

Only when Lu Han returned safely by special plane from Chungking on September 9 did things change at once. But the usually active democratic figures, knowing Lu could come back safely, reckoned he must carry out Chiang Kai-shek's order and stage a suppression in Kunming, so many quietly planned to leave Kunming a while to hide. Sure enough, no sooner had Lu's plane landed than another special plane landing right after brought Xu Yuanju (Xu Pengfei), sent by Chiang Kai-shek and Mao Renfeng as the Bureau's Southwest special-district chief and second-department head of the Southwest Military and Political Governor's Office, with a great batch of agents.

On the eve of liberation, the largest-scale mass arrest in Kunming, the "September 9 affair," in which over four hundred were seized, followed at once. After I brought Xu Yuanju and the great batch of agents to my house, without even a sip of tea he at once carried out the first thing Chiang Kai-shek had ordered him to his face: arrest Yang Jie and Chen Fuguang and take them back by the same plane to Chungking. As for the several men Mao Renfeng had earlier ordered me to assassinate in Kunming, they were not to be arrested for the time, nor shown anything, lest Lu Han lose too much face. But when Xu Yuanju and I rushed with agents to Yang Jie's house to seize him, we drew a blank: Yang Jie had, just as Xu Yuanju landed, boarded the CNAC flight resumed only that day and flown to Hong Kong. Xu Yuanju was so vexed he stamped and cursed the Yunnan-station men for failing to watch Yang, so that what Chiang Kai-shek had ordered to his face could not be done and there was no way to report back.

While the agents were searching Yang's house, Chen Fuguang delivered himself up: he had come at Yang's request to see to Yang's household affairs. An agent who had once watched Chen Fuguang recognized him at a glance, detained him and the jeep he rode, and brought him to my house for interrogation. Just then the Southwest-district agents were torturing Yang Jie's aide downstairs. Unable to bear the pain of the makeshift electric torture rigged from a hand-crank telephone, the aide gave out piercing shrieks. Chen Fuguang, hearing them from the upstairs sitting room, trembled and could not sit still. When Xu Yuanju harshly pressed him on how Yang Jie had got away, he said the Revolutionary Committee's other Yunnan head, Zhu Jianfei, had bought Yang's Hong Kong ticket for him and sent Yang to the airfield in Zhu's car.

That afternoon Lu Han, in the name of director of the Yunnan Pacification Headquarters, issued a proclamation dissolving the provincial assembly and closing the Kunming papers. The proclamation said the assemblymen's speeches and resolutions over the past half-year had not only flouted the rules but strayed from the norm; that most papers, trimming and playing tricks, had fanned the flames and given the Communists a voice, disturbing society and men's minds. It said too that many papers had manufactured rumours that Yunnan was to be liberated, and that had he not flown to Chungking to explain, risking himself to save his homeland, Kunming would first have become ashes and the whole province been visited by the ruin of war.

After Lu Han's proclamation, a section chief of the provincial-government general-affairs department, with a platoon of the provincial guard corps, went to the assembly at Jinhu Park, drove out the assemblymen then in session, took away all the assembly's files, documents and seals, and put the provincial government's seals on the gate. At the same time Lu Han himself issued a written order to Xu Yuanju to arrest the deputy speaker Yang Qingtian and the assemblymen Ma Bo'an, Ma Yin, Jin Handing, Li Qunjie and over twenty others. That night began a large-scale arrest, and the *Zhengyi*, *Guancha*, *Daguanlou*, *Fuxing*, *Xiandai*, *Morning Post* dailies and other papers, and the radio stations they had set up, were sealed. Before the arrests, Xu Yuanju and Shen Zui first convened the Kunming deputy police chief Li Zhizheng, the 13th military-police regiment commander Wang Xu, the Kunming criminal-police corps head Zhou Boxian and others, explaining that Lu Han had accepted Chiang Kai-shek's order and agreed to a large-scale suppression in Kunming, to be carried out seriously and thoroughly.

That night's sweep was agreed to be carried out by military police and police together with the criminal-police corps agents, in groups by separate routes. Each group was made of several military police and police plus one criminal-police corps man, first arresting the twenty-odd assemblymen named in Lu Han's order. The rest of the police were split three ways: one part sealed the several papers and arrested their staff, a small part sealed the radio stations, and the greater part went to Yunnan University, the Teachers' College, Nanjing Middle School, Wuhua Middle School and other schools to seize teachers, students and staff.

From ten that night a curfew was declared, the Kunming Garrison Headquarters leading the security corps in guarding the whole city. Once the large sweep began, Shen Zui, Li Zhizheng, Wang Xu, Zhou Boxian and others went round the various places to watch its progress. The Security Command chief of staff Xie Chongwen also went to the schools and many places to watch. All traffic in the city was cut, barbed wire strung across the roads, and military telephones set up in all key districts — as tense as facing a great enemy.

The sweep went on tensely all night until broad daylight, when it stopped and the curfew was lifted. All Kunming was thrown into a pall for a night, no household at peace. By dawn the dozen or more rooms of the police-bureau lockup were packed full: in one night over three hundred and eighty had been arrested. Most were newspaper staff, for as they worked at night the editors, proofreaders, typesetters, printers and apprentices of each paper were all seized together. Of the twenty-odd assemblymen, only a few were not caught.

The next day the sweep continued, smaller in scale but going on day and night. On the morning of the 10th, Xu Yuanju led a great batch of military police, police and agents in person to the 53rd Arsenal at Haikou in the western suburbs of Kunming, and with the arsenal's guard-inspection group agents, arrested over thirty progressive workers and staff by the group's blacklist. Elsewhere the Dian-Chuan and Dian-Yue railway police agents arrested over ten progressive railway staff; the fourth-district highway bureau and fourth transport office agents arrested over twenty of their workers and staff; and the criminal-police corps arrested no few suspects in various places.

Because Yang Jie had not been seized in Kunming, and two or three days of tense sweeping had failed to catch the Communists' Yunnan heads, Chiang Kai-shek was much displeased and sent the Bureau director Mao Renfeng himself to Kunming to direct the continued sweep and suppression. On the morning of the 12th, Mao Renfeng flew in by special plane with the Bureau's Southwest deputy chief Zhou Yanghao (Yangzhai), the Bureau's Guizhou station chief and Guiyang deputy garrison commander Chen Shixian, the Bureau's senior judge Xu Zhongqi, and attendants. Mao Renfeng was extremely dissatisfied with the previous sweep, holding it had not been organized or prepared beforehand and so had no results. On reaching Kunming he at once decided to set up a temporary command and, while continuing the sweep, conduct interrogation and sorting through the nights.

The command was directed by the Kunming mayor and police chief Zeng Shuhuai, with the deputy chief Li Zhizheng and the military-police regiment commander Wang Xu as deputy directors bearing the real responsibility. Under the command was an action group headed by Shen Zui and Zhou Boxian for the sweep; Chen Shixian and Zhou Yanghao as head and deputy of the interrogation group; and Zhu Ziying as head of the general-affairs group. The command was set up at the house on Huancheng East Road of the former Kunming police chief, the Juntong agent Wang Xu, and Mao Renfeng held a conference there in person each day.

Under Mao Renfeng's personal direction the sweep went on nearly a month, over four hundred arrested in all, but never a single Communist Yunnan head was caught, nor any Communist organization broken; only a few Revolutionary Committee heads were caught, which left Mao Renfeng most vexed and disappointed. He complained on one hand that the Yunnan station's past work had been badly done, so that even his personal appearance yielded nothing and he could not show his ability before Chiang Kai-shek; on the other, he held that among the four hundred-odd arrested, effort could surely turn up some Communist lead. So he ordered that the command might only arrest — anyone suspect could be seized — but on no account release or bail any of the arrested.

This only-in-never-out method left the police-bureau lockup unable to hold them. A room two zhang square held over forty men, who had to take turns even to sit, let alone sleep. Only after the 16th was it decided to move a great batch to the Yunnan Model Prison on Qianju Street, and those judged more heavily suspect, especially the few thought connected with Communist outer organizations, were tortured many times, yet never a lead was found. Mao Renfeng still would not relax, hoping to sort out slowly from the arrested some connected with the Communists, so as to widen the sweep further.

The large-scale arrests, close interrogation and sorting still fell short of Mao Renfeng's ideal, so he studied the cause. Later many facts proved it: Lu Han had used expedient tactics, not earnestly carrying out Chiang Kai-shek's order to sternly suppress the Yunnan democratic and patriotic movement. On the surface Lu agreed to arrest the relevant people, and even ordered the arrest of over twenty assemblymen who habitually opposed him, but in fact it was a beating of the grass to startle the snake, deliberately letting a batch of important people slip away. When Lu ordered the arrest of the assemblymen, he first sent men to seal the assembly; before sweeping Yunnan University and the Teachers' College, he sent men to notify the school authorities to prepare, so that a batch of progressive students on the old blacklists fled at word. Especially, before the arrests began, Lu detained the Juntong senior agent Qiu Kaiji. Qiu, a Yunnanese, a former Yunnan police-security commissioner, knew Kunming society best and knew many people; Lu, fearing he would talk wildly at this juncture, detained him first. Mao Renfeng, having confirmed all this from Lu's secretary-general Zhu Jingxuan, the Juntong agent and former Kunming mayor Pei Cunfan, and from Xu Yuanju's and Shen Zui's reports, made a detailed report to Chiang Kai-shek. Chiang at once sent Xu Yongchang and Yi Su to Kunming to inspect, meaning to strengthen the suppression further and find a way to deal with Lu Han.

The result of Mao Renfeng's repeated study with me was to expand the Yunnan station organization by asking Lu Han to agree to set up a security-defence department within the Yunnan Pacification Headquarters, with the Yunnan station chief Shen Zui as concurrent head. Under it were three offices — a secretariat, a staff office and an inspectorate; three sections — business (in charge of intelligence and arrests), interrogation and general-affairs; an agent detachment; and student-movement, labour-movement, postal-censorship, air-censorship and other groups; with security-defence groups set up besides at Kunming, Baoshan, Mengzi, Kaiyuan, Zhaotong, Zhanyi, Xinping, Simao, Pu'er and elsewhere, to use an open name to suppress the Yunnan people.

When Mao put this to Lu, Lu, pleading financial difficulty, sought to delay; Mao at once agreed the Bureau would bear all the cost, asking only that Lu grant the open name. Lu, unable to refuse, agreed, and allowed a special subsidy of three thousand yuan a month in Yunnan half-open hard coin (worth then about one silver dollar per two and a half). The security-defence department was hastily set up on October 1, 1949, Mao Renfeng temporarily drawing nearly two hundred agents from Chungking, Guizhou and elsewhere to Kunming, and moving in over twenty radio sets and a great many US-made pistols by forced marches. This open secret-service organ, set up on October 1, had barely got some of its Kunming units going, well short of Mao Renfeng's ideal, when it was all wiped out at Yunnan's peaceful liberation on December 9.

### Mao Renfeng's Plotting in Kunming

From his arrival in Kunming from Chungking on September 12, 1949, until October 13, in that month and more, Mao Renfeng spent all his strength scheming how to accomplish the Chiang Kai-shek clique's task of securing Yunnan as the last anti-Communist base on the mainland, and how to strengthen, by bloody means, the suppression of the Yunnan people's democratic movement. On first reaching Kunming he stayed in a splendid Spanish-style residence newly built by Pei Cunfan in Xunjin New Village. After four days, finding Pei's house inconvenient for his activity, and my family having by then been sent to Chungking so my house was empty and had a set downstairs communicating directly with Taiwan, he moved to my house on the 16th and stayed until he left. So I could learn something of his various schemes in this period.

The day after Mao Renfeng moved to my house he asked me: "Has the Zhu Huiqing matter been passed on?" I said I had seen the headquarters circular but, being too busy, had not passed it round widely, only spoken of it to a few unit heads. He said: "Better let me pass it on myself." What exactly was the Zhu Huiqing matter, that Mao Renfeng valued it so?

It turned out that in August 1949, forty-four people led by Huang Shaohong had jointly issued in Hong Kong a statement titled "Our Understanding of and Position on the Present Stage of the Chinese Revolution," mainly supporting the Chinese Communist Party's leadership, willing to work together to build a New China, and sharply condemning the crimes of the Chiang Kai-shek reactionaries in ruining the country and the people. Chiang Kai-shek, on hearing this, called Mao Renfeng in for a dressing-down, saying he had not taken precautions beforehand, and ordered him to send agents to Hong Kong to assassinate the several ringleaders, to show he still had strength to control things and forestall imitation. Chiang also sent people to Hong Kong to dissuade others, and imposed expulsion from the party and warrants on some of the senior Nationalist officials among them.

Just as Mao Renfeng was hurriedly preparing a large-scale assassination in Hong Kong, he suddenly heard that one of the forty-four, Zhu Huiqing, being a former Juntong senior agent, though he had joined this telegraphed uprising, had not been invited by Peking, while the other forty-three would all go to Peking. Zhu, feeling he could go neither forward nor back, was in agonies of regret. Mao Renfeng was delighted and at once reported it to Chiang Kai-shek. Chiang too held it a good thing worth valuing and to be passed on at once to all agents. Chiang pointed out smugly that in speaking to Juntong agents in the past he had said that apart from following him in anti-Communism to the end, a man who had been an agent had no other road, for the Communists would surely settle accounts for these men's past anti-Communist crimes. Mao Renfeng then wired this direction to Kunming by most-urgent telegram, and I, being very busy, had no time to pass it on to each agent as directed.

On the morning of September 19, Mao Renfeng had me notify the various open secret-service units at Kunming and some Yunnan-station agents to gather at Wang Xu's house for him to pass it on in person. He said a great string of stale platitudes, slandering the Communists at will, and greatly intimidated those present, saying Juntong agents must be anti-Communist to the end, could only be anti-Communist to the end. Many then believed it, and at the last even shouted excitedly some slogans of firm support for Chiang Kai-shek's anti-Communism to the end.

Newly come to Kunming, Mao imagined he could work smoothly and get his way, but after a few days found it not so simple. He had meant to stay only a few days at Kunming, set the work in order and go; but the change of circumstances took him by surprise, and he had to stay on. What most disheartened him then was that not only had the "September 9 affair" caught no Communist Yunnan head, still less broken an underground radio set, but even the minimum of killing a few men to make a suppression could not be done to his satisfaction. Though he cursed Lu Han behind his back daily, he still had to deal patiently with Lu Han.

When he found Lu wavering in thought, without firm resolve to be anti-Communist and anti-people to the end, he advised Chiang Kai-shek to replace Lu Han early, or at least strip Lu of his military power in Yunnan. He held Lu unreliable, sure to turn sooner or later. But Chiang Kai-shek all along heeded Zhang Qun's view that Lu would not go over to the Communists. Several of Chiang's key men, such as Xu Yongchang, Yi Su and Xiao Huazhi, also held that from Lu's own life and family ties he could not possibly go over to the Communists — for Lu was fond of comfort in life, had put both his sons under foreign nationality, had sent his family to Hong Kong and had much money deposited abroad. But Mao Renfeng, judging from what he learned in Kunming himself, held all along that Lu might well turn against Chiang to the Communists.

Chiang Kai-shek, thinking his own prestige could make Lu loyal to the end, specially made a detour to Kunming before flying from Chungking to Taiwan on October 22. Chiang Ching-kuo had rushed to Kunming two days ahead to prepare. The moment Chiang Ching-kuo landed he sought the three of us — Mao Renfeng, Xu Yuanju and me — to ask whether Lu Han might suddenly change his mind and stage in Kunming an action like the "Xi'an Incident." We told Chiang Ching-kuo of the troops Lu could then command and the Centre's troops in Yunnan and the people's armed forces there, and told him we judged Lu Han would not act rashly and Chiang Kai-shek's coming to Kunming would be safe. Then Chiang Ching-kuo passed on Chiang Kai-shek's direction: Lu Han must be told to go to the airfield to welcome him only after the special plane reached the sky over Kunming — on no account told beforehand, as a precaution. He also passed on another direction of Chiang Kai-shek's: that for the present Lu Han could only be won over, not provoked, and every means used to steady him, but that at the same time preparations to assassinate Lu Han must be made at once and, if necessary, he be finished off. He said Chiang Kai-shek too held Lu Han unreliable, sure to betray sooner or later. Chiang Ching-kuo asked me again and again whether finishing off Lu Han in Kunming was a sure thing. I said finishing off a man was not a matter of saying so and doing it, especially a man with great power in hand like Lu Han: to do it one must succeed at one stroke, not beat the grass and startle the snake, so it needed close arrangements and bold action men to be sure. He then told Mao Renfeng that whatever money or men I wanted must be met in full, no discount, for this bore on the great question of holding Yunnan as an anti-Communist base. About nine on the morning of the 22nd, Mao Renfeng, Xu Yuanju and I waited in the office of the air-force fifth-region deputy commander Shen Yanshi at the airfield. Chiang Ching-kuo came after. Shen Yanshi directed the ground crew to make ready and had the radio room listen at all times for telegrams from Chungking and Chiang Kai-shek's plane. Chiang Kai-shek was then like a startled bird, fearing his movements would be spotted by the PLA and dreading interception in the air above all, so he dared not disclose his destination beforehand. He laid it down that the moment his plane took off, several big cities' airfields must all make ready and keep calling, yet none knew where he would land, none could make out his plane's route. His plane listened only for the calls of his destination and made no reply, waiting until it was near the place to notify that airfield to make ready for his landing. We waited till near eleven with no word and all thought he would not come. But Chiang Ching-kuo said he surely would, having contacted Chungking by phone in cipher only the night before. Sure enough, at ten fifty the plane signalled Kunming to make ready for its landing. Only then did Chiang Ching-kuo phone Lu Han, saying Chiang Kai-shek, going to Taiwan, had decided on the spur to stop at Kunming and wished to see him, and asking him to come to the airfield at once and tell no one else.

At half past eleven the plane landed; the moment the cabin door opened, before the gangway was pushed up, Chiang Kai-shek stood there looking both ways. Seeing that, by the standing arrangement, I had posted the great batch of plain-clothes agents holding newspapers in the left hand all round the field, he came down composedly. We few followed behind Lu Han and saluted him. He only shook Lu Han's hand, nodded slightly to us, and gave a faint snort through the nose in return. On the short walk from the plane to the car, Chiang Kai-shek turned to me and said: "Manage your relations with the locals well here, and consult Chairman Lu often." I answered: "Yes." At the time I really admired his double-faced trick — on one hand having Chiang Ching-kuo tell me to prepare to assassinate Lu Han, and before Lu Han's face telling me to consult Lu often. Though Chiang Kai-shek stopped only two hours-odd at Kunming that time, ate one lunch and hurried off, this trip showed how much he then valued Yunnan. Mao Renfeng did not talk with him at the field, only conferred a little with Chiang Ching-kuo.

After Chiang Kai-shek's visit to Kunming, Lu Han's attitude still did not change much. Mao Renfeng grumbled behind his back that the old man (meaning Chiang Kai-shek) grew more and more muddled, heeding Zhang Qun's words and slighting his views. But he did not lose heart, and resolved to make Lu Han share a great blood-debt, meaning to leave Lu's hands so steeped in blood that he could no longer waver, no longer turn, and could only follow Chiang Kai-shek in resisting to the end.

When Mao Renfeng heard that Lu Han had shot dead, before the Central Bank on Nanping Street in Kunming, a run of people who had come to redeem counterfeit notes, he praised the deed over and over as very clever, holding Lu, who loved killing, a like-minded friend. He resolved to use Lu's hand for a great massacre of those arrested in the "September 9 affair," neither meaning to send them to Chungking to be dealt with nor to use the Juntong's usual secret executions, but bent on carrying out open shootings in Lu's name. His first thought was to shoot half of the arrested, and he went to talk with Lu to sound him out first. Back from Lu he told me Lu thought half too many, and added with a laugh: "I never thought this killing friend would take a turn to mercy." He then decided to halve it again, killing over a hundred.

To draw up charges in haste for the massacre, Mao Renfeng worked in person with the Bureau's judge Xu Zhongqi from about September 20, day and night without break, often to midnight. Of the arrested, not only was there no evidence to sentence them to death by the reactionaries' decrees, but some had not even been formally questioned once before charges were drawn up. Especially the twenty-odd assemblymen Lu had ordered arrested were nearly all listed for death. The Hui assemblyman Ma Bo'an, in a verse of feeling written in prison, had the line "the charge against me is groundless," which was construed as slandering the government and inciting the prisoners to riot, warranting death. Mao Renfeng directed Xu Zhongqi that for those to be executed, one need only fix a charge under the Nationalist killing decrees; there would be no chance of appeal in any case, but military justice would be used. So for each of the more than a hundred to be executed, only a "crime" of a few dozen characters was written, ending with the four characters "recommend shooting." He imagined this would count as the legal formality of open killing, that Lu Han would approve his sentences, mark a "granted," and he could carry out a great open massacre in Kunming.

About September 25, Mao Renfeng first formally took a roll sentencing over a hundred to death to Lu Han for approval in person; Lu held it too many and the evidence too thin and advised him to be careful. Mao came back displeased, saying to me several times: "Lu is too little of a friend — even killing a hundred-odd people he begrudges." Holding that by his standing his views should not be discounted, he was furious. But to make Lu share the blood-debt he swallowed it, bargaining, cutting from a hundred-odd to eighty-odd, at last even to forty-odd, and still Lu would not agree. Lu held that if it was to be open shooting there must be sufficient reason, not haste.

So it dragged on to early October, when Mao Renfeng, urged repeatedly by Taiwan to return, could wait no longer at Kunming and left in a fluster. On the morning he boarded the plane he still talked secretly with me alone, enjoining again and again that Lu was unreliable, that I must be especially watchful and, when necessary, at once carry out the directions he had given me. In the gravest tone he said: "When I order you again to carry out the sanction [that is, assassinate Lu Han], you cannot fail again. If it is as with Yang Jie again, there will be no way to answer for it. You know the organization's discipline perfectly well." His meaning was plain: if I did not faithfully carry out his killing plan, he would kill me to keep discipline.

Seeing that Lu Han would not kill people, and that many matters displeased him, and holding Lu unreliable — that unless he struck first there was no turning the Yunnan situation — Mao Renfeng decided to assassinate Lu Han first if need be. He held that Li Mi, a Yunnanese with an army in hand, could replace Lu in running Yunnan. So while at Kunming he often gathered the two army commanders Li Mi and Yu Chengwan for secret talks, urging them to cooperate well and put the party and state first, and hinting again and again that Li Mi could take Lu Han's place. Mao enjoined me repeatedly to assassinate Lu Han if need be, and inspected the preparations I had made.

By then I had rented a house at No. 8 Cuihu East Road near Lu Han's residence, and set a machine gun upstairs that could sweep Lu's bedroom, sitting room, dining room and so on. I also meant to use another house near Lu's — the residence of the Hekou superintendent Wang Xiaofeng — as the point for the agents to lie in wait during the assassination. Mao Renfeng was well pleased. This was because Wang Xiaofeng's house was nearest Lu's; Wang himself was at Hekou, and one of his concubines, a Hunanese, was on close terms with my family, and there were empty rooms upstairs and down. When Lu's car turned in and out past Wang's house, the agents could strike at leisure.

Fearing the few assassination specialists the Yunnan station had were not enough, Mao Renfeng specially picked and sent Jiao Yuyin and several others who had done assassination work for years. Jiao Yuyin and the rest had, at Hunan's peaceful liberation, assassinated in Changsha the Changsha police chief and Juntong man Liu Renji, who had joined Cheng Qian's uprising. Mao Renfeng held that once Lu was finished off, Lu's subordinates, a snake without its head, could not act, and with Li Mi's fairly good ties with some Yunnanese and closeness to Lu's subordinates, the wreckage could be gathered up and the Yunnan situation steadied.

Mao Renfeng's proudest work during his time in Kunming, by his own account, was having General Yang Jie assassinated in Hong Kong. After Yang Jie fled from Kunming to Hong Kong, the agents found some of Yang's Hong Kong connections in Kunming, and so could kill Yang the day before he was to leave Hong Kong for Peking. It turned out Mao Renfeng had read through in person the diary and correspondence seized from Yang's house, found that Yang Jie had regular dealings with Li Zongli and others, and sorted out the letters between them, sending them to Taiwan, where the Bureau's action-department head Ye Xiangzhi had someone imitate the handwriting and write Yang a letter. Ye Xiangzhi took four killers to Hong Kong in person, and when they found Yang he was just reading this forged letter; the killers shot him dead off guard and fled. The next day, when the Kunming *Central Daily News* carried the news of Yang's assassination in Hong Kong, I hastened to tell Mao Renfeng. He said, much pleased: "Of course he couldn't escape our grasp. Hong Kong is more troublesome than Kunming, but it shows our strength the better." Then he showed me Ye Xiangzhi's telegram to him, which read: mission accomplished, returned safely to Taiwan.

What displeased Mao Renfeng during his time in Kunming, besides not carrying out a large-scale killing, was that his sending men from Kunming to Hong Kong to kill Long Yun had failed too. Soon after reaching Kunming he asked me what reliable men the Yunnan station had once placed at Long Yun's side. I named a few — Jiang XX, Liu XX and others who had been Long Yun's secretaries or aides and had Juntong ties — and he thought Jiang XX's ties best and usable. He received Jiang XX in person and directed him to find a way to Hong Kong to contact Long and work his way further into Long's Hong Kong residence. Jiang XX did get Long's leave to go to Hong Kong and imagined it would succeed, but for lack of care in acting was suspected by Long's daughter-in-law and could not strike. Before leaving Kunming, Mao Renfeng, still turning this over, told me to find another reliable man as an inside line to realize his scheme and stage a second Yang Jie bloody case in Hong Kong.

During his time in Kunming Mao Renfeng valued the Yunnan local reactionary armed forces highly, taking a special fancy to men like the Xinping county despotic landlord Li Runzhi. Li Runzhi had put a notice in the papers proclaiming himself commander-in-chief of the Yunnan Anti-Communist National Salvation Army, set out anti-Communist proposals, and called on other local reactionary forces to cooperate with him. Mao Renfeng praised him as a man who dared to act, with methods, proposals and firm attitude, and told me to keep good ties with him. Mao also had the security-defence department set up a group at Xinping to keep close touch with Li Runzhi, and had me send men to Chungking to draw some US-made light weapons for Li. Later the Juntong agent Yu Qian recommended to Mao Renfeng Lü Yiwen, once Wang Jingwei's puppet-government ambassador to Germany. Lü, it was said, was deep in with Li Runzhi, and besides being willing to work for the Juntong himself could draw Li Runzhi to work for the Juntong through him. Mao at once received Lü Yiwen, encouraged him to his face, and told him to keep in touch with me. Later Lü Yiwen drew up a "Plan for Setting the Yunnan Local Armed Forces in Motion and Strengthening Yunnan Local Anti-Communist Strength" and gave it to Mao. Mao, much pleased, promised him a batch of US weapons and radios to support it, and had me give Lü some travel and special funds and send him first to Xinping to work with Li Runzhi, hoping Li would come to Kunming to talk with him in person.

Mao Renfeng's month of activity in Kunming was, by his own account, the longest he had stayed in one place since becoming Bureau director. This other cruel running dog most favoured at Chiang Kai-shek's side, heir to the Juntong chief Dai Li's mantle, imitated Dai Li in everything and was fiercer than Dai Li in killing, killing more. But this Kunming trip was a bitter disappointment. He could not, as at Shanghai, Chungking, Chengtu and elsewhere, kill a hundred at a stroke. The world-shaking massacre of over three hundred in the SACO compound at Chungking was his proud work. In a month at Kunming he killed not even one man, and left at last in resentment.

### The Juntong Agents' Last Struggle in Kunming

Before leaving Kunming, the task Mao Renfeng gave the Yunnan station was, chiefly, to use every possibility and strength to fit in from all sides with the Nationalist government's plan to secure Yunnan; to grasp beforehand the ever-changing Yunnan situation so that Chiang Kai-shek could have it all clear and handle it in time; to guard against Long Yun and Lu Han working together, watching especially to gather all Lu Han's words and deeds; and besides, to display the Nationalists' strength in every way, striking heavy blows at the progressive forces at every chance, and making the Juntong agents' role felt. Mao also directed that in accomplishing these tasks the Yunnan station alone was not enough, and that one must keep good ties and close cooperation with the central military commanders stationed in Yunnan, the Central Statistics agents, the military police and the local despotic forces.

Mao Renfeng pinned his hopes on me, promising men, money, guns and radios on demand. But to guard against my having trouble and to make me serve him to the death, he repeatedly demanded I send my whole family to Taiwan. Though I promised faithfully to do my utmost, I knew I had no assurance of success, and knew that sending my family to Taiwan was only to hold them hostage. After he left, Mao wrote me three letters in his own hand, stressing again and again the importance of these tasks and how Chiang Kai-shek valued the work.

What Taiwan then valued most was intelligence work, hoping to grasp Yunnan's situation exactly so as to act in time. The Yunnan station worked with all its strength, day and night, yet fell far short of their needs, telegrams flying and reproaches trading almost constantly. The important intelligence I got was not much; what most stumped me was the impossibility of penetrating the progressive organizations. Though I had no few ties at Lu Han's side — even a secretary-general, a chief secretary, a chief of staff, a department head who could give me some things — these men had no deep ties with the progressive side. Those who had always dealt with progressives, like the civil-affairs commissioner An Enpu, kept the Juntong at arm's length and often showed disgust. Some closer to Lu would not say much about Lu's affairs.

To break the deadlock, I constantly used their contradictory frame of mind — supporting Lu Han yet fearing and not understanding the Communists — for all manner of discord-sowing and rumour-mongering, so that some men of money sent their families to Hong Kong in numbers. In this I helped them all I could, getting them plane tickets at any time and having friends in Hong Kong find them houses.

I let slip no usable man or chance. After Mao Renfeng left, the Juntong senior agent Qiu Kaiji was released on Lu's order. Qiu Kaiji had been Lu's aide-de-camp chief and had ties with some at Lu's side. Out of prison, very displeased with Lu, he was used by me to gather news of Lu. He not only volunteered but urged his wife Ao Wanhua to work hard at the task too. Some of Lu's intimates and senior aides went almost every evening to play cards at the home of Li Xiyao, long Long Yun's police commissioner. Li's house was spacious, with good Chinese and Western cooks, a gathering-place of Yunnan's political figures. Though I knew them, they were always a little on guard with me and would not speak freely; Qiu Kaiji could chat freely with them, and his wife could get into many homes and talk of anything with their families, which helped my work no little.

There was also the huadan Yu Suqiu, then playing opposite Ma Lianliang in Kunming, niece of the Juntong agent Yu Qian, long in touch with Juntong senior agents and well acquainted with me. She often went to various engagements, and from the after-drinks talk of many people picked up much, which she passed to me whenever she heard it. To find her more connections, once when I gave a dinner for Ma Lianliang I specially invited some Pacification Headquarters department heads and Lu Han's confidential men as guests, for they all liked to hear Ma Lianliang and Yu Suqiu perform. I used the dinner to let her meet more of these men. A dinner may seem nothing, but planned by a Juntong secret-service chief it was far from simple. Ma Lianliang ate one meal of mine and was used by me once, and perhaps only when I write this will Mr. Ma realize why I found so many irrelevant people to keep him company.

Zhu Ziying, who best knew Lu Han's life, was indeed a great help to me. This aide-de-camp chief, letting fall a bit here and there unwittingly, gave me my most valuable material. For instance, he told me Lu had resolved to give up opium; on reporting it to Taiwan I at once drew great attention. They studied what this act of Lu's reflected, holding that Lu might mean to go abroad as an idle rentier and find smoking inconvenient there, or, more likely, meant to go over to the Communists, since after liberation he could no longer smoke. In any case it showed, at the least, that Lu had no confidence in keeping up Yunnan's precarious peace and did not mean to go to Taiwan, so he was breaking the habit.

To strengthen intelligence work, the security-defence department set up a postal-censorship group, headed by An Chuili, to censor incoming and outgoing mail and tap long-distance and city phones. The Yunnan postal director Shen Songzhou actively helped this work, freeing several rooms in the post office; but by liberation it had not got the work going. For censoring and controlling air transport, on the wartime model, the security-defence department set up an air-censorship group in October, headed by Li Ruifeng. Besides checking travellers, it controlled tickets strictly, so that even airline staff had to apply by involved formalities to fly. Many, to get a ticket, had to bribe the air-censorship agents, and more to plead and give rich gifts to some responsible security-defence agents. Some who had bought tickets by the formalities were often pulled from their seats by air-censorship agents on some pretext just as the plane was to take off, to let powerful dignitaries and their families go first. Such things happened almost daily, truly making heaven and man complain.

The Yunnan station, to strengthen cooperation with other reactionary units, made friends even with the Central Statistics agents usually mutually suspicious. In the past, though working in the same district on the same work, Juntong and Central Statistics men had rarely dealt with each other. Now, to discuss meeting the Yunnan situation, the Bureau's Yunnan station chief Shen Zui, deputy chief Xiao XX, and the several Central Statistics Yunnan heads Zha Zongfan, Sun Binli, Long Shuming, and later the Ministry of Interior Investigation Bureau (the name the Central Statistics took after reorganization) Southwest head Chen Qingyi, kept in constant contact, exchanging intelligence. Besides, the Juntong Yunnan station and the 13th military-police regiment commander Wang Xu, the military-police Southwest-region commander Li Chufan and others also dealt with one another often, cooperating fully to steady the Yunnan situation.

In the past secret-service organs had not stressed propaganda, but after the Juntong was reorganized into the Ministry of National Defence's Bureau of Secrets Preservation, the second department, on the American model, set up a psychological-warfare section and constantly sent the Yunnan station rumour-and-slander material for psychological warfare. In this period the papers the Yunnan station controlled included the Kunming *Modern Evening News*, sealed in the "September 9 affair," of which the Juntong agent and acting Nationalist Yunnan party head Pei Cunfan was publisher and the Juntong agent Wang Biyu director; besides psychological-warfare propaganda, they carried on intelligence work under cover of the paper. Others — the *Common People's Daily* reorganized from the former *Yunnan Daily*, the *Morning Post*, the *Saodang Evening News* and so on, whose heads Ding Zhongjiang, Wang Gongfei and Ding Yanshi had all long had work ties with me — regularly ran the Bureau's psychological-warfare pieces, slandering the Communists in every way. These papers ran the material Taiwan sent, such as "The Yangge-Dancing Dynasty" and "Sights and Sounds of the Northern Capital."

In November the Nationalist propaganda-ministry head Ren Chixuan (Ye Qing) hurried specially from Taiwan to Kunming, and the Yunnan station warmly welcomed this renegade who styled himself an "anti-Communist theorist," helping him with all its strength to carry on slanderous propaganda in Kunming and forcing merchants and citizens to hear his lectures. But though this rumour-specialist did his best to hearten the Nationalists, in private talk with me he much disheartened me. He had wholly lost confidence, saying again and again that Yunnan could not be held sooner or later, that the future was only to hold out on Taiwan and wait for a Third World War before a counter-attack to recover the mainland was possible. I was much displeased to hear this, and besides advising him to his face never to say such things again in Kunming, reported his words to Taiwan word for word.

After Chiang Kai-shek's visit to Kunming, the Nationalist vice-president and acting president Li Zongren also came to Kunming in November to be active. This Guangxi general was still, at this point, planning to expand his turf and to draw Lu Han in, hoping to reach the Guangxi clique's power into Yunnan. Before he began his "palace-forcing" of Chiang Kai-shek, the agents had long, on Chiang Kai-shek's orders, kept close watch on him and had once prepared to murder him in Nanking. His sudden coming to Kunming, to fight Chiang Kai-shek dog-over-bone on this last patch of soil, became another temporary urgent task for the Yunnan station.

I now concentrated all my strength on Li Zongren's words and doings, daily learning the situation from some close to Lu Han and reporting it to Taiwan that day. What pleased me was that Lu on the surface fobbed off Li Zongren, and Li knew his place and dared not rashly discuss openly with Lu the Guangxi troops' entering Yunnan. Li did openly voice complaint and dissatisfaction with Chiang Kai-shek, saying he was "acting but not ruling," unable to exercise the acting president's powers, chiefly because that man (meaning Chiang Kai-shek), "retired but not withdrawn," was still pulling the strings behind the scenes and would not let go an inch.

The Dian-Yue railway director Tang Yuzong, long close to the Guangxi clique, let fall in talk that Li Zongren's real aim was to bring the Guangxi remnant forces from Guangxi into Yunnan, creating a fait accompli, not fearing the Yunnan local government's refusal. To gather Li Zongren's activities in Kunming, besides sending a great batch of agents to follow him, even when he went sightseeing at the Stone Forest and had Tang Yuzong send railway police to protect him, I had agents change into railway-police uniform to follow at his side. Only when Li was seen onto his plane did this added temporary task end. Chiang Kai-shek, though he could spare no troops for Yunnan then, still regarded it as his turf and would not let the Guangxi clique in. The moment Li Zongren reached Kunming, I gathered his activities daily and reported them that evening, and Taiwan kept directing me to watch closely.

After the PLA's great advance on the Southwest, Lu Han's attitude changed ever more plainly. By then Chiang Kai-shek had recalled Liu Bolong's 89th Army, which had reached the Yunnan border from Guizhou, back to Guizhou from Zhanyi and Qujing, dreaming of a mantis stopping a chariot to bar the PLA in the strong Guizhou mountains. Chiang Kai-shek held that Lu Han would be loyal, so though the Yunnan station kept reporting all intelligence to Taiwan and hoped for early handling, Chiang still heeded Zhang Qun and would not replace Lu Han. Only about November 20, when Lu Han suddenly announced that, with acting president Li Zongren's approval, all those arrested in the "September 9 affair" would be released unconditionally, was Taiwan greatly shaken and thrown into confusion. But by then the PLA had smashed the only Nationalist forces to enter Guizhou, Liu Jiashu's corps and the newly formed He Shaozhou corps, and crossing Loushan Pass, the Wu River and layer on layer of natural barriers, was driving on Sichuan like a broken bamboo. With the Southwest's centre Chungking about to be liberated, Chiang could no longer control Yunnan by force. While Mao Renfeng was still in Kunming, Lu Han had already seen through Chiang Kai-shek's criminal scheme, via Mao Renfeng, to force Lu to share the blood-debt, so he kept putting off dealing with the "September 9 affair" arrests and would not kill a single one. In the end Mao Renfeng had to defer to Lu's view and send men from the Southwest Governor's Office to try the cases jointly with Yunnan Pacification Headquarters men before deciding. In late October, the joint trial by the Southwest Governor's Office's section chief Lu Jianru and judge Lei, and the Yunnan Pacification Headquarters' judge-advocate Yang Zhenxing and security-defence head Shen Zui — four men — had just begun when Mao Renfeng sent the Bureau's senior judge Xu Zhongqi to take part. Lu ignored Xu Zhongqi's coming. Mao Renfeng wired urgently again and again to demand why I would not let Xu take part, and I answered each time that I had not got Lu's consent.

This method of Lu's succeeded: because Xu was not let into the joint trial, not one of the arrested lost his life. During the trial, Yang Zhenxing, on Lu's direction, released on bail in batches even those Mao Renfeng firmly refused to release, and released in court some who had been muddleheadedly seized. Some workers and students who had only sung progressive songs or danced the yangge were labelled Communist suspects, and were released one after another in the trial too. Those whose thinking and conduct were fairly progressive, and against whom there was more or less some so-called evidence, were still detained for the time.

When I reported the trial's progress to Taiwan daily, Mao Renfeng still thought a few could at least be killed, and urged me again and again to hold to this principle: no matter how many, so long as a few heads fell it counted as achieving the aim. He told me not to argue over numbers any more but to make sure that the moment one was decided for death, he be killed at once, not all together at the close. But Lu still would not agree, insisting the joint trial finish before deciding. So it dragged and dragged, until about November 20, with the trial still going, Lu suddenly ordered all released, and over three hundred were freed at a stroke.

On my report, Mao Renfeng at once sent Zhang Hu — the detachment captain who had guarded General Yang Hucheng and taken part in his killing — as head of the Bureau's Yunnan detention house, and told me to ready housing at once and detail a few agents, within three days, to help Zhang Hu set the detention house up. Mao Renfeng directed that the Communist suspects, the several Revolutionary Committee heads and the progressive assemblymen Yang Qingtian, Chen Fuguang, Zhu Jianfei, Li Qunjie and dozens of others whom Lu had released must be re-arrested and handed to Zhang Hu's keeping to await orders. I really did not want to carry this order out in earnest, for fear of a head-on clash with Lu Han and becoming its victim.

Weighing it again and again, I judged that since Lu Han had, with acting president Li Zongren's approval, openly released these men, and Chiang Kai-shek had known that very day and shown not a sign, while Mao Renfeng secretly had me re-arrest some, Lu, learning of it, would surely pursue it, even coerce Chiang Kai-shek and demand I be punished. In that case Chiang would surely defer to Lu, and to show support for Lu, sacrificing an agent or two was entirely possible. So when Zhang Hu, telegram and letter from Mao Renfeng in hand, pressed me, I always put it off on the excuse of not finding a house. Right up to Yunnan's peaceful liberation, though the Bureau's Yunnan detention house was nominally set up and its men detailed, its site was never settled, and Zhang Hu and a few keepers stayed in the security-defence department with nothing to do.

In late October the Bureau ordered the Yunnan station to draw up a practicable "contingency plan" and decided to expand the Yunnan station. Besides, the Ministry of National Defence ordered the setting up of a Ministry of National Defence Yunnan Guerrilla Command, with me as concurrent commander, the Yunnan-station men all folded into the Guerrilla Command, the Dian-Yue railway police head Su Zixiu as concurrent deputy commander. Su Zixiu pressed me repeatedly to make this name public; I held it would disturb public order, and put it off, saying Yunnan would not be liberated and the name could not be used before it was necessary. For the security-defence head could not unify command of the Bureau's open units in Yunnan; by the old Juntong custom, only a secret unit — that is, each province's station — had the right to lead uniformly the open units the province's Juntong subordinate units controlled. Had I not been Yunnan station chief as well as security-defence head, I could not have led the other open secret-service organizations in Yunnan, nor addressed them in the security-defence head's name; so I had always used the Yunnan station's alias and the station chief's alias to direct all the open secret-service organizations in Yunnan.

The Yunnan station's alias was then Chang Niannong, the station chief's alias Wu Chongyu. I had begun using these two aliases only after becoming Yunnan station chief in May 1948. As for whether, once the Yunnan Guerrilla Command was set up, the commander would appear under my real name or still as Wu Chongyu, it was not yet formally decided, so every time I called the open units' heads to a meeting I still notified them as Wu Chongyu.

From early November, the agents in charge of the various Kunming units, to discuss the contingency plan and the setting up of the Guerrilla Command, often met until deep in the night. The "guerrilla plan" agreed on was: the men in two batches; one, led by me, of the security-defence department, the criminal-police corps, the security-police corps, the fourth-district highway bureau and fourth transport office traffic-police corps, the 53rd Arsenal police group and guard corps, plus the gang ruffians the Kunming agents controlled — some five or six thousand in all — to withdraw with Li Mi's 8th Army toward western Yunnan and find a base at the China-Burma border; the other, led by the railway police head Su Zixiu, of the police-department agents and railway traffic-police forces and some railway staff, to withdraw along the railway toward the China-Vietnam border.

The most sinister and vicious part of the whole "contingency plan," the part Mao Renfeng most admired, was the scheme, before the PLA advanced on Kunming, to arrange a false withdrawal and true arrest, taking in one net all Kunming's democratic figures and Communist underground men. This scheme was not even told to some minor backbone men of the Yunnan station and security-defence department; orders were to be issued separately only at execution.

We meant, after the Nationalist troops withdrew from Kunming to near Bijiguan, to have a few merchants, doctors and the like arranged in advance come forward to sponsor a preparatory committee to welcome the PLA into the city and keep order in Kunming for the time. Those willing to do this were the Kunming chamber-of-commerce head Deng Hefeng, the Southwest Silver Shop manager Sun Zishun, the Laotianbao Silver Shop manager Zhu Wengao, the Jipu Trading Company manager Zhang Yunlei, and the doctors Wang Zihan, Gan Lieming, Qin Zuomin and over a dozen others. They were all not going to leave, and did not understand our scheme. They thought that, having my understanding for coming forward to welcome, and being able to show themselves progressive, whichever side came it would be to their good; little knowing that I had readied a batch of agents to wait in Kunming, waiting only for the committee to form, when the truly progressive, thinking it truly "dawn," would come out to take part. It was reckoned Communist underground men and the Yunnan working committee might also send a few to appear. Anyone not pre-arranged joining this organization could be taken for certain as a progressive. The agents could then watch their movements closely and find out their addresses. At their last gathering the agents lying in wait in the suburbs would carry out another planned mass arrest in Kunming, taking those seized overnight to Bijiguan, or killing them in Kunming, and then fleeing.

In this action plan we meant to set fire to some of Kunming's public works — this because we had not enough explosive to destroy them thoroughly. I recall that when I saw Lu Han in early December I told him the Juntong did not mean, before withdrawing, to wreck Kunming as it had other big cities, and Lu much approved, holding that destruction did the Nationalists no good and only made the people hate them more. The Yunnan station's strength was then quite weak; even Chiang Kai-shek's order to detain the Guizhou governor Gu Zhenglun in Kunming could not be carried out, and Gu was let fly safely to Hong Kong with a great batch of US and Hong Kong dollars.

Here let me mention in passing why Chiang Kai-shek wanted Gu Zhenglun detained, and the farce of a power-and-revenge killing within the Nationalists at Guizhou's liberation. After Guiyang was liberated, the Guizhou Pacification Headquarters director and governor Gu Zhenglun fled to Qinglong county. Everyone knew Gu Zhenglun had been military-police commander for years, a butcher who killed like cutting hemp. By then he had only a few remnant security corps as guards. Another famed executioner of the Kang Ze secret-service system, Liu Bolong, was then 89th Army commander in Guizhou, with a batch of beaten troops. These two demons who killed without blinking had been on very bad terms, each wanting a chance to kill the other.

When Gu Zhenglun fled to Qinglong, Liu Bolong seized the chance to force his hand, wanting Gu to yield the Guizhou Pacification directorship to him. Gu had long found this junior an eyesore, and seeing him overbearing, would rather give up the directorship and lose Guizhou than not kill him, to vent the grievance in his heart. Gu Zhenglun conferred with his deputy Han Wenhuan, and on the surface agreed to yield the post to Liu Bolong to act in, inviting him to come and take it over.

Many of Liu Bolong's aides advised against his going himself, for the place-name "Qinglong" sounded like "catch the dragon," and they feared trouble. But Liu Bolong, long wanting the directorship, could not wait, and went to Qinglong with two guard companies in several trucks. When he went to see Gu, the two guard companies were disarmed by a regiment set in place beforehand. When Liu came out in high spirits with the order Gu had entrusted him to act in the post, he was swept with hand-held machine guns by guards Han Wenhuan directed at the county-government hall, and died with over ten bullets in him.

Gu Zhenglun, having killed Liu, was still unsatisfied, and had his guards throw Liu's body into the gully behind, forbidding burial, and prepared to hunt down his family. When it happened, the Bureau's investigation-group head attached to the 89th Army, Zhang Liruo, at once wired the details to the Yunnan station to pass to Taiwan. Chiang Kai-shek was furious and had Mao Renfeng order the Yunnan station that when Gu fled to Kunming he must be detained and sent to Taiwan. By then six regiments of Nationalist military police had withdrawn to Kunming, and Gu, guessing Chiang Kai-shek would pursue him, first found several military-police regiment commanders and saw them. These were his old subordinates, all willing to protect his safety. On getting the order I not only had no way to detain him but had to give him several of the plane tickets I always controlled. When the military police turned out in numbers to protect him onto the plane, I could only go along to see him off, watching helplessly as he fled to Hong Kong.

By then the Yunnan station was even short of guns and ammunition and could not act tough. The US-made weapons Mao Renfeng had promised to allot from Chungking had not come, for lack of air-lift. In the end I had to have the 53rd Arsenal agents stage another self-theft: the arsenal's guard-inspection group and guard corps together stole a great batch of guns and ammunition from the arsenal's stores, and so the agents readied for ambush were armed.

In late November Mao Renfeng directed me to make ready all preparations to deal with Lu Han. In two personally-decoded telegrams he wired me, he twice mentioned "the task discussed at parting must be fully prepared, execute the moment the wire arrives." These bald twenty-odd characters showed that Chiang Kai-shek, while agreeing to Lu Han's demands for more petrol and gold, was preparing to strike his poison blow. But the Nationalist Centre's and the Yunnan military chiefs' and secret-service heads' estimate of the situation was wrong: they thought it would not worsen so fast, and never imagined Lu Han would suddenly declare his uprising ahead of time.

I recall that when I saw Lu for the last time on the afternoon of December 6, after reporting the numbers of agents withdrawn into Yunnan from various places, I also reported intelligence that Communist underground men were already conducting a local peace movement in Kunming. All the military and civil unit heads, myself included, had received the PLA's notice, and I asked him what to do. Lu, after a moment's thought, answered that one should be watchful but need not sweep them up at once, lest it beat the grass, startle the snake and cause chaos and unrest. When he asked just who was doing this, I did not care to say that my intelligence was that his subordinates An Enpu, Yang Wenqing and others had begun contact with Communist underground men, saying only that the specific men were still under investigation. From the content of our talk and his expression I inferred he had long known this, and it was not hard to guess that he himself was the very leader of the activity. So when I asked when the Pacification Headquarters and provincial government would move west, he only answered vaguely that the time had not come. When I asked him to reassign Li Jiajie as Kunming police chief and recommended a Baoshan county magistrate, he gave no definite answer either. Thus the matter grew ever plainer.

Coming out of Lu's house, I saw several senior aides close to Lu, and from their talk confirmed my judgment. That evening I invited the 8th Army commander Li Mi, the 26th Army commander Yu Chengwan, deputy commander Peng Zuoxi and others to secret talks at Yu Chengwan's headquarters at the North Parade Ground. After giving them my talk with Lu that day and my intelligence, they added some on the Yunnan local forces' movements. Li, Yu and the rest all judged Lu's attitude quite plain, the situation past saving, and only full preparation left. They also held that Lu would act at earliest by mid-December, since his dispositions were not yet ready and the PLA was still a good way from Kunming. That night I reported all this and our estimate to Taiwan and asked whether to carry out at once the task Mao Renfeng had left me on leaving. But right up to December 9 no reply came, Mao Renfeng being then not in Taiwan but gone with Chiang Kai-shek to Chengtu. On the morning of the 9th Li Mi, Yu Chengwan and Long Zehui went to Chengtu and saw Chiang Kai-shek. Chiang, seeing Long Zehui had come too, judged Yunnan would have no trouble for the time and that Zhang Qun could handle it, and told Zhang Qun to return to Kunming with them that afternoon. When Lu Han got the news that Zhang Qun was coming to Kunming, he may have thought he could delay no longer, and acted that very day. First the security corps were moved repeatedly and readied for battle. From noon that day all planes arriving at Kunming were barred from taking off again, and even the fuel depot was guarded by security troops, planes forbidden refuelling. Many senior Nationalist officials and their families fleeing from Chengtu via Kunming to Taiwan were detained. The Nationalist air-force fifth-region command had meant to bring the great numbers flying from Chengtu to Taiwan first to Kunming and then on to Taiwan slowly; but as the airfield radio was not yet blockaded, the planes reaching the sky over Kunming that afternoon got the ground's notice and mostly turned back to Chengtu.

By then the Yunnan station's radio could not reach the Chengtu station either, and this sudden emergency at Kunming brought no immediate direction. At noon the Bureau's Southwest special-district chief Xu Yuanju, deputy chief Zhou Yanghao, the Bureau headquarters' management-department head Guo Xu and general-affairs head Cheng Xichao flew in from Chengtu, meaning to go to Taiwan the next day. My mind was then in great turmoil: to leave Kunming at once meant punishment in Taiwan; not to leave meant liberation was at hand. On going or staying I kept wavering. Xu Yuanju and the rest, seeing my unease as I lunched with them, knew things were bad too, but still trusted that Zhang Qun's arrival meant no trouble for at least a few days, and believed Zhang could persuade Lu Han and steady the situation.

But I held that Zhang Qun's very arrival might drive Lu to resolve on an early uprising. To be safe I did not offer them this view, nor tell them of the afternoon's change, fearing they would all flee at once. If peaceful liberation came, I could not then produce these men and would bear the responsibility. For I knew they had not only killed General Yang Hucheng's whole family at Chungking, but killed over three hundred revolutionaries in the SACO compound, killed a great batch more at Chengtu, and wrecked the Chungking and Chengtu arsenals and some public works. These men, reaching Kunming, first sought me. I settled them each separately, meaning to see the day's change before deciding.

Just as I was preparing emergency measures for the worst, an orderly delivered a notice from Zhang Qun inviting me to a meeting at Lu Han's house at ten that night. Xu Yuanju snatched it and looked: it was indeed Zhang Qun's usual seal, and he thought there could be no problem. I saw the Bureau's two department heads who had come with him to the Queen's Hotel to rest, and invited Xu Yuanju to my house. I took out a letter Mao Renfeng had written me on November 20 in his own hand and showed him. It said mainly that, to secure Yunnan as a base and let me command uniformly the guerrilla forces in Yunnan, he had petitioned Chiang Kai-shek's approval to change the Ministry of National Defence Yunnan district commissioner's office into a commissioner's headquarters and the Ministry of National Defence Yunnan Guerrilla Command into a general command, promote me to lieutenant general, enclosing commissions appointing me Yunnan commissioner and guerrilla commander-in-chief; at the same time it pressed me to send my whole family to Taiwan, and stressed again that if Kunming could not be held, southwest Yunnan linking Burma and Vietnam must be kept. I urged Xu Yuanju not to go to Taiwan but to stay in Yunnan and choose one of these two new posts; if he wanted both, I would be his deputy. I held he had more methods than I. Having read Mao Renfeng's letter and heard me out, he thought a while and shook his head: "At this point, make me Southwest Governor to take over Zhang Qun's work, and I still wouldn't do it! Only now to add rank and office — what use is it? I say you'd better leave early too!" I said: "How could I leave, with old subordinates and a great many families who have followed us all these years?" He said: "You mind them — who minds us! This time they can fly straight to Taiwan, and send us here to face the danger. Isn't that lesson enough?"

That night I turned over and over whether to go to the meeting at Lu Han's house on Qingyun Road — whether to go, what would happen, how I would cope if the unexpected occurred. By the Bureau's chain of command, Zhang Qun, though Southwest Governor, could not command me. But as security-defence head I had to go. To be safe I phoned several other Pacification Headquarters department heads; none had been notified. I phoned Lu Han's house to ask Zhang Qun, and was told Director Zhang was very busy, that I should come on time to the meeting and speak to him in person.

Seeing things were very bad, likely a going with no return, I sent Mao Renfeng a last telegram, saying the situation had reached a point past saving, that my own judgment had failed and I had fallen short of my duty and could not accomplish the task. I at once gathered the responsible agents of the Yunnan station and security-defence department, enjoining them that after I went to the meeting, the deputy head Pei XX would be in charge; they were to make all necessary preparations and await my word. If I had not phoned back by eleven, they were to burn the files and rolls and, fully armed, break through to the nearby 26th Army troops. Before leaving I handed my diary, pistol and the coded telegrams to Pei XX, and went carrying nothing. Getting into the car, thinking mine was faster and handier in emergency, I even swapped it for the old car Pei used, and drove straight to Lu's house. I understood well that the task Chiang Kai-shek and Mao Renfeng had given me I could no longer accomplish, that staying in Kunming and fleeing Kunming were both death, with no third road, and I could only steel myself and go to the meeting.

### The Peaceful Liberation of Yunnan

At ten on the night of December 9, 1949, in a sitting room of Mr. Lu Han's house on Qingyun Road in Kunming, sat seven men in trim uniforms with decorations on their chests. None spoke; they looked at one another and chain-smoked. They were: the Nationalist 8th Army commander and Yunnan district training commander Li Mi; the 26th Army commander Yu Chengwan; the 26th Army's Xth Division commander Shi Butian; the military-police deputy commander-in-chief and Southwest-region commander Li Chufan; the military-police Southwest-region command chief of staff Tong Helian; the air-force fifth-region deputy commander Shen Yanshi; and the Bureau of Secrets Preservation's Yunnan station chief and Yunnan Pacification Headquarters security-defence head Shen Zui. These seven were then the chief obstructors of Yunnan's peaceful liberation, the Yunnan people's fiercest enemies. They had racked every wit and spent every strength to stop this day from coming. But now they were helpless, turtles in the jar, unable to flaunt their savagery any longer. They had all come to an emergency meeting on notice from the Southwest Governor Zhang Qun just arrived at Kunming, yet had not even the chance to see Director Zhang. Several men coming in from Mr. Lu's new mansion on Cuihu East Road, seeing Zhang Qun sitting alone in another large sitting room, could only shrug, put out the tongue, spread the hands — everything was over. This most trusted, famous mastermind at Chiang Kai-shek's side was now without a plan. From the arrangement of the sitting room, inside and out, it was already fully plain what this was.

In such tension I still tried to phone the Yunnan station once, but was politely stopped. Then Shen Yanshi suddenly fished a telegram from his pocket and handed it to Li Mi and Yu Chengwan. It was Chiang Kai-shek's personal wire, relayed by the air-force radio that afternoon after he learned of the sudden detention of planes at Kunming, telling Shen Yanshi to notify Li and Yu at once to return to their defences, lead their troops on Kunming, and comply with all speed. Li Mi, reading it, at once threw the telegram to the floor and demanded of Shen Yanshi why he had not notified them earlier. Shen said he could not find them at the time and had thought the evening meeting would do, so had not sought them again. The two quarrelled, and after others' intercession held that, things being so, they could only submit to fate.

About eleven, Lu Han's guard-battalion commander Long, with fourteen pistol-armed soldiers, walked into the sitting room, said "ordered to search," and had two soldiers watch each man while Long searched us one by one in person. They watched me most closely and searched me first. This much disappointed them: on me, besides a few taels of gold, was nothing, and even my overcoat hung outside was brought in and searched carefully, still nothing. They could not hold back and asked me: "Where's the pistol you carry every day?" I said with a smile: "A pistol's no use in this situation; I left it at home long ago." Only then did they search the others at ease. I knew well that at any move of mine they would shoot me dead first. They likely thought me the harder to handle and reckoned I might resist, but I had long made full mental preparation.

When they searched Li Chufan, a soldier's pistol, perhaps from too much tension, went off; a shot rang out, and all thought Lu meant to finish us off in this splendid sitting room, and looked at one another. Only when Commander Long harshly rebuked the soldier did all grow a little easier. After the search the sitting room grew quiet again; all still silent, each with his own thoughts, only smoking and drinking tea for dear life. From then even going to the toilet was forbidden, so we had to relieve ourselves in the spittoons in the sitting room. Had these several men, used to lording it over the people, become so tame? No! Though they dared not resist on the surface, in their hearts they were reckoning how to take revenge — as could be seen from their grinding teeth and their neurotic pounding of the sofa arms with their fists. None was reconciled to death; each still fancied finding a chance for one last struggle to vent the day's grievance. About two in the deep night, Lu's aide called Li Mi and Yu Chengwan out of the sitting room into a car and drove off. The rest guessed whether they were being taken out to be finished off separately. In under half an hour the aide came again for me and Li Chufan. In the passage I reckoned this was execution, and drawing the gold bars from my pocket said quietly to the guard: "Mate! Shoot straight." For I thought one shot to finish it was well enough, but half-dead-half-alive was hard to bear. But the guard paid me no heed, and I could only follow them to the front door, where the aide had us get into a car. Seeing the car drive straight toward the Wuhuashan provincial-government building, I was relieved. On the roads now stood a post every three paces, a sentry every five, barbed wire and sandbags at the crossings. At Wuhuashan we were taken to the chairman's office on the fourth floor, and seeing Li and Yu, who had come first, talking with Zhang Qun's aide, the Southwest Governor's Office secretariat chief Zhou Junliang, we knew Mr. Lu had this time made the greatest resolve. When Zhang Qun came, Lu had not even seen him once but put him under house arrest, his aides lodged in the chairman's office. We seven were brought in one after another, each provided a camp bed and bedding — it seemed we were to stay here a while.

Worn out by the day, I undressed and lay down to sleep. Li Mi came over and kicked me, saying angrily: "How can you still sleep?" I said with a smile: "Losing our heads is tomorrow's business; today, if I can sleep, I sleep." As I spoke I pointed out the window, meaning what was there to discuss in such a case? Toward four, the Kunming mayor Zeng Shuhuai and Li Shuoqiao and a crowd of acquaintances came in, reassured us again and again, told us to settle down, and explained this was a last resort, hoping we would support Mr. Lu's action. By then no one had anything to say, and we could only fob them off and, dressed, drop onto the beds to sleep.

About nine on the morning of the 10th, the commissioner Yang Wenqing took us out separately to the meeting room on the third floor and introduced us to Mr. Song Yihen, public-security head of the just-formed Yunnan Military and Political Committee. They took out copies of a circular telegram prepared in advance supporting Lu Han's uprising, had us each sign, and asked all to produce their "capital."

The circular they had drafted for me had some phrasing a little at odds with the Juntong's habitual style. I thought a moment: if this document they had drafted were published, the agents would see at a glance it was not written by me and would not, as it required, turn themselves in, hand over weapons and radios. To be honest, I did not then really mean to go over to the Communists and follow Mr. Lu's uprising with a whole heart; only, having lost my freedom, I feared that if I could not gather up the mess I had made, I would in the end not escape responsibility. Weighing personal interest, I explained this to Yang and Song and offered to change some of the phrases to the Juntong's habitual style, and even to copy out a version myself for publication and broadcast on the radio. They much praised this and asked me to produce my whole "capital." I handed over Xu Yuanju (Xu Pengfei), Zhou Yanghao (Shen Yangzhai), Guo Xu and Cheng Xichao, and handed over the Yunnan station's twenty-odd radios large and small and the dozen-plus radios of other provinces moved into Yunnan, weapons, all important agents, undercover radios and undercover organizations. Yang Wenqing, seeing my conduct, was much surprised, having thought me the most diehard and hardest to handle, and was pleased I could produce that "capital" of my own accord.

When all the formalities were done, Yang Wenqing asked me whether I wished to leave or to stay and work with them. He said if I wished to leave there were still planes to Hong Kong. I said at once I wished to stay. For I knew well: if I went to Taiwan, they would hold that I had neither managed to hold Yunnan and stop Lu Han's uprising, nor "died for the cause" for them, but had meekly handed over these men and organizations, and going there I would surely be killed. And I had done all the PLA's Kunming leaflets required, so at least I would not be sentenced to death. My family was all in Hong Kong, not yet gone to Taiwan, and would suffer nothing and could be brought back later, so I chose to stay.

When I got back to the fourth floor I saw Li Mi leaning at the window. As I went over, tears streaming, he took my hand and said: "See how we can face people like this — better to die!" With that he made to jump from the fourth-floor window; I seized him and, after much persuasion, he said furiously: "Damn it, if we're to rise, we couldn't rise ourselves — we had to wait to be led by the nose!" On the 11th the *Yunnan Daily* came out, reorganized from the *Common People's Daily*. Its front page carried Mr. Lu's circular of uprising and the circular of Li Mi, Yu Chengwan, Li Chufan, Shen Zui and others supporting Lu Han's uprising. The radio kept broadcasting the speeches of us several men, calling on our subordinates to lay down arms and answer Yunnan's peaceful liberation. That morning we saw a large passenger plane take off from Kunming airfield, and at lunch, when Long Zehui, Yang Wenqing, Zeng Shuhuai and the rest came as usual to keep us company at table, we learned that Zhang Qun and his aides, and the Juntong chief and acting Nationalist Yunnan party head Pei Cunfan, had gone to Hong Kong by Cathay plane. After Mr. Lu sent off the senior agent Pei Cunfan, he had Yang Wenqing ask me again whether to go or stay, and I still said stay.

On the morning of the 12th, at Li Mi's proposal, we seven were to become sworn brothers, having this rare chance to share life and death, and all approved. By age, Yu Chengwan was eldest, Li Chufan second, Li Mi third, Shen Yanshi fourth, Shi Butian fifth, Tong Helian sixth, and I youngest, seventh. By then Li and Yu had been named members of the Yunnan provisional military and political committee, but were still dissatisfied at having no freedom of movement. None of the seven was reconciled to the death of the old order, and they kept studying how, once free, to stage a revenge action and put liberated Yunnan back under Chiang Kai-shek's and their own rule. They picked up again the decorations and collar-tabs they had torn off and thrown in the wastebasket, and kept them for reuse. After dinner that day, Long Zehui told us the circular had gone out but no reply had come from Peking, which worried him much. After he left we were all pleased, hoping Peking would slight the action and let them regret it, for with a little pressure from outside there might yet be another turncoat move.

From the 13th the 26th Army began attacking Kunming from the southeast corner, and a replenishment regiment of the 8th Army stationed at Dabanqiao clashed with the security corps. That morning Long Zehui came with Li Mi's wife to see Li Mi. Yang Wenqing and Long Zehui passed on Lu's wish that Li Mi tell his subordinates not to attack but await reorganization. Li wrote a letter for his wife to take to Dabanqiao and explain to them. That afternoon Li's wife returned with a reply agreeing to accept Li's order but hoping Li himself could return to the troops soon. Li, having read it, handed it to Yang Wenqing. But once Yang walked off, Li lifted his wife's cheongsam to show Li Mi: inside was a secret letter whose content was the very opposite of the open reply. They said they would not stop attacking but would concentrate all their strength on Kunming to rescue Li Mi. Li's wife also told Li, Yu and the rest that the security corps had killed and wounded no few of the 8th Army officers' families, and they meant revenge and would not stop. All were furious and, after conferring again, resolved to find a chance to leave Wuhuashan first and then plan the next step.

On the 14th, the 26th Army deputy commander Peng Zuoxi, having gathered the units stationed nearby, charged fiercely on Kunming's southeast corner, and gunfire was heard in the city. Lu Han agreed to Yu Chengwan's proposal to let the division commander Shi Butian go out to pass Yu Chengwan's order to stop the attack. In fact this was a scheme Yu Chengwan and Shi Butian had arranged in advance, secretly telling the troops to press the attack harder. That afternoon Shi Butian came back saying that unless Yu Chengwan were let out there was no stopping the 26th Army's attack.

By then the few agents still lurking in the city who had not surrendered were also stirring, preparing to work inside in concert with the outside. Though they saw my circular in the papers they did not believe it. The Military and Political Committee had what I wrote in my own hand made into a zinc plate and printed, posting it in many places. The great majority of these remnant agents were caught or reported and surrendered that evening or the next day, but a few still fancied that once Kunming was broken they would have another go. By then Nationalist light bombers too came often, two or three at a time, dropping bombs on Kunming to intimidate, and the situation grew ever tenser.

On the 16th and 17th, Yu Chengwan, Shi Butian and Li Mi were released in succession. Before leaving they said farewell to their newly sworn brothers one by one, telling us to hold out and wait for them to fight into Kunming and rescue us. The moment these three were released they turned their faces: the fighting not only did not stop but pressed on Kunming more madly, gunfire audible all night and drawing nearer. A few 26th Army units had already reached Deshengqiao near Tuodong Road, and the provincial-government office building at Wuhuashan came under fire too.

On the morning of the 19th, Lu decided to move me, Shen Yanshi, Li Chufan and Tong Helian to the Model Prison on Qianju Street, for Wuhuashan was bombed daily and no longer safe, and with the two armies attacking ceaselessly, Kunming might have to be given up for a time. When we four reached the Model Prison, we saw several hundred Nationalist military and civil officials and agents already held there. That night machine-gun fire from the nearby city wall could be heard clearly. All were very tense and torn: on one hand hoping the Nationalist troops would break into Kunming at once and rescue these men, on the other fearing being killed before Kunming was taken.

After the 21st the gunfire receded, and a section chief Chen in the prison told me: "At the tensest time I was nearly your filial son [ready to bury you]; now it's no longer a problem." The few security corps then defending Kunming were far below the Nationalist troops in equipment and numbers, but with the full support of the Kunming people they at last drove this savage enemy back. In an emergency of near-exhausted ammunition and grain, the whole city's people brought out all they had to support their own sons and brothers holding Kunming, and drove the enemy off at the last.

After the 22nd the threat to Kunming lifted, and the whole city's people burst into joy, drums and gongs sounding everywhere, singing and dancing wildly; the Kunming people won their final victory before a strong enemy. The whole of Yunnan was liberated. This was the strength of the Yunnan people, and still more the victory of the Communist Party's and Chairman Mao's wise policy, and it was a contribution Mr. Lu Han made to his country and to the people of Yunnan.
